Project Summary

Contribute to improve the food security status and reduce the negative coping mechanisms of food insecure people in Northwest Syria through the distribution of
1) Monthly food assistance through Cash for Food or E-voucher ($70 per household regardless of household size; subject to change) for vulnerable displaced persons and host community members in need;
2) Monthly food baskets for vulnerable displaced persons and host community members in need. One food basket (25kg) per household per month regardless of household size, based on the monthly needs of a family of five
3) Emergency MPC ($150 per household as per the Cash Working Group’s current recommendation, regardless of household size; subject to change) for vulnerable HHs recently displaced by conflict or natural disaster as part of a rapid response mechanism;
4) Ready-To-Eat-Rations (RTERs) assistance, one-off, for newly displaced or crisis-affected households as part of a rapid response mechanism. Two RTERs (14kg each) per household for one month regardless of household size, based on the monthly needs of a family of five.
5) Short term income generating activity through cash for work (CfW) in agricultural activities.
For beneficiaries living in camps, blanket selection will be applied. Before implementing CVA, IOM will conduct a market functionality assessment to ensure local suppliers are able to absorb the extra demand for goods and services. IOM will also monitor local prices to mitigate risks of inflation as a result of the injection of cash in the community.

3 - Needs

Over four million people continue to be in need in NWS. With 2.8 million people still living in displacement, there continues to be a high need for life-saving humanitarian assistance to affected households as well as communities in addressing short- and long-term needs. The economic situation in NWS remains dire, and 90 per cent of the population is in poverty. Key drivers of food insecurity in the region include low agricultural output, ongoing hostilities, and disruption to global food markets linked to the war in Ukraine. Over half of IDPs (58%) report borderline or poor food consumption. Moreover, the price of the standard monthly food basket in Syria is currently among the highest ever reported (USD 111.51). Food is consistently ranked among the top three priorities of IDPs and returnees in NWS.

4 - Implementation of activities

• Selection of implementing partner(s).• Selection of locations based on needs and gaps in food severity ranking and FSL Cluster’s gaps analysis.• Registration of beneficiaries by IPs. Verification of 20% of beneficiaries by IOM M&E through commissioned TPM. • Initial assessment of FCS and CSI scores.• CfW: Identify activities through community assessments and consultations.• Provide CfW opportunities to selected beneficiaries • Food baskets and RTERs: Selection of food vendor via competitive tendering, production of boxes, laboratory testing of samples at source to ensure safety and quality and storage of boxes in vendor warehouse. Transportation of boxes into Syria, Storage of boxes in IP warehouse selected in coordination with IOM.• Conduct sensitization sessions with site managers, IDP committees and IDPs.• Distribution of food assistance under the observation of IOM’s TPM.• Cash: contract a financial service provider (FSP) in Syria approved by IOM Compliance.• Distribution of cash to beneficiaries from the FSP’s funds; IP to reimburse the FSP within three days of the distribution.• E-voucher: contract food vendor in Syria approved by IOM Program and Compliance.• Distribution of E-vouchers to beneficiaries; beneficiaries to procure food items from vendors within the month.• Conduct distribution monitoring by IOM TPM in 100% of distribution sites as well as exit interviews.• Conduct satisfaction survey (PDM) end of the project to measure change in FCS and RCS

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risks: 1. Beneficiaries can be exposed to violence, theft, or other kinds of abuse at or after leaving the distribution sites. 2. Cash can be used for anti-social purposes such as the purchase of arms or alcohol. 3. Corrupt diversion or seizure of cash by armed groups and may expose beneficiaries to security risks.4. Pressure from the local authorities on who should or should not receive the assistance. 5. The selection of beneficiaries can lead to tensions among the communities.6. Risk of Covid-19 spreading at distribution sites, or of distributions being cancelled due to Covid-19. Mitigation measures: 1. Set the point of delivery close to a beneficiary’s homes or provide transport to ensure safety.2. Financial service providers are vetted prior to each project and never have access to the beneficiary list so as to mitigate any interference from local actors.3. Thorough needs assessments and targeting ensures that the beneficiaries are likely to use the cash assistance primarily to cover their food needs.4. Raise awareness with implementing partners about the importance of principled humanitarian assistance, and reporting any incidents of interference.5. Explain the selection and vulnerability criteria clearly to the communities via the IPs and IOM AAP.6. Enforce all required hygiene measures including social distancing, sanitation, PPE, spread distributions over a longer period with fewer beneficiaries per day, or distribute door-to-door.


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

Risks:- Abuse or exploitation of beneficiaries by IP staff during beneficiary selection and distribution processes.- Lack of staff awareness and code of conduct on PSEA.- Lack of availability of and information on reporting mechanisms for beneficiaries.- Lack of female IP staff in the field. Mitigation/prevention measures:- Code of conduct includes PSEA language.- All IP staff are trained on PSEA.- Internal SEA reporting mechanism for staff and investigative capacity exist and are known within the organization. - PSEA awareness raising activities and materials for beneficiaries and local communities are conducted and shared during the activities and reporting mechanisms for beneficiaries are available. - All field activities are supervised by humanitarian staff, including female staff members to mitigate PSEA and other protection risks.- Beneficiaries regularly receive information regarding the free and unconditional nature of humanitarian aid and know about and have access to complaint mechanisms and hotlines.
