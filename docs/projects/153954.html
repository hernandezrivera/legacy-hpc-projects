Project Summary

NRC's overall objective is that Iraqi children and youth affected by conflict and displacement are able to achieve improved protection and socio-economic prospects vital for achieving durable solutions. Education interventions will target displaced (in and out of camps), returnee, remainee and host community children and youth, including persons with disabilities. NRC seeks to improve access to and strengthen basic formal education and non-formal education and address the three-year learning gaps many children face. Children will be supported to perform at grade level within the formal school system, passing national examinations and completing the academic cycle, enabling them to access further education. Youth are able to access existing or even create new livelihood opportunities, contribute to community initiatives and generation of household income and are more acknowledged as valued members of their communities.

Needs

2.3 million school-aged children require assistance to access education in 2019. 41% of children have limited or no access to formal or non-formal learning, with girls and adolescents particularly affected. At least 70% of IDPs expect to remain, rather than to return to their areas of origin in 2019. The 2019 HNO found that 27% of in-camp households and 30% of remainee households reported education as a priority need. Ninewa and Anbar have the lowest net enrolment rates for primary education and secondary education, with an average of 83% and 88% of children enrolled in primary education in these governorates. In Ninewa and Anbar, only 80% and 86% of females respectively are enrolled in primary education, dropping to 25% and 39% female enrolment in secondary education. In KR-I, formal school enrolment data for 2017-2018 shows gender disparity in access to primary education (55% male students and 45% for females). These findings demonstrate the prevalent issue of access to education, and equitable access by gender, across conflict-affected governorates in Iraq.Schools are often in poor condition and are inadequately equipped to serve large numbers of students. More than 50% of the existing schools in conflict affected areas need rehabilitation, including schools which were heavily damaged by active fighting. Shortage of school infrastructure results in the use of multiple shifts to accommodate the increasing numbers of children. There is a 32% gap in the required number of teachers across the conflict-affected governorates, affecting the quality of education provided. Inequitable distribution of teachers across schools, low and irregular salaries and poorly trained teachers compound teaching quality issues. Teachers are also ill equipped to support children who may have psychological distress, and schools often lack teaching and learning materials.Children and youth have lost three to four years of education, and require significant support to re-enrol in formal education and to make up the learning competencies they missed. 30% of families reported not being able to afford to send their children to school. Out of school children continue to face structural barriers to education, including a lack of documentation or accurate information to complete the enrolment process, and distance from the nearest formal school. Many children need to support their families either in the household or through child labour.Children and youth face obstacles in accessing and enrolling into educational facilities including vocational schools and ALP centres. In some locations, these facilities are unavailable. Employment remains one of the top priority needs, particularly in areas of return. Youth often lack the basic education level, skills, trades and assets or capital support to enable them to find employment or self-employment opportunities. This leaves youth vulnerable to negative coping mechanisms and despondency through their lack of agency, empowerment or alternative options. Early marriage affects both female and male youth, with pressure on males to provide household income causing early drop-out rates, and females dropping out due to household responsibilities. Iraq has one of the youngest populations in the world, with an estimated youth unemployment rate of 18% in 2017. In areas affected by conflict, youth unemployment is significantly higher. For example in west Mosul 80% of youth aged 18-25 are currently unemployed, while 91% lost at least three years of education.

Activities

1.1 Conduct teacher training and mentoring on the Teachers in Crisis Contexts (TiCC) training pack 1.2 Provide training on the Better Learning Programme (BLP) to provide teachers with the skills required to embed generalised psychosocial support within classroom learning1.3 Provide training on Preventing Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) and Child Safeguarding to reduce the risk of violence and harm against children 1.4 Put in place reporting mechanisms and referral pathways for PSEA and Child Safeguarding cases 1.5 Ensure the inclusion of internationally recognized learning assessments (e.g. ASER) in projects at baseline and end-line 1.6 Distribution of teaching and learning materials (TLM) relevant to the context and training teachers on their use 1.7 Provide remedial classes to enable children to catch up with key skills and learning they have missed (specifically for children who are below their grade level in literacy and numeracy standards)1.8 Deliver targeted summer school programmes to support children who are either: out-of-school to return to learning; for those who have missed significant periods of education and are in the lower quartile for learning competencies for their age-level, and for children who are preparing to sit national examinations 1.9 Coordinate and lead back-to school enrolment campaigns before the start of the next academic year1.10 Provide stationary for children (including back to school kits), recreational items and school furniture 1.11 Provide teacher incentives when they are not provided by the government, to support qualified teacher retention 1.12 Provide cash for transport stipends to enable the most vulnerable children to attend secondary schools 1.13 Engage outreach workers to assess what would be needed for out of school children to return to learning and provide internal and external referrals for additional cross-sectoral and integrated assistance.1.14 School rehabilitation to continue to improve the number of usable classrooms available in schools1.15 Provide WASH support, including the construction or rehabilitation of gender-segregated latrines and water points 1.16 Provide water storage tanks, hygiene materials and sanitary materials for adolescent girls 1.17 Support formal schools with the development of enrolment and attendance databases, training Deputy Head Teachers and focal teachers on the data entry, analysis and use of these databases to improve school record keeping and to follow up with children who drop-out or are at risk of dropping out (those who show infrequent attendance).1.18 Support Parent Teacher Associations (PTAs) through regular trainings and coaching, with the PTA manual revised to make it more relevant to the current context.1.19 Provide youth with vocational and classroom based learning. This would include literacy and numeracy classes, life skills training and business management training.1.20 Engage youth with community initiatives to support social engagement and to contribute positively to their community.1.21 Provide youth with mentoring and career guidance. 1.22 Support youth to access capital and participate in income generating activities linked to supply chains and apprenticeships upon completion of training and online courses1.23 Implement youth programmes with more flexible hours and courses, including blended ICT training and learning1.24 Conduct market and labour assessments to identify trade, skill and product gaps in local markets and the opportunities available for youth programmes1.25 Consider childcare arrangements for learners with young children within the programme.

Protection Risk Analysis

Protection risks include: children and youth being unable to attend school, or dropping out of school; corporal punishment in school, and risk of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse pose critical protection risks with girls having a heightened vulnerability; early marriage and pregnancy are further protection risks; and child labour (whether it is girls being kept at home to support their siblings and/or mothers with household chores or boys who drop out of school to support the family income). The exclusion of marginalised or more vulnerable groups from education programmes poses another protection risk, as does the denial of access to education services due to a lack of civil documentation and ID. Schools, or the grounds of schools, are still threatened by unexploded ordnance, posing an extreme protection risk, as well as trauma from associated from seeing badly damaged school buildings, due to rocket or gunfire, as well as IS graffiti.

Mitigation Measures

NRC will conduct awareness and sensitisation campaigns about the importance of girls' education through outreach workers and mother groups. Specific programming will be considered for children with disabilities to encourage their access, participation and integration in education programmes, and teachers will be trained in Child Safeguarding and Preventing Sexual Exploitation and Abuse in Schools. This would include rolling out focal points, a no tolerance policy, code of conducts and referral pathways. Where possible, NRC will seek to recruit at least 50% female teacher facilitators. NRC will also carry out the construction of gender segregated latrines in schools and the distribution of hygiene/dignity kits for adolescent girls. and will conduct school assessments, to establish which schools are prioritised for rehabilitation. If the presence of unexploded ordnance is suspected, NRC will engage with an appropriate mine clearing agency. Flexible programmes for youth would include childcare provision and flexi-hours, and opportunities for women who cannot work outside the household. All teacher training will emphasize the unacceptability of corporal punishment, and will provide alternative methods which teachers can use to support classroom management.

How is your project accountable to affected population

NRC would ensure a participatory approach in project and programme design through qualitative and quantitative measures. Beneficiaries will be able to input directly into the project design and implementation in different ways. NRC’s M&E system for this project will emphasize the following key principles: emphasis on participatory approaches, beneficiary participation, relevance, complaints, response, accountability and timeliness of information and data. To provide beneficiary and non-beneficiary feedback about the project, NRC will establish an accountability system to ensure information sharing and participation from beneficiaries to improve the quality if programmes offered by NRC at all stages of the project cycle. Feedback will be collected through public advertisement, phone hotlines, beneficiary complaint boxes, FGDs, and dedicated staff in the accountability focal point on the education teams. NRC will also conduct periodic evaluation of project activities and this will enable the team to capture the lessons learned from past successes and failures, with the goal of improving future performance. It will be an opportunity for the team to reflect on a project so that lessons learned can be generated and shared within and outside the organisation. Parent Teacher Associations (PTAs), Mother Groups and Youth Committees would be established or strengthened in project locations to facilitate community level inputs and decision making.
