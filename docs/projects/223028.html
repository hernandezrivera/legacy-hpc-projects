Project Summary

For 2025, this project will provide primary and secondary healthcare services to refugees and host communities in Cox’s Bazar, following the "Minimum Package of Essential Health Services for Primary Healthcare Facilities in Refugee/FDMN camps, Cox’s Bazar" and the "Standard Operating Procedure for Patient Referrals to Secondary and Tertiary Healthcare Facilities for Refugees/FDMNs, Cox’s Bazar." It will align with Cox's Bazar Health Sector Strategic Plan.
The project will support eight primary healthcare centers (PHCs), one health post, and five drop-in centers to serve diverse demographics. The PHCs will include two in-patient mental health facilities to treat severe mental health cases. Access to services for persons with disabilities (PWDs) and elderly individuals will be facilitated through supported referrals and tailored services, including specialized equipment.
Mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) services will continue to be incorporated in all primary healthcare facilities, and evidence-based psychological counseling approaches will be strengthened at the community level. Trained para-counselors and community psychosocial volunteers will ensure more accessible care and strengthen community-based MHPSS. While psychologists and mh-GAP trained healthcare staff will provide specialized support to refugees with moderate and severe mental health needs. The project will strengthen MHPSS integration with the WASH, nutrition, legal protection, child protection, and gender-based violence (GBV) sectors.
The project will ensure secondary and tertiary medical referrals as per the SOP from all health facilities supported by UNHCR. In addition, lifesaving emergency cases from UNHCR areas of responsibility as agreed earlier will be supported. Emergency patient referrals will be provided through ambulances. A robust community health system will support disease surveillance, early detection, and response to health threats, while health promotion activities will strengthen community resilience. Coordinated by the Community Health Working Group (CHWG), partners will ensure harmonized community-based health interventions, and CHWs will receive toolkits, IEC materials, and capacity building.
Monthly health forums will allow community feedback and include refugee involvement in service delivery. Camp health focal points will continue collaborating with camp actors to plan and supervise health services at the camp level using established UNHCR and Health Sector tools.
The project will conduct monitoring through age- and gender-disaggregated data collection systems, including the community-based health information system, UNHCR's integrated refugee health information system (irHIS), the MOHFW's District Health Information Systems-2 (DHIS2), and the Health Sector’s 4Ws and Early Warning Alert and Response System. Data will be used to guide program improvements. For the quality of care, UNHCR global tools Balanced Score Card will be used in all health facilities supported by UNHCR.
Project coordination will extend to the Health Sector and key working groups (SRH, Epidemiology, MHPSS) and the Emergency Preparedness and Response Committee. The goal is to foster partner-led, integrated health and nutrition services by the end of 2025, with collaboration extending to WASH, protection, and GBV sectors.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

While the health of Rohingya refugees and nearby host communities remains stable, it is vulnerable to emergencies and in need of improvement. As of September 2023, the crude mortality rate in UNHCR-supported areas was 0.27 per 1,000 people per month. However, maternal and neonatal mortality rates remain concerning. By October 2024, the SRH Working Group reported 38 maternal deaths, with stillbirths and neonatal deaths making up 24% of mortalities (Epidemiological Working Group). By October 2024, UNHCR-supported facilities had handled 371,131 consultations, with respiratory tract and skin infections being the primary morbidity drivers along with high number of diarrheal cases. The 2024 dengue outbreak resulted in 13,084 cases and six fatalities as of October 2024 (Health Sector Dengue Sitrep, October 2023). Since June 23, 2024, there have been 234 confirmed cholera cases and 35 suspected AWD/cholera cases pending confirmation, with recent spread to Camp 13. No cholera-related deaths have been reported. The Community Health Working Group (CHWG) plays a key role in delivering awareness messages at the household level and coordinating campaigns like the Oral Cholera Vaccination campaign. In 2024, sporadic outbreaks of dengue, diphtheria, and cholera underscored the need for consistent community engagement and collaboration with WASH to prevent and respond to communicable diseases, alongside strengthening case management. Prioritizing vaccinations for children under five is crucial, as low coverage increases the risk of preventable diseases. The role of a strong community health system is evident; by October 2024, the community health workforce had referred 1,200,212 refugees for medical care, including 30,215 for AWD and 36,321 with dengue symptoms.Efforts to increase family planning uptake must include leveraging the community health workforce to distribute family planning commodities as per the family planning strategy. Training CHWs in measuring blood pressure and referring patients to health facilities is essential for better non-communicable disease (NCD) management. Despite 84% of births occurring in facilities, maternal mortality remains disproportionately high. There is a gap in identifying high-risk pregnancies, contributing to maternal deaths even with an ANC 4 coverage rate above 80%. Reducing maternal and neonatal mortality requires targeted capacity building and regular on-the-job training for healthcare staff. Conducting timely audits of maternal and perinatal deaths and implementing actionable recommendations are key to lowering mortality rates. Referral systems in the camps continue to face challenges. In September 2024, UNHCR supported 5,910 referrals for life-saving interventions, connecting patients to secondary and tertiary care outside the camps. However, access to these services remains limited for chronic cases due to resource constraints and gaps in public health facilities. By September 2023, over 6,230 individuals had received clinical mental health consultations in UNHCR-supported facilities. Psychologists provided counseling to 3,985 people, and 214,718 were reached through community-based MHPSS activities, including 2,133 follow-up visits. Moving forward, maintaining consistent quality support supervision and addressing technical capacity gaps will be essential.For adolescent Rohingya, previous research findings indicate that over half (55%) of adolescents interviewed in the Rohingya camps in Cox’s Bazar show signs of at least mild depression (O'Connor & Seager, 2021). However, there remains a critical need for linguistically appropriate and culturally adapted psychotherapeutic methods for this age group, a gap that UNHCR plans to address in 2025 by leveraging specialized expertise in this area.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

The project will ensure that mental health and psychosocial support services (MHPSS) are available in the areas where not only health, but also protection services, are delivered. This includes community-based protection, gender-based violence (GBV) services such as clinical management of rape in women's and girls' safe spaces and child protection services. Health facilities will promote accessibility for persons with physical and mental disabilities through service provision and infrastructure enhancements. The project will ensure that religious and faith leaders, GBV case workers and child protection staff are engaged in order to support vulnerable populations. To link registration services with health services, the project will utilize the global distribution tool for health services when advantageous, such as for the distribution of general health cards. The project will ensure that the different needs and diversities of the population, including people with disabilities, are taken into account in planning and service delivery through community-based feedback mechanisms.

2.3 - Age & Disability

The project will provide services for specific age groups, disaggregating data based on the various age groups. Specific needs of different age categories will be considered when planning health services, including separate consultation rooms and in-patient facilities for children and adults. Adolescent sexual and reproductive health services will continue at health facilities and adolescent-friendly spaces will be maintained to support responsiveness to the needs of adolescents. The community health workforce will support older persons needing healthcare services by escorting them or arranging for transfer to health facilities and back to the household. Programs for women of reproductive age (15 – 49 years) will be strengthened, to ensure that the reproductive health of women is safeguarded. UNHCR is committed to preventing child abuse and exploitation of refugees; all partner staff will be trained on child safeguarding principles in order to understand the standards and conduct expected from all staff, volunteers, and contractors working in health facilities. Messages to improve the prevention of any form of abuse among the beneficiaries of all ages, boys and girls aged under five years, adolescent girls, and women of reproductive age will be included in health education sessions to increase awareness. Reporting any form of child abuse or behaviors violating the safeguarding principles will be monitored by UNHCR and partners, and immediate actions to resolve the issues identified will be followed up.The project will additionally provide services to people with disabilities at the health facilities including eye care, hearing losses, physiotherapy among other. The health facilities will be disability friendly to facilitate access of services to this vulnerable group.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

UNHCR will monitor the feedback and complaints mechanism put in place by the partner to ensure that they adhere to the acceptable UNHCR standards, including capacity enhancement of all programme and clinical managers to ensure the AAP mainstreaming as a continuous monitoring point (with support from AAP focal point in UNHCR).Accountability to the affected population will be ensured throughout the project cycle, and participation of the refugees in the different stages of programming through consultations will be implemented. UNHCR will ensure that partners establish feedback and complaints mechanisms in all the centres with a clear plan to address any issues that arise in an integrated manner. This will also include the provision of different methods of communication with refugees, who will be engaged in awareness raising efforts with regard to the different programs, new programmes or any changes that may have an impact on their access to health services.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

In, 2024 UNHCR has strengthened the implementation of recommendations for improvements of the sites and training of staff on GBV, referrals, and linkages with relevant sectors for additional support. Assessments of partners' understanding of key protection principles and mainstreaming will be jointly implemented by UNHCR's protection and health units. All UNHCR health partners including the staff in the field have been trained on the prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA), gender-based violence (GBV), child protection and referral pathways between health and protection in the camps, and the refresher training will be carried out in 2025 to ensure the necessary staff capacity to support refugees to access services in a dignified manner. In addition to the above, other components identified as potential protection risks will be monitored and mitigation strategies identified. Regular monitoring of protection-related issues will be ensured throughout the project implementation, as well as necessary actions to prevent GBV experienced by women, girls, boys, or men who are receiving assistance through health programs.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Yes, the project incorporates climate action through Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR), Disaster Risk Management (DRM), anticipatory actions, and climate adaptation strategies. Climate change intensifies frequent hazards like dengue, cholera, fires, and cyclones, demanding proactive health measures: Disease Prevention and Response: Dengue & Cholera: Warmer temperatures and rainfall increase vector- and water-borne diseases. The project strengthens community surveillance through CHWs and promotes hygiene practices to limit cholera. Early warning systems and stockpiling enable rapid responses to outbreaks.Health Education: CHWs share climate-sensitive messages on preventing dengue (e.g., reducing stagnant water) and cholera (e.g., proper sanitation).Disaster Preparedness: Cyclones & Flooding: Pre-positioned medical supplies, CHW emergency training, and evacuation plans mitigate health risks during cyclones. Health facilities are reinforced to remain functional during extreme weather events.Fire Hazards: Fire safety training and early warning systems address fire-related injuries in dry, windy conditions.Environmental Health Initiatives: Collaboration with WASH partners ensures sustainable waste management, clean water, and sanitation, crucial for cholera prevention.UNHCR’s Role as CHWG Chair: As Chair, UNHCR coordinates health, WASH, and shelter partners to enhance community resilience. Regular CHW training and IEC materials build awareness and strengthen anticipatory actions for climate-related health risks.These actions strengthen climate-resilient health services, protect public health, and reduce vulnerability to climate-related health hazards in refugee and host communities.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

Yes, the project has Emergency Preparedness and Response (EPR) measures embedded. From a public health perspective, the project includes key components to ensure effective emergency response and continuity: Business Continuity Plan: A plan is in place to ensure continuous health service delivery during emergencies. This includes pre-positioning medical supplies, maintaining essential infrastructure, and training staff to respond to health crises like disease outbreaks, cyclones, and floods.Emergency Response Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs): SOPs are established for rapid response to emergencies such as dengue, cholera, fires, and natural disasters. These procedures guide the deployment of medical teams, emergency supplies, and care facilities during a crisis.Contingency Funds: Contingency funds are allocated for emergency health interventions, enabling rapid mobilization of resources during unexpected events like disease outbreaks or natural disasters.Coordination with Health Sector and Partners: The project works with the Health Sector and other partners through the Community Health Working Group (CHWG) to ensure coordinated emergency responses. This includes joint planning, resource sharing, and real-time information exchange.These measures ensure the project can respond quickly to emergencies, maintain public health services, and protect vulnerable communities.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

YesYouth are engaged as trained and supervised service providers as well as service receivers.In addition to being part of the targeted population, the Rohingya refugee youth constitute a huge percentage of the Community Health workers' force as well as the MHPSS colunteers (CPCs and CPVs). Addressing youth mental health needs through contextualized and adapted methodologies is a priority for 2025. Adaptations will be done through close close engagements with Youth males and females.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

Yes. This project intends to build the capacity of refugees health volunteers through its work coordinating the community health working group to promote positive health seeking behaviours and practices among refugees. Additionally, by increasing knowledge on disease prevention, timely seeking of health care, the refugees will be empowered to make positive health seeking decisions, that will contribute to improvement in their health status, saving lives. The trained refugees volunteers will continue providing support even as refugees pursue durable solutions such as repatriation and resettlement.

Additionally, the organization utilizes its voice to advocate for support of development actors to strengthen the health systems of surrounding host community.
