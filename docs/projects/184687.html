Project Summary

Somalia continues to face dire humanitarian situation, with an estimated 7.2 million people in need of humanitarian assistance. This is attributed to cumulative shocks, including erratic 2021 Gu season, widespread and severe seasonal flooding, desert locust infestation, socio-economic impacts of COVID-19 and protracted conflict and flood-related displacements.
According to the 2021 Post Gu Seasonal Food Security and Nutrition Analysis for September (FAO/FSNAU), an estimated 3.5 million people in Somalia will face acute food insecurity Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse outcomes in late 2021. An additional 3.7 million people are expected to be Stressed (IPC Phase 2) and without any form of humanitarian assistance; the likelihood of an increase in number of people facing acute food insecurity to 7.2 million is very high, going into 2022. Specific to children under the age of five, approximately 1.2 million are likely to be acutely malnourished, including nearly 213 400 who are likely to be severely malnourished.
Desert Locust continue to pose a serious risk to both pasture availability and crop production across Somalia. In Galmudug, the locust have decimated pasture land and affected the livestock value chain disproportionately. Incidences of poor rains in the Gu and the expected below-average rainfall during the 2021 Deyr (October-December) season across most of the country, would adversely affect food security and nutrition outcomes.
Further, it is expected that the Deyr rains will exacerbate the risks of flash flooding especially in low-lying areas of Galmudug resulting in more displacements and deteriorating nutritional statuses at the household level and necessitating humanitarian response/assistance.
Centre for Peace and Democracy (CPD) plans to reach a total of 15186 of people or 2531 HHs facing severe acute food insecurity (IPC 3&4) in Banadir and Galmudug region respectively; mainly IDPs and host communities affected by the impacts of floods, desert locusts, conflict and socio-economic impacts of the Covid-19 pandemic by improving access to food through unconditional and conditional transfers. Weak, vulnerable members of the IDPs and the host communities such as women and child-headed households, people with disability and the elderly will receive unconditional support through mobile cash transfers as per the CWG recommended transfer values per region (Banadir $80 for multi-purpose, Galgaduud $110 multi-purpose and $100 for food and Mudug $85 for both multi purpose and food as per Somalia Cash and marketing Dash board recommended transfer values July-September 2021). The men and the youth will be engaged in conditional transfers e.g. cash/food-for-work for livelihood interventions that will support community recover their livelihood and meet their immediate needs. The associated activities for cash/food-for-work will include rehabilitation and establishment of community infrastructure such as water catchments, food markets, etcetera.
Covid-19 measures and guidelines will be incorporated in project implementation.

2.1.1 - What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

• Flexibility and choice: One size does not fit all! Using cash or vouchers recognizes that beneficiaries are best placed to decide what their households need, and gives them the flexibility to choose according to their own diverse needs and priorities. • Multiplier effects: By stimulating local markets, cash based interventions may benefit more than the direct recipients of cash. Local traders and service providers, transport workers and suppliers and transporters of goods may all benefit from households having money to spend. • Time and cost efficient for agencies and participants: When goods are available and accessible locally, cash or voucher-based interventions have the potential to be more rapid than bringing supply from out of country. When beneficiaries use local markets, agencies also eliminate logistical and import costs. However the additional staff, security and logistical resources sometimes required to distribute cash or manage a voucher programme must also be factored in.• Dignity: In allowing beneficiaries to control what goods and services their households need, cash transfers are considered by many as more dignified than receiving in kind, and recognizes beneficiaries as active participants in providing for the welfare of their families after a disaster. • Empowerment: The availability of cash gives households a sense of restored power over their immediate situation. In addition, there is evidence that receiving cash can at least temporarily empower women within the household. • Access to financial structures such as bank accounts may empower beneficiaries, in particular women, in the longer-term as well. • Supports or revitalizes local trade: Injections of cash or the use of commodity vouchers with local traders benefits the recovery of trade and the local economy.• Cash-based support for livelihood rehabilitation can potentially also provide a link between relief and longer-term economic recovery and may offer the opportunity for beneficiaries to increase income and create savings. • Reduces negative coping strategies: In the modern world, most households depend on cash to some extent. When aid is given in kind, households are often forced to seek cash in ways that may be detrimental to longer-term livelihoods, for example selling productive assets, or giving children to the care of relatives. Evidence shows that cash transfers reduce the negative coping strategies used by beneficiaries.

2.1.2 - Explain how the project design considered market functionality?

The project is informed by various conventional tools for market related assessments under the purview of CVA. Particularly, CPD deployed the Market Functionality Index (MFI) designed by WFP, with specific adoptions of the new Business Process Model for cash-based transfers. Through this model, CPD was able to map the possible market risks and establish mechanisms for 'Market situation monitoring' processes of cash-based assistance operations. Within the framework of market assessment; CPD established the following: 1) Assortment of essential goods, 2) Availability 3) Price 4) Resilience of supply chains 5) Market competition, 6) Infrastructure 7) Services 8) Food quality 9) Access to markets and protection.Ultimately, the proposition of this project was informed by positive scores on all the rubrics listed above. The acceptance of Somalia shilling as a legal tender in markets has been factored in within the design of the project and the choice of mobile money transfer. It was necessary and prudent that the choice to convert the token to a local currency or not to convert be left to the recipients of the project activities.Further, CPD expects little or no fluctuation in supplies because the prevailing weather conditions cannot support significant changes in production levels (harvest) and trust that the modernity of transfer will be pro-recipient and cannot initiate a market response that will cause inflation ( the proposed CVA will support normal operations within HHs without necessarily occasioning panic buying).

4.6 - Mitigation Measures

Risk that minority clans/ethnic groups are not included in consultations and/or are excluded from humanitarian services:• Develop livelihoods programmes in a way that protects and supports household caring responsibilities and promotes family unity. Participation in livelihoods opportunities should not undermine child protection or other caring responsibilities. Consider employing care providers, providing care facilities (nursery) or supporting community care mechanisms to enable women’s participation in such programs. • Promote equitable and impartial access to all.• Apply a systematic outreach process in registering beneficiaries – otherwise vulnerable persons, such as older people, may easily be excluded as other more mobile and vocal population groups are registered. • Ensure lack of documentation does not exclude individuals from accessing services;• Use this information to inform monitoring activities and identify any barriers to access, discrimination against particular groups or whether assistance is being diverted. Take this into consideration when determining locations for food security and livelihood interventions.• Ensure the location, distance and access routes to the distribution points is accessible to all members of the community, including the most vulnerable groups.• Separate male and female queuing areas and ensure female staff presence at distributions so that they are accessible to women and girls.• Introduce special arrangements for persons who have difficulty accessing the distribution point or unable to carry the rations (such as persons with restricted mobility or marginalized groups) community mobilization, home/mobile distributions, youth groups assisting older or chronically ill persons. It should not be assumed that friends and family will do it.• Ensure that both men and women have equal and fair access to work opportunities (including cash/food for-work schemes), micro-credit, cash transfers, agricultural inputs and skills training and that they receive the same benefits for their input and work. Risk of exposure of violence as a result of distance to and location of humanitarian assistance/services or indiscriminate attacks from armed actors.• Keep lists of beneficiaries and personal data confidential and safe from unintended use - apply effective data protection measures• Ensure the location of and access routes to distribution points and services are safe with enough space for orderly distribution.• Identify central locations - beneficiaries should not have to traverse long distances or pass through conflict zones to access distribution points.• Distributions should take place in daylight hours giving beneficiaries sufficient time to travel home before nightfall.• Identify groups at risk of unsafe livelihoods practices (eg. child labour survival/transactional sex) and prioritize provision of alternatives for these groups.
