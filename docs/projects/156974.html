Needs

The war in Syria continues to negatively affect livelihoods opportunities for individuals and communities in Northwest Syria. Idleb and the surrounding areas hold nearly three million people, with almost half internally displaced. According to the HNO 2019 sector overview, "eight years into the crisis, the main drivers to food insecurity are prolonged displacements, loss and lack of livelihoods and reduced production capacity which in turn resulted in limited physical and financial access to food, high prices and inflation contributing to reduced purchasing power and continuous depletion of livelihoods assets of the most vulnerable populations.” While markets are still functioning, the lack of job opportunities, critical community social services, and the increased cost of living mean that people are more vulnerable to shocks and stresses and more likely to resort to negative coping mechanisms. Restrictions on the Turkish border as well as disruptions in previous trade routes with other cities in Syria have led to large price increases in certain goods and commodities. Per the 2019 HRP, an estimated 64-to-84 per cent of the Syrian population is living below the poverty line. Women face additional barriers seeking services and employment due to cultural norms, though the IRC and other actor’s finds more and more are now more active in income generation for their households, this is often because of disruption in male household member’s income sources. In a recent Gender Assessment, the IRC found that women have limited options for employment, and are usually constrained to low-skilled professions include farming crops, rearing livestock, sewing, and hairdressing, working in factories and cleaning houses. Teaching is a possibility for women who have a degree. While men can work in more professions and vocation types than women, the availability of work has significantly reduced. IRC’s Gender Assessment found often only those with connections and networks can find work. This means finding work is particularly difficult for IDPs in their new locations and tend to lack the capacity to meet basic needs. Many educated men take on low-skilled labor and risky or inconvenient jobs that require traveling far distances. The same assessment found that women are paid less than men for the same job in both formal and informal labor. Youth, both male and female, have also missed critical educational opportunities as a result of years of war, and are unable to find skilled employment; instead they rely on daily labor and are more likely to turn to dangerous jobs such as working in fuel burners

Implementation of activities

1) Labor intensive cash for work (CfW) projects aimed at improving community infrastructure while providing temporary employment opportunities for unemployed or under employed men and women ages 18 60 including PwD. Projects can include rubble removal, waste cleanup, street repairs, school or health center rehabilitation, agricultural harvesting, production of winter clothing items, and tutoring. Focus group discussions (FGDs) held with women have identified olive harvesting as a CfW activities they would be interested in other than the regularly requested knitting, tailoring, and hairdressing activities. Labour Market Assessments held early 2019 and analysis of FGDs conducted in late 2018 will identify other projects women that are culturally appropriate and do not expose them to risks. The timing of CfW projects will consider community wide needs, for example IRC will avoid large scale CfW projects during the planting or harvesting season unless the project is agricultural in nature. 2) Market relevant skills training programs through two Livelihoods Center in Idleb governorate; the centers have one training space for women and girls, and one for men and boys. Activities will include business and life skills training and vocational training followed by an apprenticeship placement targeting males and females ages 17 35 years old. Specific sectors will depend on the findings of the labour market assessment, and projects for women will be based on the previously mentioned results from the FGDs to attempt to diversify vocational training projects targeting women. The IRC provides childcare with all center based activities to encourage participation for all beneficiaries including female headed households, and will explore how to offer child care in off-site trainings. In addition, the IRC will offer computer trainings to interested beneficiaries, including resume and job-seeking advice, building off a pilot conducted in 2018. Providing individuals with targeted technical training, along with resume and job seeking training, will support them to access skilled employment in the local market or on line. 3) Microenterprise grants and entrepreneurship training to both startups and existing businesses in the community; the IRC will provide business training and small scale cash grants to individuals who have developed a business plan. The project will provide specialized support women to start small home based projects, and will encourage youth (16 30) to gain practical work experience and start low risk projects that require little start up capital. Continued follow up after the distribution of grants will support individuals to leverage their grant and course correct during their business plan implementation. Building off of best practices established in 2018, the IRC will offer mid-project refresher trainings as well as link businesses to vendors and suppliers in the market. Learnings from a Gender Assessment that IRC conducted in autumn, 2018 as well as a qualitative research study the Syria Resilience Consortium conducted in 2018 will be implemented during design and implementation of all projects; including the importance of on-time payments to beneficiaries as that is identified as a risk to household dynamics – especially for women. These studies also identified that timings for projects need to be carefully determined – as any projects that occur over summer months may mean female participants need to find childcare in order to attend. Through an existing partnership with Humanity International, people with disabilities will receive specific support in all projects.

Protection Risk Analysis

Focus group discussions held as well as qualitative research on possible protection concerns have shown a number of protection risks within livelihoods programming, that IRC will seek to mitigate. One risk is that delayed payments negatively affect house-hold dynamics, especially for women who are pressured into dropping out of projects if stipends are not paid on time. The IRC has identified multiple payment methods for livelihoods projects to mitigate this risk, as well as multiple money transfer agencies to ensure on-time payments. Another risk, also outlined in the Protection Risk Analysis (PRA), is the perception of biased beneficiary targeting and selection. IRC has found this individuals especially believe either host, or IDPs, are favored during humanitarian activities. During project design and planning, IRC considers do no harm principals and conflict sensitivity concerns, including a solid understanding of the number of IDPs in a targeted location, how long they have been there for, and the level of integration they have in the community and markets. IRC does not target activities solely for host or IDPs, but instead included and encourages both host and IDPs in every project. Anecdotal end-line data shows that this is very beneficial to IDP’s integration in a community. To address the perception of bias, IRC does not take lists from local councils and prefers open registrations that are widely announced in various mediums within a community. Selection is conducted by staff in Amman, not in the field, to ensure transparency. Information sessions conducted during registrations clearly outline the eligibility for a certain project, and the beneficiary feedback mechanism establishes a redress process for any individual (who was part of a registration, or just a community member) who feels there was bias. The IRC has identified the risk of beneficiary exploitation, particularly related to perceived power that would-be beneficiaries think local authorities have over registration and selection processes. Exploitation can include sexual exploitation by local authorities or staff. The IRC has conducted PSEA trainings for all staff, including field staff. Confidential and trust worthy reporting mechanisms have been shared with all staff, and both staff and beneficiaries are encouraged to use the IRC’s beneficiary feedback and complaints mechanism. When a complaint regarding protection concerns, including sexual exploitation is found, this complaint is handled and investigated sensitively per best protection practices. The second risk identified by the PRA is an escalation of the security situation. IRC’s temporary income schemes through cash for work, also address community-level priorities, such as rehabilitation of schools, roads, and rubble removal; but can also be leveraged in an ‘emergency’ manor, providing temporary income for a shorter period, and adjusted to meet the needs of a particular at risk target group. An example of this is knitting cash for work for women that is done in their own homes, reducing the need for women to have to travel between a center/site and their homes. Women are paid based on output of the knitted items (which are then donated to in-need individuals for winterization), rather than for working hours. This allows for flexibility and also reduced the need for beneficiary and staff movements during times of heightened conflict. As discussed further in the next section, IRC will also ensure contingency planning for all activities, have planned duty of care for staff, and target locations based on thorough security assessments (inclusive of future conflict scenarios).

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks to project implementation include loss of access to project areas due to conflict (inter armed groups or due to Government of Syria offensives). As mitigation, IRC conducts pre-project security assessments that includes risk of future offensives. IRC will have ‘exit plans’ for all locations and planned measures to implement to ensure staff safety and duty of care, building on lessons learned from the South Syria offensive in summer 2018. Another high risk is interference by armed groups or local councils, who may attempt to control or influence projects by requesting or demanding IRC to take certain actions. IRC adheres to humanitarian principles and the established Joint Operating Procedures for northwest Syria. IRCs well-resourced humanitarian access team will conduct principled negotiation and conflict resolution in order to continue project plans and meet the identified needs of a community. IRC has seen success in leveraging local communities to put pressure on armed groups to drop their demands. When no negation is possible, in order to abide by humanitarian principals, IRC will withdraw from the area. Due to the conditionality of these ER Cluster projects, the IRC sees less attempted interference There is a risk of local authorities or armed groups targeting IRC beneficiaries for taxation or exploitation. IRC holds open registration for most ER Cluster activities, and when this is not possible, does household-level verification of lists that are provided to IRC. This reduces the power that local councils or others have on applicants. IRC also provides information sessions at registrations that include clear communication that interested individuals are not required to provide anything to be part of the project, and that selection will be done by IRC staff in Amman. IRC also widely advertises IRC’s beneficiary feedback and complaints mechanism at multiple stages of the project, including the initial information session. Post distribution monitoring and project spot checks, conducted by IRC’s independent Monitoring, Evaluation, Accountability and Learning department elicit further feedback from project participants and specifically ask questions on exploitation and taxation.
