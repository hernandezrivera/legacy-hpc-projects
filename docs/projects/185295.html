Project Summary

Mercy Corps proposes a $200,000 USD seven months project to mitigate the socio-economic impact of COVID-19 in Kayes region. As the historical source of emigration from Mali, the widespread reliance of Kayes’ communities and households on remittance payments from Europe has made them uniquely susceptible to a global pandemic. The knock-on effect of Europe’s lockdowns on Malian migrants – especially susceptible to the massive economic disruption due to their heightened involvement in Europe’s informal economy - has arrived in Mali, as interrupted remittance payments have pushed entire communities in Kayes into a state of heightened vulnerability and triggered urgent household crises right at the time when COVID-19 cases are rising in Mali. Similarly, state-society violence in Kayes is worsening, as frustration over poorly communicated or understood government-mandated COVID-19 restrictions merge with a wide range of both longstanding and new grievances against the state.
Mercy Corps therefore proposes to strengthen the capacity of especially vulnerable households and communities in Kayes to manage COVID-19’s economic and socio-political impacts. We will achieve this by helping the most impacted households and communities in these areas mitigate the economic impact of severed diaspora support or other COVID-19 secondary impact via unconditional food assistance programming rendered through three distributions targeting 300 programme participants.
All activities will be conducted in alignment with Mercy Corps’ USAID/CMM-funded social-cohesion programming in Kayes and broader USAID-supported social cohesion programming in the area to strengthen their mutual impact.

1 - Contexte

COVID-19 is spreading slowly but steadily in Africa, with 563408 deaths across Mali as of October 31 th, 2021 April 13th, and more than 16,00011,844 confirmed cases - though testing capacity remains low, and the true number is likely to be many times higher, especially as Mali enters a third-wave. Mali is already among the poorest equipped countries in the world to deal with a major public health crisis over and above the day-to-day challenges of overcoming endemic poverty, illiteracy, conflict and ensuring adequate access to food for their most vulnerable populations. In response to COVID-19, Mali has both tried to implement social distancing and place curbs on economic activities, though the country is unable to fully lock-down. Housing density and socio-economic conditions are not conducive to maintaining sufficient social distancing and quarantine to halt the spread of COVID-19. A typical Malian household lives in a multi-generational family, in a single compound, reliant on a smaller number of breadwinners who depend largely on the precarious, informal sector for earned income and travel by crowded public transport to and from work. The Malian government has rolled out a nationwide vaccination campaign through the COVAX initiative with the provision of 396 000 doses of Astrazeneca and later Johnson and Johnson. Despite this, vaccination uptake by the Malian population is extremely low and government communication efforts around vaccination could be reinforced. Given this reality, we can expect that the less than effective quarantine and social-distancing measures will go hand-in-hand with strident attempts by the vast majority of the population in urban and rural areas to keep some economic activities going in order to feed themselves and their families. COVID-19 will therefore continue to spread incrementally (if not exponentially) throughout the population, especially given widespread misinformation around COVID-19 which either prevent communities from observing basic hygiene and social distancing rules, or to volunteer to receive the vaccine against COVID-19. The Government of Mali, local and international NGOs and public health authorities have had very limited success in busting the myths surrounding or in raising awareness to ensure mass vaccination of Malians. Given trends of infection with the latest strand of the virus (delta variant) in other African countries such as Senegal, Côte d’Ivoire, Liberia, Sierra Leone, Uganda or Kenya, there is reason to fear an exponential spread of the virus in Mali with serious primary and secondary effects. Still, the above is not to say that the pandemic has not already had an effect on Malian life – it has already lent itself to a significant economic disruption for some communities, and the restrictions put in place by the government are increasingly contributing to state-society tension and frustration. Regarding the latter, the socio-political impact of mid-2020 government-mandated restrictions and a curfew - well-intentioned but poorly communicated policies - created fear, distrust, and an environment where misinformation on COVID-19 is spreading rapidly across social media. While no one incident should be attributed directly to the pandemic – a myriad of long-standing frustrations preexist COVID-19, as do new grievances linked to Mali’s latest coup and future presidential elections scheduled to take place in February of 2022 – in this powder keg environment, improved civic engagement of the sort provided via Mercy Corps’ USAID/CMM-funded social-cohesion programming in Kayes is needed to ensure that any later restrictions imposed due to a spreading pandemic are fully understood by the populace. Regarding the economic impact of COVID-19, while the overall death rate so far is relatively lower than some of the harder-hit countries in Africa, there is nonetheless a vulnerable community whose unique set of circumstances has already put them in the front line of COVID-19-caused socio-economic harm – the diaspora-reliant communities and households in Kayes region. Notable as both the current the point of entry both for COVID-19 in Mali as well as the centuries-old cross-roads for Malian emigration abroad, Kayes region is characterized by a massive diaspora community and the integration of remittance payments into social dynamics, community development, and state-society relations. Emigrants from Kayes form the vast bulk of Mali’s diaspora community abroad in Europe (over 80% of Malians in France are from this one small region), and flows of remittances back to family and rural and vulnerable communities to support both household and community needs are well documented, with “migration [being] crucial for assuring the well-being of the household by covering all day-to-day expenses, in particular food, health expenditures and school fees.” While COVID-19’s arrival in Kayes via a member of this diaspora in March 2020 and resulting creeping spread across the country has not yet triggered the same level of severe societal disruption as other countries across the world, preliminary information from Kayes and Mercy Corps’ team there suggests that these diaspora-reliant communities are suffering from knock-on effects of Europe’s COVID19-induced lockdowns and resulting socio-economic disruption. The impact of Europe’s lockdown on the informal economy has cut this lifeline of monthly remittance payments, triggering a severe socio-economic shock for households and communities in Kayes right at a time when COVID-19 is spreading. This disruption and current vulnerability has been further explored and confirmed by an exploratory July 2020 Mercy Corps’ assessment in Kayes region, which also found evidence of heightened socio-economic needs and COVID-19 secondary impact amongst Sonike traders along the Mali-Senegal border due to the border closure.
