Project Summary

This intervention aims at provision of equitable access to and utilization of health services through 5 static primary health care facilities including Rirnyang Primary Health Care Unit (PHCU) in Ulang County, Keichkuon Primary Health Care Centre (PHCC) , Makak PHCU, and Wandeng PHCU in Nasir and Jekow PHCU in Maiwut. To reach to the population in hard-to- reach locations in these 4 counties, RI will establish eight Mobile Medical Units (MMUs) and also implement Boma Health Initiative (BHI) targeting 2 bomas per county. The project will target 89,179 (21,119 women, 24,683 girls, 25,228boys, 21,119 men) and 13,377 people with disabilities. RI will recruit and deploy qualified health workers including clinical officers, nurses, midwives, community health workers, and vaccinators to all supported Health facilities and mobile medical units. Boma health workers will be selected at the community level to provide both selected curative services and health promotion as per MoH’s BHI package across the 4 counties.
The intervention will further focus on continuous screening and treatment of communicable and non-communicable diseases through patient consultations. The intervention will also provide the management of injuries, antenatal care, skilled deliveries, postnatal care, and clinical management of rape (CMR) services in the health facilities and mobile medical units. The intervention will also integrate and strengthen mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) and services for people with disabilities. The project will continue streamlining and strengthening the referral pathways for MHPSS, sexual and gender-based violence survivors and, maternal and child health emergencies. RI’s health team, through this response, will conduct routine and outreach immunization services to children under 59 months of age. Throughout the provision of health services, RI will ensure proper infection prevention and control protocol including against COVID -19 and Ebola. All static health facilities, MMUs and BHWs will be equipped with Infection Prevention and Control, Water, sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) materials, Personal Protective Equipment (PPE), and Information, Education and Communication (IEC) materials on prevention epidemic prone diseases such as COVID-19, Ebola, measles etc. Ebola virus disease (EVD) prevention, surveillance, and response readiness will be continued as part of disease outbreak, prevention and response. The health activities will be coordinated closely with the RI WASH and nutrition teams to ensure integration of interventions for more effective community mobilization and sensitization on health-related messaging including on availability of immunization services, ANC, skilled deliveries, CMR services, and MHPSS services. This project will engage all communities in the targeted counties throughout the implementation stages to enable the sustainability of the program. Additionally, RI will also institute contextualized feedback mechanisms such as regular focus group discussions, meetings, feedback boxes and Accountability to Affected Persons (AAP) groups to continue providing feedback and complaints. RI will involve the communities while selecting the Mobile medical unit (MMUs) sites and the community health workers to ensure acceptability of the project. The selected MMUs and health facilities will be equipped with essential medical equipment, drugs and test kits. RI will ensure the staffing is gender representative and trained on core humanitarian and protection principles including Non maleficence principle.

1 - Needs

South Sudan’s health system relies heavily on international humanitarian assistance and is still unable to cope and respond to humanitarian emergencies and provide essential life-saving health services. Limited health facility functionality, capacity, and poor coverage have made it difficult to meet health needs of people worsened by floods, food insecurity and frequent conflicts. As of September 2022, about 140,000 1people were displaced by flooding in Upper Nile alone with Maban and Nasir County as some of the hardest hit. Women, children, the elderly and persons with disabilities are particularly vulnerable and most frequently face limited access to healthcare. In upper Nile state just like other parts of the country, the main barriers to access and utilization of care services were lack of medicines, staff and equipment transportation cost, insecurity, floods, long distance to health facility and high cost of healthcare. 66 per cent of the population has difficulty in accessing health services; women and girls specifically faced additional barriers. In Eastern part of Upper Nile state, accessibility to health facilities is worsened by ever worsening flooding. For instance, Keichkuon payam of Nasir County, lacks a functional static health facility with the closest Primary health care Unit, Kierwan PHCC about 2 day walk which increases to 3-4 days during flooding.According to South Sudan health service functionality report (October 2022), only 11% of the 1,980 Health facilities are providing the full package of BPHNS and 46% are moderately functional while 58% of the Bomas in the country have no access to Boma services. These indicate inadequate health care services to the population. In Upper Nile only 56% of the functional Health facilities are partner supported. Further, attacks on health care continue to negatively impact the provision of and access to health services.There is extremely low immunization coverage (49% Penta 3 coverage) while Malaria continues to be the leading cause of morbidity and mortality, accounting for 63% of all morbidities and 69% of all mortalities in 2022. Further, Hepatitis E cases significantly rose to over 3,473 cases with 26 deaths and still remained above epidemic thresholds in 2022.The threat of infectious disease outbreaks like COVID-19 and EVD also drive humanitarian needs. Yet, according to South Sudan Ministry of Health (MoH) the vaccination coverage of COVID-19 still remains low with Upper Nile at 28.82% with Nasir county recording lowest coverage (18.4%).Maternal mortality in the country is one of the highest in the world, with 789 per 100,000 live births. Only 39% women are achieving recommended four Antenatal Care Services while skilled birth attendance is at 14.7%. Eastern Upper Nile (Nasir, Maiwut and Longechuk) lacks secondary healthcare facility for women with complicated deliveries. Teenage pregnancy is estimated at 30% among girls 15-19 years while contraceptive prevalence rate stands at 6% and unmet need for family planning estimated at 29.7%. Gender inequality and gender-based violence are widespread and perpetuated by several factors including socio-cultural norms and armed group’s presence in communities.Access to Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) remains inadequate. WHO prevalence estimates mental disorders in conflict settings in South Sudan to be approximately 2.5 million Cases, emphasizing the risk of mental health burden in the affected population. A research study shows 41% of probable occurrences of Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) in six states. Although there was context specific for Nasir, Ulang, Maiwut and Longechuk, Cases of PTSD are expected to be high as these counties had prolonged conflicts with high number of IDPs and missing family members.

2 - Project activities

This intervention aims at improving equitable access to and utilization on health services by 112222 people through static primary health care facilities including Rirnyang PHCU in Ulang County, Keichkuon PHCC, Makak PHCU, and Wandeng PHCU in Nasir and Jekow PHCU in Maiwut. RI will establish eight MMUs and initiate ICCM activities to serve the hard-to-reach populations. RI will recruit and deploy qualified male and female health workers including clinical officers, nurses, midwives, community health workers, and vaccinators to all supported static facilities and mobile medical units. Boma health workers (BHWs) selected from the community level will support delivery of assistance under the Boma health initiative (BHI) and ICCM activities. RI will continuously build the capacity of health workers to ensure the quality of service provision. Training topics will include integrated disease surveillance and case management, immunization in practice (IIP) and effective vaccines monitoring (EVM), BEmONC, Mental health Gap action program (mhGAP), SGBV/CMR, IMNCI, and use of malaria rapid diagnostic test (mRDT). The BHWs will be trained on case management using the BHI training materials and guidelines for effective ICCM management. The intervention will also focus on continuous screening, treatment of communicable and non-communicable diseases through patient consultations, the management of injuries, antenatal care, skilled deliveries, postnatal care, and CMR services in the health facilities and through the mobile medical units. The intervention will integrate and strengthen mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) and services for people with disabilities. The project will continue streamlining and strengthening the referral pathways for mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS), SGBV survivors and, maternal and child health emergencies. RI’s health team, through this response, will conduct routine and outreach immunization services for children under 59 months of age. Throughout the provision of health services, RI will ensure Covid-19 prevention and appropriate response activities are integrated. All static health facilities and MMUs will be equipped with Infection Prevention and Control, WASH materials, Personal ProtectiveEquipment (PPEs) and Information Education Communication (IEC) materials on Covid-19 to further strengthen the prevention. EVD prevention, surveillance, and response readiness will be continued as part of disease outbreak, prevention and response to mitigate the impact of an Ebola outbreak. The project will integrate with WASH and Nutrition programming for more effective community mobilization and sensitization on health- related messaging including COVID-19 prevention, availability of immunization services, ANC, skilled deliveries, CMR services, and MHPSS. Patient Exit interviews will beConducted at the static facilities and MMUs. RI will also implement community feedback mechanisms such as regular community consultative meetings, feedback boxes and Accountability to Affected Persons groups tasked to continue providing feedback and complaints. This project has engaged the communities during the needs assessment and will continue to engage all communities in the targeted counties throughout all Implementation stages. Using this approach enables the community participate in and directly influence project management. This encourages community ownership and sustainability of the program. For the successful implementation of this project, RI will engage the communities during the selection of mobile medical units (MMUs) and seek community support for storage spaces for the essential drugs and MMU equipment. The selected MMUs and health facilities (PHCCs and PHCUs) will be equipped with essential medical equipment, drugs and test kits. RI staff and CHD will jointly conduct supportive Supervision to the health facilities, MMUs and BHI

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Safety Audits will serve as an additional Feedback and Complaints Mechanism (FCM) modality during the intervention. During all focus group discussions occurring as part of Safety Audits, RI will ensure the presence of an enumerator who is the same sex as the participants to ensure the participants feel comfortable sharing information. Exit audits will be conducted at the facilities and MMU with every beneficiary with the aim of capturing feedback from women, girls, boys, men and male and female individuals with disabilities . As part of the MEAL system RI will target women, girls, boys, PWDS, the elderly and men particularly during the Men Group Discussions, participants will be specifically asked to share their concerns with the program and suggest improvements. In each of RI’s project sites, four FCM modalities are utilised as “minimum standards”: 1) Monthly staff meetings with first-line implementers, 2) Focus Group Discussions with direct beneficiaries, 3) Individual interview form usage, 4) Suggestion boxes installed at all supported facilities and communities. By using a variety of methods to receive feedback, RI will ensure that feedback from vulnerable and marginalized groups is captured. The identities of all participants using the feedback and complaints mechanism (FCM) will be kept strictly confidential by program management staff. We have standard, context appropriate FCM IEC materials (posters, stickers, flyers), which are displayed at all project sites to inform all project participants of avenues with which to give feedback and make complaints. IEC materials include images and text in both English and the local dialect to ensure comprehension by a maximum number of individuals. During all community meetings, trainings, and sensitization sessions, a standard script will be used by staff to inform beneficiaries on available FCM modalities. Depending on the urgency, feedback will be delivered to the first line program implementers during the monthly meetings. Specific responses will also be discussed bi-monthly at the beginning of focus group discussions at the site level to inform beneficiaries of what changes are being made given previous feedback, or if any concerns are being addressed at the national level. Urgent issues will be addressed immediately by the project leads through appropriate channels.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

RI will conduct community sensitization and mobilization prior to start of project, through consultations with community members and leaders, both formally and informally. Chiefs, project management committees, Boma health Committees, women and youth groups will be provided with full details of the intervention and assistance to be provided. Project introductory meetings will be held in all project areas and will include an explanation of the entitlements under this project as well as how the community members can access the Feedback and Complaints Mechanism (FCM) in order to give feedback and make complaints. Responses to the beneficiaries will be managed within the FCM loop to ensure that all feedback and complaints are dealt with internally within RI or referred/ redirected to partners who can assist to resolve. Moreover; the RI staff and local authority staff will be trained on how to orient, collect, record beneficiaries’ feedback and complaints received, as well as how and when to respond. All staff will be trained on core data protection and confidentiality principals as well to ensure that client data is kept confidential. RI has already conducted an Accountability to Affected People study in each location proposed. During this study RI consulted a statistically significant sample size on what the preferred means of receiving information and giving feedback were. Overall, the sampled individuals expressed that they felt more comfortable giving feedback verbally through discussions with RI staff or community leaders. Females community members preferred female community leaders or speaking direct to RI staff. Further, all participants stated that they preferred to have information shared verbally from RI staff, and then by community leaders. A significantly smaller cohort stated that they preferred giving feedback to local authorities. The verbal nature of feedback and information was emphasized throughout. This is unsurprising given the literacy levels of the wider population in all counties, and the limitation or lack of access to electricity and mobile phone connection which could be used for more advanced feedback mechanisms such as SMS, hotlines or email. The following community engagement approaches will be employed throughout the project lifecycle: • Project kick-off and close meetings with community leaders, women leaders, and local authorities, • Monthly Focus Group Discussions with disaggregated cohorts of the target population • In-depth interviews with sample beneficiaries • Patient exit interviews to measure satisfaction • Documentation of case studies• Complaints hot-desk • AAP champions represented at trainings and mass health education campaigns

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

All RI staff and volunteers who are based in the four locations are trained on RI's PSEA and safeguarding policies, and ways to report incidents. Each RI field office has safeguarding champions who are given more intensive training on RI's safeguarding and PSEA approach. During project kick-off meetings with communities RI will inform communities of the PSEA policy, who the safeguarding champions are, what constitutes PSEA, emphasize that all project activities are delivered free of charge and that beneficiaries should not be asked to accept anything in exchange for any goods or services. RI will place posters and visuals outlining RI's policy and where community members can report at distribution sites. Additionally, RI will have a help desk manned by RI staff at each distribution site, to collect complaints and feedback and to oversee distributions. Finally, RI's independent MEAL team and project management staff from Juba will conduct ad-hoc monitoring visits to project sites, meeting with community members and will reiterate the training and RI's position on PSEA.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

RI will use the beneficiaries’ feedback to shape and adapt the project design and implementation. RI will undertake routine Patient Exit Satisfaction Interviews to get beneficiaries feedback on beneficiaries’ satisfaction levels for the services they receive. Specific responses will also be discussed bi-monthly at the beginning of focus group discussions at the site level to inform beneficiaries of what changes are being made given previous feedback, or if any concerns are being addressed at the national level. Urgent issues will be addressed immediately by the project leads through appropriate channels with RI adapting its programme to address any concerns or gaps highlighted. Additionally, RI's MEAL team will conduct quarterly FGDs with community members to gain feedback on RI's health project activities. During the monthly consultation meetings, RI will then provide feedback to the community leaders and beneficiaries on any changes made to the project based on feedback received. Where necessary action plans will be developed with RI's project team and actions points followed up to improve the implementation of the project. In some cases where the response is not within RI's control , RI would refer the issue to other stakeholders with specific expertise to deal with the particular issue.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

The project will continue to create awareness in the community on the early mitigation of flooding by building dykes and ensuring settlements on higher lands. RI will be holding community meeting on climatic mitigation with special focus on mitigating population displacement during flooding.

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

The project will continue to provide lifesaving essential health care services through fix health facilities, MMUs and BHI/ICCM. Activities around health education and promotion activities, disease surveillance, timely detection of outbreak and response with 48hours, streamlining and strengthening the referral pathways for mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS), sexual and gender-based violence survivors and, maternal and child health emergencies will be undertaken. RI will build the capacity of health providers to ensure quality health services provision to affected population. This improve the health situation of the affected population. RI has existing protection programs in 2/4 locations proposed under this intervention. These actions have established referral pathway mappings already in place - following extensive safety audits and service mapping. All project staff, regardless of sector, are trained in these referral pathways covering where possible all aspects of the referral pathway. in the other two locations, RI has already conducted service and referral mapping, as well as safety audits. All staff have bene trained on PFA and GBV basic concepts and are aware of the referral pathways available in each location. RI already has links with key protection actor in all location to refer any cases for structured case management and follow up. As RI is the main health actor in all locations, all SGBV referrals receive CMR from RI in each location. RI is able to conduct PSS and case management in 2/4 locations. In the remaining 2 - RI health staff are able to refer cases for this support. The team will provide screening, psychosocial support and referral of GBV cases to appropriate care facility. As this might require emergency intervention within 72hours for SGBV survivors, the MMU Midwife will be equipped with PEP kits and trained on its use before conducting the referral. The team will raise awareness to community about timely referral of SGBV case and where to seek care for the survivor. RI Protection's Team will train all healthcare staff on GBV basic concepts and GBV referral pathways, to ensure that treatment and referrals are conducted in safe, survivor centred approach.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

One of the key drivers of malnutrition in South Sudan is poor health status and limited or lack of access to any form of primary or secondary healthcare for adults and children alike. By improving the health status of the population as a whole, through increasing access to curative and preventative health services - free of charge - RI is reducing morbidities and mortalities and enabling populations to continue working in livelihood opportunities. This reduces the likelihood of the caregivers and children becoming malnourished as a result of disease and poor health status.
