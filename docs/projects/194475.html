Project Summary

The IRC Child Protection program will continue to provide comprehensive protection response services and create a safe and protective environment for children who are at risk of or have experienced abuse, exploitation, neglect, and violence as a result of recurrent crisis and conflict, assisting them in healing, recovering, and feeling safe again while collaborating with their caregivers and the local government and community-level prevention mechanisms in accordance with the Minimum Standards for CP and national level SOPs, guidelines, and protocols. The IRC CP program will reach 66,201(32,3232M, 33869F) people through an integrated response of static and mobile response approaches in the CP AOR priority locations (severity 3 and 4) of Panyijiar, Mayendit, and Koch Counties in Unity State; Aweil East and Aweil South in Northern Bahr el Ghazal State, and Juba IDPs in Rajaf Counties in Central Equatoria State.
The proposed child protection responses are linked with the strategic objectives of the cluster and three domains of the CP AOR for HRP 2022. Case management services will be provided to children who have concerns about their safety. FTR services, alternative care arrangements for unaccompanied and separated children (UASC), and protection cash aid will also be provided to meet the basic needs of the children. The Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) service will be provided for children and caregivers with psychological distress using a combination of approaches such as Safe Healing and Learning Space (SHLS) and schools for Social and Emotional Learning, Families Make the Difference Curriculum for parenting skills of caregivers, Supporting Adolescents and their Families in Emergencies Curriculum (SAFE) for life skills of adolescents, and IRC’s Brighter Futures Model for malnourished under five years children with protection concerns. Finally, the community-based child protection (CBCP) structures will be established/strengthened to the lower structure of the community, community outreach on CP awareness and COVID-19 prevention messages, and capacity building of the frontline staff and community volunteers.
The CP response will be complementary to the existing IRC’s GBV, general protection, nutrition, health, and livelihood programs to ensure comprehensive care. The existing health and nutrition facilities will serve as screening and awareness-raising spots and IRC will establish CP help desks. IRC’s GBV centers will also serve as a point of referral for rape survivor girls, and the livelihood section will support the cash-based intervention. In addition, the CP team will be part of the integrated mobile team in all locations where IRC is operating.

1 - Needs

It has been three years since South Sudan's revitalized peace agreement was signed, but child safety has deteriorated, increasing the number of children at risk of violence, abuse, and exploitation who require critical child protection services from 2.1 million in 2021 to 2.9 million in 2022 (SS HNO, 2022). The country continues to be vulnerable to cyclical and protracted risks, such as food insecurity, flooding, intra-national violence, and disease outbreaks. The child protection needs in the IRC proposed locations such as Panyijiar, Koch, Mayendit, Aweil East, Aweil South, and Juba are classified as severity three, and the needs of the children are very enormous. Security is unpredictable, and reports of revenge killings and inter-communal conflict remain a source of concern in Mayendit, Panyijiar, and Koch Counties.As a result of the recurrent flooding in Panyijiar, Mayendit and Koch children continued to experience psychological distress, abuse, exploitation, and family separation. For the third time since 2019, an estimated 31,245 individuals (58% of children) in Panyjiar and 24,000 individuals (55% of children) were affected, displaced, and relocated to higher ground in open spaces and school compounds. Due to a lack of alternative shelter options, they are forced to settle under trees by making temporary plastic shelters due to limited humanitarian response. According to the IRC team's observation, majority of the IDPs were children, including unaccompanied, separated, child-headed House holds, orphaned, and children with disabilities who experienced emotional abuse. This crisis has increased children's needs for social-emotional skills development and psychosocial support (PSS) in order to strengthen their resilience capacity to overcome adversity. As IRC's case workers in Panyijiar and Mayendit counties witnessed, the flooding exacerbated parents' negative coping strategies of engaging children in child labor such as fishing, and canoe rowing, as well as increased child marriages. As a result, a significant number of children have dropped out of school and are working in cattle camps without adequate facilities, jeopardizing their health and exposing them to additional harm during cattle raiding incidents. Children from displaced communities and conflict areas, particularly children with disabilities, unaccompanied and separated children, and child-headed households, suffer profoundly from prolonged exposure to multiple protection risks, including violence, recruitment into rebel groups, family separation, psychosocial distress, and exploitation. As of March 2022, there are 1088 (562 boys, 526 girls) children with various protection concerns, including unaccompanied and separated minors in alternative care arrangements, child neglect, child labour, domestic violence, and sexual exploitation in Panyijiar, Koch, Mayendit, Aweil East, Aweil South, and Parinag counties in IRC’s case management caseload that need continued support in line with their respective case plans (SS CPIMS +, Mar 2022).Following the school’s closure during COVID-19, child protection incidents such as teenage pregnancies, early/forced marriages, physical abuse, and gender-based violence increased due to lack of a functional protection referral mechanism (SS National Education Assessment, 2021). According to IRC's research on corporal punishment in Panyijiar (Jan 2022), despite all of the CP and positive discipline techniques training provided to 36 school teachers, the trend of school-level physical and emotional violence is increasing, owing to a combination of factors such as flooding and economic burden that trigger teachers' emotions during classroom management. The community views corporal punishment as an effective method of instilling child discipline, which requires continued efforts to shift social norms and behavior regarding violence against children in Panyijiar County.

2 - Project activities

The IRC proposed child protection interventions are in line with the Protection Cluster objectives, particularly for the three main focus areas of the child protection AOR, such as: Provision of mental health and psychosocial support services; strengthening case management approach, including through cash-based assistance; and community-based child protection (CBCP), capacity building, and outreaches. The IRC will reach 66,201(32,3232M, 33869F) most affected and vulnerable populations, including UASC, GBV survivors, CAAFAG, and children with disabilities from the IDP, returnees, and host communities of Panyijiar, Mayendit, Koch, Aweil East, Aweil South, and Juba (IDP Camps). IRC will continue creating a safe and protective environment for children and providing quality protection services to empower vulnerable girls and boys and work with their caregivers, while strengthening the capacity of the local government and community level prevention mechanisms in line with applicable standards, including the Minimum Standards for Child Protection in Humanitarian Action, IASC Guidelines for MHPSS in Emergencies, and Core Humanitarian Standards. In addition, the IRC uses standards and guidelines developed and adapted by the Child Protection AoR such as CP Comprehensive Case Management SOP; Information-Sharing, and Data Protection Protocol (ISDPP); Guidelines on Alternative Care Provision for Vulnerable Children in South Sudan; and Guidance Notes on conducting Family Tracing and Reunification (FTR) in SS. The proposed CP activities include (a) Comprehensive Case Management: The IRC will continue to provide case management services including FTR to the most vulnerable children, including children with disabilities affected by the flooding, conflict, food insecurity, and COVID-19 as per the South Sudan vulnerability and risk assessment criteria. The IRC will use paper-based inter-agency case management forms and the system-based Child Protection Information Management System (CPIMS+/Primero) to manage individual cases, conduct child and family assessments, develop case plans, provide direct and indirect services to meet needs identified in the assessments, conduct case follow ups as per the plan, and close cases when goals have been achieved. The caseworkers and supervisors will be trained on version two to equip them with an updated feature of the CPIMS+. Besides, the existing service mapping and referral pathways will be updated in all the operational areas in collaboration with the CP working groups in all the states. To ensure dignified and safe service delivery, IRC will prioritize cash assistance for children identified for case management support as per their child case plan and the cash and voucher for protection SOP. IRC will cooperate with UNICEF and UNMISS when CAAFAGs are released for reunification and economic and social reintegration in the community. (B) MHPSS: The IRC will also provide PSS/SEL services through a variety of methods, including Safe Healing and Learning Space (SHLS), PSS clubs in schools, a child protection help-desk, SAFE, and parenting skills training. The rehabilitation of existing centers and the construction of new SHLS will create a safe environment for children to attend PSS/SEL activities. Teachers, PTAs, and SHLS facilitators will be trained on UNICEF’s PSS manual and IRC’s SEL and SHLS management standards. Furthermore, schools will be supported with the establishment of CP help desks, PSS training for the teachers, and provision of playing materials for clubs as part of the PSS activities. (C) CBCP: Finally, the CBCPC will be better equipped to prevent and respond to child abuse, neglect, exploitation, and violence. The CBCPC members will be trained on CPiE, case identification, and referrals to the CP actors. In addition, continuous community awareness of CP and COVID-19 prevention messages will be conducted using different platforms.

3 - Project indicators

Success will be measured using the IRC’s IMPACT standards which identifies a set of monitoring actions across the project life cycle. The project will dedicate funds to recruit monitoring and evaluation staff that will be charged with setting up project M&E systems and check points to track project implementation progress and to document learning. The project will be implemented in line with the overall strategic objectives of the Humanitarian Needs Overview and Protection Cluster (particularly CP AOR) strategic objectives to ensure that activities align with the overall prioritized needs of affected populations. The indicators tracked will be in line with the CP AOR priorities and will be disaggregated as much as possible by age, sex and disability. This will further inform access and utilization of project assistance by vulnerable groups in the community. Quantitative and qualitative data will be collected using CPIMS+, participant attendance sheets (for training, SHLS/CFS participation, and plays), pre/post training assessment, satisfaction assessment tool, safety and well-being assessment tool, SAFE Adolescent Girl and Boy Survey, post-distribution monitoring for cash assistance, baseline and endline survey. To ensure the quality of case management service, the CP Officers/Supervisors will regularly review the case files using the standard file checking tool and conduct a weekly meeting with caseworkers. All case management and FTR related information will be shared as per the Information Sharing and Data Protection Protocol-SS. Regular review meetings will be held at the project locations where project staff and partners will review data on all indicators and use the data to make critical decisions about how to best deliver on the project outcomes and make necessary adjustments. Project cycle meetings will be held on a monthly basis to discuss performance across service delivery sites, share experiences and learnings and make high-level evidence-based decisions geared towards strengthening the project delivery mechanisms and success pathways.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

The IRC's Economic Recovery and Development (ERD) team will keep an eye on the prices, availability, and quality of goods and services in the market on a regular basis as part of the CVA's core monitoring activities. They will work with the national Cash Working Group. IRC decides on the inclusion of CVA in the project during the project design based on the assessment of the market functionality in that particular location. Cash is recommended if safety and markets are favorable and the client prefers it. In-kind assistance can be preferred if cash is not feasible or can’t be delivered fast enough. If there are safety concerns with providing cash, an individual case plan should be used to mitigate the concern. Once the functionality of the market is identified, IRC will continue to assess the market price to decide the transfer value. The IRC has also identified protection needs in the Minimum Expenditure Basket, and the corresponding prices are also regularly assessed. The exercise provides information about the functionality of the market, an early indication of any problems in the market, signs of inflation, or supply or transportation problems. It also allows changes in the availability and demand for goods/services by beneficiaries to be measured and tracked over time. The timing of the monitoring will vary to collect information at different points of the month and capture data across various monthly cycles. The market monitoring is to ensure if beneficiaries are still able to access basic items with the cash value transferred and to monitor any impact on the market as a result of the increased demand generated by the program.

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

The IRC Strategy 100 prioritized cash and voucher assistance (CVA) as one of the program delivery modalities to maximize clients' choice and agency to decide how best to meet their own needs and to support local market systems. Accordingly, IRC South Sudan has a commitment to channel 15% of the direct program service costs through CVA in the next three years. In addition, CVA is one of the fastest growing evidence-based interventions in the humanitarian community; evidence around CVA is rapidly growing and an increasing number of interventions harnessing CVA are now evidence-based, complementing existing humanitarian assistance modalities such as in-kind and direct service provision. CVA shows promising evidence to improve child protection outcomes, such as reducing abuse within homes, reducing child labor and exploitation, reducing early marriage and pregnancy, and improving family reunification and childcare. There is an understanding that the use of CVA in protection programming suggests that cash assistance — along with in-kind assistance, case management, and other services through referral systems with complementary services, has the potential to contribute to protection outcomes. However, the type of cash, location, the vulnerability status, and cultural factors can all play a role in how it affects people, especially when it comes to child labor and early marriage.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

We will collect, analyze and use feedback from beneficiaries regardless of their age, nationality, gender, residency status, education status, disability status and other diversity factors. We will use the Washington Group Short Set of Questions to disaggregate feedback and complaints by disability status. The IRC’s feedback channels will disaggregate feedback and complaints by gender, age, nationality, residency status and disability status to enable us to understand the needs and perspectives of different clients, including the most vulnerable members in the communities we serve. We will pay special attention to the most vulnerable and gather their feedback through key informant interviews, focus group discussions and community meetings. The community-led feedback management committees in IDP camps and refugee settlements include persons with special needs to ensure their needs are into account.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

The project beneficiaries, including UASC, GBV survivors, CAAFAG and children with disabilities, will be informed on the scope of the project, timelines, key decision gates and community accountability mechanisms and systems during project opening meetings, project review and coordination meetings, stakeholder and community forums, information boards and using IEC materials. IRC has established community-led feedback management committees in IDP camps and refugee settlements who will inform community members on the available client feedback and complaints mechanisms that they can use to provide feedback about project assistance. The community-led feedback management committees will also follow up with IRC on actions taken on the feedback and complaints provided to ensure that the feedback and complaints received from clients are included in adaptive program management. As a client-centered organization, the IRC has a well-structured client responsiveness and accountability system in place where clients’ complaints and feedbacks are collected, analyzed, and used to inform program decision making. We will use both the proactive and reactive feedback channels to solicit client feedback that will guide project implementation and strategies for improvement. This feedback will be used to inform decision-making and adapt interventions to achieve better outcomes for beneficiaries in line with their priority needs and preferences.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

IRC is committed to clients and staff safe from harm. It has a safeguarding policy that encompasses the PSEA. The client safeguarding policy mainly focuses on the protection of child and adult clients from exploitation and abuse - physical, verbal, emotional, mental, economic, or sexual – by those working for or associated with the IRC. The IRC has two Safeguarding focal leads at the country program and two safeguarding champions in each field office who are leading the staff orientations, community awareness, and receive any allegation from the staff and clients. All the reporting lines and means are displayed in all the field offices to be accessed for all clients and staff. In addition, the program team disseminate safeguarding/PSEA information to all beneficiaries coming for the IRC services and during the community outreach program.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Experience and research has shown that if beneficiaries do not know what has become of their feedback, then they will lose trust in the feedback process. At the IRC, we respond to beneficiaries’ feedback by asking them how we should respond, selecting the appropriate channels, identifying the timing for the response, planning and allocating resources, recording and keeping track of client feedback, and monitoring and reviewing our feedback mechanisms. Complaints and feedback will be analyzed and presented during weekly field site meetings and monthly project cycle meetings where practical actions to address them will put in place with clear timelines and responsibility allocated. These actions will then be followed up in subsequent meetings to ensure they are implemented and to measure the results. Key actions to adapt project assistance will include, but not limited to, making programmatic changes on assistance delivery, changing policy, provision of goods and services, providing information on available assistance, providing linkages with other projects, providing external referrals and including the needs of clients in project funding proposals.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

The IRC anticipates the risks that beneficiaries are exposed to violence during distribution as a result of the distance to humanitarian service location; beneficiaries’ exposure to sexual violence; beneficiaries may not be using complaint and feedback mechanisms and interference of external stakeholders like local government/administration in the impartial selection of beneficiaries. The IRC will continue to work with the CP AOR, humanitarian agencies, local government, and community structures to respond to child protection needs by mainstreaming protection principles in every response. The do no harm, promoting non-discrimination, meaningful access, safety, dignity, and participation of children and caregivers will be given high priority. To ensure the participation of all beneficiaries throughout the project cycle, the program will set clear targets disaggregated by gender, age and vulnerability status including disability so that nobody will be left out in the dignified CP program responses. IRC has an integrated mobile response to actively reach marginalized population in hard-to-reach areas to minimize risks associated with beneficiaries traveling long distances for service and increase the access of the service. This mobile response and prepositioning of program consumables in field location would increase access for the beneficiaries especially in areas inaccessible because of rains and poor road conditions, active armed conflict, restrictions of movements as a measure to curb Covid-19 spend, and bureaucratic impediments.
