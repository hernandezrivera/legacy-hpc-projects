Project Summary

In June, Crisis (IPC 3) or Stressed (IPC 2) outcomes persist across the country. Nearly 20 percent of the total 2.7 million people in need of food assistance did not access it, including in riverine and rural areas of the South, where humanitarian access is low. The impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the Somali economy is significant. Poor urban and IDP households, as well as pastoralists in northern Somalia, are likely to be most affected by an estimated 30-50 percent decline in annual external remittances, anticipated 25-35 percent decline in annual livestock exports, lower labor demand, and above-average imported staple food prices. In most IDP settlements and urban areas, at least 20 percent of the population still face food consumption gaps or are engaged in negative livelihoods coping strategies indicative of Crisis (IPC Phase 3) even after food assistance distributions. Additionally, Crisis (IPC Phase 3) outcomes are expected from June to September in parts of central Somalia, where livestock holdings are lowest, and in northeastern Somalia, where the economy is more dependent on livestock, seafood, and frankincense exports. The erratic distribution of rainfall during the April to June gu season has worsened Gu cereal production prospects in south-central Somalia. During the July to September dry season, up to 3.5 million people are anticipated to be unable to meet their minimum food needs and in need of urgent humanitarian food assistance. Further, the NOAA/CPC NMME forecast predicts an increased likelihood of a below-average deyr rainfall season from October to December 2020. FEWS NET’S research shows the NMME forecast has a strong capability to predict below-average rainfall during the October to December rainfall season in eastern East Africa. Combined with the persistent threat of desert locust, the impact of below-average rains on crop and livestock production is expected to lead to widespread deterioration to Crisis (IPC Phase 3) in northern and central pastoral areas and southern agropastoral areas in the absence of sustained food assistance. The conflict between insurgents and the FGS – supported by AMISOM and other forces – and conflict between clans over the control of land and resource management is expected to continue to periodically restrict humanitarian access, cause loss of life and assets, cause displacement, and disrupt trade and population movements. Conflict is most likely to affect Bakool, Bay, Galgaduud, Gedo, Hiiraan, the Jubas, and the Shabelles regions. Thus, AADSOM will support disaster-affected populations in Hirshabelle, and Puntland to protect their livelihoods, support local food production, and prevent the adoption of crisis strategies. AADSOM will carry out the following activities to reach the above objectives:
1. AADSOM will increase access to food and improve nutrition through Unconditional Cash Transfer to crisis-affected and underserved 1,000 IDPs and host communities HHs in Bari, and Hiran regions, benefiting around 6,000 women, men, boys, and girls including disabled people and marginalized communities. The supporting period will be 3 months and the rate is 60% Minimum Expenditure Basket (MEB) which corresponding to 60 USD.
2. Supporting 2000 farmers in Hiran and Bari regions with land preparation and input distribution;
3. To keep the sustainability of the project and increase the production of the beneficiaries, AADSOM will train 100 selected lead farmers on Good Agricultural Practices (GAP) to increase their livelihoods production and adapt the new technologies.
4. Rehabilitation of 5 primary canals in Hiran region through CFW, benefiting 500 workers for a period of three months, benefiting around 3,000 (450 men, 600 women, 900 girls, 900 boys and 150 disabilities).
During the project implementation, women, men, children, disabled, marginalized communities, and IDPs will be heard their voices and consulted.

Project Activities

CO1: Improve 1000 households’ immediate access to food through the provision of unconditional Cash Transfer (UCT) depending on the severity of food insecurity phases, vulnerability, and seasonality of the livelihoods. Act.1.1: Provide unconditional Cash Transfer (UCT) to improve the nutrition and food security to 1,000 IDP & Host Community HHs in Bari, and Hiran regions benefiting around 6,000 (900 men, 1200 women, 1800 girls, 1800 boys, and 300 disabilities) for a period of 3 months depending on the severity of food insecurity phases, vulnerability, and seasonality of the livelihoods.CO2: Protect and restore livelihoods, related food, and income sources, through the provision of seasonally appropriate livelihood inputs and technical support to 2,000 households in rural and peri-urban settings of the affected by the crisis in the target locations. Act.2.1: Distribute farm inputs and tools to 2000HHs, benefiting around 12,000 (1800 men, 2400 women, 3600 girls, 3600 boys, and 600 disabilities) including minorities and prepare their farmland (2000ha) with tractor in Hiran and Bari regions; each hector with 3 hours’ time for the ploughing. The farm input composed of farm tools, fertilizers, seeds, and vegetables. Act..2.2: To keep the sustainability of the project and increase the production of the beneficiaries, train selected 100 lead farmers (50 men & 50 women) on Good Agricultural Practices (GAP) to increase their livelihoods production and adapt the new technologies. CO3: Support household and community resilience to shocks, through rehabilitation and/or restoration of productive assets to 500HHs in Hiran region, enhance livelihood strategies and disaster preparedness. Act. 3.1: Rehabilitate 5 primary canals in Hiran region through CFW, benefiting 500 workers for a period of three months, benefiting around 3,000 (450 men, 600 women, 900 girls, 900 boys, and 150 disabilities) selected through agreed criterion, giving priority to women-headed HHs, HHs with disabled people, marginalized communities and HHs with below poverty income.

What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

(1) For transparency and accountability to the donor, implementing organization, and the beneficiaries, it is better to transfer the money through beneficiaries' mobile numbers. On the contrary, if the beneficiaries provide cash-in-hand or vouchers, the field staff might corrupt (without the knowledge of the top management).(2) For the security of the beneficiaries from in-kind assistance looting, it is very safe to use cash transfer because the beneficiaries will get the cash transferred in their mobiles without knowing others. Secondly, no looting can occur, while in-kind looting can happen during the distribution or taking the in-kind assistance to their homes. (3) In a range of circumstances, beneficiaries receiving food transfers may resale them in full or in part in local markets. This may happen not necessarily because the food was not needed per se, but because of the need to meet non-food expenditure priorities (for example, medicines) or for buying foods of different quality. Given that resales may entail transaction costs by beneficiaries and sales may occur at prices below market ones (and at a cost below that of procurement incurred by the agency).(4) The potentially large logistics costs entailed by food-based programs, including procurement, transport, storage, and distribution, may posit particular risks for accountability, transparency, and “leakages. Such losses throughout the logistics chain should also be accounted for comprehensive cost-effectiveness.

Mitigation Measures

(1) Risk that beneficiaries are exposed to violence during registration/distribution as a result of the distance to or location of humanitarian assistance and services:Mitigation measures:During the registration of the project beneficiaries, the local authorities, community leaders, elders, women groups, and minorities will select the project beneficiaries with the guidance of AADSOM through the agreed selection criterion; the distribution location will be near the beneficiaries' settlements; the distribution will be done in the presence of the community stakeholders.2. Risk that marginalized groups, persons with disabilities, women, boys, and girls, do not participate in consultations or decision-making processes for the project or are excluded from humanitarian services/assistance/activities. Mitigation measures:AADSOM will ensure that the marginalized groups, persons with disabilities, women, boys, and girls, participate in consultations or decision-making processes during the project design, implementation, monitoring, and evaluation. To meet the specific needs of women separate meetings will be held with the women and girls respectively facilitated by female staff. Targeting is a joint process involving community representatives. During the sensitization meeting, action plans will be drawn with a clear division of roles and responsibilities. The capacity building of the beneficiaries is planned to empower them to participate fully in the implementation process. Local vendors will be given priority for contracted works and supplies and the project will promote participatory monitoring and monthly feedback to all stakeholders to enhance accountability. 3. Risk that beneficiaries are not accessing/using complaint and feedback mechanismsMitigation measures:AADSOM will ensure that the project implementation process is accountable and transparent to the community and to the stakeholders including women, people with disabilities, and underserved communities. One of the ways of achieving this is through the establishment of a feedback and complaint mechanism to record and act on expression of satisfaction or dissatisfaction about the quality of the project services. The key objectives of AADSOM’s feedback and complaints mechanism include: Recognition and protection of the beneficiaries’ rights; Preventing and mitigating risks before they escalate; Building and maintaining good relations and dialogue with the community; Promoting accountability towards the stakeholders (beneficiaries, local authorities, donors and partners); Identifying weaknesses and areas for improvement in the service delivery. The complaint mechanism contributes towards creating a safer environment for the most vulnerable members of the community as well as increasing their voice during the implementations of the project. AADSOM complaint mechanism is accessible to everyone and beneficiaries are mobilized regularly. In addition to using the feedback and complaint mechanism, the project shall deliberately involve the beneficiaries in the implementation cycle. IDPs and host community leaders, elders, women, men, religious leaders. Local stakeholders, local authorities have specifically been consulted during the project implementation cycle. Sensitization and coordination meetings will be held before and during the project implementation with the local authority and communities in targeted locations to create awareness on project objectives, activities, target locations, expected outcomes, scope, and type and selection criteria for beneficiaries and beneficiary entitlements.
