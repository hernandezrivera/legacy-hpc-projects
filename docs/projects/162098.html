Project Summary

DRC in Cox's Bazaar was the first GBV actor in host communities and boasts robust capacity for capacity building in PSS services due to a history of MHPSS specialists in key internal GBV and other protection roles. It is also the only INGO actor serving hijra communities hence attends to gender concerns beyond binary gender. In planning its GBV response services, DRC ensures capacity to service CP-GBV cases and male survivors among case workers and in GBV prevention services, is one of the three INGO actors implementing the specialised prevention intervention of Engaging Men and Boys in Accountable Practice. DRC GBV currently serves Rajapalong wards 6 and 9; Palongkhali wards 5 and 6 and camp 6. Case management services for camp 2W are planned.
Women and girls’ safe spaces within integrated community centres are in Rajapalong ward 6 and Palongkhali ward 5. Construction for a WGSS is ongoing in Camp 6, where case management is currently provided at a health post.
For the JRP 2020 period, DRC strives to retain this diverse package of programmes in its existing locations-Rajapalong Wards 6 and 9; Palongkhali Wards 6 and 9, Camp 6 and Camp 2W and extend it among the host and refugee communities viz. Rajapalong Wards 3 and 4 on community request; and camp 2E based on analysis of gaps in GBV service provision provided by the GBV SS. Specific programming with the response and prevention packages will continue to evolve in accord with DRC experience of needs in implementation viz. a move to structured PSS corresponding to Layers 2 and 3 in the IASC MHPSS pyramid as the post-emergency context in the camps demands deeper engagement; inclusion of PSS as well as income generating programming in host locations where financial dependence regularly deters survivors from exercising autonomy and the experience of poverty is cited as a trigger for violence. An additional hijra safe space will be planned. Multiple service entry points (and where considered safe, service points) will be planned in each location committed to optimise access to response services; as well as structuring the mobile outreach component to reach women and girls who may not otherwise be reached with GBV prevention services that can facilitate entry to GBV response services.
In order to demonstrate DRC's commitment to sustainability and in acknowledgement of the technical capacity gaps among GBV actors and low rate of referral among non GBV actors, key experienced DRC staff will be available for capacity building for GBV SS and other response partners.
DRR has been considered in the project planning with budget allocation to enhance structural integrity in the event of disasters for new centres, training of all staff on psychological first aid (PFA) with a voluntary emergency pool in place and a plan for dignity kit distribution by these staff in a PFA informed manner should a disaster arise.

Needs

According to the MSNA, in the host communities, only 38% of married women and 19% of unmarried women self reported that they could go alone to a marketplace. For the refugee community, the corresponding figures are 31% and 15% respectively. This is a powerful pair of figures serving as a GBV proxy indicator for the Cox's Bazaar context: imposition of restriction on women's movement is normalised and perception of threat of GBV external to the home is high. Given that GBVIMS data in 2019 for these locations has consistently shown that the frequently reported form of GBV is intimate partner violence, it is reasonable to conclude that women and girls are significantly unsafe in the space where they spend the most time.Moreover, with regard to the refugees who belong an ethnic minority whose situation is a protection crisis at the core, multiple reports from Myanmar have also documented Rohingyas acknowledging increased GBV in their communities, which they directly link to their internal displacement. Following August 2017, human rights groups reported that refugees in Cox’s Bazaar had endured multiple forms of physical and sexual gender-based violence. The camp context brings in potential of abuse of power from those with access to the refugees and increased risk from within the refugee community. Consequently, further restriction of women’s and girls’ movements and early marriages, which are forms of GBV themselves, are perceived as protective. Hence the typical survivor within the context has experienced multiple forms of GBV multiple times. According to the Joint Response Plan 2019, at least 115 more service points for GBV case management are needed.Among the host communities, DRC's own trailblazing programme experience underscores underserved needs in GBV case management. Caseloads are high and require relatively long term support or reopening in response to needs.

Implementation of structured group psychosocial support and income generating activities for women and girls in host locations6. Implementation of structured group psychosocial support and life skills activities for women and girls in refugee locations7. Implementation of mobile outreach sessions based on WGSS consultations and gaps identified through GBVIMS and case supervisions for GBV prevention in refugee and host locations8. Retention of a weekly hijra safe space in Palongkhali and introduction of a weekly hijra safe space in Rajapalong9. Dignity kit distribution in case of emergency response need (to be utilised in routine activities should emergency contingency stock not be required during cyclone season)Within its case management services, DRC will facilitate referral of reported sexual violence to medical care within 72 hours and track receipt through the GBVIMS hence making this data available to GBV SS. However, a specific target has not been set to avoid promoting active case finding. Feedback Mechanism: For DRC GBV case management, upon case closure, a case worker distinct from the one who worked on the case does a brief satisfaction interview with the survivor’s permission. (An interview is necessary as most survivors have low if not no literacy). Results are used to improve service quality. In EMAP, feedback from participants and staff are built in to curriculum implementation and reports. For centre and outreach group sessions, design of activities is in consultation with participants and feedback regularly taken by facilitators. For Code of Conduct (CoC) concerns, staff, volunteers and other stakeholders have access to DRC CoC reporting mechanisms which include phone, email and, in person availability. Staff and volunteers are systematically trained on the CoC and how to report suspected misconduct. In camp locations, participants are also regularly informed of the Complaint, Feedback and Report Mechanisms within Site Management services.PSEA: DRC has been a regular participant in the PSEA Network and has a PSEA focal in place. Allegations of PSEA against DRC staff or volunteers can be channelled through the DRC CoC reporting mechanism mentioned above. DRC HQ is in charge of ensuing investigations. In cases where a DRC staff or volunteer alleges PSEA by a member of another INGO or UN organisation, the communication is between the DRC PSEA focal and the organisation’s PSEA focal. In GBV case management, DRC includes a PSEA exception to confidentiality in the informed consent process. PSEA implications of a case are considered within DRC’s case management follow up process.
