Project Summary

CARE is proposing a comprehensive WASH, Health, Nutrition intervention responding to the immediate basic needs of the refugees hosting in Sudan and sustaining access through improving WASH, health & Nutrition and food and livelihoods services. All the proposed services will be delivered in line with the Refugees forum’s and relevant clusters priority of providing basic services to the refugees and asylum seekers from south Sudan and other refugees in three states of Sudan Kassala, East Darfur and South Kordofan.
The project will seek to provide live-saving interventions for WASH, Health & Nutrition through safe, clean water & adequate sanitation facilities, quality primary health care, preventive & curative nutritional intervention. The services will be provided in a holistic & collective manner to enhance peaceful coexistence between refuges & host community & improving the peoples’ wellbeing. Gender, protection &accountability will be mainstreamed throughout the implementation. CARE with national partners will work in coordinated manner to provide the needed support, staff will participate & attend coordination meetings at different levels to provide assistance in coordinated & collaborative manner. The proposed intervention will cover activities of integrated health and Nutrition services, such as Provision of mental health consultations to provide essential primary and secondary health care services, Management of moderate acute malnutrition for PLW, Refer cases for specialized treatment (between levels of care across Sudan) to provide essential primary and secondary health care services, Increase access to health services by supporting mobile medical units and referral pathways. Under WASG the project will provide installation, operation, maintenance and provision of water services, provision of sanitation services i.e communal and household latrines, showers and hygiene promotion activities. Under Food security and livelihoods the project will be providing agricultural and livestock support and vocational training and training for youth and women. The project will also conduct PSEA and GBV awareness sessions and dignity kits distribution as cross cutting activities. The primary focus will be the affected women, men, girls, and boys, as well as disabled people in East Darfur, South Kordofan, Gedaref and Kassala states.

1 - Needs

In September 2022, the UN Refugee Agency (UNHCR) estimated that Sudan hosts about 1.14 million refugees, of whom 43 per cent are children, 52 per cent are adults and five per cent elderly. UNHCR estimates that this number will be reduced to 926,000 refugees in 2023. This is still Africa's second largest refugee population. The largest group of refugees is from South Sudan, with an estimated 811,000 South Sudanese refugees in the country by September 2022, of whom 52 per cent are female. From January to August 2022, more than 36,000 South Sudanese crossed into Sudan, mainly into White Nile State, East Darfur, West Kordofan and South Kordofan. Ethiopian refugees also continued to arrive, but to a lesser extent in eastern Sudan and Blue Nile State. As of September 2022, almost 58,000 Ethiopian refugees have crossed into Sudan since the start of the crisis in northern Ethiopia in November 2020. Approximately 38 per cent of refugees in Sudan live in camps, while about 50 per cent of the refugees live in Khartoum and White Nile states. As per the government encampment policy, refugees are required to reside in designated camps with restricted freedom of movement, while some degree of flexibility is exercised for South Sudanese. Before the influx of Ethiopian refugees in 2020, most new arrivals to camps in eastern Sudan were from Eritrea, with about 133,000 Eritreans registered (September 2022). The majority arrived decades ago and represented one of the most protracted refugee situations in the world. Sudan receives, on average, 6,000 additional Eritrean refugees every year. Sudan hosts about 24,300 refugees from the Central African Republic (CAR), most of whom arrived in 2019 and mostly lived in remote areas in South and Central Darfur. Over 4,500 Chadian refugees live in Central Darfur after conflict broke out in Chad between 2005-2007. Refugees from Arab nations are considered “brothers and sisters” by the Government of Sudan under the Arab/Islamic notion of asylum. Upon arrival, they are not required to register with UNHCR or the Commissioner for Refugees (COR). UNHCR and COR estimate that about 95,500 Syrian and Yemeni refugees live in Khartoum. The Nutrition Cluster’s 2023 PiN is 4 million. It remains at the same elevated levels as in 2022 out of which 67K are refugees at the exposing them to heightened morbidity and mortality risks. The Health Cluster's 2023 PiN is 10.1 million oyt of which 751,000 are refugees. . The most prevalent cause was obstetric haemorrhage (33.1 per cent), followed by hypertensive disorders (13.1 per cent), and sepsis (12.8 per cent).The Food Security and Livelihoods Cluster's 2023 PiN is 11.7 million. IDPs; returnees; people stuck in conflict regions; 926,000 refugees from South Sudan, Ethiopia, and other neighbouring countries; poor groups from agro-pastoral and pastoral communities in rural parts of western, eastern, and northern Sudan—whose livelihoods are directly impacted by the influence of lean season and macroeconomic crises—are the groups most in need of assistance. Recently, the fighting in Sudan has increased vulnerabilities of refugees and asylum seekers across Sudan. In Khartoum it has caused secondary movements of refugees and asylum seekers, including South Sudanese, Eritreans, Ethiopians, Syrians and other nationalities from Khartoum to White Nile, Kassala, Port Sudan, Aj Jazirah, Gedaref and other states. By mid-May White Nile has received additional 75,000 refugees. PIN refugees and asylum seekers are 1.1M where refugees response targeted 1.1 M people with life saving emergency support.

6.2 - Explain how your project aims to ensure protection mainstreaming during its design and implementation

Gender and protection are main pillars and primary focus of CARE. Community protection and GBV are regularly factored in the center of each project. Needs of women, girls, boys, and men are systematically assessed, concerns, capacity, and opportunities of equal access are usually identified and mitigated in each single project. In this nutrition intervention, a risk assessment related to access and GBV, couples with a Rapid Gender Analysis (RGA) recommendation will be used to inform planning safe nutrition intervention with maximum protection measures and minimum GBV cases. Front line staff members, contractors, partners, and volunteer community groups will be trained and mobilized on GBV prevention measures and how to deal with GBV cases and referrals. Further, key GBV messages will be developed matching to the context, tested by Gender specialist, and handed over to nutrition outreach staff to implement and monitor.

7.2 - Please outline how the project or your agency will inform and interact with inter-agency approaches to accountability to affected people and feedback mechanisms, including SEA and abuse complaints?

CARE has an active sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) mechanism and policy in place for prevention, investigating and reporting. The compliance sector in collaboration human resource sector mandated to handle complains, investigation and safely reporting to the Country Director for further actions. Staff member in direct contact with communities in the target nutrition sectors will be trained and mobilized around issues related to sexual exploitation and abuse, messaging through leaflets and banners using local languages will be made available in nutrition centers for ensuring communities are aware of their rights to protection from sexual exploitation and abuse. Complain mechanism boxes will be available and accessible by the target communities in the nutrition facilities. Protection, including protection from sexual harassment, exploitation, and abuse (PSHEA), will be mainstreamed in the response. Key implementing staff from CARE, national NGOs, and seconded government staff will receive protection and PSHEA training and develop action plans to mainstream throughout the response period.
