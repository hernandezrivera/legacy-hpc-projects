Project Summary

The IRC is proposing to directly implement child protection programming in addition to supporting partners, including government institutions such as the Ministry of Youth and Sport and the Ministry of Justice. The IRC will provide protection services and prevention activities to extremely vulnerable IDP, returnee and remainee children in non-camp settings to ensure their safety at home, school and in the community. These activities include empowering Child Protection Committees (CPCs) and local leaders, providing parenting skills training, conducting Focused Psychosocial Support (PSS) activities for children, facilitating youth groups for adolescents, carrying out intensive care management services, as well as training and mentoring partners in Ninewa, Anbar and Salah Al Din.

Needs

Children are disproportionally affected by crisis and armed conflict, and have been amongst the most severely affected by war in Iraq. They face increased risks such as separation from caregivers, and exposure to exploitation and abuse. With children accounting for almost 50% of the population in need in Iraq according to the 2018 HRP, it is essential that they have access to a protective environment at home, in school, and within the community, acknowledging the long-term support required to help them heal from the conflict and displacement. IRC caseworkers report that girls and boys are differently affected by conflict and displacement with girls being vulnerable to sexual abuse and harassment, at risk of early marriage. Girls in displacement settings are also often not allowed to leave their shelters and as a result their education prematurely ending their education. Boys have been recruited into armed groups or continue to face pressure to do so, are being pressured to work in harsh conditions and are also out of school. The lack of access to specialized services and safe spaces can lead to long-term detrimental effects on children’s health and development into adulthood, causing lifelong physical, mental and emotional damage. The IRC is currently the only CP actor providing case management services and parenting skills training in the Al-Karama and Al-Methaq neighborhoods of Mosul. In these urban areas where the IRC is present, community members have requested additional parenting skills trainings and focused PSS activities for children and youth, two oversubscribed activities for which the IRC currently maintains participant waiting lists. According to the UNHCR Iraq Bi Weekly Protection Update in March 2018, more than 20,000 persons currently reside in Salamiyah 2 Camp, and the capacity of CP actors to deliver services is overstretched (UNHCR). The IRC is the only actor providing dedicated case management services and parenting skills training. As such, camp management has requested that the IRC continues to provide services in the camp, especially as IDPs continue to arrive. In January 2018, the IRC conducted a scoping mission in Sinjar, after a gap in existing services was highlighted at the CP Sub-Cluster level. Despite being retaken by Iraqi Security Forces (ISF) from the Islamic State of Iraq and the Levant (ISIS) in November 2015, the provision of services and support to families within Sinjar is limited. According to respondents during the scoping mission, an estimated 3,000 Yazidi women and children previously abducted by ISIS have returned to Sinjar, since it was liberated including children who are now displaced within Sinjar. Some children have no family to return to and are currently under the care of other families, with no support from the government. These children continue to face challenges with accepted and reintegrated into their community. With nine out of ten parents reporting changes to children’s behavior, including nightmares, unusual crying or screaming, and sadness, there is a high need for PSS activities. Seven out of ten parents reported that they do not allow their children to leave their home. Although other actors are providing CP services in Sinoni, gaps in CP case management services continue to be highlighted in the CP Sub-Cluster meetings both at the national and governorate level, and no CP service provider is currently present in Sinjar city.During an IRC multi-needs assessment, the psychosocial wellbeing of children and adolescents was highlighted as one of the primary protection concerns in Anbar; with more than 80% of those assessed reporting a noticeable change in their children’s behaviour, including increased feelings of fear, aggression, disengagement with school, and forgetfulness. At recent workshops held in preparation for the HRP, CP actors responding in Salah al-Din and Anbar reported that child labour, psychosocial distress, and gender based violence including child marriage where amongst the key concerns facing children in their Governorate and that there is a need to support or continue to support children to receive specialised services including case management, strengthen community based child protection specifically including support to parents on the premise that caregivers are best placed to meet the psychological needs of their children.

Activities

The IRC will provide facilitation skills, mentoring and other support to community based CPCs. CPCs will identify and refer vulnerable children and families, support alternative care arrangements for unaccompanied and separated children (UASC), conduct parenting skills trainings for vulnerable families, facilitate child participation in the identification of CP concerns, and conduct community awareness raising to reduce CP concerns. Following CPC trainings, the IRC will mentor CPCs, supporting them to facilitate their own meetings, and ensuring that meetings are regular and documented. The IRC will also advise CPCs on how to identify child protection issues, and help them identify the proper focal points for advocacy messages. The IRC’s parenting skills program has been developed and tested specifically for displaced parents in the region and is building on the existing partnering curriculum rigorously tested by IRC. The parenting program aims to reduce parental stress, decrease the use of violent discipline and harmful parenting practices, and increase positive caregiving practices for parents living in a conflict and displacement context. Two adapted parenting skills packages will be developed, one to support parents of former Children Associated with Armed Forces and Armed Groups (CAAFAG) to assist their children to reintegrate into the community and another prevent children joining armed groups will be developed. Community Outreach Workers, partners and CPC members will be trained to deliver parenting skills training to parents of young, school-aged, and adolescent children. The IRC will work with CPCs, school staff, social workers, and members of the community, to target parents who would most benefit from the trainings and will create parenting groups to be trained weekly during a ten week session. Where possible, the IRC will provide training to partners and support them to deliver parenting training. The IRC and partners, where applicable, will work with CPCs to identify out of school and at risk boys and girls between the ages of 6 and 12, including children with disabilities, for focused PSS interventions. The objectives of the training are to create a safe space where children can learn to identify and manage their emotions, develop emotional and social resources, and build resilience through structured group play activities delivered by a trained CP worker aimed to develop these skills and resources. The focused psychosocial activities sessions are: (1) Creating a safe space; (2) Building trust; (3) Naming, acknowledging and expressing emotions; (4) Sense of Safety; (5) Dealing and coping with anger and guilt; (6) Dealing and coping with sadness; (7) Reconnecting with here and now: fostering resilience, self-esteem and dignity; (8) Reconnecting with here and now: strengthening psycho-physical, emotional and social resources. The sessions will be carried out by outreach workers specifically trained on the curriculum, with support from their supervisor. The IRC will establish youth groups in Ninewa and Anbar to engage adolescents, aiming to building youth resilience and foster dialogue amongst youth. The curricula will provide children with coping skills and problem solving by including life-skills and PSS sessions which focus on collaboration and team building, building positive relationships, conflict resolution, nonviolent communication methods. Furthermore, the establishment of youth groups also seeks to increase leadership skills and meaningful engagement of youth in the development of their community, by taking a leading role in identifying and implementing small community improvement projects. There continues to be an incredibly high demand for case management and there are significant gaps in the provision of case management services. Social workers will work with partners, Child Protection Outreach Workers, and CPCs to identify children needing support. Using a thorough assessment tool the needs, risks, and protective factors for each child will be identified. Social workers will develop individual case plans to address protection issues related to risks identified for each child. This could include provision of alternative care arrangements, training of caregivers in parenting skills, accessing registration, enrolling in school, accessing medical care, counselling, and/or psychosocial services, or providing support to extremely vulnerable individuals. Where possible the IRC will support partners, including government, to conduct case management through a training and mentoring approach. Case management teams will also be equipped to deliver early childhood development messages to parents of young children. In order to establish a strong sustainable protection environment for children at home, in school and within the community, the IRC will provide CP trainings to strengthen the capacity of community members, local authorities (police, judiciary, social workers), and CP actors.

Mitigation Measures

1. The IRC will work closely with partners at a national and local level so that the working relationship exists at all levels ensuring partners understand the objectives of the project and child protection approaches. Where applicable we will formalize government relationships by developing Memorandums of Understanding.2. The IRC will ensure a self-care plan is in place for both IRC and partners’ staff, including methods to provide psychosocial support and time off to the social workers who are in direct contact with beneficiaries and survivors.3. The IRC will manage this risk by working closely with the security team to ensure that the areas of intervention are safe to access for both beneficiaries and staff and activities can be implemented without major interruptions4. The IRC will use Child Protection Committees and workshops with community leaders receive feedback on child protection issues in the community and receive feedback that informs the IRC on how to best support children in the community throughout the project cycle. Where the IRC needs support to access sensitive cases in the community, the IRC will draw on community members trained by the IRC to gain access and collaborate on reducing stigma through, for example, community based messaging and awareness raising.

How is your project accountable to affected population

The project will be implemented in collaboration with the target population by working closely with a trained, gender balanced and diverse Child Protection Committee (CPC) who will as part of their role serve as an advisor to programme activities. The IRC will proactively invite different social groups, genders, ages and traditionally marginalised voices to serve on the committee. The CPC will have various roles, such as taking part in the selection committee for projects developed by the youth groups, developing action plans to govern the focus of CPC activities, supporting case management teams in the identification and referral of vulnerable children and their families, and where appropriate, assisting with low risk cases. In order to ensure that traditionally marginalised voices will be considered by the committee, the IRC will provide training and guidance tools to encourage the consideration of all children and young people in the local area. The IRC’s M&E team will support the inclusion of perspectives of the community and beneficiaries throughout the project by conducting interim satisfaction surveys with activity participants aimed at understanding their experiences, ensuring the activity is of value, and asking for suggestions on how the activity could improve. Where practical, the IRC will amend activities based on the results of these surveys. Through the IRC feedback and response mechanism, different channels will be used to receive feedback following both solicited and unsolicited approaches to help clients channel their voices. These channels include satisfaction surveys, onsite monitoring, suggestion boxes, helpdesk, and individual interviews where all feedback/complaints are welcome by IRC. At least one female M&E staff member will visit each operation site once a month to inform beneficiaries of the existence of feedback channels and to receive individual or group feedback. The feedback is then entered into one database, analyzed by Senior M&E officers, and followed up with the Manager and any other key relevant staff. Only relevant staff with specific authorization will have the right to handle complaints. These authorities are based on priorities and categories of feedback/complaints that determines the level of escalation for decision making. All sensitive issues identified through the feedback mechanisms will pass through the appropriate reporting channels to ensure that issues are dealt with and only appropriate people are involved if an investigation is needed. Breach of confidentiality or staff misconduct can lead to disciplinary action, including dismissal. For those who provide feedback/complaints, IRC aims to inform them about the progress and final decision following the handling of their feedback/complaint. Anonymous feedback is also used to help IRC track trends that allow the IRC to consider course corrections where needed to improve overall services for beneficiaries. Both the direct and anonymous feedback will be documented as learning to inform improvements in the future project designs. In case beneficiaries request information that falls outside of the IRC scope of work or mandate, IRC staff will share the IDP hotline number to link them with the needed service providers.
