Project Summary

As of January 2021, there are more than 314,000 refugees from neighbouring countries sheltering in South Sudan. Despite enormous socio-economic, security and political challenges, as well as the COVID-19 pandemic, South Sudan has maintained its open-door policy for people seeking international protection, the majority of whom are women and children, including from Sudan, Democratic Republic of Congo and Central African Republic.
At the same time, against a backdrop of fragile peace and cautious optimism for long-term stability, some 357,500 South Sudanese refugees have spontaneously returned to the country since November 2017, 122,000 of those in 2020 alone. While UNHCR is not promoting or facilitating return to South Sudan at this stage, the increase in numbers warrants a scaling-up of support following an area-based approach, including strengthening linkages with development actors, to help returnees and the communities they settle in live in safety and dignity, with greater security and services.
Refugees largely reside in conﬂict-affected regions, challenged by an unpredictable security environment as well as at times fragile relations with host communities. Displaced communities will continue to rely on life-saving humanitarian assistance provided by UNHCR and its partners as a result of enduring economic hardship, weak rule of law, limited development support and a lack of social services and infrastructure in refugee-hosting areas as well as in areas of origin. A reduction of basic assistance, especially during a global pandemic, will immediately lead to the deterioration of the humanitarian situation to emergency levels, due to the absence of a safety net, including social services, community support networks and limited access to livelihoods in displacements sites hosting refugees and South Sudanese refugee returnees.
Under the refugee response, all refugees and asylum seekers registered with UNHCR will beneﬁt from various forms of protection interventions, including individual registration and documentation, and multi-sector assistance. Speciﬁc protection services, such as SGBV response and child protection will focus on the identiﬁed at-risk groups. While water, health, nutrition and education services will continue to maintain indicators within global standards for refugees living in camps, provision of other assistance, including shelter, non-food items and livelihoods support, will be undertaken in a targeted manner, focusing on persons with speciﬁc protection needs and vulnerabilities. This will primarily be achieved through participatory assessments, surveys as well as veriﬁcation exercise to identify vulnerable refugees and their needs. Impact monitoring will be used to gauge the eﬃciency of projects implemented and for the purposes of course-correction in terms of better targeting of assistance. The needs of persons with disabilities and the elderly will be further mainstreamed in various multi-sector response services. Vulnerable refugees in urban settings will also continue to beneﬁt from targeted protection interventions and facilitation of their access to national services.

1 - Needs

By the end of 2020, South Sudan hosted more than 314,000 refugees and 3,769 asylum seekers. The majority of refugees fled as a result of the on-going ﬁghting in South Kordofan and Blue Nile States in Sudan. Refugees from Sudan represent 93% of the total refugee population and the vast majority (91%) residing in the remote locations of Pariang and Maban County. A smaller number of refugees from the CAR, DRC and Ethiopia have been displaced and are mostly residing in Central and Western Equatoria States. The majority of refugees (98%) are living in camps and settlements in remote locations. The vast majority are women and children (82%) and approximately 11% are facing one or more specific need. All refugees are in need of international protection and multi-sectoral life-saving assistance to enable them to live in safety and dignity. Ensuring the safety and security of refugees remains a fundamental concern and a major protection challenge. In recent participatory assessments, refugees indicated insecurity and violence, presence of armed elements, recruitment of children, SGBV, insuﬃcient food and education facilities, limited socio-economic opportunities and skills training as major protection concerns. Maintaining the civilian character of asylum will necessitate continued advocacy with the government and other stakeholders. Refugees in camps rely heavily on multi-sectorial assistance and any scaling back of such basic assistance will immediately lead to the deterioration of the humanitarian situation of refugees to emergency levels. The refugee population is expected to increase to some 350,000 in 2021, considering new refugee arrivals from Sudan, CAR and the DRC amongst others, and natural population growth, which require adequate measures to ensure they can be accommodated and services expanded to maintain minimum standards. The onset of the COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated the already weak systems in South Sudan. The closure of schools since March 2020, jeopardizes critical gains made over the years and risk refugees and host communities’ students – particularly girls - to dropout or not return when schools reopen in April 2021. This has had a large ripple effect on refugee’s future livelihood prospects as 61% of the refugee population is under the age of 18. Health needs remains below minimum standards with congested refugee camps, limited WASH facilities the threat of disease outbreaks including COVID-19, Ebola, cholera, hepatitis E, malaria, and yellow fever is ever present. In 2020, approximately 2.2 million South Sudanese refugees resided in one of the six surrounding countries while 110,000 South Sudanese refugees spontaneous returned. In a country with limited services and stretched humanitarian interventions, the ability to absorb additional caseloads is limited. While hosting communities are largely welcoming, refugee returnees are returning to areas with limited services and poor infrastructure. The number of South Sudanese refugees spontaneously returning is projected to increase to a cumulative 500,000 in 2021 which will require further strengthening of refugee returns monitoring at border crossing points and in areas of return, as well as community-based support. The years of conflict crippled the infrastructure of the country, creating conditions where resources are scarce and humanitarian assistance is stretched. The socio-economic impact of COVID-19 will continue to have significant implications on the already limited livelihood opportunities for refugees including loss of income, restricted movement of persons and goods, reduced access to markets, inflation in prices of essential goods. Competition for scarce resources may increase which will which will in turn heighten tensions between refugee and host communities, further aggravating the situation for refugees and refugee returnees. All of these challenges will likely lead to an increase in protection concerns and negative coping strategies.

2 - Project activities

In 2021, UNHCR will continue to focus on ensuring access to territory (safety), reception, registration, status determination, and documentation and improving the humanitarian and civilian character of asylum. In line with government policy designed to protect the civilian nature of asylum, UNHCR will also aim to transfer new arrivals and the residual caseload in the Yida settlement to Pamir/Ajoung Thok camps. UNHCR will continue to promote peaceful co-existence with host communities and address the needs of children, women at risk, SGBV survivors, persons with speciﬁc needs. Other activities include; promoting access to justice, attaining complementary pathways for durable solutions for protracted cases. UNHCR will increase focus on promoting durable and transitional solutions. Key elements of the strategy include; 1) Focusing on community-based solutions, 2) Promoting peaceful coexistence between spontaneous refugee returnees and receiving communities using quick impact projects and supporting community self-management. UNHCR will continue to co-lead with Relief and Rehabilitation Commission (RRC) the coordination and response of systematic returnee monitoring, strengthen regional data collection, build upon intention surveys conducted in countries of asylum and apply a community-based assistance strategy with targeted support for extremely vulnerable. UNHCR will not facilitate, promote or encourage returns to South Sudan as long as UNHCR’s non-return policy remains valid. UNHCR will continue to periodically review its strategy in view of the evolving situation in terms of political stability and security situation in the country. UNHCR will also continue life-saving assistance including; • Facilitation of essential services in the area of health, nutrition, WaSH, education and camp management to meet refugees' basic needs. • NFI distribution will target new arrivals and most vulnerable households including female headed households. • Shelter support will be limited to emergency shelter for new arrivals and provision of transitional shelters to the most vulnerable. • Maintenance and upgrade of service infrastructures, including schools, health facilities, water distribution, shelters as well as access roads and airstrips. • Use of renewable energy will be pursued for better sustainability of services and environment protection. • Protection monitoring, analysis, training, and advocacy, addressing various protection concerns related to men, boys, women and girls of different ages and backgrounds. Activities will also focus on bolstering community protection mechanisms. • Multi-sectoral GBV prevention and response, including installation of solar lights, information management, awareness raising, psychosocial counselling and referral services.

3 - Project indicators

UNHCR will collect and analyse data against the set protection indicators for the response plan regularly. This will include disaggregated population data and profile, sector data and routine collection of project indicators to inform protection analysis and success of the project. Protection monitoring and participatory assessments will inform a robust situation analysis and ensure targeted population, including those with specific needs and the most vulnerable, are consulted during the design, implementation and following implementation of the activity. Rights-based and the Age, Gender, and Diversity Approach principles are applied throughout the programme cycle to ensure that the voice, needs and feedback of persons of concern, particularly those with chronic medical issues, older persons, separated and unaccompanied children, persons with disabilities, women and children are taken into account, and that gaps, challenges and solutions are identified. Various community outreach channels and communication modalities will be used to ensure accountability to affected communities in the response. This includes bolstered feedback and complaint desks, hotlines, focus discussion groups, community-based networks, and community coordination meetings.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

When people are forced to flee their homes, they leave with little more than they can carry. They also lose their ability to earn and spend in the process. Cash or vouchers seek to protect displaced persons by reducing the risks they face and to maintain their capacity to spend. CVA can be used in a variety of settings, as long as there is a stable market and a safe way to provide displaced persons with cash or vouchers. The flexibility that CVAs offer makes them a more dignified form of assistance, giving displaced persons the ability to immediately prioritise and choose what they need. CVA is contingent on a market assessments and protection assessments. Therefore, the CVA response will be conditional and limited.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

UNHCR's age, gender and diversity (AGD) policy is at the forefront of each intervention as it ensures that all persons of concern fully participate in decisions that affect them and enjoy their rights on an equal footing with others.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

UNHCR takes a community-based approach in all its work. Through consultation and participation, communities engage meaningfully and substantively in all programmes that affect them and play a leading role in change. UNHCR recognizes that, without the engagement of persons of concern, external intervention alone cannot achieve sustained improvement in their lives. UNHCR endeavours to harness the knowledge and resources of communities and to strengthen their capacities. If communities affected by crises are empowered, they are in a stronger position to protect and support their families, promote social cohesion and peaceful coexistence with host communities, respond to the aspirations of young people, and rebuild their lives. Feedback and complaint mechanisms, community-based networks, coordination meetings, and regular meetings with refugee leaders have been put in place to ensure the involvement of persons of concern.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The feedback will be taken into consideration for project amendments with the operational context and protection analysis.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

UNHCR and partners maintain its oﬃces in the refugee camps since they have greatly increased the interaction between refugees and UNHCR, and also provide effective complaint mechanisms to address persons of concern’s views. Camp interagency coordination meetings are held on a monthly basis. Community Outreach Volunteers (COV) network is also reinforced since they crucial to provide real-time information to UNHCR on issues affecting their lives. In 2019, UNHCR will work with its partners to strengthen community-based complaint mechanisms and expand awareness on PSEA across key areas of operation. Building on the Cluster approach to ensure consistent messaging and centralization of complaint mechanisms will strengthen prevention awareness and integrated response, when necessary. Working closely with the GBV Sub-Cluster and the multi-agency PSEA Taskforce will allow boosting the implementation of the guidelines for the Community Based Complaint Mechanism (CBMC), amplifying, therefore, the eﬃciency of reporting mechanisms using the methodologies the communities prefer.
