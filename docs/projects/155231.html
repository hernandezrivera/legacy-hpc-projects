Project Summary

The project is built upon the Ecological Model of Child Development with the intention to strengthen various levels to support to the most vulnerable children, including adolescent boys, adolescent and frequently home-bound girls and children with disabilities. The project seeks to promote social-cohesion through opening services to both host and refugee communities in an effort to integrate children and caregivers in specific activities. The project will bring new focus to meeting the needs of marginalized groups; reaching the most vulnerable children and families through integrated mobile teams; developthe capacity of local community structures and promotes self-reliance at the individual and household level, while integrating host community programming that combines both target groups with the aim of generating better understanding.

Needs

Children continue to experience high levels of distress after witnessing extreme violence, exacerbated by stressful living conditions in Bangladesh and uncertainty over the future. Risks of separation, exploitation, abuse and injuries are present in the congested camp settings, where younger children are sometimes unattended or carrying heavy loads to support caregivers as they find suitable living conditions, collect relief and so on. Children living in overcrowded spaces with insecure care arrangements face increased risks related to SGBV and the worst forms of child labour including sex work predominantly affecting adolescent girls and organized crime and exploitation affecting adolescent boys (SC SitRep, Oct 2018). In the camps, families are often forced to resort to negative coping mechanisms such as child and forced marriage as well as child labour. Those outside of the camps are seeing strained services and increasingly require comprehensive support. Save the Children's Consultations with both Rohingya and affected Bangladeshi children conducted in February 2018 revealed that Rohingya children are scared of being harmed whilst doing household chores such as collecting firewood, water or aid. Children also emphasized the need for recreational space and learning opportunities, which reinforces calls to integrate child friendly space and informal learning activities and to provide a robust approach to meet psychosocial and recreational needs. Additionally, while we currently don’t have figures on the number of children living with disabilities in the camp we have seen from the UNHCR Population Data and Key Demographic Indicators (Oct 2018) that over 8,000 families have been registered as caring for a person with a disability. In Cox’s Bazar there are extremely vulnerable girls and boys who require dedicated case management support, which SC is able to provide through a holistic and integrated model, based on international best practices, with the aim to address all levels of the ecological model to promote protection at the individual, household, community and systems level. In-house services can provide for children affected by SGBV, mental health concerns, and family separation, combined with referrals to other specialist service providers. There remains a high need for robust family-based alternative care arrangements for unaccompanied and separated children (UASC), as well as family tracing and reunification and the provision of support to foster care families. These activities are essential to ensure the protection needs of UASC and at-risk children are met. Recent research by Save the Children provides new insight in to the experiences faced by orphaned and separated Rohingya children. This research also sadly confirmed that an overwhelming number of children interviewed (71%, n=99) were separated from their primary caregivers during attacks and that, 70% of those children (n=70) either witnessed or have strong reason to believe that their parent or caregiver died during the attack. This reinforces the need for mental health and psycho-social support for children and their caregivers and for support to alternative care arrangements and family tracing and reunification.

Activities or outputs

Result 1. Risk factors are decreased and protective factors are increased at the individual level | Output 1.1. Children and families can access timely and quality case management support for child protection concerns appropriate to the best interests of the child that meet the Minimum Standards for Child Protection in Humanitarian Action, including specialist SGBV, alternative care, and FTR services | Output 1.2. Adolescent girls and boys gain increased access to self-reliance and skill development opportunities which reduce the risk of resorting to negative coping mechanisms | Result 2. Risk factors are decreased and protective factors are increased at the household level | Output 2.1. Parents and caregivers are supported to create a safe and nurturing home (Positive Discipline and Every Day Parenting, Foster Carer training, Peer-to-peer MHPSS support groups, etc.) | Output 2.2. Parents and caregivers are provided with opportunities for self-reliance opportunities (vocational training, livelihoods) to contribute to family-systems strengthening by reducing financial pressure | Result 3. Risk factors are decreased and protective factors are increased at the community level | Output 3.1. Community members can actively prevent children from being exposed to abuse, exploitation, violence, and neglect especially during natural disasters (CBCPCs, awareness-raising on the Lost Child and Caregiver Meeting Points, IEC to prevent separation and what to do in the event of an emergency, capacity building of CBCPMs to follow up on high risk cases, etc). Additionally, CBCPCs and volunteers will be strengthened to systematically monitor the situation of children to identify key areas of concern and alert relevant focal points. | Output 3.2. Adolescent girls and boys are provided with the tools and space to practice meaningful decision-making throughout project design, implementation and monitoring and protective behaviours (adolescent friendly spaces, adolescent clubs, life skills, protective behaviours, audio-visual self-expression and self-esteem project etc.) | Output 3.3. Girls and boys are engaged in age-appropriate recreational and psycho-social activities and receive relevant information in a safe space facilitated by trained staff (static CFS/AFS hubs, mobile CFS, protective behaviours, child resilience, social-cohesion through specific adolescent lead activities), peer-to-peer MHPSS support groups, integrated WASH, Health, and Nutrition information and services in CFS). Save the Children intends to strengthen the current structures while improving quality and reach of the mobile activities. This will be complimented by CP hubs that aim to serve the several layers of the ecological model with direct activities, meeting spaces, and safe points in the community. | Output 3.4 Mainstream child protection indicators and activities across the seven sectors that Save the Children works in to ensure all staff are aware of their responsibility and role in promoting the best interest of the child (i.e. training on identification and referral, child-focused outcomes, etc.). | Result 4. Risk factors are decreased and protective factors are increased at systems level | Output 4.1. The child protection case management system for Rohingya children and families is more localised and effective (Co-lead CMTF, build capacity of national pool of trainers on case management topics, work closely with DSS and UNICEF on systems-strengthening) and utilize the newly contextualized coaching and supervision package. | Output 4.2. Advocacy at local, national and international levels puts Rohingya children’s voices including children with disabilities at its center (Child-led/participatory advocacy projects, child-focused advocacy briefs and meetings, etc.)
