Project Summary

WHO remains dedicated to enhancing the health and well-being of Rohingya refugees and host communities through a coordinated approach with health sector partners and a comprehensive strategy. This commitment focuses on sustaining a robust disease surveillance and response capacities and addressing emerging health priorities.
Disease Surveillance and Outbreak Response: Rohingya refugees face epidemic-prone diseases like cholera, dengue, and scabies, plus emerging threats like multidrug-resistant TB, hepatitis B and C, HIV, and STIs. WHO's interventions have reduced mortality and morbidity through vigilant surveillance and outbreak response. Using EWARS (the Early Warning, Alert, and Response System) and the District Health Information System, WHO and partners track and respond to health threats effectively.
Hepatitis C Response: "WHO surveillance data (September 2024) indicates a significant Hepatitis C burden among refugees. Of 1,118 RDT-positive patients tested for HCV RNA, 71% had active infections. MSF studies (June 2024, Nov 2023) show a 20% prevalence rate, meaning 1 in 5 adults has an active infection. Approximately 17% of pregnant women are infected, indicating a high risk of mother-to-child and person-to-person transmission."
The Hepatitis Task Force and health sector agreed on a 71% active infection rate and 95% non-cirrhotic cases, anticipating 101,084 active cases in camps. WHO and partners are mobilizing resources to provide medication for 96,030 non-cirrhotic (12-week treatment) and 5,054 cirrhotic (24-week treatment) cases.
In 2024, WHO launched the “Hepatitis C Surveillance Linked to Treatment” program to enhance access to diagnostics and treatment and collaboration with partners.
Laboratory Strengthening: WHO supports 60 field laboratories managed by health sector partners by providing equipment and consumables. Additionally, WHO assists the sole molecular IEDCR Field Laboratory at Cox’s Bazar Medical College, focusing on hepatitis C surveillance, antimicrobial resistance, and cholera culture.
Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) are worsened by weak IPC and improper antimicrobial use, leading to Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR). Limited diagnostics and inappropriate prescribing require safe water, IPC protocols, and antibiotic monitoring to reduce HAIs and mitigate AMR.
In September 2024, WHO, with Bangladesh and health partners, launched an AMR Surveillance program in Rohingya camps at Cox’s Bazar, enhancing public health and combating antimicrobial resistance.
Expanded Immunization Efforts: WHO, in partnership with Bangladesh government and other partners, aims to boost vaccination for diphtheria, measles, and rubella to 95%, ensuring universal health coverage for displaced Rohingya, per Immunization Agenda 2030.
Addressing Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs) and Mental Health: WHO identifies gaps in the treatment of chronic conditions and is dedicated to improving NCD and mental health management. WHO aims to vaccinate 90% of girls under 15 against HPV and screen 70% of women for cervical cancer. With 51% of Rohingya being women and girls, their inclusion is vital. WHO provides training, tools, and medications to ensure comprehensive clinical services.
Training and Capacity Building: WHO trains government personnel and healthcare workers from partners in disease surveillance, IPC, diagnostics, and case management, with a focus on mental health for holistic care.
Risk Communication and Community Engagement: WHO promotes positive health behaviors via targeted risk communication, focusing on high-risk groups like women, individuals with NCDs, older adults, and people with disabilities, to reduce barriers to care and encourage health-seeking behaviours and improve health outcomes.
Health Sector Leadership: WHO, as the health sector lead, addresses gaps in lifesaving supplies, coordinates health sector, supports partners, and promotes sustainable health outcomes through governance structures.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

The current cholera outbreak that started on June 2024 is by far the largest in scale as it has affected 26 of 33 camps as the total number of confirmed cases are triple the number reported in 2022 and 2.5-fold higher than that for 2023 and higher than 2019 outbreak. As of 12 October 2024, a total of 213 confirmed cholera cases and zero deaths (CFR-0%) have so far been reported making it the largest outbreak in six years.Additionally, heightened attention is required for other infectious diseases such as Hepatitis, HIV, and sexually transmitted infections (STIs). In conjunction to MSF scientific study, and WHO Hepatitis C Surveillance data confirmed the HCV RNA detectable rate around 20%, that means one in every five individuals is having active Hep C infection above 18 years of population. Approximately 17% of pregnant women are infected with HCV (WHO EWARS Data, 30 September 2024). There is high risk and probable ongoing person-to-person transmission in children and adults due to this high prevalence. Furthermore, multidrug resistance tuberculosis (MDR TB) poses a significant public health crisis among the Rohingya population. Till 2024, there are 33 patients reported as HIV +TB co-infection in the refugee population.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

WHO encourages the incorporation of cultural and gender-sensitive approaches in healthcare services. WHO will conduct need assessments and analyze the findings by collecting data on age and gender through different programs. In addition, will continue to monitor gender sensitive indicators in vaccination program through life course like proportion of male to female vaccination across all age groups to ensure equitable access and socio-cultural barriers are addressed accordingly through Community Health Working group. WHO applies gender analysis in all the trainings/workshops conducted by WHO to ensure female participation and empowering them with knowledge and decision-making capacity. Findings from different assessments and surveys are utilized to design and tailored the policies to overcome socio cultural barriers to access health services like promoting physical activities in women to reduce the risk of noncommunicable diseases particularly Diabetes and Hypertension. Furthermore, WHO support the activities to improve the access to Sexual and Reproductive Health services and continue to provide technical assistance on clinical case management of rape and intimate partner violence in both the Rohingyas and host community. Furthermore, disease burden related to HIV continue to remain high in Rohingya population as compared to host community. WHO is spearheading testing expansion initiatives in critical areas like HIV External Quality Assurance, and other emerging diseases. This strategic approach aims to ensure not only a timely response but also to uphold the standards of quality and sustainability, transcending the emergency phase. WHO will capitalize on service delivery to gender diverse population, supporting gender perspectives within health facilities and supporting establishing necessary linkages. WHO through the health sector will continue to ensure that facilities have at least one female doctor.

2.3 - Age & Disability

WHO advocate for persons with disabilities to have equitable access to effective health services are included in health emergencies preparedness and responses; and can access cross-sectoral public health interventions to achieve the highest attainable standard of health. Strategic Tool for Assessing Risks (STAR) assessment by WHO in health facilities for refugees recommended one entrance per facility with ramps for disabled. Ramps and path to be finished with material that are non-slip, surface free of irregularities with minimal joints with handrails on both the sides. Focus is provided on NCDs services including mental health and vaccination across life course for the elderly and disabled population. WHO led health sector works to ensure the humanitarian response to the Rohingya crisis is inclusive for men, women, boys, and girls with disabilities and older persons, at all age.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

The project incorporates integrated activities related to the health sector strategic planning. Findings from the independent community assessments regarding service availability for both the Rohingya and host community population will be utilized to enhance quality of health response as one of the key objectives of AAP. WHO will provide comprehensive technical guidance to the Risk Communication and Community Engagement Working Group for effective communication with communities. Additionally, WHO will seamlessly integrate the principles of AAP into discussions with the Community Health Working Groups, ensuring a synergistic approach to effectively address health care challenges and promote community wellbeing. Key focus will be provided towards guiding principles of Gender Action Plan (2021) and Accountability to Affected Populations (APP) Framework.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

WHO will continue to regularly assess and monitor SEA risks and will ensure the timely and effective implementation of mitigation actions from different SEA risk assessments. Furthermore, WHO will contribute to joint IASC SEA risk assessment. Identified risks will be addressed in respect of WHO PSEAH policy and in line with standard operating procedures (SOP) for SEA complaint referral mechanisms in the refugee response.WHO will closely collaborate with different humanitarian stakeholders in integrating PSEA across emergency and humanitarian service delivery and will ensure the joint workforce capacity strengthening for PSEA safeguarding in program operations. As PSEA Network active member, WHO will continue to contribute to the implementation of PSEA Network joint activity plan and initiatives. Moreover, WHO will contribute to community engagement efforts for PSEA awareness PSEA awareness raising initiatives such as developing and disseminating PSEA safeguarding IEC materials including key PSEA messages on available Community Feedback and Reporting Mechanisms (CFRMs). For operationalization of Survivor Centered approach, jointly with PSEA Network, WHO will continuously ensure safe, accessible, confidential, culturally appropriate, and victim-centered reporting channels for sexual exploitation, abuse, and harassment, tailored to beneficiaries' needs and informed decisions. WHO will ensure organizational staff, contractors, vendors and third parties engaged in the project sign the code of conduct and abide by PSEA principles. WHO will ensure that project staff (e.g., field staff, volunteers, senior management, etc.) and related personnel (including vendors, suppliers, third parties) receive mandatory PSEA training and this include orientation and regular refresher PSEA trainings.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

WHO Emergency Sub Office spearheads the Emergency Preparedness and Response Technical Committee to coordinate with Health Sector, Civil Surgeon’s Office - Health Emergency Operations Center (HEOC), and Office of the Refugee Relief & Repatriation Commissioner to scale up the preparedness and response readiness capacity of WHO Cox’s Bazar and the Health Sector, including the health facilities in the Rohingya camps to key hazards identified in the Multi-Hazard Response Plan and specific contingency and response plans for other hazards including biological such as cholera. Besides that, technical assistance is provided to Health Sector, Mobile Medical Teams, Emergency Preparedness and Response Technical Committee members to guide and calibrate their public health interventions to respond health emergencies and disasters within the framework of disaster risk management.Furthermore, WHO remains actively engaged to detect and monitor potential health threats through EWARS, conducting comprehensive risk assessments to identify hazards and vulnerabilities, providing a foundation for targeted interventions. During crisis, continue to provide support to Mobile Medical Teams ensuring a rapid and coordinated response to health emergencies. WHO will continue to preposition stockpiles for emergency use in camps. In addition, WHO will continue to monitor activities under domains of WASH FIT which includes water, sanitation, and health care waste management in health facilities in camps. In addition, build the capacity of health care workers for safe disposal of infectious and noninfectious waste generated by health facilities in the Rohingya camp settings thus minimizing the impact of waste generated by health facilities on the environment and on climate change.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

Yes, the project has EPR measures embedded such as it includes – Development of emergency preparedness and readiness plans for the most impactful hazards, Risk Analysis to identify the recurrent and evolving hazards that could trigger a crisis with ranking them by impact and likelihood, Preparation of strategies, SoPs, and contingency plan & updating Health Sector-specific Response and Contingency plans such as Cyclone and Monsoon Preparedness & Response Plan, Mobile Medical Team Operational Plan etc, Identification of the surge capacity to augment the response capabilities and also to preserve the emergency contingency funding etc.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

The WHO Global NCD Action Plan and the WHO Global Action Plan on Physical Activity 2018–2030 (GAPPA) set out the global target of a 15% relative reduction in the prevalence of physical inactivity in adults and adolescents by 2030 (from a 2010 baseline). The Objective 2 from WHO GAPPA mentions “Create active environments – creating and maintaining environments that promote and safeguard access to safe places and spaces, for regular physical activity”. Population in emergency settings are more vulnerable for NCDs however, findings are limited with refugee settings. According to a study published in 2022, 89.6% among Rohingya refugees were found with insufficient physical activity which poses as a risk factor for future increase in prevalence of overweight and obesity. Developing an open Gym in the Rohingya Camp settings will be an example of system approach with the structural step taken to reduce inactivity and support sustaining mental health in children, young and adolescents.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

WHO continues to promote self-reliance and resilience among Rohingya refugees by enhancing access to essential health services and implementing comprehensive risk communication strategies. This project includes capacity-building initiatives of key community group of women (currently serving as traditional birth attendants- TBA) for non-clinical labor room support, and act as referral linkage, and health promotion for Rohingya mothers. WHO also focuses on preventive care, vaccination programs, and health education to build a healthier, more resilient community. Collaboration and cross-sector coordination with partners ensure sustainable health outcomes and strengthen healthcare infrastructure. It aims to use real-time PHEOC data to inform planning and emerging challenges, monitor health, and track outcomes of the refugees. Integrating PHEOC with community strategies, Rohingya refugees can improve resilience, minimize health risks, and recover more quickly from crises in the camps at Cox's Bazar.

4.2 Do project activities in the host community link to development initiatives, and/or align with development priorities in the Cox’s Bazar district? If yes, briefly describe.If yes, briefly describe.

Yes. WHO assists the sole molecular IEDCR Field Laboratory at Cox’s Bazar Medical College, focusing on hepatitis C surveillance, antimicrobial resistance, and cholera culture which supports the whole Cox's Bazar District. WHO-Supported Multi-disciplinary skills Lab in Cox’s Bazar Medical College. The multi-disciplinary skills lab is a practical training hub that teaches clinical procedures through a structured and supervised teaching concept. The trainees learn and practice the targeted skills in a safe environment until they reach the required minimum standard for patient treatment. The training also includes a rigorous evaluation process that tests the knowledge and skills of the trainees before and after the training and through an Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE).
