Project Summary

The primary goal of this project is to reduce avoidable morbidity, mortality and disability as a result of NCDs and MH complications in remote and hard to reach locations. The project establishes a comprehensive access of NCDs and MH services to vulnerable people including migrants, displaced families, returnees and people with special needs. In this project, WHO will strengthen the capacities of the health facilities with the provision of basic and supplementary medical equipment for the treatment of NCD and MH disorders and deliver mobile medical teams to remote and hard to reach locations. In addition to strengthening MoH capacity on managing gender based violence (GBV) during emergencies as well as pre and post emergencies.

1 - Needs

Hospitals and primary health care (PHC) facilities have been badly affected. 23-25% of the 1 145 public PHC facilities assessed in October 2019 are currently closed (see Annex 1 for more detailed information). Health sector partners have reported acute shortages of medical specialists and nurses, as well as huge gaps in coverage due to the uneven distribution of general internists, most of whom are working in urban areas. In many remote and hard-to-reach locations, poor and vulnerable communities continue to have very limited access to health care including noncommunicable disease (NCDs) and mental health (MH), which account for 77% of the burden of disease and are the leading cause of death in Libya. Forced displacement with loss of access to existing medication or assistive devices, loss of prescription, lack of access to health care services due to displacement leading to prolongation of disruption of treatment. Degradation of living conditions, loss of shelter, shortage of water and regular food supplies, and lack of income adding to physical and psychological strain. Interruption of care through the destruction of health infrastructure, disruption of medical supplies and inability to access health care provider who been killed, injured, displaced or are unable to return to work. With given the current emergencies in Libya, leading to the increasing numbers of IDPs, difficulties to access health facilities either damaged or deserted, sever lack of essential medicines and absence of professional health staff providers. Many people lack sustained health access related to NCDs either to primary health care facility or even to secondary and tertiary centres. Professional health staff and medicines are the main factors to ensure minimum quality of essential services to NCD population. The Gender Based Violence (GBV) Health Responses in Libya Scoping Mission Report by the GBV WHO regional technical officer in March 2020, highlighted the limited availability of trained staff, health services and referral options for GBV Survivors. With the Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR) having the second highest prevalence of violence against women in the world, with an estimated 37% of women reporting physical and/or sexual violence in their lifetime, a proper response from the health sector has emerged as a public health issue and priority.

2 - Activities

1- Improve access to comprehensive NCD and MH services through primary health care system;• Train relevant health professionals on NCD kits contents and how to maximise benefits of kits content. • Distribute NCD kits with essential primary diagnostic material and equipment with preliminary focus on area with higher vulnerable groups such as IDPs and migrants.• Procure personal protection equipment (PPE) to ensure continuity of essential NCD and MH services during current COVID-19 pandemic.• Conduct screening campaigns for diabetes and hypertension in IDPs camps. • Train facility health workers on NCD and MH guidelines. 2- Improving capacity of health emergency department to provide NCDs and MH essential services through facility based and outreach services; • Train senior level MoH staff at central and district level WHO PEN/HEART technical packages.• Conduct training for facility health nurses on MH emergency Settings.• Reactivate referral mechanism in cooperation with municipality ambulance health services.• Reactivate NCD and MH mobile clinics in post-conflict and hard to reach areas. • Establishment of outreach teams that provide psychosocial support (PSS) to the community, IDPs, and Migrants. • Train a cohort of community health workers based on NCD and PSS training module of the regional office 3- Strengthen MoH capacity to implement GBV services during pre and post phases of emergencies;• Raise community awareness in collaboration with the MoH and other relative stakeholders about GBV as a public health issue.• Train senior level MoH staff at central and district level to improve GBV services and response pathways.• train primary health care physicians on the latest WHO guidelines regarding the Clinical Management of Rape and integration of GBV into the health services and response plans.

5 - Mitigation measures
5.1 - For each risk mentioned under 4 above, please describe the ways you plan to mitigate, prevent and/or address these.

4.1: WHO’s network of field officers work at the municipality level, where all emergency activities are implemented. The field officers report to WHO’s office in Tripoli on a regular basis. Local community networks, CBOs and community influencers ensure the accountability of WHO and partners’ activities at the municipality level. Foster mutual accountability on the domestic budget made available for NCDs and MH, as well as for external pledges. WHO is also a member of the United Nations Evaluation Group, which brings together the evaluation units of UN departments, specialized agencies, and affiliated organizations to share best practices and advocate for the importance of evaluation for learning, decision-making and accountability. WHO’s fundamental principles of integrity, accountability, independence, impartiality, respect and professional commitment are documented in its code of ethics and professional conduct. 4.2: Previous implementation of NCD and MH related activities had the experience of communities against PHC-facilities and doctors, splitting distributed supplies between East and West, tribe approach when distributing supplies. This would bemitigated by ensuring distribution of supplies based on equity and equality starting from distribution, using the most closest sea port available in the region for supplies in that region instead of receiving it far away from the area and advocating foraddressing these risks at the highest levels by leadership of WHO in Libya. 4.4: Project activities not only expose the WHO staff to COVID19 but as the COVID assistance increases, more people get involved in the field in COVID related activities which are indispensable for the COVID response itself. The project at the same time addresses the issue by providing PPE supplies and IPC training and testing assistance to the healthcare workers, WHO staff in office and the field and the communities. 4.6: It is made sure that no personal data regarding case management of patients is being collected during any of the project activities. WHO ensures a strict internal and external controls for data protection and data privacy.4.23: The project will achieve its overall objective by coordinating the financing and response actions needed to reduceavoidable mortality and morbidity and reduce the economic, social, and security impacts of the pandemic both for migrants and the Libyan population. The main challenges faced by the Libyan population during COVID-19 that need to be addressedinclude the failure to rapidly mobilize financing for appropriate detection and response efforts in Libyan migrants in particular and the Libyan population in general will result in increased mortality and morbidity, significant economic consequences and reduced resilience. By focusing on the containment, diagnosis and treatment of patients, this Project seeks to help control NCDs and MH disorder during pandemic and limit socioeconomic losses. Some of the project activities, including those focused on training and capacity building, will help strengthen the preparedness of the country to combat NCDs emergencies during other future epidemics, and they are expected to be sustained even after this project closes. There is a growing awareness in Libya of the importance of preparedness against pandemics and emergencies in general, and this is an agenda that is expected to receive growing support and financing in the future.
