Project Summary

Palestinians in the West Bank and East Jerusalem are subject to an extreme coercive environment, worsened by the ongoing tensions and conflicts, resulting in a wide range of humanitarian needs. The frequent escalations in the WB and EJ further exacerbate the situation of protracted violence and increased the protection needs of Palestinians living in vulnerable communities. More specifically, the use of live ammunition and of force by the Israeli Forces, the settler´s attacks, and the demolition of Palestinian houses, have drastically risen in the last years in East Jerusalem and the West Bank, which affects, in a devastating way, mental health wellbeing of the victims, but also of the communities to which they belong and increasing the needs for emergency response.
In line with the strategic objectives, priority needs, and most vulnerable populations established by the Protection Cluster and HNO, and considering the regular SWOT analysis conducted as part of MdM-CH’s standard project cycle management procedures, as well as the periodic need assessments and risk analysis conducted by our staff and its experience in the area of operation, in addition to the feedback of the beneficiaries of our intervention and our partners, our action follows two components, one toward resilience building, one toward providing an emergency response:
1) To strengthen most vulnerable communities' resilience capacities and coping mechanism to face the coercive environment in the area of intervention. At the same time, public awareness will be conducted to inform on the impact of critical incidents on Palestinian communities. The result expected is the improvement on the psychosocial wellbeing of children and adults, developing tools to mitigate the impact of occupation related violence, reducing their level of stress and preventing trauma, with a special focus on the groups identified as most vulnerable: communities under high risk, first liners, ex-detainee children, people presenting suicidal behaviors or ideations, PWDs and GBV victims
2) To improve protection mechanisms for Palestinian living in communities at risk of IHL violations in the WB and EJ through increased access to quality mental health and psychosocial services with a gender sensitive approach. The expected result is the reinforcement of access for every victim of occupation-related critical incident to MHPSS Emergency Response and protection case management in the area of intervention.
In coordination with the WBPC and the Protection Cluster.
This project comes as a support to MdM's work on MHPSS-Emergency Response in the West Bank under the PUI technical supervision for the West Bank Protection Consortium (WBPC) since 2020. It further collaborates with WBPC's efforts to improve the mental health wellbeing of Palestinian population through individual and community resilience building work, with special attention to the most vulnerable groups, also in coordination with WBPC members, mostly in Area C of the governorate of Hebron, Bethlehem and in East Jerusalem, areas where we are already active.
In addition, it seeks to improve emergency response by providing assistance and case management to, at least, 90% of ALL kind of occupation-related incidents that may need for MHPSS (people under home detention, demolitions, excessive use of force by the Israeli Forces and settler's violence), and by coordinating with WBPC members, mostly in Area C of the governorate of Hebron, Bethlehem and in East Jerusalem, areas where we are already active. Lack of capacities at the moment is allowing us to provide a response to approximately 60% of the triggers in these areas. These percentages will probably be modified in case of high escalations of hostilities.
The intervention will respond to the needs of people affected by occupation related violence, with special attention to women, adolescents, ex detainees, GBV victims, people presenting suicidal ideations or behaviors and PWDs.

1 - Needs

Palestinians live in a permanent state of extreme tension due largely to the encroachment by settlements and the occupation policy promoted by the government of Israel. Palestinians are subjected to military occupation, as well as to generalized poverty and lack of services. Surrounded and controlled by a strong military presence, Palestinians are submitted to regular requisitions at checkpoints, and random limitations in movement and access permissions, destruction of crops and other livelihood means, limited control over natural resources, daily violence and harassment from settlers, repeated home demolitions, and the revocation of their residency rights, facing daily violence and harassment from settlers and intensified settlement activity in EJ, H2 of Hebron, and the West Bank’s Area C. According to OCHA, during the first eight months of 2022 a total of 590 Palestinian-owned structures have been demolished or seized, forcibly displacing 707 Palestinians, and adversely affecting 16,768 more across the West Bank and EJ. The increased use of live ammunitions and disproportionate use of force by Israeli forces across the West Bank, including EJ, has led to an alarming increase in Palestinians fatalities. With 84 Palestinians killed between January and 20 September, 2022 is so far the deadliest year for the West Bank since 2008. The same period accounts for 7564 injured, including 747 children. Settler violence against Palestinians has reached a peak since 2005, the number of arrests and administrative detentions are at the highest since 2017 and 2008 respectively. Since the beginning of October alone, 15 Palestinians, including six children, have been killed by Israeli forces during search-and-arrest operations, exchanges of fire, or confrontations between Israeli forces and Palestinians in the West Bank including EJ, often following settler attacks or incursions into Palestinian villages. In EJ, and specially Essawiyyeh, Jabl al Mukabir and Shu’fat Refugge Camp, communities have been witnessing an alarming escalation in violence, fueled by Israeli practices over this year, along with an unprecedented number of ISF operations recorded, with continuous violent clashes and search operations. Tuqou’, in Bethlehem, or H2 in Hebron have also witnessed worrisome escalations especially in settler attacks, confiscations for the construction of bypass roads, as well as demolitions following the absence of building permits. This context is leading to feelings of insecurity and powerlessness, and couples with economic precariousness are causing most of the mental health issues experienced by Palestinians. According to a recent study conducted by Médecins du Monde and AIDA (No Peace of Mind, 2022), 80% of respondents indicated they experienced feelings of extreme fear and stress, with some explicitly saying they feared for their lives. There is also a general perceived threat to the ability to make ends meet and protect their herds, fields, personal belongings and assets, such as their homes, water tanks and cars. All this leaves the Palestinian population with chronic MH disorders such as anxiety, depression, and behavioural impact such as sleeping disorders and isolation, and psychosomatic disorders (mostly headaches and stomach pain) across age and genders — especially those who experience severe traumatizing incidents such as arrest, excessive use of force, and self-demolitions. Women as well as children are at higher risk to be victims of the circle of violence that might result from the ongoing tensions due to the occupation and growing socioeconomic difficulties as well as the traditional patriarchal norms and values rooted in the society (OCHA, 2022). GBV cases tend to increase in case of escalation of violence, individual or collective (UNFPA). Women and girls from Area C and H2 suffer from higher incidence of GBV, intimate partner violence, sexual abuse and exploitation. Bedouins women notably are particularly vulnerable. Women with disabilities face additional barriers as the lack of access to information about GBV prevention and response, and where and how to access relevant assistance (OCHA, 2022). The stigma around mental health disorders is preventing access to MHPSS, consequently this project targets to pursue MdM wide work on awareness raising on mental health in the West Bank. Children are particularly vulnerable to this context and a high majority of them report troubles of minimum concentration, sadness and bad mood, as well as a feeling of lack of safety, inhering their academic and social development. Further, child detention remains an issue of high concern given the fragility of children who undergo such traumatizing experience. Each year, between 500 and 700 Palestinian children (12-17 years old, mostly boys) are detained and prosecuted by the Israeli court system. According to info provided by Save the Children, 8 in 10 child detainees were physically beaten while 9 in 10 experienced verbal abuse. They face treatment such as beatings, strip searches, psychological abuse, weeks in solitary confinement, and being denied access to a lawyer during interrogations. In addition, in conflict environment, good parenting is also affected by trauma and toxic stress and children are therefore also victims of the cycle of violence. There is a high level of violence at home, with 90% facing some form of violent discipline at home (92.3% of the boys and 97,9% of the girls) in the oPt. This includes 20,1% of children subject to severe physical violence from their caregivers (23,3% of boys and 16,9% of girls), with the rate of 13,7% in the West Bank (HRP, 2022). First liners as health staff, journalists or even policemen are witnesses and victims of occupation related violence. Our recent work with this population showed us the great needs for this population in terms of MH support, in-depth knowledge of their coping mechanism and detection of symptoms of MH disorders.

2 - Activities

In cooperation with local CBOs, public institutions, and the WBPC members, our action is following two aims: - to improve the psychosocial coping mechanisms and resilience capacities to face the coercive environment - to conduct MHPSS Emergency Response (ER) to Critical Occupation-related Incidents in the governorates of Hebron, Bethlehem and in EJ. In terms of resilience building: ► Group & community psycho-educational sessions on the impacts of traumatic events on MH and connection to available resources. These sessions address occupation-related critical incident impacts on women, men, girls and boys and the potential ways of coping with them. ► MHPSS peer support groups in recurrent exposure locations to critical incidents to mitigate the impact of violence and coercive environment. A special emphasis will be given to the most vulnerable beneficiaries namely women, children and/or youth for high risk of detention in the most affected and uncovered areas. ► Individual psychosocial counseling for most vulnerable groups: members of communities under high risk, ex-detainee children, first liners: policemen, firefighters, health staff or journalist. ► Group psychosocial counseling for most vulnerable groups identified before. ► Family counseling and PSS for caregivers and family members of most vulnerable groups identified before. ► Group family counseling and PSS for caregivers and family members of most vulnerable groups identified before. ► Use data collected in the field for designing and implementing a campaigning and developing awareness raising materials for MHPSS promotion at a community level in the targeted areas. Awareness sessions can target broad public to sensitize on MH and inform on the available service for support, but also more specific population as first liners, to sensitize them on the topic of MH in relation with their job, leading to better management of the topic, as well as improving their own coping mechanisms. ► Provision of financial support and capacity building to SAWA for their MHPSS services, including case management, medical and legal consultation in the West Bank and EJ, through a toll-free 24/7 helpline, WhatsApp, or in-person consultation. These various means allow the provision of support despite difficulties moving, hearing, speaking or writing, related to disabilities, distance, security reasons or fear of social stigma (as victims of GBV). It operates under strict protocols on privacy and confidentiality. MdM-CH and SAWA refer each other's cases and have been working together since 2016. In terms of emergency response: ► MHPSS ER mobile units will be activated upon the reception of OCHA or community/ media triggers for different types of Settler violence, demolition and ISF excessive use of force incidents. ► Subsequently, an initial visit to the survivors within the first 72 hours after crosschecking the critical event with the local community will take place. During this initial visit, Psychological First Aid (PFA) will be provided, if needed and with beneficiary consent. This screening will aim at detecting MH and psychosocial wellbeing issues and other related concerns resulting from the critical event or pre-existing (but exacerbated). PFA & screening will follow MdM Clinical SOPs. ► 10-15 days after the initial visit, MHPSS ER mobile units will conduct a second visit with prior beneficiary consent to further follow up on emerging MHPSS needs resulting or intensified by the critical incident/s. The second visit will include a complementary individual assessment catered to survivors, further detecting MHPSS-specific individual and family needs. At this stage, the assessment will be more comprehensive, as the persons' and families' initial distress might have subsided. ►Based on the results of the in-depth clinical assessment of the second visit, beneficiaries provide consent to receive: Problem Management (PM+) sessions, individual or group counseling, referral to advanced MH services. These are conducted following MdM clinical SOP for MHPSS ER. In this activity, the most vulnerable groups will benefit from tailored in depth MHPSS interventions, according to their specific needs Referrals are made for people in need to other actors such as members of the WBPC or SAWA for different types of protection services (shelter, livelihood, financial, medical assistance, WASH, and/or advanced MHPSS). The referrals pathways are based on FRM Inter-agency referral mechanism and are considering high protective procedures regarding the data by using the national referral system. MdM-CH MHPSS team is made up of men and women psychologists and social workers to ensure a gender-sensitive provision of the sessions. Special attention is given to women, girls and boys during counselling sessions to prospect if they are victims of GBV or other intrafamilial violence. Necessary support and referrals are made according to the needs of the survivors. Family counselling sessions or peer-support groups with caregivers are notably implemented in order to break the circle of violence inside the family. The activities are notably made to tackle the taboo of MH and an effort is made on sensitizing men, the most reluctant, on the importance of MHPSS. Pre and post-tests adapted for children and adults are implemented at the beginning and the end of the follow up of cases. These tests allow to measure the improvement of the MH, behaviors and/or stress management. MdM focal point review all these tests with a gender and age sensitive approach and adapt our approach according to the results. MdM-CH works in close collaboration with local CBOs in each locality or DBOs facilitating our access to the population and referring us beneficiaries. We provided them training on MHPSS in emergency settings based on IASC guidelines. Our MHPSS field staff is made up only of local professionals, ensuring also a higher acceptance by the beneficiaries.

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

MdM-CH’s Feedback and complaints mechanism are in place in Palestine as part of the M&E process, following WHO globally recognized complain mechanisms, procedures, and standards (rules of conduct for abusive behavior as part of MdM prevention alert and management standardized system since 2018). Feedback & complaint channels are handled exclusively by designated office staff (focal point). Response is provided regardless of whether corrective measures are feasible to be implemented or not. Complaint & feedback is handled on the basis of sensitivity: - Less sensitive complaints & feedback are handled on country level, with support from our focal point at HQ. - Sensitive complaints (PSEA or corruption) are handled on an HQ level, with support from field staff. MdM CH is a member at the oPt´s PSEA network. 2 staff members have received TOT training on PSEA from the PSEA International Coordinator, and one staff member was designated officially as PSEA focal point. She handles all the F&C channels, to guarantee full confidentiality. All complaints and feedback are taken seriously and, if further investigation is needed, other professionals might be involved in handling the case depending on the matter raised. Since 2020, one of our main partners in the West Bank, Sawa Helpline, is one of the main reporting channel for PSEA for the entire oPt, meaning that any beneficiary from the West Bank, East Jerusalem or Gaza can use the phone number 121 to report misconduct. The results of MdM-CH’s community surveys and the results of its regular Beneficiary Satisfaction Surveys (BSS) for MHPSS ER, have informed the design and strategy of the proposed intervention.

All our beneficiaries and partners receive a card with information on how to provide feedback & complaints using different mechanisms (email, phone number, orally to the field staff, complaint boxes) to guarantee accessibility to provide feedback or complaints for all of them, including people with disabilities, with or without internet access. 10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

At all stages of the Health Project Cycle management: Diagnosis, Implementation and M&E, protection mainstreaming is a cross-cutting theme for MdM-CH’s programs and projects. MdM-CH understand and fully adhere to the core principle of “Do No Harm” as a humanitarian imperative, a cornerstone in all programs followed by the pyramid of IASC minimum standards regarding MHPSS & PSEA. We follow strong PSEA policies to protect beneficiaries and staff, following international standards under the umbrella of the PSEA network. MdM-CH remains vigilant throughout project implementation to identify and prevent unintended negative consequences of their actions. In this regard a number of steps and measures are adopted to ensure that its interventions maximize positive impacts for community stability, mainly by strengthening PIN capacities and resilience, while minimizing harm. Our MHPSS team is made up of local professionals trained and used to work with children, especially the most vulnerables such as ex-detainee children and children survivors of political violence. Safe identification and referral procedures are known of the staff. MdM-CH has been working with ex-detainee children since 2016. Child-friendly spaces are provided for the children as well as the highest confidentiality measures. Ex-detainee children are mainly advised to participate to peer counselling sessions to tackle the feeling of isolation due to their particular individual experience. A deep work is made on the acceptance of their feelings and pain. More generally, every counselling session is held in safe and accessible spaces provided by the community. In addition, SAWA’s provision of MHPSS through its helpline allows to overcome to fear of social stigma. Moreover, to further enhance protection mainstreaming the project will ensure that activities are designed considering the following references: MHPSS IASC minimum standards: Guidelines on Mental Health and Psychosocial Support in Emergency Settings (WHO guidelines). MdM-CH is indeed an active members of the relevant clusters and work in cooperation with MoH and MoE, we contribute to the evaluation of the needs and advocate on them, the M&E includes the beneficiaries and CBOs, appropriate referrals are made according to the guidelines, our staff is local and aware of cultural sensitivity to provide safe, dignify, culturally and socially appropriate assistance, they regularly participate to trainings and supervision is provided for them, resilience building is emphasized, as well as access to supportive education, etc. GBV: Minimum Standards for Prevention and Response to Gender-based Violence in Emergencies (following UNFPA standards). Child Protection Minimum standards in Humanitarian action (following Unicef and Sphere guidelines, as well as MdM guidelines). MdM-CH is an active member of the CP sub-cluster and work in coordination with the MoH, CBOs and WBPC, our staff is trained and used to work with children, our staff is local and aware of cultural sensitivity to provide safe, dignify, culturally and socially appropriate assistance, they are also trained on data protection and appropriate referrals, caregivers are deeply integrated in our actions to reduce the cycle of violence, work is done on improving the supporting system of the children and sensitizing the community on mental health, etc. Disability mainstreaming and health: CBR (Community based rehabilitation guidelines) health component of WHO. PSEA IASC guide our feedback & complaint mechanisms. Our mobile team is well aware of the security protocol when they go on the field to do counseling sessions in remote and at-risk of violence due to the occupation. An assessment of the risks is made before any movement through the attentive follow up of official and unofficial (social media) security notifications. In terms of psychological risks, MdM-CH is financing supervision session for its team.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

MdM-CH is basing their interventions on ethical and humanitarian principles. This project aligns with the principal approach that is widely accepted in the humanitarian community through such vehicles as the SPHERE Humanitarian Charter (and SPHERE MHPSS). Ethical principles in healthcare include respect for persons to advance human dignity and for individuals’ autonomous choices; beneﬁcence, the promotion of others’ well-being, commonly known as “do no harm,” as described above and justice, encompassing both the fair distribution of resources and fair processes for decision-making. Humanitarian principles is the second source of values and norms that animate both applicants’ actions. HP include humanity, requiring that suffering must be addressed wherever and for whomever it is found; neutrality, the duty of humanitarian actors not to take sides in a conﬂict; impartiality, which stipulates that humanitarian actors must not discriminate or give preference to any nationality, physical ability, race, religious belief, class, political opinion, or similar status; and independence, which demands that humanitarian actors retain their autonomy and remain independent of political or military objectives of other actors. The activities are implemented notably in remote areas (bedouins communities) thus our team is mobile and coming to the closest to the beneficiaries (mostly to the house of the beneficiaries or to easy to reach facilities) to ensure the provision of the services for everyone with and without disabilities to our services and to our information. Information are also given both orally and by writing to allow their right transmission. Consultation are made in an inclusive way by the MHPSS team. They are given in accessible, safe spaces and child-friendly spaces. In cases of psychological disabilities, if the disability is mild to moderate, our MHPSS team made of psychologists and social workers is trained to do a follow up with the beneficiaries. If the psychological disability is severe, referrals are made to appropriate organizations. Our local partner SAWA is notably providing case management through a helpline, by writing through WhatsApp, as well as in person, to ensure the accessibility of MHPSS for people with disabilities, with difficulties to speak orally or to hear, or afraid of social stigma. CBOs and DBOs support us in neutralizing social stigma that limit the participation of the beneficiaries in the activities.
