Project Summary

The United Nations (UN) estimates the number of crisis-affected people in need of humanitarian assistance in Syria to be 14.2 million (48.7 % male and 51.3 % female), of which almost six million are in acute need. The above figure of people in need includes 6.7 million protracted internally displaced persons (IDPs); estimated 320,000 spontaneous IDPs return; 480,000 Palestine refugees and the rest overburdened host communities. More than 70% of the population in need of humanitarian assistance are accessible from within Syria.
Overall, 12.4 million of people are estimated to be food insecure (including 1.27 million severely food insecure and 9.5 million moderately food insecure) and 1.9 million at risk of food insecurity. In this context, FAO has designed a project to assist 217,000 small-scale herder households (equivalent to 1,300,000 people) in Syria. The objective of the proposed action is to protect the livestock assets of these vulnerable herders through the provision of vaccinations and treatment of livestock. The expected project outputs are:
Output 1. Access to veterinary services to protect against and treat transboundary animal diseases (TADs) and common livestock diseases increased to benefit 217,000 herders. The output aims at providing animal healthcare, vaccinations against foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), Enterotoxaemia and treatment against ecto endo parasites (as per sector response package under emergency livestock vaccination and treatment).common and urgent diseases
Output 2. Capacity of veterinarians and veterinary technicians for vaccination and treatment of livestock strengthened. This output aims at training of veterinarians and veterinary technicians and equipping them on TADs and zoonotic diseases control and eradication.
It is expected that the support to protect livestock assets through improving access to animal health services against TADs and common livestock diseases and enhancing the capacity of veterinarians and technicians to conduct vaccinations will help ensure the survival of the remaining livestock herd while increasing their productivity, which will lead to improvement of livelihoods, increase local availability of food, support self-reliance and improve the food and nutrition security of small-scale herders in Syria, especially the most vulnerable.

4 - Needs

Keeping livestock alive, healthy and productive is vital for the food security and livelihoods of large numbers of poor, vulnerable households in rural areas. Historically, Syria had a highly developed and effective animal health system that reduced the likelihood and impact of animal diseases and ensured that animal populations were generally healthy. The healthier animals are stronger and they are less prone to diseases. This helps maintain their value and quality, thereby increasing their importance for food and nutritional security and household incomes. Animal health services focuses on two broad areas: (1) preventative health treatments, to keep animals healthy (mainly through treatments to reduce endo and ecto parasites, provide multivitamins etc.) and (2) vaccination programmes, to protect animal populations from disease outbreaks. It is important to note that healthier and stronger animals are less prone to diseases on the one hand and that weak, unhealthy animals are often unsuitable for vaccination programmes, particularly those that involve live vaccines. Syria’s animal health system has suffered significant damage during the crisis, meaning that many livestock owners do not have access to either preventative animal treatments or vaccination programmes. This has increased the scale of livestock losses and has led to the widespread distress sale of livestock to neighbouring countries. Livestock is a key factor for food security and resilience for vulnerable people in times of crisis. 6 - Protection Risk Analysis

1) Risk: Beneficiaries being targeted as a result of being selected for project/risk of community tension arising from beneficiaries selection. Mitigation measures: Inclusion of different categories of affected population (examples: IDPs and host communities; returnees and refugees; men, women, boys, girls and youth based their specific needs and clear selection criteria agreed upon by local committees).2) Risk: Inclusion and exclusion errors. Mitigation measures: Establish a local beneficiary selection committee with equitable representation of vulnerable groups; putting in place clear and precise guidance on beneficiary selection criteria and process; and ensuring an effective communication for guidance in local language.3) Risk of diversion of assistance/assets. Mitigation measures: Establish community-based beneficiary selection and community-based monitoring.The design of the project has taken into account protection elements, in line with the do not harm principles. To the maximum possible extent, all measures will be taken to limit the risk of beneficiaries being targeted as a result of being selected for the project. Where IDPs are selected, representatives from the host communities will be included as well, to limit possible frictions between the two, and a special attention will be provided to the returnees to help them in their settlement and resuming their production activities through protecting and rehabilitating their assets.
