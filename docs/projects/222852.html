Project Summary

The project aims to reduce the increasing malnutrition rate and improve maternal and child health and nutrition outcomes in Rohingya and adjacent host communities. In this project, Save the Children (SC) will implement integrated preventive health and nutrition services to supplement the direct nutrition partners by doing nutritional assessment, early identification and maintaining strong referral mechanism with nutrition partners through 3 health posts and 2 Primary Health Care Centers (PHCCs) in 5 camps. The nutrition project will continue providing comprehensive, integrated, and coordinated nutritional services to the affected population by targeting Pregnant and Lactating Women (PLW), young children, and under-five children who attend the health facilities for clinical needs and continue the linkage with direct implementing partners to supplement the nutrition sector.
The services encompass screening and managing at-risk mother-child pairs by implementing the Management of At-risk Mothers and Infants (MAMI) care pathway, anthropometric measurement and nutritional assessment of 6-59 months children, pregnant women, and lactating mothers with need-based counselling. Furthermore, SC will focus on early detection and prevention of Non-Communicable Disease (NCD) by addressing the risk factors specially the unhealthy diet, tobacco consumption and lifestyle modification. SC will continue to provide anti and post-natal care, protect, promote and support of breastfeeding and appropriate complementary feeding counselling, immunisation, treatment of diarrhea with zinc, Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS), contributing to achieving more integrated and sustainable outcomes and aligned with the nutrition sector strategy objectives.
In addition, SC will support under-five malnourished children with clinical complications by establishing a Stabilization Centre in PHCC integrated with skilled psychosocial support to ensure desirable child growth and development. The stabilization centre will establish in Camp-21 PHCC by dedicating two pediatrics beds to support severely malnourished under-five children with complications that referred from the Integrated Nutrition Facility (INF) and identified children in the PHCC. The service also included the lifesaving support like re-lactation by Supplementary Suckling Technique (SST) and services will provide by trained health professionals.
As per the Social Behavioral Change Communication (SBCC) approach, SC will engage existing Community Health Workers (CHWs) to continue community mobilization through door-to-door household visits and develop Information Education Communication (IEC) materials related to NCD such as lifestyle adaptation approach and building healthy food habits, and related topics. These will contribute to improving health and nutrition outcomes. Additionally, by continuing community-based mental health support, this project will directly contribute to sustaining and building the community's resilience.
Finally, SC will continue leading the Infant Young Child Feeding in Emergency (IYCF-E) technical working group and provide strategic guidance to the nutrition sector and partners for evidence-based decision-making, capacity building and quality intervention.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

The levels of acute malnutrition among Rohingya refugees living in Cox’s Bazar remain high. According to the SENS survey of December 2023, the GAM rate is 15.1%, and the stunting rate is 41.2% among the 6-59 months children, which is alarming, and both are very high public health significance. On the other hand, exclusive breastfeeding and blanket supplementary feeding programme coverage is good, but Minimum Acceptable Diet (MAD), and Minimum Dietary Diversity (MDD) rates are still on a worsening trend (16.5% and 26.9%). Moreover, the anaemia rate in under-five children (38.2%) and women (24.1%) was found to be worse and considered as medium public health significance. In addition, 3% of children were found as non-breastfed, 43.8% of children did not consume fruits or 54.5% consumed unhealthy food and 28.8% consumed sugar-sweetened beverages. Though Minimum meal frequency (MMF – 47.3%) and consumption of protein as egg and/or flesh food (51.8%) were found in a improving trend, there is gaps in household level practice which is associated with stunting and underweight among children and hampered growth and development. According to Health Sector (Jan-Sep 2024), the crude death rate among refugees is 1.84 per 1,000 people (1,946 deaths), and the maternal mortality rate is estimated at 186 per 100,000 live births with infant mortality at 25.2 per 1,000 live births and under-5 mortality at 28.4 per 1,000 live births. Moreover, the non-communicable disease rate is increasing. Enhancing nutritional knowledge, behaviors, and practices of parents and caregivers of children aged 0-59 months through nutrition education, counselling, and SBCC sessions are needed to improve dietary practices and ensure sustained changes for cases of malnutrition in the camps and host community, incorporating the nutrition sector strategy priorities. Despite ongoing efforts in nutrition curative and preventive services, the pace of decline in malnutrition rates has been gradual in recent years. Several factors contribute to this trend, including a limited diversity in dietary offerings, prevailing unhealthy social support systems, cultural norms, and hesitance to act on referral recommendations. Emphasizing preventive integrated approaches delivered at each service point, boosting the messages during sickness episodes when changes of nutrition status faltering are high, and integrated service provision of clinical, mental, and nutrition assessment with counselling avoiding referral time laps will give an edge to accelerate the gain. Addressing these gaps requires service modality beyond a single nutrition service point, at least for preventive components, reaching each possible service facility available for multiple exposers to counselling and follow-up of compliance. As child centered organization, Save the Children observed during nutrition service delivery at PHCC and consultation with relevant actors in Camp 21, it is challenging to refer the programme participants to a distant stabilization centre, rather they preferred this specific service at their nearest health facility. Also, self-transportation and approval to leave the camp is a challenge for them.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

SC is actively engaged in protection activities, focusing on safeguarding children from harm and advocating for their rights. SCI has mainstreamed the protection theme from the project designing phase to needs assessment where vulnerable groups, pregnant and lactating women, adolescents, persons with disabilities, and diverse population’s potential requirements are identified. Besides, community mobilization activities ensure community participation of all age and gender groups. Analyze the identified needs, risks, and community strengths, the project will plan for necessary resource mobilization and capacity enhancement initiatives. Protection principles will be mainstreamed with particular attention while responding to infectious disease outbreaks and when designing and implementing emergency plans in case of any climate change impact and natural disasters. To manage special cases in the nutrition aspect, SC includes a protection lens to protect non-breastfed children (unaccompanied), underaged pregnant and lactating women, and adolescents and ensure a safe environment. Particular attention will be dedicated to all mechanisms of the project to protect refugees from Sexual Abuse and Exploitation by humanitarian workers. Sector Specific Tip Sheets with a measure to establish a Protection Mainstreaming Action Plan will be part of the staff orientation, monitoring, and reporting process.

2.3 - Age & Disability

SC Health post services, including nutrition corner, is designed for all groups of population, male, female, persons with disabilities (PWD), and all gender orientations Sexual Orientation, Gender Identity and Expression and Sex characteristics (SOGIESC). The inclusion of PWD and SOGIESC are the priorities, and health posts, including nutrition corners, will be made structurally suitable for their access and comfort in seeking service. Children with special mental and physical needs will be referred to appropriate service providers for feeding technique support. Community workers will ensure that PWD and diverse populations are practicing optimal nutrition practices and barriers can be minimized through regular household visits. SC also make sure the services facility is accessible for the programme participants. SC has a comprehensive safeguarding policy and conducts risk assessments of every project at the design stage, including programme participants based on age, a person with a disability, and other diverse populations. The mitigation plan is part of the program and budget accordingly. Safeguarding orientation is part of staff induction. Awareness materials and reporting channels are well circulated and displayed in facilities, and participants are informed about the process. There is a dedicated safeguarding team in the regional and country offices to gatekeeping the policy. Any report or flagging is addressed with highest priorities. A safeguarding report is generated quarterly to be shared among staff, concern stakeholders and donor.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

The existing SC accountability framework applies both proactive and reactive channels. (e.g. Community meetings, feedback box, exit interviews, toll-free hotlines, helpdesk, 8 different child-friendly tools, post-distribution monitoring, home visit) to collect feedback. SC ensures capacity strengthening of the Complaint Feedback and Response Mechanism (CFRM) and reporting process by ensuring that information is shared and accessible with the project participants, staff and volunteers. SC will conduct assessments for this project through FGD, KII and participatory consultations, both in Rohingya and host communities through a dedicated and trained accountability team, aligning with the Core Humanitarian Standard, and applying child-friendly reporting tools. SC will develop half-yearly key performance indicators to ensure the effectiveness of the program and assess the ratio of the resolved feedback and response, progress of the activities, and other factors. The needs and suggestions of the programme participants will be considered in programming by ensuring their participation in different platforms and events, sharing learning sessions, and findings reflected in quarterly/monthly and/or need-based reports. Besides, SC will follow adaptive management as part of the project’s accountability process through conducting needs assessment, consultation, baseline, midline and end-line surveys. SC has an accountability focal for its Nutrition programme, who will provide technical guidance and support to ensure the functional and organizational effectiveness of the accountability in this project. Morover, this project will also ensure the inclusion of persons with disabilities, and gender-segregated feedback and response (boy, girl, man, woman, transgender) to respond to the unique needs of vulnerable individuals.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

In this project, SC will dedicatedly mainstream the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) by ensuring that all staff, volunteers, and partners adhere to the highest standards of protection for programme participants. SC will focus to embrace the protective measures by community engagement approach, training and follow effective referral pathway. At the facility level, staff will be trained, and all services regarding safe, respectful service delivery will be strengthened by ensuring a dignified environment for all programme participants. SC will conduct awareness sessions with adolescent groups, pregnant and lactating mothers, socially influential persons and community gatekeepers on gender-based violence, abuse, and exploitation to ensure a safe and dignified environment for women, girls and vulnerable groups by making everyone aware of PSEA. Moreover, by mainstreaming SC’s child safeguarding activities, the project will ensure that the rights of all children are protected and nurtured and children are free from all forms of violence, abuse, neglect, maltreatment, and exploitation as set out in the Convention on the Rights of the Child. SC will contribute to improving project staff’s awareness of PSEA through conducting regular trainings, developing learning materials (posters, flyers, videos, Safeguarding Pocketbooks, Volunteer Flipchart etc.) and placing those materials in the services point, and inspiring the community by establishing Community Champions. SC will ensure a zero-tolerance approach in this project to any PSEA concern and confidential, accessible, and culturally appropriate Complaint and Feedback Mechanisms (CFM) like toll-free hotline numbers, and complain box are available in every facility. In this regard, SC will take necessary actions after receiving a SEA complaint that are in line with the SoP for the SEA Complaint Referral in the Rohingya Refugee Response in Bangladesh along with the survivor-centered approach.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

SC DRR team has a comprehensive work plan based on risk assessment done in the camps and is actively engaged in mitigating any damage to the facility structures due to natural calamities and to support in service continuity in case of any emergency by establishing temporary/mobile service points and fast recovery work to regain the regular services. SC is Maintaining Climate-resilient healthcare infrastructure and environment-friendly programme activities through implementing low-carbon emissions such as the use of solar systems, waste reduction & proper waste management and water supply systems in the health facilities resulting in strengthening structural resilience through durable and adaptive approaches. SC will organize workshops for DMCs and vulnerable communities to develop early action plans based on different thresholds, enhancing their preparedness and messaging capabilities. Through this project, SC planned to provide orientation on the Household Preparedness and Safety Plan based on threshold and triggering settings to empower the community in disseminating this information to a larger audience. It includes awareness raising on long-term climate impacts on nutrition, promoting climate-resilient homestead gardening practices, and promoting LPG fuel efficiency practices to ensure HHs reserve energy as much as possible. Finally, SC will follow an environment-friendly approach through green office initiatives such as tree plantation and digitalization or less use of paper which contribute to reducing carbon emissions.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

SC has robust Emergency Preparedness and Response (EPR) measures in place to ensure timely and effective response to emergencies, such as fires, outbreaks, flash floods, heavy rainfall, and national disasters. A dedicated team of two Mobile Medical Teams is readily available to deploy to affected areas within the camps, providing essential medical care and support. To enhance preparedness, SC maintains a comprehensive Business Continuity Plan, which includes the stockpiling of essential medical supplies and medicines. This proactive approach ensures that critical resources are available to meet the needs of affected populations. In this project SC will support the catchment areas programme participants by raising awareness of early warnings and advisories to the adolescent, pregnant and lactating women to take time precautions of any natural calamities and include the childcare during the emergencies. Furthermore, SC actively collaborates with the Emergency Preparedness and Response Working Group (EPR WG) to coordinate efforts and adhere to established guidelines. By working closely with this interagency body, SC can leverage collective expertise and resources to optimize emergency response operations.SC has developed a yearly country EPR plan, which serves as a roadmap for emergency preparedness and response activities. This plan is regularly reviewed and updated to reflect evolving needs and emerging challenges. By maintaining a flexible and adaptable approach, SC will effectively respond to unforeseen circumstances and ensure the well-being of affected populations. Through these robust EPR measures, SC is committed to minimizing the impact of emergencies and promoting the resilience of vulnerable communities. By investing in preparedness, fostering strong partnerships, and maintaining a flexible approach, SC is well-positioned to effectively respond to crises and protect the health and safety of those in need.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

SC engage the youth in the direct health and nutrition services as community health volunteers (aged 18-25 years) creating opportunities to work and community-based approach like training them to serve their own community effectively. SC also enrolled “Girl Decide Module” through the active involvement of girl children at the camp level by reflecting on needs and interests through the adolescent and youth-led project. By implementing this SC will empower young women to play a central part in health outreach and nutrition support. Moreover, the project continues the mobilization and sensitization by community adolescent support group, build capacity on hard skill in the facility level to empower the youth and adolescents to prepare them as a future change maker, contribute to family and community.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

SC actively promotes self-reliance and resilience among refugee populations through a comprehensive approach that integrates diverse group sessions and intersectoral collaboration. The organization facilitates a range of initiatives, including community influencer engagement, parent group sensitization, and adolescent and youth capacity building, to empower individuals and communities. By fostering self-awareness, skill development, and social support networks, SC aims to equip refugees with the tools and knowledge necessary to navigate challenges and build sustainable futures. Additionally, SC actively participates in intersectoral coordination meetings and knowledge-sharing initiatives, leveraging a Social and Behavioral Change Communication (SBCC) approach to promote positive behavioral change and strengthen community resilience. Through these efforts, SC seeks to create a supportive environment that enables refugees to thrive and contribute meaningfully to their host communities.

4.2 Do project activities in the host community link to development initiatives, and/or align with development priorities in the Cox’s Bazar district? If yes, briefly describe.If yes, briefly describe.

SC's direct service provision to the host community will help alleviate the services quality and vulnerability of the adjacent host community, ensuring proper access to essential pediatric care and improving health and nutrition care seeking behaviors. This aligns with the Cox's Bazar District Development and Growth Priorities (DDGP)'s objective of strengthening the healthcare system and improving health and nutrition outcomes.Furthermore, SC's development of SBCC materials will play a crucial role in empowering the host community. By providing accurate and culturally appropriate information on child health, nutrition, and hygiene, these materials will promote positive health behaviors and contribute to the prevention of child illnesses and malnutrition.
