Project Summary

RI Nutrition sector primary goal is to promote malnutrition prevention and provision of improved treatment of acute malnutrition to promote good health among conflict affected and vulnerable groups. In 2023, RI’s Nutrition interventions will aim to increase malnutrition prevention activities and provide continuous improved and timely community-based management of acute malnutrition projects in the Greater Upper Nile with Health, WASH, FSL, Protection sectors linkages to address interrelated and under-lying causes of malnutrition, such as food insecurity, suboptimal childcare and feeding practices and lack of safe water and sanitation
RI’s aim is to support South Sudanese including refugees, internal displaced persons, returnees and vulnerable population including people with disabilities. Our response will prioritize life-saving treatment services targeting children under five years and pregnant and lactating women (PLWs), in compliance with relevant national guidelines. These are the most vulnerable groups to malnutrition due to their increased biological and physiological needs. Geographically, our response will continue to prioritize states in the Upper Nile namely Maiwut, Maban, Nasir, and Longechuk which are characterized by high malnutrition, food insecurity (IPC Phase 3 and 4), conflict and internal displacement.
The RI nutrition sites will act as hubs for integrated programming. The RI health sector will provide malaria testing and treatment among the severely acute malnourished (SAM) children being treated at the nutrition sites, while the RI WASH sector will provide NFI kits to severely malnourished children’s families and the RI FSL sector will establish kitchen gardens at nutrition sites to demonstrate recommended feeding practices.
Furthermore, the RI nutrition sites will integrate the early childhood development stimulation activities and will be considered as potential entry points for GBV victims looking for assistance, with nutrition staff trained on GBV referral pathways.
To further localize the RI’s response and to ensure sustainability, RI will strengthen the County Health Department (CHD) responsibilities to oversee the nutrition interventions, to coordinate the response with various stakeholders at the county level and provide stakeholder’s capacity building on the new national guidelines on community based management of malnutrition and maternal, infant, and young child nutrition.
RI will provide technical support to the CHD to review the county level nutrition intervention’s strategy which includes coming with nutrition county action plan, routine monitoring and supportive supervision of routine nutrition site activities, facilitation of Community-based Management of Acute Malnutrition (CMAM), Maternal, Infant and Young Child Feeding Nutrition (MIYCN) training, VASD campaign, FSNMS surveys, advocacy and focusing Family malnutrition screening for timely cases managements and follow up.
RI will reinforce the nutrition information system and the use of nutrition data at the county and the community level by employing a gender-sensitive approach to mitigate any obstacles compromising women and girls’ ability to receive nutrition support. Women’s participation will be encouraged in programme design and feedback mechanisms. RI will also collaborate with existing and planned development interventions on nutrition, such as those funded by the World Bank in the greater upper Nile.
RI will pursue four objectives including:
• Prevent under-nutrition by increasing access to maternal, infant and young child nutrition interventions and VASD campaign.
• Deliver life-saving management of acute malnutrition for the most vulnerable and at-risk U5 children and pregnant and lactating women
• Enhance nutrition situation monitoring, analysis and utilization of nutrition information for early warning and decision making.
• Increase access to integrated nutrition, health, WASH, FSL and protection interventions

1 - Needs

According to the Humanitarian Needs Overview 2022, an estimated 2 million people in South Sudan will be at risk of acute malnutrition, including children, pregnant and lactating women (PLWs) and refugees who will need treatment for acute malnutrition. Acute malnutrition has been a protracted problem in South Sudan and one that requires sustained investment in life-saving nutrition services and the scale up of prevention actions to address the major causes of malnutrition. This response will prioritize life-saving treatment services targeting children under five years and pregnant and lactating women (PLWs). These are the most vulnerable groups to malnutrition due to their increased biological and physiological needs. The drivers of acute malnutrition include food insecurity, sub-optimal feeding and hygiene, practices high prevalence of disease, inadequate sanitation. Upper Nile State is projected to have a 15.2% GAM rate and 2.6% SAM rate in 2022 based on assessments conducted by 2021 FSNMS. The critical needs include access to treatment of acute malnutrition, safe drinking water, and treatment of maternal and child diseases. The high water level in some flooded areas is constraining vulnerable people’s physical access to basic services and restricting humanitarians’ ability to assess and respond to needs. Following the recurrent flooding, there is a pressure for NGOs to maintain their presence in Greater Upper Nile and to deliver nutrition interventions to the vulnerable populations residing here. Malnutrition in Upper Nile remains critical and the causes are multifaceted. The continued conflict has led to displacement of thousands, the deterioration of economy which has in turn affected the food security. Following the signing of the revitalized peace agreement (RPA), local authorities are already witnessing increased movement of returnees from surrounding countries, including Ethiopia. Such spontaneous returnees are putting pressure on already-depleted host community resources. The population of these counties are highly reliant on natural resource-based livelihoods, including agriculture and livestock rearing, which have been significantly disrupted by conflict, displacement, cattle raids and climate shocks. Three of the four target counties (Nasir, Longechuk and Maiwut) are in ‘very severe’ GAM and SAM level.Coverage and access to regular health and nutrition services, remains challenging due to local conflict, long distances, remoteness, and floods in the rainy season. Basing on the national nutrition cluster 2022 GAM release, the proposed counties for the intervention have the following GAM rates: Longechuk, Nasir and Maiwut having U5 proxy SAM 2.6%, proxy MAM 13.5% and proxy GAM 16.1%; Maban with proxy SAM 0.8%, proxy MAM 7.7% and proxy GAM 8.5%. PLW MUAC malnutrition prevalence according to the 2021 FSNMS+ analysis by county are as follows: Nasir 30.4%, Maban with 13.9%, Longechuk with 27.7% and Maiwut with 32.7%. Based on same season historical data of food security and nutrition monitoring system, SMART nutrition surveys and admission trends for 2021, it is estimated that 2,017,344 people (host and refugees) will need treatment for acute malnutrition in 2022. This includes 302,162 children (148,059 boys and 154,103 girls) suffering from severe acute malnutrition, 1,039,472 children (509,341 boys and 530,131girls) from MAM and 675,548 pregnant and lactating women from acute malnutrition. (HNO 2022)Insecurity in the counties contributes to increases cases of severe acute malnutrition. RI continually monitor the security situation in the four counties and ensure that the necessary mitigation measures are taken. Throughout 2021 the insecurity in Upper Nile States has remained unpredictable. Inter-communal fighting, including cattle raiding, has caused peaks in tensions, displacement, and deaths; and drive the same causes of malnutrition.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Project introductory meetings will beheld in all project areas and will include an explanation of the entitlements under this project as well as how the community members can access the Feedback and Complaints Mechanism (FCM) in order to give feedback and make complaints. Responses to the beneficiaries will be managed within the FCM loop to ensure that all feedback and complaints are dealt with internally within RI or referred/ redirected to partners who can assist to resolve. Moreover; the RI staff and local authority staff will be trained on how to orient, collect, record beneficiaries’ feedback and complaints received, as well as how and when to respond. All staff will be trained on core data protection and confidentiality principals as well to ensure that client data is kept confidential. From current RI programs individuals have expressed that they felt morecomfortable giving feedback verbally through discussions with RI staff or community leaders. Females community members preferred female community leaders or speaking direct to RI staff. Further, all participants stated that they preferred to have information shared verbally from RI staff, and then by community leaders. The verbal nature of feedback and information was emphasized throughout. This is unsurprising given the literacy levels of the wider population in all counties, and the limitation or lack of access to electricity and mobile phone connection which could be used for more advanced feedback mechanisms such as SMS, hotlines or email.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

RI will conduct community sensitization and mobilization prior to start of project, through consultations with community members and leaders, both formally and informally. Project introductory meetings will beheld in all project areas and will include an explanation of the entitlements under this project as well as how the community members can access the Feedback and Complaints Mechanism (FCM) in order to give feedback and make complaints. Responses to the beneficiaries will be managed within the FCM loop to ensure that all feedback and complaints are dealt with internally within RI or referred/ redirected to partners who can assist to resolve. Moreover; the RI staff and local authority staff will be trained on how to orient, collect, record beneficiaries’ feedback and complaints received, as well as how and when to respond. 7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

All RI staff and volunteers who are based in the four locations are trained on RI's PSEA and safeguarding policies, and ways to report incidents. Each RI field office has safeguarding champions who are given more intensive training on RI's safeguarding and PSEA approach. During project kick-off meetings with communities RI will inform communities of the PSEA policy, who the safeguarding champions are, what constitutes PSEA, emphasize that all project activities are delivered free of charge and that beneficiaries should not be asked to accept anything in exchange for any goods or services. RI will place posters and visuals outlining RI's policy and where community members can report at distribution sites. Finally, RI's independent MEAL team and project management staff from Juba will conduct ad-hoc monitoring visits to project sites, meeting with community members and will reiterate the training and RI's position on PSEA.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

RI will use the beneficiaries’ feedback to shape and adapt the project design and implementation. RI will undertake routine Exit Satisfaction Interviews to get beneficiaries feedback on satisfaction levels for the services they receive. Specific responses will also be discussed bi-monthly at the beginning of focus group discussions at the site level to inform beneficiaries of what changes are being made given previous feedback, or if any concerns are being addressed at the national level. Urgent issues will be addressed immediately by the project leads through appropriate channels with RI adapting its programme to address any concerns or gaps highlighted. Additionally, RI's MEAL team will conduct quarterly FGDs with community members to gain feedback on RI's nutrition project activities. During the monthly consultation meetings, RI will then provide feedback to the community leaders and beneficiaries on any changes made to the project based on feedback received. Where necessary action plans will be developed with RI's project team and actions points followed up to improve the implementation of the project. In some cases where the response is not within RI's control , RI would refer the issue to other stakeholders with specific expertise to deal with the particular issue.
