Project Summary

In 2019, Mercy Corps aims to strengthen our protection interventions to support of the overall protection cluster objectives towards the provision of quality and integrated protection services with a focus on community-based approaches. In 2018, Mercy Corps supported community based child protection programming in North Syria. In 2019, Mercy Corps will deepen the commitment to protection programming, to projects that support youth and livelihoods, social cohesion partners in NES and integrating Mercy Corps projects to support vulnerable persons Mercy Corps has a three pillar approach to our protection response:
a) Continuing child protection programming in Syria with partners, with a focus on youth/adolescents and livelihoods where possible across northern Syria.
b) Providing cash assistance to vulnerable individuals and households, in coordination with protection actors in NES.
c) Embedding protection mainstreaming across all our activities, including strengthening data collection and referral systems.
The protection response supported by Mercy Corps is in line with the protection needs overview (including child protection) and protection severity ranking, with partners operating in locations ranked severity 5/6 in the HNO severity scale and in coordination with the child protection AoR. Partners are encouraged to coordinate their operational planning and response closely with their respective AoR.

Needs

The conﬂict in Syria remains the primary driver of protection threats and corresponding issues. The conﬂict continues to be marked by violations of international humanitarian and human rights law, including grave violations against children during armed conﬂict. MC and partners have witnessed the deepening of negative coping mechanisms and the need to orient programming to individuals and households (HH) that have traditionally not beneﬁted as much from the response (e.g. youth and adolescents). NWS continues to be affected by active conﬂict, displacement and negative coping mechanisms, which are often heightened for particularly vulnerable groups (women, youth, PWD, elderly). In NES, pockets remain under ISIS control. IDPs returning to former ISIS controlled areas have increased protection risks to civilians due to increased exposure to ERW. The 2018 drought in this area has affected people’s livelihood capacity and community reliance, prolonging negative coping mechanisms. Children drop out of schools to work, ﬁnd themselves being married early, and struggle to access health assistance and legal remedy. The 2018 Multi-Sector Needs Assessment found that the top three coping mechanisms were reported as 1) dropping out of school; 2) early marriage; and 3) relying on humanitarian assistance. 13% of districts in Idleb and 25% of districts in Aleppo report that children are forced to beg, and almost 20% of districts in both governorates report that people engage in illegal (and often high risk) activities to cope with the context. When exploring this at a HH level, in a survey conducted by MC partner organizations; 26% of all identiﬁed HHs engage in emergency coping, such as marrying off underage daughters, engaging in high-risk or exploitative labor, and engaging in child labor; 22% of girls aged 15-24 are married and 3% are unmarried with a child; 60% of boys aged 15-24 are out of school and without a job; and 96% of women over 25 are not earning an income. This lack of income is preventing people from accessing positive coping mechanisms to protection threats and accessing services such as health facilities and education. MC’s research further shows that restrictions on women’s and girls’ physical movements have increased during the conflict; women and girls are more at risk of Sexual Gender-Based Violence (SGBV); and they have fewer opportunities to be socially active, engaged and listened to in their communities. Male adolescents and youth are at risk of forced recruitment into armed groups. In 2018, the protection cluster has highlighted the importance of youth-targeted programming and livelihoods interventions, recognizing the economic drivers of protective decisions, as a group who are both at risk from protection threats and can also become perpetrators of violence (e.g. as child soldiers and/or violence in the home). Issues such as female/child headed HHs, separated children and people living with disabilities also impact upon the economic capacity of a HH and their ability to access assistance, which can entrench isolation and protection poverty. The conflict is putting adolescents, youth and women in a position of profound and prolonged distress, to which existing psychosocial services (PSS) are unable to respond. To mitigate this stress and promote healthy development, adolescents, youth and women need to be able to have a safe space in their community where they can congregate and create positive social connections while taking part in activities that support their social-emotional intelligence and wellbeing. Youth and women, who are a part of MC programs, identiﬁed a strong need for psychosocial support, life skills, and peer support, and educational opportunities. The crisis has also impacted caregivers, social safety nets, and community support structures, exhausting the main sources of protection for children, exacerbating dependency and encouraging discrimination and/or exploitation.

Implementation of activities

MC will take a multi-sector approach to protection, and participation in referral pathway and case management systems, emphasizing provision of child protection activities to children and adolescents. While Mercy Corps partners follow different child protection intervention models depending on their capacity and response locations, protection partners will provide a range of services, including outreach and awareness raising, PSS, capacity building, and (where possible) case management support. Beneﬁciaries will be identiﬁed through a mixture of outreach, referral and centers of protection partners. MC will provide additional support to the partners for their capacity development, linking them to the cluster system and strengthening reporting systems. MC partners primarily engage in child and adolescent protection issues, e.g. child labor and engagement in hazardous and dangerous work, early marriage, and the effects of violence in the community for boys and girls. Where possible, MC will link survivors and vulnerable populations to other MC programs. In targeted communities, together with implementing partners, MC will form a minimum of 5 local Safe Spaces to support adolescents, youth, and women, with the result that adolescents and youth are better protected, less vulnerable and have a decreased use of negative coping mechanisms thanks to increased engagement within safe and cohesive social networks, access to local economic opportunities, and active participation in decision making. To ensure this response has a lasting impact of protection interventions for adolescent and youth, Mercy Corps will provide new, tailored and inclusive social and economic opportunities to the most affected boys and girls, adolescent and youth. This holistic approach will include access to informal education, life skills training, tailored psychosocial support as well as positive economic and social opportunities (i.e. job placements, vocational training, youth community groups for social participation). Youth, and women where relevant, will be targeted for participation in a life skills training course based on a contextualized version of the MC’s Transferable Skills (TS) Program. The design of this program is informed by feedback from girls, boys, and young women and men, ensuring that the curriculum addressing their specific concerns. Building on previous community awareness raising activities and recognizing that MC partners are already conducting mass awareness activities, MC’s awareness activities will be targeted and focus on direct participants in the Safe Space activities, such as parenting skills and integration of awareness on protection concerns in life skills sessions. In addition to protection mainstreaming training for MC staff and partners, MC supports the two-way referral of protection cases. MC supports referrals of vulnerable individuals from MC programs to protection actors, but also supports receiving referrals from protection actors into MC other programming, to support the economic and material capacity of vulnerable populations. Where possible, MC will link survivors and vulnerable populations of our child protection partners’ activities to other MC programs. Provision of cash assistance in NES: As part of a referral process, MC is piloting a targeted intervention for vulnerable families and households with particular threats, including those affected by ERWs. In close collaboration with protection partners (with an MoU), MC is accepting referrals of vulnerable persons onto MC cash programming, as part of the case management intervention of the protection partners. While this pillar is relatively small, MC aims for it to beneﬁt the individual/household economic capacity to assist in mitigating the protection threats/consequences. This cash-based response is coordinated through the protection working group and cash working group.

Protection Risk Analysis

MC has rolled out a protection risks analysis tool for staff and partners since 2018, as part of the protection mainstreaming strategies. Noting that the MC submission is reflective of protection programming incorporated into several donor grants (often multi-sector), each project and key interventions will have a protection risk analysis. MC staff and partners are trained on key principles of risk analysis and protection assessment, staff will be gender balanced and complaints followed up on. MC acknowledges that there are a significant number of protection risks that individuals and communities can face as a result of poor programming or programming in a difficult environment. This does not capture them all. 1. Risk of exploitation or abuse by staff members/service providers/partners: MC has existing codes of conduct and safeguarding practices for partners and staff. MC monitoring and evaluation systems include accountability frameworks, and MC has a beneficiary complaints mechanism. 2. Partners are engaged in PSS and case management exercise in areas where there are limited/no services for onward referral. MC will ensure that partners are engaged in the appropriate cluster service mapping exercises. Partners are also provided resources to conduct service mapping (and feed back to the cluster). Where it is not possible for partners to do service mapping, third party actors may be recruited to conduct service mapping. Where services do not exist, partners will be able to pay for transport and other means to support persons accessing services. Cash-based assistance for survivors is implemented based on clear selection criteria and through established MoU’s with protection partners. 3. Programming exacerbates community tensions due to perceived discrimination/favoritism: MC has selected our partner organizations based on their existing community relationships and networks. This is triangulated through MC monitoring and engagement with other key stakeholders. This issue is noted in particular in NES, where staff are working between different ethnic groups and authority structures to ensure that the program intervention and approach is conflict sensitive. 4. Local authorities restrict activities of partners due to lack of support for protection interventions. Similarly, where programming is sensitive to authorities but a priority for local communities, MC will endeavor to support partners in their intervention methodology and engagement with local actors to increase opportunities for operating. 5. Persons without documents refused assistance: MC does not require beneficiaries of protection or non-protection projects to possess national ID documents. In line with protection cluster guidelines, information can be triangulated using local verification processes. Protection interventions can increase stigma and risk to beneficiaries. MC and partners support project designs which mitigate risk to beneficiaries. MC supports projects targeting youth and adolescents and PWD who are historically excluded from target interventions6. GBV: MC mainstream protection principles throughout all activities, this includes ensuring all distributions, whether cash or in-kind, and activities are conducted in secure locations that do not put beneficiaries at risk of being targeted for theft or Sexual and Gender Based Violence (SGBV). MC has also linked with a protection actor to provide multipurpose cash assistance to GBV survivors to support their financial independence.7. PWDs: MC incorporates protection of PwDs into our protection mainstreaming approach. Due to the limited mobility of many Syrians, MC will provide door-to-door distributions where feasible. MC explores wherever viable opportunities to design resilience-focused programmatic activities which specifically target and include the elderly, PwDs, chronic disease or injury. Accessibility to Safe Spaces is enhanced through transportation support, wheelchair ramps, etc.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

cluster analysis, Mercy Corps operational planning strives to minimize risk to beneficiaries, partners and staff. 2019 is a challenging operational context in northern Syria. The humanitarian situation in NWS deteriorated in 2018. The establishment of the demilitarized zone (DMZ) in Sep 2018 saw the numbers and frequency of displacement subside, however, inter-AOG conflict in January 2019 has the potential to further destabilize the area. According to the HAT’s Dec 2018 Report, “The DMZ Agreement”, there is likely to be a partial or complete breakdown in the political process if key provisions are not fully implemented, leading to a surge in IDP movement. Administered largely by the Kurdish Self Administration (KSA), NES contains pockets under Islamic State in Iraq and ash-Sham (ISIS) control. The large coalition presence is signaling a reduction, with the potential to destabilize a delicate equilibrium between conflict actors which has been an enabler of humanitarian access since 2017. Ongoing clashes in DEZ continue to cause periodic access problems. Tens of thousands of IDPs remain in camps and informal sites, unable to return to their homes. This has meant that there are questions of operational viability across northern Syria in 2019. Mercy Corps remains in close coordination with our donors and partners for appropriate contingency planning to enable and adaptable programming. Our partners will remain in close dialogue with the protection cluster and child protection cluster on operational priorities during context changes, and with mine action/GBV to use Mercy Corps distribution programs as opportunities for distribution of key messages and information. MC’s minimum standards require each program to be based on a gender analysis, to have adequate diversity among its staff and to adapt programing to be gender and age responsive and accessible for PWD. Regular mapping of existing forms of protection violations and feedback from participants inform program design, ensuring the specific needs across different age and gender groups are addressed. Mercy Corps and partners take a do no harm approach and ensure all staff and partners are trained on conflict sensitivity and protection mainstreaming. Through a robust community accountability and response mechanism, Mercy Corps and partners collect feedback and complaints, which are subsequently assessed and addressed in a timely manner.
