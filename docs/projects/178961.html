Project Summary

Save the Children puts a great emphasis in rolling out integrated approaches in order to address the multifaceted needs of the most vulnerable populations inside Syria, therefore, this project will explore synergies with our ongoing child protection, WASH, nutrition, FSL and health programs.
In 2020, 2.25 million children were out of school in Syria, with the numbers expected to have increased in 2021 (Education Cluster). According to SC’s “Reversing Gains” report (December 2020), learning opportunities have been traded to meet the pressures of new economic emergencies for households, and are further limited by a lack of access to technology which can support remote leaning. The report confirms loss of educational access of approximately 50% of the current learning population in the NES. Without access and the availability of services, the numbers of children resorting to child labour and other unsafe coping mechanisms rises. A surge in COVID-19 cases in the 2nd half of the year has further compounded the situation.
SC’s integrated education programming is centered on the provision of basic Non-Formal Education (NFE) via learning spaces with a concerted focus on developing basic literacy and numeracy skills, while also providing integrated CP, MHPSS and other basic services. Building on existing programming, SC will continue to provide NFE in TLSs for children aged 3-18, specifically OOSC and children with delays in
their educational attainment. New students will be assessed using grade placement tests and the SC’s contextualized tool based on the grade levelling assessment for each child. Due to COVID-19, adaptations continue to be made to complete these assessments as accurately and safely as possible.
SC will continue providing Basic Literacy and Numeracy classes in the TLSs for children displaying low competency levels during their initial assessments. All new education personnel will undergo comprehensive capacity building while existing personnel will receive refresher trainings with expanded technical content by SC technical units. For newly contracted teachers, these trainings will cover Teachers in Crisis Contexts. SC will also introduce the new Social Emotional Learning Foundations programme which will be delivered for children in grades 1-5 with an aim of supporting their social and emotional skills in addition to learning outcomes. The project expands education support through the establishment of new TLSs and expansion of mobile education. Community capacity will be strengthened through Parent Teacher Associations. Monthly Teacher Learning Circles will support personal wellbeing and teachers will receive incentives based on cluster guidelines. School safety and security protocols will be developed.
The project will enable safe access to quality education, with a specific focus on OOSC, and children at risk of dropping out, through the provision of non-formal education with the consideration of alternative learning modalities in light of the pandemic. The proposed project directly contributes to all three Education 2021 HRP objectives.

4 - Needs

In June 2020, Save the Children (SC) conducted a global research to assess the impact of COVID-19: of all children interviewed in Syria, 97% in South Central Syria reported closed schools versus 84% in North East and 57% in North West (SCI, Global COVID-19 Research, June 2020). There are multiple negative consequences on children’s rights to survival, protection and education. Children have been exposed to prolonged distress and as a result they report nightmares, lasting sadness and anxiety. A Multi-Sectoral Needs Assessment in 2020 carried out by SC and partners in NWS shows that girls and boys experience financial barriers in accessing education and have to walk long distances due to a lack of transportation to education facilities. Online education is typically too costly for poor households. The COVID-19 pandemic’s curfews and lockdown have prevented continuous access to learning and recreation for children. As a result of the worsening economic situation in NWS, coupled with the pandemic, children are often engaged in income generating activities through child labor and therefore they are likely to drop out of school (iMMAP COVID-19 Situation Analysis, Oct-Nov 2020).In NES, nearly 40% of both residents and IDPs reported instances of early marriage and over 60% children aged 15 or below are sent to work in order to meet basic needs. Among IDPs, less than 50% of adolescents are reportedly in school, with adolescent boys at just over 41% compared with adolescent girls at 48% (HNAP IDP Report Series 2020: Socio-Economic Overview). SC’s recent Barriers Analysis reveals both a lack of key services and where services are available, there are often accessibility barriers related to distance and cost of transportation.

5 - Implementation of activities

The proposed Cash Voucher Assistance (CVA) in education programming is integral and complementary to the core education and protection activities currently delivered by SC to tackle economic, protection, socio-cultural and education service barriers that affect vulnerable children. Where children still face multiple economic barriers to access schooling, it leads to increase in child labor and child marriage cases while displaced families move to other camps to seek livelihood opportunities. The provision of CVA to vulnerable children will contribute to increase the enrollment rate of OOSC and enable at-risk children to regularly attend school and avoid dropping out.A monitoring plan is developed during the inception phase of the project in coordination with the project team to ensure that the plan is both feasible and aligned with the implementation plan. Data will be collected on a monthly basis by the field teams on activity reporting and monitoring (e.g. training reports, activity reports, attendance lists). FDGs will be held regularly with children and caregivers taking part in child protection activities to ensure that children’s voices feed into programming. Participants will be a mix of women and men, girls and boys. In addition, qualitative data will be routinely collected through monitoring visits. Project quality will be monitored through quality benchmarks. A range of mechanisms will be applied to ensure accountability to the affected population. Information about the project will be made available to target communities through appropriate media. SC will also create opportunities for communities, including children, to participate in programming decisions and provide feedback throughout the project cycle.

7.1 - Please list any risks related to sexual exploitation and abuse that have been identified during the project design phase and any risk mitigation and prevention measures that have been adopted and built into the project

SC is invested in the Centrality of Protection and is developing an action plan that focuses on capacity building of staff and partners, embedding of conflict sensitivity and protection analysis, strengthening safe programming and child safeguarding approaches, improving risk management and integrating protection into advocacy work. In addition, SC integrates protection mainstreaming across its programs, and these principles guide the implementation team to ensure safe and quality programming. Building on the foundation of all staff members being trained on Child Safeguarding, protection against sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA) and Safe Programming, SC is also expanding capacity building of staff in conflict sensitivity, protection analysis and participatory approaches. A child safeguarding risk assessment, gender and GBV audit, and conflict sensitivity analysis are conducted for each program. These identify gaps, inform the project design, and mitigate protection risks for children and their families while accessing SC facilities and using SC services. In addition, SC has prioritized more sustained capacity-building for staff related to disability and disability inclusion, as well as on gender mainstreaming and GBV.

8 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Unstable security situation: conflict escalation, poor acceptance from local stakeholders and/or change in lines of control can prevent access to different program areas, and the population’s access to service. Inappropriate project design can exacerbate tensions between communities and lead to unintended negative or harmful outcomes during implementation including suspension of activities. In order to manage and mitigate this risk, SC regularly monitors the local context, and SC employs security focal points. SC staff complete mandatory security trainings. Context-specific Safety and Security Management Plans are regularly reviewed/updated.Failure of money transfer mechanisms or change in banking regulation restricts movement of funds; and currency fluctuations: SC may be unable to deliver program activities due to lack of funds or program staff may be put at risk due to a lack of personal funds to hibernate or due to deterioration in local acceptance as a result of unpaid balances to other staff, suppliers etc. SC mitigates this risk by using multiple money transfer channels and agents who have strong reputations and have been able to deliver funds in almost all circumstances inside Syria. All SC agreements are denominated in donor currency.Risks due to the COVID-19 pandemic: The pandemic leads to risks to beneficiaries due to lack of access to programming sites and services; further exposure to protection concerns, including violence; and health risks to beneficiaries and SC staff members. Relevant programme adaptations have been developed and are currently in place. SC has also introduced Standard Operation Procedures for staff working remotely and to identify COVID-19 focal points in each programme area.
