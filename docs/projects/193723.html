Project Summary

HNO 2022 Two years after Sudan embarked on the path of political transition and one year into the Juba Peace Agreement (JPA), humanitarian needs continue to grow across the country, despite progress and gains on the road to a new Sudan. These needs are driven by an economic crisis, exacerbated by COVID-19, protracted internal displacement that is yet to find durable solutions, increased insecurity and localized violence in parts of the country, coupled with floods, disease outbreaks, and more than 1.16 million refugees and asylum seekers hosted by Sudan. 1 - Needs

With significant increases in food and other commodity prices, a reduced harvest, and continued conflict, acute food insecurity in Sudan continues to worsen rapidly. Latest acute food insecurity data indicates that around 9.6 million people across Sudan were highly food insecure and classified in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse from April to May 2022. This includes 2.3 million people in IPC Phase 4 (Emergency) and 7.3 million in IPC Phase 3 (Crisis). The prevalence of the population in IPC Phase 3 or above has moved from 13% (October 2021 – February 2022) to 20% (April-May 2022) to 24% (June-September 2022), driven by a plummeting economy, poor harvests and conflict. The situation is projected to worsen between June and September, with up to 11.7 million people (an increase of 2 million) likely to be in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse. This includes 3.1 million people in IPC Phase 4 (Emergency) and 8.5 million in IPC Phase 3 (Crisis) or above. Northern, Western and Central Darfur, Khartoum, Kasala, White Nile host the highest caseload of IPC Phase 3 and Phase 4 during the projection people from June to September 2022. Urgent and timely interventions are needed to prevent these populations from slipping into worse phases In terms of trend analysis, the prevalence of the population in IPC Phase 3 or above has moved from 13% (October 2021-February 2022) to 20% (April-May 2022) to 24% (June-September 2022). This represents an increase of around 2 million food insecure people when comparing the same seasons in 2021.With an additional 2 million people classified in IPC Phase 3 (Crisis), the results reflect a significant increase in the expected magnitude compared to the same period last year (June to September 2021), when 9.8 million people were classified in IPC Phase 3 or above. This is due to the SMoH and County Health Department (CHDs) having limited capacity to provide basic health services and change poor health, nutrition and hygiene practices. White Nile state has some health facilities, but many are non-functional due to lack of qualified staff, medical equipment, and drug, with often only 1 health worker for each PHCU. There remains a significant need to ensure beneficiaries have access to and attend focused prenatal and antenatal care, and this leaves huge gaps in health service delivery in the White Nile region not included under Global funding/UNDP. According to Interagency SMART Survey, (2019), 23% of children were not vaccinated for measles, 82.3% did not receive PENTA vaccines in host and refugees, 24.6% were not vaccinated for measles, and 54.9% did not receive PENTA vaccines in refugee. There are high childhood morbidity rates of 41% in host communities and 41.2% in refugees (interagency SMART surveys 2019). Skilled health workers respectively attended only 35.3% and 28.4% of mothers in host and refugees during childbirth. White Nile was classified under IPC 3 with 10% of population (14,000) at IPC 4. 6.2 - Explain how your project aims to ensure protection mainstreaming during its design and implementation

HFO understands different vulnerabilities within target communities that may exclude target beneficiaries in their access to feedback and complaint mechanism. Therefore, HFO will consult with the target community, the local chief and state authorities to understand the overall target population profile, access to communication channel and nature of vulnerabilities. While establishing the Feedback and Complaint mechanism, HFO will consider such vulnerabilities and access constrained therefore the target population will be consulted on their preferred mode of complaint/feedback channel. Different mode of feedback such as complaint boxes, phone numbers, email, verbal feedback/complaint during HFO monitoring visits, in-person to HFO office and Help desk at health sites will be used to avoid exclusion of access to feedback mechanism. HFO will use both pictorial, written and verbal channels in local languages to communicate the feedback mechanism to target beneficiaries. The individuals living with disabilities, illiterate, women and girls can also share their complaint/feedback through their local representative and relatives. In addition, during monitoring and support visits FGDs will be conducted separately with women and men to seek feedback and provide opportunity to discuss any concern/issues or complaint.

7.2 - Please outline how the project or your agency will inform and interact with inter-agency approaches to accountability to affected people and feedback mechanisms, including SEA and abuse complaints?

HFO has a Code of Conduct and its associated policies including Program Participant Protection Policy (P4), Child Safeguarding, and Anti-Trafficking in-person Policy. HFO staff and any one engaged by HFO including consultants, contractors, partners and visitor are required to understand and comply with HFO safeguarding policies. HFO’s Code of Conduct and Associated Policy is consistent with the six core principles of IASC Task Force on PSEA in Humanitarian Crises. During project inception and regular consultations with the beneficiaries and relevant stake holders, Concern informs them about right to assistance, scope of services, right to appropriate behavior including HFO’s commitment to zero tolerance on Sexual exploitation and abuse by humanitarian workers. The beneficiaries will be informed about the Complaint Response Mechanism (CRM) to raise their voice in case of any issue pertaining to PSEA. The monitoring mechanism will ensure that the beneficiaries are aware about PSEA and how to complaint in case of any exploitation and abuse. HFO will ensure that health clinic staff is gender balance. HFO regularly coordinate with protection partners and safeguarding/protection issues are referred the Protection partner. In all locations, HFO has a Complaints Response Mechanism (CRM) where beneficiaries can register complaints about gender related or protection concerns. All recruitments of staff and trainings ensure that there is equal access to women and men to ensure balanced representation. Monitoring will collect data on any information on safety and protection HFO and to improve future programme designs.
