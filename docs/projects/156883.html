Project Summary

The project has precious value as it will continue to provide sustainable MHPSS services which is the cluster strategic priorities and in an area that no other NGO there to provide community based MH services activities. The project was designed to contain wide range of services on level 2,3 and 4 of the IASC pyramid of intervention, strengthening the WHO strategies of expanding MH services in the North Of Syria, establishing a referral pathway with other health and protection services, enable the strengthening of community resilience to cope on their own through community- based mental health. HRO teams in all locations that can be reached to beneficiaries which will be remarkable in the area that was assisted for the first time by our organization over the past year through this year with a community based multi approach protection services. , the team consist of mh gap doctor, 2 PSW and 2 CHW in the countryside of Ariha. The project will also be located in another location, Khirbat al-Jawz. HRO is currently running mh gap based mobile clinic in Kherbet Al Joz and surrounding camps over the past 18 month, along with fixed clinic covering wide range of services in the same area (including the services of clinical psychologist) with 2WGSS in nearby camps. Another community based multi sectoral approach including mh gap based MH services in Darkoush Town-Idlib governorate.
For the other project, HRO will continue to strengthen and expand their existing MHPSS services at 3 health facilities, in Maaret Alna’aman, Ariha and , Khirbat al-Jawz. HRO’s proposed project will improve the access to and the quality of MHPSS services through the provision of a comprehensive package that links community based services to health facility based activities. It is designed as a multi-layered approach that incorporates support to static facilities, the establishment of robust referral systems and community outreach activities. Mental health services will be available to host populations and IDPs who are experiencing extreme adversity and distress from living in a war zone for the past five years. HRO’s psychosocial workers, and trained doctors from primary health care centers, will address issues such as anxiety, acute stress, grief, despair, anger issues, attention deficiencies and problems with interpersonal relations, among other symptoms that result from humanitarian emergencies. Meanwhile the community health workers will conduct awareness raising sessions within the communities to inform them about the newly available services, and to increase knowledge and understanding about mental health and well-being, which HRO believes will in turn reduce stigma.

Needs

Seven years into the crisis, additional support is urgently needed to scale up services to address increasing mental health needs, with an estimated 27,635 people in need of mental health services through health centers, 61,788 people needing outpatient psychiatric care and almost 12,000 requiring inpatient psychiatric care .WHO estimates that one in thirty people in people living in Syria suffers from severe mental health conditions (such as severe depression, psychosis, or disabling anxiety) and at least one in five suffers from mild to moderate mental health conditions (such as depression or anxiety disorders) There is a critical need for mental services inside Syria focusing to protect people from and provide treatment to people with mental health concerns. The 2017 Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO) and Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP) identify mental health care as a priority need, and reports that an estimated 600,000 people suffer from stress related conditions and severe mental illness.The Brookings Institute reports that 54% of all Syrian refugees meet the criteria for one or more of the following: post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, anxiety disorders or prolonged grief disorders. One UNHCR study conducted with children ages 5–17 in a Turkish refugee camp found the case rate for trauma, depression, and PTSD was 10 times that of the general Turkish population. Many of the children interviewed for the study reported witnessing or experiencing physical violence; 79% of the children reported having a family member killed in the war.There is an acute shortage in mental health professionals and mental health facilities in Syria where, about 60 psychiatrists serve the entire country. In the north, there are presently only four psychiatrists serving more than two million IDPs.HRO teams were a part of MHPSS needs assessment conducted by MHPSS WG under Health Cluster led by WHO in the north western parts of the country that are out of control of Syrian regime in June 2018. The report will be published soon. The survey was conducted in Madiq Castle along with other areas in Idleb and its countryside to provide a situational analysis at the community and health facility levels. It confirmed the high need to establish MHPSS services in these areas. This primary indicators of the assessment also revealed that the Syrian population in those areas would seek help with their mental health conditions from either unqualified person claiming to be mental health professionals or primary care physicians without specialized training. Reasons for seeking unprofessional help likely include desperation, misinformation, lack of information about services, lack of services, and easier access to unqualified persons. However, HRO highlights the dangers associated as including misdiagnosis and mistreatment. Further, HRO highlights that the lack of a strong cadre of mental health service providers, which enables unqualified persons to promote their services, reinforces the stigma associated with being a professional mental health care provider, as it misconstrues them as pseudoscience practitioners.Technical working group of north Syria conducted a needs assessment in 2017 stressed on some of the above mentioned topics in addition to other related issues e.e ( Referral in between organizations, child mental health related issues especially children with physical or/and mental disabilities and behavioral problems, Monitoring and Evaluation in mental health, capacity building in brief psychological interventions like PM+ and Group IPT and Inadequate outreach activities for awareness raising and psychoeducation to fight stigma)

Protection Risk Analysis

1- Risk: Unavailability of transportation means for people with disabilities to attend the session, especially the awareness raising sessions and PSS sessions.Mitigation: Providing means to facilitate their attendance such as wheelchairs.2- Risk: Inability of the team to deal with people with disabilities and vulnerable groups such as elderly.Mitigation: Training the team on the method of receiving all ages and specifying programs to these categories. As for the people with disabilities, they enjoy the priority to attend all of these sessions.3- Risk: The team is not aware of risks related to PSEA.Mitigation: Training the team on PSEA.4- Risk: The team and beneficiaries are not aware of the importance of accountability and having high quality services.Mitigation: Establishing complaint mechanism, familiarizing beneficiaries with the method of reporting complaints, familiarizing the team with the importance of this mechanism in order to develop the sense of accountability. 5- Risk: When conducting referral for a MHPSS case, the case may be exposed to a higher risk from the new service provider which it has been referred to him/her such as inability of the service provider to deal with the case, or even abusing the deal with him/her.Mitigation: Higher level of coordination with the service provider prior to referring MH cases, along with raising the awareness of the provider on how to deal with cases. Also, providing transportation means and accompanying the referred case to the location of the service provider.6- Risk: Difficult access to the centers to which MHPSS cases are referredMitigation: Provide transportation for MH cases and their families to reach the centers referred to it, or MHPSS case managers will go with them to the referral centers. 7- Risk : mental health stigma Mitigation: Conducting awareness sessions for the casees Family and community on mental health issues and psychosocial support

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Assumption 1: We assume that the security situation in the defined area is staying the same.Risk 1 : Changing the security situation that interfere with implementation of the project.Mitigation Strategies: We will establish a response plan that can be activated according to the situation.Designing the activities in a flexible way where we can change the way of conducting the activity according to the situation so as to achieve the wanted results. and we can focus on more suitable activities like doing PFA . Stopping the activities for short period. moving the center to another place if deemed necessary after negotiation with OCHA. Assumption 2: Local council and authorities are continue to allow for our work, as the case right now with other projects.Risk: The local authorities decide to prevent us from implementing the project or part of it.Mitigation: as preventive measures we will coordinate with the local authorities and negotiate with them about the best way to implement the project and discuss the importance the project that will help. Assumption 3: The attitudes and needs of the population stay the same as we found in the needs assessments .Risk 3: changing in the attitudes or priorities of the population that will affect their willingness to benefit from the project activities.Mitigation strategies: Will work on awareness raising through out the project period and will get the feed back from the beneficiaries about the services and adapt accordingly . Assumption 4: There are experienced people in the targeted communitiesRisk 4 : Due to lack of experienced people in the targeted communities, IDP may be recruited based on their previous experience.Mitigation Strategies: equality in opportunities among host community and IDPs will be ensured throughout selecting people who are residing in the targeted area regardless of being IDP or from host community. Job vacancies advertisements will be shared with the local councils. Assumption 5: The materials will be purchased from local vendors in the target areaRisk 5 : Risk of triggering sensitivity with the local vendors during the procurement and purchasing materials from outside the targeted areas.Mitigation Strategies: All materials that are available in the targeted area should be purchased locally in accordance with HRO procurement policies. Assumption 6: the political and security conditions doesn’t change in the targeted areas Risk 6 : Change of the political and security conditions in the targeted areasMitigation Strategies: If the GoS does assume control of an area that then prevents HRO from being operational, HRO would most likely make the necessary arrangements to move staff, equipment and structures to opposition-held areas where mental health needs are not covered.
