Project Summary

The proposed project (approved 2019 HRP HPSE19-EDU-150611-1) aims to provide emergency response to the most vulnerable schools in Area C through integrated and targeted protection.
The response will be implemented by upgrading the educational environment in schools through the rehabilitation of classrooms and WASH facilities. It will also supply and construct new classrooms in overcrowded schools to facilitate separate spaces for students from different grades. Activities will also target the schools’ staff and parents to improve their capacities in delivering knowledge as well as dealing with risk reduction; risk mitigation (settlers attacks, clashes and natural hazards); and to give the students the needed support in the face of these risks and hazards (evacuation procedures, ﬁrst aid, etc).
Simultaneously, the project will respond to psychosocial issues faced by students and teachers alike as a result of the coercive environment they are subjected to. The proposed psychosocial response will be innovative; various artistic and recreational tools targeting students, teacher and parents, will be adopted to ensure the maximum effect on the targeted students.
The proposed interventions are in line with Education Cluster 2020 Strategic Objective 1 "Increase equitable and safe access to inclusive, quality educational services for vulnerable children” and objective 2 "Most vulnerable schools are supported in responding to emergencies through preventive measures, catch-up classes and school-based psychosocial services".
The project uses a community-based approach, with students (girls and boys) and teachers participating in all stages of the project including at the assessment, design and implementation stages. Hence, the students, teachers as well as parents’ fears, needs and ideas will be discussed and analysed to inform effective technical and psychosocial activities considering gender and age disaggregated data, resulting in adapted assistance.
As outlined, the proposed intervention ensures protection mainstreaming as it is guided by protection principles and considers safety and dignity; meaningful access; accountability to affected population (AAP) as well as participation and empowerment.

1 - Needs

The ongoing protracted conﬂict in the occupied Palestinian territories has had a signiﬁcant impact on all aspects of people’s lives, varying according to the relevant sector and its relation to the complex political context. The Education sector in Palestine has been affected in a variety of levels. According to the 2020 HNO, during the first half of 2019 in Area C, H2 and East Jerusalem, more than 112 education-related violations and incidents were documented. 98,000 (43,000 females and 44,000 males) children and teachers, of whom 1,900 are living with disabilities are affected. 50 schools in the West Bank’s Area C have received demolishing orders, hence they are under threat of imminent demolition. Many students are forced to travel long distances, exposed to delays at military checkpoints, harassment and violence, military activity in or around the school, or have to cross military areas and Firing Zones in order to access schools. Students have to travel between 2 to 8 kms through unpaved and diﬃcult roads by walking or using unregistered cars to reach the nearest school, consequently their daily trips are associated with various risks (long distance, weather conditions, wild animals, settler and IF violations). Moreover, school children face additional challenges that negatively affect educational attainment and a sense of safety and security, due to the poor educational facilities, unsafe and unfriendly overall educational environment, and the lack and inadequacy of school facilities. All these factors affect the educational level of enrolled students leading to high dropout rates, particularly among girls. The Palestinian Authority has been unable to signiﬁcantly intervene in these communities as they are in Area C i.e. under Israeli Occupation. Additionally, all the existing school structures have been implemented under emergency conditions following minimum standards aiming to ensure an access to education and keeping space for further upgrading works in future. Consequently, all the schools have become uncomfortable spaces for students who are already struggling to reach them due to access restraints, particularly during winter. In most of the schools, especially the older ones, the uninsulated classrooms, the inadequacy of WASH facilities, overcrowding, mixing students from different ages in the same class, and the absence of safety measures such as school fences, clean and paved surrounding, have driven many families to adapt negative coping mechanisms such as withdrawing their children from school or displacing part of the family to city so the children can go to better schools there, and make the students unwilling to go to schools. In August and September 2019, AAH updated a multi sectorial needs assessment and protection analysis involving 76 communities in Area C using a community-based approach. The assessment indicated that communities in the E1 zone, Firing Zone 918, Jordan Valley and Road 317 clusters are the most affected by Israeli restriction policies, affecting the educational sector in the targeted communities. Girls’ school dropout, access to schools and schools’ unsafe environment were the main concerns for the children in these communities. In Hebron H2 area the withdrawal of TIPH has left a gap in human rights monitoring, more than 35 schools exist in H2 area serving more than 12,700 students, 88% of them are obliged to pass through a military checkpoint to reach their schools, 60% of them indicated weekly harassment on the military checkpoints. The intervention aims to coordinate with the different organisations (including Medecins du Monde, UNICEF and NRC) acting in the area to try to support these students within school spaces and on their way to school.

2 - Activities

In September 2019, AAH, VIS and GTC conducted needs and technical assessments in 13 schools adopting a participatory methodology targeting the students, the school teachers and households. This was developed with a comprehensive understanding of the problems schools face due to the unsuitability and inadequacy of school facilities, unsafe educational environment and the associated psychosocial problems faced by students and their families. Therefore, based on its experience in implementing over 40 educational interventions in Area C, AAH has proposed the needed technical solutions for each school in order to create a more healthy, safe, and friendly educational spaces for students and considering ICA construction restrictions in Area C. It will do so by rehabilitating the existing infrastructures to ensure the basic and humanitarian needs that will thereby support students in continuing their education. To complement, VIS, who has extensive experience in conducting psychosocial activities at both community and school level, and its local partner GTC, who has more than 23 years’ experience working on child and families psychosocial and mental health related issues, has conducted different focus group sessions with students, teachers and parents from different Area C communities. Throughout these sessions VIS and GTC’s social experts identiﬁed the psychosocial problems that targeted groups suffer due to school access and environment; out of which they were able to propose technical solutions and share them with relevant stakeholders (MOEHE) and school headmasters, etc. AAH and VIS have experience in implementing educational activities in coordination with (MOEHE). For this project the coordination with MOEHE will be on two levels, ministry level: to ensure that all the project activities will be implemented according to the ministry strategies and priorities, and on directorates level: to ensure that the education directorates in each area have been consulted regarding the assessment, design and even during the implementation of the intervention. Furthermore, the activities will be handed over to the Ministry of Education as the service provider of the educational sector. The following activities will ensure meaningful access to education and psychosocial support, prioritizing safety and dignity: A1 Provision of emergency education supplies: targeting schools that suffer overcrowding or merging students from different grades into one classroom; schools that face a deﬁcit in power supplies, affecting the safety, health and educational attainment for students. Under this activity simple classrooms will be added, and power supply solutions will be developed (including solar systems for isolated communities). A2 Basic emergency rehabilitation to schools: to enhance the students’ learning environment, classrooms and other facilities will be rehabilitated/upgraded, creating a friendlier, more enjoyable and safer space for children. The upgrading of sub- standard structures in the most vulnerable communities will ensure a more protective educational space and the improvement of WASH facilities in schools (water fountains, latrines, water cisterns) will decrease the number of students per fountain and latrine to create more healthy and comfortable facilities. A3 Provision of school-based psychosocial support to vulnerable school children and staff: the activity will target the most vulnerable schools that suffer from a variety of threats including settler violence, natural hazards, and those with a generally unsafe and unhealthy environment. Detailed psychosocial assessments will be conducted in each school and consequently GTC and VIS’ psychosocial support teams will identify the appropriate psychosocial intervention in each school. They will use various artistic and recreational events and sessions with students, teachers and parents, including activities focusing on stress management, trauma and class communication.

3 - Indicators

Technical and psychosocial baseline surveys will be conducted by VIS, GTC and Action Against Hunger to identify the target groups’ needs; the survey will facilitate the supervision, monitoring and evaluation of the activities. Teachers and students (girls, boys and persons with disabilities) will be involved in the design, monitoring and evaluation of the activities to ensure the full participation of all groups. A post line and KAP surveys will be conducted to assess and evaluate the satisfaction and impacts of the activities on all groups, including an evaluation of improved access to schools and the students’ psychosocial status. Project activities will be monitored by 1) Working ﬁeld staff; 2) Community representatives; 3) MOE visits and participation in the activities; 4) Baseline and post line reports; 5) Donors and cluster monitoring visits; 6) Project progress and impact reports. The communities’ representatives and village councils will support AAH, VIS and GTC to provide ongoing ﬁeld monitoring of the rehabilitated schools and advise AAH on ongoing security status to determine optimal timing of ﬁeld operations. In order to ensure accountability, AAH has a Beneﬁciary Feedback Mechanism (FBM), as well as a Prevention of Sexual Abuse and Exploitation (PSEA) mechanism, which allows beneﬁciaries to provide their comments and complaints on the activities and is designed to increase their protection, in compliance with sensitive data management and adhering to the “Do No Harm” principle. Beneﬁciary knowledge of this system has been promoted through several ﬁeld assessments and monitoring visits. In addition, a service phone number will be distributed to the population in the proposed target communities enabling them to report feedback or complaints relating to project implementation by AAH and its partners. AAH will process and treat all information collected through an ad-hoc committee composed of project staff to understand the situation, identify appropriate solutions and include technical adjustment or modiﬁcations if needed. Output 1: 8 schools (722 students and teachers) beneﬁted of the emergency education supplies (caravans, solar systems, educational tools). Output 2: 5 schools (914 students and teachers) received emergency rehabilitation for the school building and facilities. Output 3: Psychosocial support through using various artistic and recreational tools (26 events) provided to 1636 students, parents and teachers received. The expected objective veriﬁable indicators and targets from the project are as follows: Outcome1: 50% of the targeted students reported that the schools became safer. Outcome 2: the total educational attainment improved by 20% in the targeted schools. Outcome 3: the average absenteeism rate decreased by 30%.To further ensure protection is mainstreamed throughout the project implementation, the indicator “% of communities/project beneficiaries reporting that humanitarian assistance is delivered in a safe and dignified manner” is added and will be measured through an end line survey of the beneficiaries.

9 - Protection mainstreaming
9.1 - Do No Harm

Through the whole project cycle AAH ensures protection mainstreaming is guided by the protection principles of Do No Harm. AAH considers safety and dignity, meaningful access, accountability as well as participation and empowerment of paramount importance. As a specific protection mainstreaming component, AAH is in the process of developing its own protection mainstreaming toolkit and to start working with its own sector specific gender and protection mainstreaming checklists. Up to date, AAH has made use of cluster specific Protection Mainstreaming Tip Sheets. Overall AAH’s aim is to take decisive humanitarian action with attention to risk mitigation thereby avoiding doing harm to the greatest extent possible. AAH in oPt prioritises this also through context specific (risk) analyses as well as the protection coordinator position stirring questions and discussion. Overall AAH seeks to complement its own actions with for instance legal support to inform risk mitigation measures and as a consequence to increase complementary humanitarian action. In terms of doing no harm, particular attention in designing this project has been paid to the exclusion of girls, children with disabilities, and other children belonging to disadvantaged or marginalized groups. The project will also ensure that schools, classrooms, child-friendly spaces, playgrounds and surrounding areas are rehabilitated in a way that poses no safety risks to children and teachers.In order to inform its assessments and implementation AAH continuously works on understanding gender roles. By understanding gender roles and household dynamics, AAH believes that the organisation mitigates the risks of doing harm. In 2019, AAH conducted a masculinity study in both Gaza and the West Bank, focused on identities/roles of men in order to fully understand men, women, girls and boys and gender dynamics. AAH has in place mechanisms to prevent and respond to sexual exploitation and abuse by all staff. All AAH staff has the obligation to adhere to the organisation code of conduct and to AAH Child Protection Policy. AAH has in place focal points at base and mission levels. as well as safe and confidential ways to report abuse. Moreover, in spring 2019, all staff received specific in person training on the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse. Even more, most of AAH staff have been gone through protection cluster facilitated trainings with the aim to know how to detect and refer potential cases of GBV. To ensure the safety of beneficiaries, rehabilitation activities will be done during the holidays in order not to expose the students to construction risks. Students will be involved in the design of the rehabilitation of school spaces, with the intent for the rehabilitations to address all sources of fears and discomfort, privacy issues, ensuring dignity of the beneficiaries.

9.2 - Equal and impartial access to aid

In 2019, AAH has conducted in its Middle East country offices a Protection Mainstreaming Internal Evaluation for which the consultant spent the month of March in oPt. Particular attention was paid to the way AAH includes vulnerable groups in its work, for instance through inclusive participatory assessments. As one of AAH’s core values, AAH thrives to prevent discrimination and to ensure equal and impartial access though transparency and accountability to affected populations. AAH aims for all community members to be involved and consulted as much as possible in project design and implementation, as well as evaluation, including through household and community consultations and focus group discussions that are accessible to all. In order for their participation to be meaningful, AAH focuses on ‘qualitative inclusion’ meaning safe spaces for beneficiaries to participate. Also, AAH started engaging with Humanity & Inclusion as inclusion experts to sensitise and inform AAH’s programming. For instance, instead of asking directly if people have a disability, AAH is increasingly using ‘The Washington Group Short Set of Questions’ to detect people with disabilities. AAH is reporting under the HRP disaggregated data by sex and age; though pending development of HRP detailed reporting format, AAH usually includes further disaggregation on vulnerability such as data on people with disabilities. In addition, AAH started in its assessments to consider ‘barriers’ of particularly vulnerable groups and ‘enabling factors’ for people with specific needs. AAH has safe and accessible beneficiary feedback mechanisms in place which allow beneficiaries to provide their inputs, complaints, and satisfaction regarding the activities implemented. They are designed to increase beneficiaries’ protection and comply with sensitive data management guidelines to uphold the principle of ‘Do No Harm’. Beneficiary knowledge of this system are promoted during field assessments and monitoring visits during which AAH and partners staff explain how the mechanisms work. In addition, a paragraph will be included in each agreement with beneficiaries explaining the steps to report complaints through the AAH beneficiary feedback mechanisms which are accessible by phone, email, confidential complaint box or in person (AAH or partners field staff or base members), according to AAH protocols. A service phone number will be distributed to the population in the proposed target communities, enabling them to report feedback or complaints relating to project implementation or AAH employees’ behaviour. AAH will process and treat confidentially all information collected with ad-hoc committee composed of project staff to understand the situation, identify appropriate solutions and include technical adjustments or modifications if needed. A monthly meeting will be conducted at community level to update beneficiaries and community leaders relating to project development and challenges. With regards to access in this specific project, AAH tries to achieve equal and impartial access through for instance working with children, teachers, community representatives and parents to support equal and safe access to education and training opportunities without discrimination of any kind. Through the project, AAH discusses with representatives and children of the targeted schools’ communities (including men, women, girls, boys, people with disabilities) to ensure all benefit from project intended outcomes and have access to the services provided.
