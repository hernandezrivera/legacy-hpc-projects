Project Summary

The intervention is interested in the framework of the objective 1, 2, 3 of the HRP 2022-2023.
Taking into consideration also the health system capacity in the context of COVID-19 and
to complement the efforts of the Health sector in all locations of intervention.
The huge burden of disease in Syria is due primarily to the fact that the health system is emerging from more than 10 years of crisis characterized by physical and psychological trauma, mental health disorders such as trauma, depression, post-traumatic stress disorders (PTSD) and others trauma and gender- based violence, particularly among displaced persons (WHO Syria Annual report 2016) as well as lifestyle diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, heart diseases, cancers which remain a major threat to a sizable proportion of the population is at risk of developing a PTSD; or have suffered lasting disabilities and gender based violence ( GBV).
As reported in WHO Syria annual report 2020, 53% of the 1,800 fixed primary health care centers monitored in 2020 were reported as fully functioning, while 28% were reported as completely non-functioning. In addition to an extreme lack of appropriate infrastructure, health workers in Syria struggle due to a shortage of essential medicines and working medical equipment, despite continued WHO health sector support through medical shipments. The provision of quality essential health services is also limited due to a
shortage in qualified health workers. Fifty percent of medical professionals fled the country over the past decade, resulting in limited access to medical services for child health, nutrition, communicable and non-communicable diseases, sexual and reproductive health, and mental health.
The project seeks to implement life-saving services for the diagnosis, follow up, treatment and covering medical prescriptions of host community, returnees and IDPs in Rural Damascus ( Kisweh), Al-Hasakeh (Al-Hasakeh city) and Deir-ez-Zor (Deir-ez-Zor city) governorates. Not only by establishing new Primary health care centers or support already established Primary health care centers with three main clinics of Internal medicine, Genecology & Obstetric, Pediatrics and laboratory to provide medical counselling, treatment, COVID-19 management and referral if needed in coordination with the implementing partners, but also by supporting physical rehabilitation activities including Physiotherapy/PSS for PwDs supported by mobile teams and also covering medical treatment of beneficiaries. So unmet needs of the most in need amongst the vulnerable returnees, refugees and IDPs in Rural Damascus, Al-Hasakeh and Deir-ez-Zor governorates can be served. Also in the context of COVID-19, supporting the primary health care system in areas of implementation – knowing its health facilities capacity and situation – will increase the capacity of its secondary and tertiary health care system to response to more complicated medical cases.
The project plans to conduct focused trainings for the staff and volunteers of SARC (Syrian Arab Red Crescent), and MoH to build their capacity, and provide structured trainings on GBV, MHGAP, CMR, case management and referral and all updates on COVID-19 shared by Health sector.

4 - Needs

As the Syria crisis enters its second decade, the health system remains heavily disrupted. Based on HRP 2022-2023 draft, more than 7.97 million people in 100 sub-districts were targeted and living below three critical emergency health standards. With just 58% of hospitals and 53% of HPC fully functional, the COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated weaknesses in the health system. As the outbreak continues, self-reported compliance with preventive measures remains below 50% while laboratory capacity and access to testing are also below recommended levels. Due to cumulative effects of concurrent crises, cost of health services; lack of medicines, supplies or health staff; and fear of exposure to COVID-19 have replaced distance, over-crowding and long wait times as the main self-reported barriers to health access. Among the 12.2 million people in need of health services in 2022-2023, 1.33 million are children U5 while 3.38 million are WRA. An additional 300,000 older people are requiring health services, as well as those with early onset NCD,3.5 million persons living with disabilities, which is estimated to account for 45% of all mortality in Syria. Notably, the cities and surrounding areas of Rural Damascus, and Deir-ez-Zor, remain at a severe level(severity score3)of need with only Al-Hasakeh (severity score4). This reflects not only the burden on the health system in areas where thousands of IDPs reside, but also the demands posed by patients arriving from all parts of the country

5 - Implementation of activities

The intervention is interested in the framework of the strategic objective 1,2, and 3 of HRP 2022-2023. It is designed to complement the efforts of the Health sector in areas of Implementation. By Increasing access to PHC through provision of essential PHC to cover health care needs of vulnerable populations, including RMNCH, and continuity of treatment for NCDs. Ensuring comprehensive referral pathways for acute and post-acute management phases. Medical referral systems must also address needs of people living with chronic disease who develop complications, emergency obstetric and newborn care (EmONC) and patients with acute illness who cannot access treatment in the area. Implementation will be through implementing partner as following: Provide essential primary and secondary health care services, EmONC and referral Provision of life-saving services through well equipped unit and well trained staff. Provision of access to PHC services through fixed clinics. Identification of pregnant women and follow up on antenatal, delivery and postnatal care by voucher system. Identification of cases in need of physical rehabilitation and follow up on Physiotherapy and PSS. Strengthen the capacity of health care providers and CHWs to provide essential health services: Training of mobile team staff and CHWs on health topics and identification of epidemic cases. In addition to the required monitoring mechanism for each health project at the program

11 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

R1: Local partners lose access to the project locations and are unable to implementactivities.M1: SARC displayed an outstanding ability to implement humanitarian projects despite constraints and limitations, However, if SARC lost the access to project locations, TDH would either identify other suitable partners or, incoordination with them and with local actors, identify other locations with same high needs, where the programming could continue.R2: bureaucracy and delay in getting needed approvals from local authorities.M2: TDH already obtained approvals to work in Northern and southern area of Syria, in addition, TDH will advocate through the local partner to have the needed approvals based on the signed MOU.R3: delay in receiving the fund due to the sanctions on the banking system in Syria.M3: TDH has different banking accounts in Syria with different pipelines to receive the fund from different sources.R4: new quarantine by the local authorities due to COVID outbreak.M4: TdH has on-line policy for approvals and working from home, also, the PHCs will follow COVIDmeasurement protocols , in addition, TdH health Advisor will conduct training to TdH staff and local partner volunteers for COVID measurements during humanitarian response and will distribute PPEs to both beneficiaries and staff.
