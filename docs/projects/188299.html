Project Summary

The 2022-2023 Syria Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP) sets out the framework within which the humanitarian community will respond to large-scale humanitarian and protection needs in Syria, flexibly leveraging the various response modalities.
Capturing these capacities and efforts under a single strategic framework, the plan aims to increase the effectiveness of the response by improving the identification of needs and gaps, reducing overlap and duplication, and monitoring context changes and progress against collective agreed response results. The Humanitarian Coordinator for Syria and the Regional Humanitarian Coordinator for the Syria Crisis lead and coordinate humanitarian action inside Syria. OCHA, in line with its global mandate, supports the humanitarian leadership in effectively coordinating the response inside Syria. To do so, OCHA maintains country offices in Syria and Turkey, and a regional office in Amman. OCHA Turkey coordinates cross-border humanitarian assistance delivered from Turkey to Syria as mandated by the UN Security Council resolutions 2449 under the leadership of the Deputy Regional Humanitarian Coordinator (DRHC).
OCHA requires sustained levels of funding to be able to maintain and strengthen effective humanitarian response coordination from these various offices, facilitate strategic and operational coordination platforms, provide information management services, manage pooled funds, support humanitarian access analysis and negotiation, ensure civil-military coordination, and provide public information and advocacy on the
humanitarian situation. Funding requirements for this project reflect the needs of OCHA's three offices directly involved in the Syria response.

4 - Needs

A decade into the crisis, factors driving humanitarian needs amongst the population in Syria have multiplied. An estimated 14.6 million people continue to require some form of humanitarian assistance in 2022, an increase of 1.2 million from 2021. Across the country, the long-standing needs of an estimated 6.9 million IDPs, including 2 million people in 1,760 informal settlements and planned camps, as well as of overstretched host communities and hundreds of thousands of people having returned to their places of origin in recent years have remained staggering and become more deeply entrenched. Despite some reduction in violence, Syria remains a major protection crisis, with civilians exposed to multiple protection risks related to ongoing hostilities; the effects of new and protracted displacement; and the depletion of socio-economic resources triggering negative coping strategies. The large scale of needs requires dynamic and flexible coordination support and systems to facilitate effective humanitarian response. Provision of humanitarian assistance across Syria by numerous Syrian NGOs, international NGOs, the Red Cross/ Crescent Movement, and United Nations agencies occurs through Syria-based humanitarian program, interagency convoys, and cross-border response. The different response modalities provided by the diverse humanitarian actors working on the Syria response, stress the heightened need for ensuring effective and efficient coordination structures.

10 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

OCHA recognizes the risks of instrumentalization of aid in the Syrian context and the effects this may have on the protection of vulnerable people. OCHA also recognizes risks related to GBV, SEA, and safety of humanitarian workers and the potential negative affect this may have on affected populations. OCHA will support inter-agency efforts to mitigate protection risks for humanitarian workers, such asthe SSG-endorsed Duty of Care Framework for humanitarian organizations working in Syria, and the work of the WoS PSEA network. OCHA will support and strengthen inter-agency PSEA mechanisms to mitigate the risk of sexual exploitation of abuse by humanitarian workers. OCHA will continue to disseminate information to its staff on the zero tolerance for SEA and sensitize them regularly about the need to prevent any SEA. This includes facilitating all staff completion of mandatory training on PSEA; facilitate that all new and current staff in the field sign the SG’s Bulletin; Conduct regular internal dialogue with all staff to ensure ongoing awareness. Moreover, OCHA will also continue deepening the mainstreaming of PSEA into humanitarian processes, through integration of PSEA messaging intokey multisectoral tools, assessments, and guidance documents used by organizations in the WoS response. Mainstreaming PSEA through humanitarian planning and programming also includes ensuring that the ISG, SSG and HLG engage in strategic PSEA discussions in support of advocacy efforts.


11 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

OCHA will strive to mitigate the above-mentioned adverse effects by promoting principled humanitarian action from all the hubs involved in the Syria response and quality programming by partners. It will do so in collaboration with humanitarian partners, including the Protection sector and its associated AoRs, and the PSEA advisor. It will also continue to put affected populations at the centre of the humanitarian response and use feedback collected from affected communities to better highlight and meet needs. The potential non-renewal of the Security Council Resolution enabling UN humanitarian response from Turkey into Syria would result in a significantly altered response and coordination environment, for which OCHA in conjunction with partners is engaging in planning to mitigate.
