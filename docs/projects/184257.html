Project Summary

Somalia is facing a severe and deteriorating humanitarian crisis as we enter 2022 as a result of the compounding effects of conflict, recurrent climate shocks, diseases and pests outbreaks with increased levels of vulnerability and substantial increase in the humanitarian caseload (Somalia HNO, 2022). There is a serious risk that the impact of consecutive below average to poor rainfall in most parts of the country will develop into a severe drought by early to mid- 2022 with the number of people facing acute food insecurity on the rise. It is likely that minority groups, women, children, people with disabilities, will experience heightened protection risks as a result, unless they are intentionally targeted to receive tailored assistance.
With approximately 3.5 million individuals in IPC 3 and 4 through at least December 2021, FAO proposes to target 160,300 households (961,800 individuals- 91,500 IDPs and 870,300 non- IDPs) in IPC 3 and IPC 4 caseloads respectively. This will include 216,790 women, 254,492 girls, 264,880 boys and 225,638 men and 144,270 people living with disabilities (PWD) across 59 districts in Somalia through its flagship Cash+ approach combining short-term cash transfers with tailored livelihood support in accordance with technical and humanitarian standards. Vulnerable households will identified from at-risk pastoral, agro-pastoral, fishing, riverine farmers, IDPs and Non- IDP communities in IPC 3 and 4 areas. This intervention will integrate short-term cash transfers aimed at enabling the most vulnerable households to meet their immediate needs, with livelihood support to allow crisis-affected households to get back into production. FAO will employ community- led beneficiary selection processes and ensure adequate targeting and representation of extremely vulnerable and minority groups in community committees. Interventions proposed will employ a Do No Harm approach with a gender lens that ensures conflict- sensitivity to reduce protection risks for all individuals, and to mitigate the risk of conflict or community tensions.
Under Food Security Cluster (FSC) Objective 1, to improve access to food, FAO will deliver lifesaving cash assistance to 160,300 households through a blend of conditional (Cash-for-Work) and unconditional (UCT) transfers. Under UCT, FAO will prioritize Cash+ interventions including Cash+ Agriculture, Cash+ livestock and Cash+ fisheries. These modalities will increase households’ immediate access to food by providing sufficient cash to access the minimum expenditure basket (MEB), while supporting the rehabilitation of critical productive and community infrastructure. It will also boost household food production, increase household incomes and create employment opportunities. Cash transfer values will be informed by the Cash Working Group and are expected to differ per district/ region of intervention. They, however, will be valued at 80 percent of the food MEB, which can range from USD 50- 100 per month. All cash-based programmes will include training on good nutrition practices.
Under FSC Objective 2, to protect livelihoods and related food sources, FAO will provide in-kind, technical support and tailored trainings to 112,500 HHs to anticipate, mitigate and respond to food security and nutrition threats, including the delivery of integrated cash and livelihood support to targeted HHs. In addition, FAO shall provide transitional cash support to 13,100 HHs across agro pastoral, pastoral and fishing livelihood zones to augment the adaptive capacity of HHs, strengthen and foster resilience to future shocks, cushioning them from food insecurity and malnutrition. Trainings shall be delivered alongside input provision to build skills in sustainable use of resources provided, build skills in livelihoods management including harvest, post- harvest management, linking beneficiaries to long- term interventions that provide them opportunities to enhance their livelihoods and skills.

2.1.1 - What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

Cash stimulates local markets and economies thus benefiting communities at large. Cash payments enable households to make their own decisions based on their specific needs prioritization. Empowering households to make such decisions supports best value for money as it allows households to purchase specific items they require, in the quantities they need, when they need them. Cash increases investment in inputs and ownership of productive assets, encourages the adoption of improved agricultural practices and reduces the risk of households resorting to negative coping mechanisms. Cash can be delivered remotely through mobile money transfer, which is especially important for (i) pastoral/nomadic households that must keep moving in search of grazing ground for their livestock, and (ii) rural areas because cash stimulates production across the whole value chain when beneficiaries purchase locally from local vendors. In addition, the flexible nature of cash plays a significant role in enhancing inclusivity within interventions. For instance, women and girls can access services and goods tailored to their needs, thereby reducing protection concerns that stem from a lack of access to such basic needs.

2.1.2 - Explain how the project design considered market functionality?

This project utilizes a market- based approach to programming by employing modalities such as e- vouchers and cash that have proven effective in supporting local markets. It further seeks to spur markets by factoring in market infrastructure improvement such as feeder roads through CFW interventions. It also aims to promote household purchasing power and access to basic needs that are provided in local markets. By minimizing external procurement of assets, it provides opportunities for local market actors to interact and strengthen and stabilize the local demand and supply patterns. Markets across Somalia are responsive to increased demand. In designing this project, FAO assessed the capacity of local markets to respond to increased demand. The geographic spread, timing and caseloads of the proposed cash interventions take into account pressures on local markets and their functionality. Furthermore, FAO will continually monitor markets in Somalia through the Food Security and Nutrition Analysis Unit (FSNAU) and the Cash Working Group for any significant issues related to market dynamics that would impact the efficacy of cash and other interventions.

4.6 - Mitigation Measures

FAO mitigates the risk that beneficiaries are exposed to violence during registration/distribution as a result of the distance to or location of humanitarian assistance and services by: (i) Carrying out beneficiary registration and service delivery at village level. (ii) Providing services to beneficiaries remotely through mobile phone, including cash transfers and e-vouchers for livelihood support, disseminated by SMS. (iii) Using a consensus-based approach to beneficiary targeting and selection through a tripartite, that includes a committee of elders (representative of the local community), NGOs (ensuring compliance to FAO SOPs), and district authorities (ensuring the safety of the intervention and representing the government). (iv) Contracting NGOs and traders, authorized by local authorities to work in selected districts. FAO mitigates the risk of women, girls and boys exposed to sexual violence, including sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA), when accessing humanitarian assistance, through: (i) Developing activities that do not require travel outside of the targeted villages. (ii) FAO’s Compliance, Complaints and Feedback (CoCO) system that enables two-way communication with communities, including confidential reporting on SEA. (iii) Toll-free hotline number (disseminated by bulk SMS before activities start, monitored through systematic surveys by FAO's Call Centre). (iv) Mandatory training to partners on PSEA and AAP. FAO mitigates the risk that marginalized groups, persons with disabilities, women, boys and girls, do not participate in consultations or decision-making processes for the project or are excluded from humanitarian services/assistance/activities through: (i) The above-described consensus-based approach to beneficiary selection and targeting. (ii) Systematic Call Centre surveys, which embed AAP questions, query the fairness of beneficiary selection and ensure at least 30% of beneficiaries are women. (iii) FAO’s Hotline, through which communities are able to report back. (iv) Mandatory training to partners on how to target and select beneficiaries against FAO criteria, AAP, Gender and PSEA, among other topics. (v) Provision of UCTs, to ensure CFW and other activities do not exclude people living with disability or illness, elderly, among other groups. (vi) Mobile money, e-vouchers and applications (e.g. voice recognition, voice messages) to provide more convenient, inclusive services to people who are less mobile, illiterate, etc. (vii) Field and Third Party Monitoring (TPM). FAO mitigates the risk that beneficiaries are not accessing/using complaint and feedback mechanisms, through: (i) FAO’s CoCO system (explained above). (ii) Call Centre systematic surveys, for a sample of beneficiaries, to receive feedback and complaints. (iii) Provision of SIM cards and allowance for the purchase of a mobile device (under FAO cash-based interventions) ensuring access to complaint and feedback mechanisms. (iv) TPM and field monitors, who have direct interaction with beneficiaries and report feedback. (v) Communication and sensitization sessions with communities, bulk text and voice SMS and radio campaigns to communicate FAO’s complaint and feedback mechanisms, and to update beneficiaries on their entitlements and other key information. FAO mitigates the risk of interference from external stakeholders in the impartial and needs-based selection of beneficiaries through: (i) Building consensus on the selection of villages and beneficiaries, through a tripartite approach (as above). (ii) District targeting by FAO, based on IPC data. (iii) Training partners on beneficiary selection criteria, AAP, PSEA and other mandatory topics. (iv) Mediation mechanisms, in place with district authorities, community representatives and government (federal and state). (v) TPM and FAO field monitors, who review and mediate cases of external interferences.
