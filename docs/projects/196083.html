Project Summary

Improving Lives through Assistive Devices: Assistive devices are essential to increasing an individual's ability to function independently, improve well-being, and enable people with physical disabilities to carry out their daily activities and participate actively in their community. Unfortunately, in NES, neither the government nor the people themselves are able to provide the necessary health care and assistive devices. Therefore, SP will provide support in Hassakah and in-camp populations through the provision of wheelchairs, crutches, posterior walkers, pairs of eyeglasses and/or eye patches and corrective eye surgeries. The project will target children with cerebral palsy, paraplegia, congenital abnormalities and deformities, leg length discrepancy, mild to moderate abnormalities of the lower limbs, myopia, hyperopia, amblyopia, and strabismus.
Physical Therapy Home Visits will be provided to strengthen and improve muscle function and coach children on achieving ADLs (Activities of Daily Living) with a disability. Understanding that children in IDP camps experience unique challenges and are less likely to independently demonstrate adherence to a physical therapy exercise regimen, SP has a male physical therapist and female physical therapist assistant who will partner directly with patients and their families in their homes to address needs, encourage adherence to a physical therapy plan, and find creative solutions to achieving ADLs in a low-resource setting.
Vision Correction is integral to education, socialization, and safety. Although not formally a disability, easily-corrected vision disorders, such as myopia, significantly impact a child's development. Most families are unaware of the importance of pediatric vision screenings to identify and correct vision problems and cannot afford ophthalmologist consultations or corrective lenses. SP will provide vision consultations, corrective eyeglasses and corrective eye surgeries.
Caregivers Support Groups aim to encourage and affirm caregivers by providing respite care. Using lessons learned from 2021, SP will support caregivers in understanding the world and their experiences, a space to speak truthfully about their struggles in the past and present, and the tools to recognize and respond to trauma in their children.
Children Support Groups: Children will have their own time where they will share experiences living with disabilities. Staff trained in Psychological First Aid and TMCCD are experienced in drawing out conversations sensitive to the traumas children have gone through, which will facilitate the groups. Children will have the chance to engage in arts and crafts, games, and socialize with their peers in a safe, inclusive, welcoming environment.
Prior to the formation of the support groups, SP staff will be trained on facilitation of the groups, USAID's Training Manual for CwD as well as, psychological first aid to ensure facilitators are experienced in drawing out conversations sensitive to the traumas children have gone through, which will facilitate the groups.
For all activities- SP will conduct pre/post surveys to monitor growth, track outpatient consultations, and numbers of groups/surgeries completed. This will fall under sector and SP standards for M&E reporting.
Joint coordination between in-camp protection, CP WG, and health actors and camp management is prioritized before program implementation to avoid duplication of services. Coordination with CP WG will also ensure collaboration and learning more about best practices from other organizations in the area.

3 - Needs

Over ten years of conflict in Syria has exposed millions to physical injury and trauma while compounding risks for persons with pre-existing or non-war related disabilities, undermining their access to essential services and support. In Northeast Syria (NES), people with disabilities have reported limited healthcare access, while 59% of the children with disabilities (CwDs) do not attend school. Northeast Syria maintains the highest proportion of CwD in Syria. One in five children (21%) aged 2-4 in NES have disabilities, 39% of children ages 5-9 have disabilities, and nearly half of children aged 10-17 (47%) have disabilities. Additionally, 48% of HHs have at least one child with disabilities, while 53% of female-headed HHs have children with disabilities (25% for males). High and chronic prevalence of children at risk of economic deprivation, low education attendance rates, and increased dependence on household members. Consequently, the added burden of care for CwDs can undermine economic resilience and increase dependence on external support services. The project aims to support these caregivers and the children by providing assistive devices and psychosocial support to empower and make access to services possible. SP will partake in the CP WG for coordination and to avoid duplication of services.

4 - Implementation of activities

In 2021, Samaritan's Purse implemented the children with disabilities project in Serekaniye and Washokani camps. Continue targeting children with disabilities in the camps and collective centers indicated by the Child Protection WG. Samaritan's Purse will be providing consultations, physical therapy, assistive devices, glasses and psychosocial support to the children and caregivers. SMART goals for physical therapy patients are mutually agreed upon by the patient, caregiver, and SP physical therapist. For M&E- Patient data workbooks are updated daily and weekly to evaluate physical therapy patients' progress toward short and long-term goals. Physical therapy patients who show no progress after two months (or 12 sessions) are sent for consultations with an external licensed physician for a second opinion, diagnosis, and updated treatment plan. For M&E mechanisms, all beneficiaries receive end-line surveys indicating their satisfaction with program activities, devices provided, results obtained, and staff performance. Joint coordination between in-camp protection, CP WG, and health actors and camp management is prioritized before program implementation to avoid duplication of services. After program onset, continued coordination and robust referral systems between health and protection actors ensure good communication between actors and that beneficiaries are accepted into the program that best fits their needs.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

a) Staff or patients assaulted en route to/from doctors' appointments;b) Risk to beneficiaries related to the receipt of ready-to-use therapeutic food (nutrition program) and/or assistive devices and/or medication, the perceived economic value of which may make the beneficiaries or their families targets within the community. c) Risk to beneficiaries around inequitable access to programming for females and children, due to cultural context which gives preference to adult males as recipients of programming and/or programming commodities. d) Risk to female beneficiaries of forced pregnancy to have them qualify for nutrition programming benefiting pregnant and lactating women. All staff involved in the project are trained in managing and/or referring specific gender and protection concerns and have an operational awareness of protection risks/issues. A safeguarding focal point is assigned, and the complaints/feedback mechanism means that feedback is captured from remote/off-site locations and acted upon. Samaritan's Purse will assess the contextualized assistance needs of other vulnerable groups, including persons with disabilities and ensure their equitable participation and inclusion in the project. Across NES, the numbers of disabled people are quite high – HNAP's 2021 report puts the number at 37% of the population.


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

Risks identified• Contracted doctors violating PSEA policy;• Beneficiaries exploited by SP staff members, partners, and/or vendors. Samaritan's Purse mitigates PSEA risks with the following measures:• Background checks on all personnel, safeguarding training for all staff, protection messaging displayed in the clinic, clear reporting system for violations. There is an HR and complaints processing department to support this risk mitigation.• A safeguarding focal point is assigned, and the complaints/feedback mechanism means that feedback is captured from remote/off-site locations and acted upon.• Reporting mechanisms for each project.• Hotline.• Email hotline.


9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

• Security situation or border restrictions cause the team to lose access to NES for a period of time. This is mitigated through the CoH staff and SP staff being able to continue to activities in the interim period. • COVID-19 cases amongst health worker staff, SP or partner. Continued training and enforcement of infection prevention and control protocols. • Pockets of higher risk area due to tribal tension and ISIS sleeper cell activity. Mitigated through security assessment of the area to identify processes and protocols and coordination with local community actors and members.
