Project Summary

South Sudan continues to grapple with the food insecurity crisis; In the December 2021 Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO), it was determined that 8.3 million people (60% of the total population) are expected to face severe food insecurity (IPC phase 3) or worse at the peak of the 2022 lean season. The level of severe food insecurity saw a 7% increase compared to 2021 (REACH Jan 2022).
The assessed communities in Tonj East and Tonj North indicated that the crisis was occasioned by a number of shocks namely; insecurity and flooding which simultaneously displaced a sizeable portion of the population. Insecurity in the area restricted many households to cultivating small plots of crops within the homestead which were destroyed by the heavy flooding.
The communities in the Tonj East and North are in urgent need of food and agricultural inputs (crop, vegetable agricultural tools and fishing kits) to stimulate production and save lives.
line with the FSL cluster priorities (CO2 and CO3), Help Restore Youth (HeRY) is proposing an integrated emergency food security and livelihood intervention in Tonj East and Tonj North covering the 21 Payams of the two counties targeting the most vulnerable households for emergency distribution of livelihood kits to stimulate production.
The project targets 12,504 (6,252 households per county) of the most vulnerable households with interventions including; distribution of emergency livelihood kits; vegetable, crop, fishing kits and farming tools intended to stimulate food production to save lives while building resilience.
Households will be trained on climate smart agronomic techniques as well as good fishing practices aimed at improving yield for both household consumption and for sale. The income generated from sale of the extra yield will be utilised to cover other household needs.
The project will train extension workers and support them to provide extension services at Boma level.
Demonstration gardens managed by the trained extension workers will be established at Boma level to support continuous learning by the beneficiaries beyond project life.
Agricultural production will be integrated with nutritional education focusing on food preparation and utilisation demonstrations to support households improve nutrition especially for expectant, lactating mothers and children under five years of age.
Disaster risk reduction committees will be established and trained at Boma level. These will coordinate and support implementation of disaster preparedness and risk reduction activities including mapping of flood prone zones and safe zones where settlements would be set up during the flooding season. 1 - Needs

South Sudan continues to grapple with the food insecurity crisis; In the December 2021 Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO), it was determined that 8.3 million people (60% of the total population) are expected to face severe food insecurity (IPC phase 3) or worse at the peak of the 2022 lean season. The level of severe food insecurity saw a 7% increase compared to 2021 (REACH Jan 2022).In total, 46 counties were classified in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity, 26 in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) acute food insecurity, and 6 were stressed (IPC Phase 2) acute food insecurity (IPC classification). An estimated 119,000 people are projected to face IPC Phase 5 catastrophe food insecurity in Akobo, Pibor, Tonj East, Tonj North, Tonj South and Aweil South. Throughout 2021, insecurity and flooding simultaneously drove large-scale displacement and prevented vulnerable populations, particularly in the Greater Luachjang region of Tonj East, from undertaking displacement journeys to access food and services. Greater Luachjang communities were unable to move to the north, east or south due to tensions with communities in the neighbouring counties of Tonj North, Mayendit (Unity State), and Rumbek North (Lakes State). Insecurity also prevented many households in Tonj East from cultivating. The few Households in Tonj East that were able to cultivate largely did so in close proximity to their homestead and harvested food stocks were expected to be exhausted as early as November/December 2021. As such, communities in the east of Tonj East are likely to continue to experience a typically severe, widespread and prolonged food consumption gap. Tonj North has equally suffered the same fate with an estimated population of 46,000 people displaced by November 2021. Flooding and insecurity continued to drive large-scale displacement for the rest of the year. This prevented internally displaced persons (IDPs) from accessing their land throughout the cultivation cycle. Most IDPs were unable to harvest, while host community members only cultivated small plots surrounding the homestead, with insecurity limiting access to extended plots which typically provide the majority of the households’ food stocks. Communities in Tonj East and Tonj North further indicated that climatic and economic shocks equally contributed to their loss; Local currency depreciation, inflation, high import and fuel costs, among other factors, are sustaining high staple food prices and limiting economic activity, which in turn restricts household purchasing power. Tonj East and Tonj North counties are in urgent need of humanitarian assistance to prevent the situation from deteriorating even further therefore, in line with the FSL cluster priorities (CO2 and CO3), Help Restore Youth (HeRY) is proposing an integrated emergency food security and livelihood intervention in Tonj East and Tonj North covering the 21 Payams of the two counties targeting the most vulnerable households for emergency distribution of livelihood kits to stimulate production. The project targets 12,504 (6,252 households per county) of the most vulnerable households with interventions including; distribution of emergency livelihood kits; vegetable, crop, fishing kits and farming tools intended to stimulate food production to save lives while building resilience. Households will be trained on climate smart agronomic techniques as well as good fishing practices aimed at improving yield for both household consumption and for sale. The income generated from sale of the extra yield will be utilised to cover other household needs. Demonstration gardens will be established at Boma level to support continuous learning by the beneficiaries. Agricultural production shall be integrated with nutritional education focusing on foodpreparation and utilisation demonstrations to support households improve nutritionespecially for expectant, lactating mothers and children under five years of age.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

HeRY maintains a risk and project and program team proactively identify, assess, measure, mitigate and report on any risk exposures that are likely to affect the project results and continuously monitor and mitigate them especially in respect of the most vulnerable groups such as women, men, girls, boys, elderly and persons with disabilities. During mobilisation, identification and registration of project beneficiaries; HeRY uses the FAO/WFP beneficiary guide to share the targeting with involvement of all representative groups such as women, men, girls, boys, elderly and persons with disabilities; they are provided with information on the eligibility criteria in the project; the communities themselves pre-select the beneficiaries with the help of local leadership and HeRY only participates in validation of lists. Where pre-selection guide is not followed, HeRY will engage with all the priority stakeholders for a joint amicable solution. In agricultural service provision, HeRY is aware, aspects of GBV and child exploitation are likely to materialise in the mostly patriarchal dominated communities we have worked in. Our Staff code of conduct and PSEA documents clearly direct HeRY to advocate for elimination of these vices; our staff and service providers sign commitment for a strict zero tolerance to GBV/PSEA risks and are expected to share this information to all stakeholders. In the course of our programming, where these risks materialise, we offer referral services for survivors within the inter-cluster network especially by working with protection specialists to address the concerns while upholding confidentiality in the entire process; any data obtained during the course of protection service will only be privy to designated protection teams and survivor identity is protected. HeRY will also monitor GBV/other protection risks by regularly undertaking safety audits and use protection tip sheets to proactively identify and create awareness on the negative effects on the human rights and dignity of the most vulnerable in our project intervention areas. HeRY is also aware of some of the protection risks that emerge as a result of lack of information and involvement of priority vulnerable persons like; women, girls, PWDs, and the youth. in this regard, this project has already provided for inclusion of all representative groups of the most vulnerable persons in being part of the project implementation team; in community group leadership and information sharing through help desks, meetings, peer to peer talk, pictorial awareness information on GBV/PSEA related risks during critical service delivery points. This will minimise vulnerabilities in information access and decision making within the project and thus improve overall outcomes of the ongoing interventions. At project inception meetings, HeRY will establish and communicate all project information to all stakeholders [women, girls, boys, men, PWDs and Elderly] and every gender group will have leadership representation. This approach will eliminate risks of leaving out some vulnerable groups and improve active participation by all. Accountability by HeRY to the beneficiaries and the beneficiaries to HeRY will be improved through active participation and involvement of priority vulnerable persons who are always left out in programming and service delivery . In addition, land is a critical resource for productive food security and livelihood activities, so with the help of local leadership [paramount chiefs, village/Boma/county administrators, executive directors and RRC], HeRY will influence access to agricultural and settlement land for especially returnees, and IDPs and encourage local leadership to peacefully arbitrate in any land disputes for harmonious coexistence and above all for the vulnerable project beneficiaries to contribute to their own food and livelihood baskets.

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

The project has inbuilt mechanisms to address protection concerns in all priority locations. At institutional level, our Staff code of conduct and PSEA documents clearly direct HeRY to advocate for elimination GBV and other protection related vices; our staff and service providers sign commitment for a strict zero tolerance to GBV/PSEA risks and are expected to share this information to all stakeholders. In the course of implementation, where these risks become apparent, we offer referral services for survivors within the inter-cluster network where we work with protection specialists to address the concerns. HeRY will also monitor GBV/other protection risks by regularly undertaking safety audits and use protection tip sheets to proactively identify and create awareness on the negative effects on the human rights and dignity of the most vulnerable in our project intervention areas.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

For this outcome, the community will be trained on disaster risk reduction techniques that specifically address floods;Hazard zoning; this technique will be used to map flood prone areas within the settlement as well as the areas that remain dry when flooding inevitably starts. This will help in planning new settlement areas ahead of time and this will help to save lives, livestock and crops as such areas would not be encouraged for production during the flooding season.Building dykes: these will help drive flood water away from the settlement and access routes.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

The project is aligned to this collective outcome; under this project, HeRY will distribute crop, vegetable and fishing kits to vulnerable households and train them on climate smart agronomic skills aimed at improving yield. The resultant harvest will address immediate household food security needs thereby supporting them to move from IPC phase 5 and 4 respectively. For sustainability of this outcome, the project has a resilience component where households will be trained and supported to produce their own food for immediate, interim and future needs and on coping mechanisms for some of the shocks that have continuously led to loss, especially flooding.
