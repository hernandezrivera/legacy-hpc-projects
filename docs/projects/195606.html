Project Summary

The Republic of Sudan faces complex and overlapping challenges in 2023 including a fragile political situation,
socioeconomic instability, and limited access to basic social services. Progress made since 2019 towards more
democratic governance was halted by the military takeover of October 2021. More than a year later, there remains a
political crisis with an unclear path to civilian rule, with protests continuing regularly in urban areas. IOM’s Displacement
Tracking Matrix (DTM) data from Mobility Tracking Round 5 identified the accumulative presence of 3,779,487 IDPs,
1,181,419 permanent returnees from internal displacement, 55,045 seasonal returnees, 148,256 returnees from abroad,
and 775,570 foreign nationals currently residing in Sudan. Since the beginning of 2022, 298,000 people were newly
displaced, 1,092 injured, and 896 were killed by continued outbreaks of inter-communal violence especially in Blue Nile
(127,961), West Darfur (93,779), and West Kordofan (30,272) (OCHA Situation Report). Several of these inter-communal
conflicts have also occurred along migration corridors where mobile transhumant and sedentary herder and farming.
communities continue to clash over access to natural and factional fighting, access to public services, recurrent natural disasters, and lack of durable solutions. Security sector reform, including the full formation and deployment of joint security forces for the protection of civilians, also faces challenges, including the lack of a singular and unified military force and recurrent conflicts Rule of law, access to justice, and government social protection and programs and their capacities remain weak in conflict-affected areas of Darfur and South, North, and West Kordofan, White Nile, and Blue Nile as well as in eastern states. Police, courts, civil registries, and social welfare institutions have limited presence outside of urban centers and are poorly equipped to address the scale of protection needs among IDPs and refugees in camps, settlements, informal sites, and rural areas of return.
There are 6.5 M persons in need of some type of protection support across the country. This includes
about 3.03M IDPs, 0.97M returnees, 1.16M refugees, and 1.49 M vulnerable Sudanese. New and protracted
displacement, lack of basic services, and the deteriorating socioeconomic situation have all had
profound consequences on conflict-affected persons’ immediate physical and mental well-being, living
standards, and their long-term resilience. The United Nations reports verified 292 grave violations against 274 children (143 boys, 131 girls including killing, maiming, sexual violence, recruitment and use by armed groups and forces, abduction, and denial of humanitarian assistance mostly in Darfur. The direct physical and psychological scars of conflict on children and youth, constant exposure to high stress, repeated loss of friends and family members, isolation, and lack of protective systems are taking their toll on the mental health of children and their families. Parts of the country remain littered with Explosive Remnants of War (ERW), including landmines.

1 - Needs

The HNO 2022 indicates that armed conflict, sub-national organized violence, COVID-19, and flooding have additionally exacerbated the already extreme levels of GBV with over 6,000 GBV incidents recorded between January and September 2022. The GBVIMS statistics (2021) further indicate that 97 percent of the reported GBV incidents survivors were females (women and girls) while six of the survivors were less than 18 years old with intimate partners accounting for more than half of the alleged perpetrators. Access to GBV services across most communities remains a major challenge owing to the limited availability of the services. The global COVID-19 pandemic has further caused a devastating socio-economic impact on people, including severe economic contractions, spikes in prices of basic commodities, loss of livelihoods, increased protection risks, and disrupted access to basic services. Measures adopted to curb the spread of COVID-19 have also contributed to a rise in GBV incidents. A multi-sector needs assessment conducted by the HealthCare Foundation Organization (HFO) in July 2022 covering White Nile state, Khartoum, and Blue Nile state indicated that GBV is more prevalent within the households and mostly perpetrated by intimate partners. In addition, the majority of the community members who participated in the key informant interviews and focus group discussion acknowledged that the prevalence of GBV was high in their communities as they mentioned to have witnessed GBV-related incidents perpetrated almost daily. This is largely attributed to the deep-rooted cultural beliefs, attitudes, and perceptions about women and girls that contribute significantly to unequal power dynamics among men and women and hence resulting in normalizing violence against women. In addition, fear of retaliation through revenge killings was mentioned to be a major barrier to accessing the available services by survivors. Across the four Counties, the GBV situation has further been exacerbated by the insecurity situation, flooding, and COVID-19 pandemic. The closure of schools that serve as a haven for children has impacted significantly the well-being of adolescent girls who have been subjected to early marriage in addition to other forms of violence while boys have been exposed to crime and other forms of exploitation in the communities. The increased risks, coupled with gaps in GBV service provision, necessitate the urgent need for strengthened GBV prevention, risk mitigation, and response services. 6.2 - Explain how your project aims to ensure protection mainstreaming during its design and implementation

, HFO will incorporate the protection principles and promote meaningful access, safety and dignity in all activities. The following elements will be taken into account: 1. Prioritize safety & dignity, and avoid causing harm: prevent and minimize as much as possible any unintended negative effects of the intervention which can increase people's vulnerability to both physical and psychosocial risks 2. Meaningful Access: arrange for people’s access to assistance and services -in proportion to their needs and without any barriers (e.g. discrimination). Pay special attention to individuals and groups who may be particularly vulnerable or have difficulty accessing assistance and services. 3. Accountability: set-up appropriate mechanisms through which affected populations can measure the adequacy of interventions, and address concerns and complaints 4. Participation and empowerment: support the development of self-protection, and capacities and assist people to claim their rights To prevent causing harm and ensure the safety of the beneficiaries, HFO will conduct sessions on safety measures to adopt during the repairs and after the work. These will also be developed with the aim to reinforce the beneficiaries’ autonomy and capacity to protect themselves in case of endangerment. HFO will ensure the creation of a safe and dignified environment. HFO aims to identify the most vulnerable individuals or groups when leading assessments and surveys and to analyze their needs and feedback appropriately, and HFO will develop strong accountability mechanisms as well. HFO will develop efficient accountability mechanisms, with the local authorities and community leaders. HFO will apply the “Do No Harm” principle across all its activities. HFO will also ensure that the supported health facilities are safely accessible, particularly for vulnerable groups. An entrance accessible for people with disability, and a safe waiting area will be dedicated to women unattended cases in need of PSS and base protection assistance such as child protection-related issues, GBV cases, and severely traumatic experiences. HFO will activate the identified and relevant referral system when necessary.

7.2 - Please outline how the project or your agency will inform and interact with inter-agency approaches to accountability to affected people and feedback mechanisms, including SEA and abuse complaints?

During assessments and project design and implementation, local stakeholders and the community as well as beneficiaries are consulted to ensure a maximum understanding of their needs. Activities are designed according to their needs and our minimum quality standards. These are monitored, commented on and redirected as necessary, according to feedback from the field throughout the course of work. HFO will ensure that the field staff share with the community HFO’s commitment to accountability to the affected population on issues such as staff recruitment and training, code of conduct, information sharing and transparency. Partners will be included in the monitoring and evaluation mechanisms. The beneficiary population will also be informed of the project implementation modalities and the selection procedure if any, as mentioned above. HFO will set up a complaint and feedback mechanism adapted to the local context to collect the views and complaints of a wide range of stakeholders (affected populations, communities, local civil society actors, international humanitarian actors, local authorities or national governments and donors) and take appropriate action. A complaint response mechanism (CRM) will be designed in line with the protection principle of Do No Harm, in consultation with partners and affected communities, taking into account available internal resources (financial and human) and with the objective of gradually scaling up. HFO thus aims to guarantee the availability of appropriate Feedback/Complaints channels, while ensuring effective complaints handling, in order to avoid creating frustration or mistrust with regard to the proposed mechanisms. Depending on the context, this mechanism consists of • Community Mobilizers, who would be important messengers conveying feedback and complaints from the population, themselves being part of the community, • Complaint boxes, especially in health facilities, • Regular consultations with community focal points/community committees, • Focus group discussions led by HFO teams. Whatever the mechanism(s) chosen, HFO will: • Inform the population of the existence and functioning of the complaint and feedback mechanism.
