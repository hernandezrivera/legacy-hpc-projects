Project Summary

The overarching objective of the project is to establish secure and dignified living conditions, with a focused emphasis on mitigating physical and protection-related risks for both the FDMN and the host community (HC). The comprehensive strategy includes the construction of 400 new Mid-Term Shelters (MTS) for the FMDN at Camp 1 East and West, the repair of 100 existing shelters, and the establishment of 15 host community shelters for the HC in Jalia Palong. Guided by SCCCM Sector assessments, the distribution plan maintains flexibility to adapt based on evolving needs. Additionally, GUSS will provide 1,512 solar lights as in-kind support to benefit 1,000 FDMN and 512 host families. Moreover, 1,000 FDMN and 1,000 host families will each receive a blanket for added support. Beneficiary selection is a collaborative process, engaging stakeholders such as community leaders, local administration, and UNHCR through participatory community consultations. It is to be noted that, all activities about shelter and NFI will be done in coordination with the SCCCM sector, relevant sector focal and govt. authorities. The coordination with UNHCR and sector focal will be maintained during project implementation to ensure that there is no overlap. Initially, communication will be made with the sector and the AoR Lead Agency to carry out any kind of SCCCM interventions. In addition, relevant documentation will be prepared as per the RRRC office and relevant stakeholders.
Inclusivity is at the forefront of the gender and inclusion strategy, engaging both adult men and women in diverse activities with equal rights and payment. The active involvement of elders, persons with disabilities, and children contributes to the identification of shelter locations and the mapping of potential risks, maintaining a balanced 50:50 gender ratio. Coordination and the use of the 4W dashboard aid in the identification of vulnerable shelters.
Addressing protection and safety concerns involves careful selection of shelter locations resilient to disasters, maintaining proper distance between shelters for drainage, collaborating with other actors on protection issues, implementing accessibility features for persons with disabilities, and strictly prohibiting child labor.
Preventing Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) is a priority, adhering to IASC and UN regulations through staff training and coordination with PSEA networks. Regular communication with the SCCCM sector, meeting attendance, and training workshops are planned to ensure comprehensive PSEA integration.
Communication with beneficiaries is prioritized, and the project strives to include them actively in the implementation process. Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) materials are to be produced, utilizing branded tarpaulins, and establishing a functional Complaints and Response Mechanism (CRM). Feedback channels, including written texts, phone calls, SMS, and face-to-face discussions, maintain the confidentiality of feedback senders.
Capacity building for beneficiaries will concentrate on shelter and protection, fostering a sense of ownership and empowerment within the community. The involvement of government stakeholders throughout the project aims to strengthen collaboration with NGOs. The project aligns with a functional environmental policy, promoting sustainability through practices such as high daylight consumption, resource recycling, discouragement of deforestation, and reduction of CO2 emissions.
In summary, the project's overarching goal is to create secure and dignified living conditions, catering to the unique needs and vulnerabilities of both the FDMN and HC populations.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

In the October 2023 J-MSNA report, distinct disparities in priority needs emerged between host community households and refugee households. Host community households exhibited a higher variance in priority needs, while refugee households tended to converge. Nevertheless, both groups consistently identified food and shelter materials/upgrades as crucial priorities. Breaking down the first priority needs, 14% of host community and 9% of FDMN expressed the need for shelter materials/upgrades. In the second priority needs category, 12% of host community and 28% of FDMN highlighted shelter materials/upgrades. Regarding humanitarian assistance, 3% of host community households and a significantly higher 32% of FDMN households reported receiving various types of assistance in the 12 months before data collection. Shelter types significantly impact living conditions. In the host community, 51% have "Jhuprie" houses constructed with earth, bamboo, wood, and CGI sheets or thatch, while 41% of FDMN households have "Kutcha" shelters made of branches, bags, tarpaulin, jute, etc. Among FDMN households not improving their shelters (41%), the majority cited a lack of humanitarian support (60%) or insufficient support (34%). Commonly, refugee households in camps 20 (59%), 18 (58%), 8W (56%), and 4 (55%) reported these reasons for not improving their shelters. For the 44% of host community households not improving their shelters, reasons included not being needed (45%) or lacking funds (43%). Safety concerns were evident, with 15% of the host community feeling unsafe walking alone at night, contrasting with a more pronounced sentiment among the FDMN community at 42%. Some households mentioned paying for guards at night as an expenditure. Top reasons for shelters not being fully functional for sleeping included rainwater leaking in the sleeping area for both host community (87%) and FDMN (69%). Insufficient space was also notable (42%) among FDMN households. Concerning cooking facilities, 93% of FDMN households reported rainwater leaking in the kitchen area, compared to 66% in the host community. Lack of access to cooking facilities was reported by 34% of FDMN households. In terms of electricity, 90% of FDMN households reported issues with intermittent and insufficient electricity, while 66% of the host community faced the same issue. Additionally, 35% of the host community reported having no electricity and no solar lamp. Despite challenges, 54% of host community households expressed a need for solar lamps/panels, and 43% of FDMN households had similar needs. Regarding lighting sources, the majority of host community households used the electrical grid (95%), while FDMN households primarily used a solar home system (69%), followed by solar-powered lanterns or flashlights (11%), and electricity, including solar mini-grids (9%). Both population groups faced challenges with their shelters, with 72% of refugees and 60% of the host community reporting issues. Significantly, 56% of the host community and 59% of refugees reported having taken steps to improve their shelters over the 12 months preceding data collection. However, there were concerns about communication and feedback mechanisms within the refugee population. Only half of refugee households were reportedly aware of a feedback and complaint mechanism. Among those who made complaints, two-thirds reported that no action was taken in response, highlighting potential gaps in the feedback and response system within the refugee community. This underscores the importance of improving communication channels and addressing concerns promptly to enhance the effectiveness of humanitarian assistance.

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

Ensuring continuous funding throughout the year poses a significant challenge for us and fellow NGOs. GUSS maintains a robust presence in camp 1 (E and W), with essential facilities like health posts, a substantial number of shelters, and interventions in NFI, Food Security, and WASH. Other camps also benefit from our involvement in shelter, food, and WASH initiatives. To navigate these challenges, we commit to coordinated efforts with SCCCM sector focal points, providing regular updates through both in-person and virtual meetings, along with consistent reporting through updated 4W reports. While multi-year funding is an ideal solution for stability in refugee operations and improved relationships with beneficiaries, its feasibility depends on funding availability. We advocate for responsible handovers of project facilities and adhere to principles such as consulting with sectors/working groups, complying with technical standards and policies, being members of the PSEA network with nominated focal points, and participating in capacity-building sessions organized by NGO platforms. Our engagement is guided by technical expertise, proven geographical presence, and careful consideration of the needs assessed and approved by RRRC and the relevant sector, as we actively contribute to coordination meetings and share 4W reports with relevant sectors.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

The shelters we provide are meticulously designed for structural safety, utilizing materials resistant to rapid deterioration. Situated on sites shielded from disasters, these shelters are thoughtfully located for easy accessibility to water points, local markets, and essential services. Our commitment to safety, dignity, and harm prevention is paramount in our approach. We prioritize inclusivity by ensuring free access for all age groups in shelter construction, implementation, and monitoring. Engaging with beneficiaries is at the heart of our strategy, involving them in need assessments, site development, lightweight works, and shelter construction wherever feasible. For individuals with disabilities (PWD), our engagement extends to identifying and mapping potential risks and environmental disasters. Through collaborative efforts, we strive to create shelters that not only provide physical protection but also empower and cater to the unique needs of each beneficiary.

3.3 - Age & Disability

The shelters provided prioritize structural safety, resistance to rapid deterioration, and strategic locations safeguarded from disasters, ensuring accessibility to water points, local markets, and essential services, with a specific focus on the safety and dignity of all age groups, including persons with disabilities (PWD). We actively engage beneficiaries in need assessments, site development, lightweight works, and shelter construction, tailoring our approach to address potential risks and environmental disasters for PWD individuals. Gender considerations are integral, recognizing the social constructions of women, men, girls, and boys. In Cox's Bazar, where power structures are predominantly male-dominated, we aim to include women-headed families, widows, elder women, single mothers, and vulnerable male-headed families as beneficiaries. We are expecting to reach 10% of disabled people throughout the project. Women actively participate in project design, implementation, and monitoring, with one-on-one sessions addressing gender issues. The project advocates for the engagement of diverse age groups, including men, women, children, elders, PWD, and LGBTI individuals, in need assessments, shelter construction, lightweight works, and site development. Special focus groups, such as FGDs, are conducted to address unique needs. Shelter locations are chosen for safety, accessibility, proximity to playgrounds, local markets, and child-friendly spaces, adhering strictly to child safeguarding policies. Ongoing training and orientation ensure staff awareness and adherence to child safeguarding principles, with visual reminders onsite to promote awareness.

3.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

Ensuring Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) stands as a fundamental prerequisite for the success of the GUSS initiative. By fostering accountability, we empower beneficiaries to take ownership of the project, ultimately enhancing the overall quality of the program. To address concerns and gather valuable feedback from beneficiaries, we have established a robust Complaints and Feedback Mechanism (CFM). Our dedicated CFM personnel are actively present at project sites, facilitating direct communication through face-to-face interactions, phone calls, SMS, and written correspondence with project staff and designated CFRM/Monitoring Focal points. It is to be noted that, a need assessment will be collected from the camp in consultation with beneficiaries before the design of the project. Following this, GUSS will disseminate an active information sharing plan with beneficiaries through its health post located in Camp 1 (E) where information of assistance will be kept. A CFRM box will be set up to collect complaints and feedback from the beneficiaries. These individuals play a crucial role in developing monitoring tools to assess program quality. Information disclosure follows our transparent Information Disclosure Policy, with IEC materials sharing details of assistance. Participation of affected individuals is ensured throughout the project life cycle, adhering to inclusive principles in design, implementation, and monitoring. Engaging beneficiaries in need assessments, shelter construction, site development, and various project stages is integral, complemented by satisfaction surveys to continually improve our efforts.

3.5.1 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

Global Unnayan Seba Sangstha recognizes the paramount importance of Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) Mainstreaming in our shelter building and repair project for the Forcibly Displaced Myanmar Nationals (FDMN) and the host community in Ukhiya. We are committed to fostering a secure environment that upholds the dignity and safety of all individuals involved. Our approach includes comprehensive training programs for staff and community members to raise awareness about potential risks and preventive measures. We actively engage the community in project planning to ensure their voices are heard and protection concerns are integrated into the design. Robust reporting mechanisms will be established, coupled with a strict code of conduct, to address and prevent instances of sexual exploitation and abuse. By mainstreaming PSEA, Global Unnayan Seba Sangstha aims to contribute to the overall well-being and security of those we serve, fostering a project environment grounded in respect, safety, and protection.

3.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Our commitment to environmental sustainability is at the core of our project implementation. Ensuring zero harm to the environment, we pledge to preserve every tree and plant, safeguarding the natural landscape. Our initiatives include a comprehensive impact assessment, considering social, cultural, and environmental aspects. To fortify against natural disasters, we prioritize the use of high-quality treated bamboo, tarpaulin, and metal footing in construction. Site development activities aim to reduce vulnerability to land sliding, earthquakes, and other natural calamities. Anchored in a robust Environment Policy, we actively promote practices such as high daylight consumption, resource recycling, discouragement of deforestation, CO2 emission reduction, and environmental awareness, underscoring our unwavering commitment to sustainable practices. GUSS will comply guidance of the Energy and Environment Network (EEN) for Climate Action in Cox’s Bazar, Bangladesh adopted for the period of 2023-2025 and especially we will work to complement objective 2.1: The natural land and water resource base within the camps is maintained post-restoration and new site development and shelter works adopt nature-based and sustainable solutions.
