Project Summary

Rohingya camp: In 2024, UNICEF will continue effective treatment of severe acute malnutrition (SAM) and preventive nutrition services to reduce the risk of mortality and morbidity among children under five, pregnant and lactating women (PLW) across 26 integrated nutrition facilities (INFs) in 19 camps. UNICEF will also be responsible for nutrition supply chain management including procurement, distribution, and monitoring of supplies such as RUTF for SAM treatment in 14 UNHCR-supported camps. A total of 12,175 children with SAM (745,3 in UNICEF and 4,722 in UNHCR supported camps) will therefore be admitted and treated. Children identified with medical complications will be treated in stabilization centers, that will be integrated into primary healthcare centers in 2024. Nutrition interventions are also implemented at the community, and household level using trained community nutrition volunteers who conduct house visits for active case identification of children with SAM.
UNICEF’s nutrition universal premise is that prevention comes first, in all contexts. When efforts to prevent fall short, early detection and treatment of children with acute malnutrition is a must. UNICEF is strengthening preventive services especially to protect and promote appropriate maternal nutrition and infant and young child feeding (IYCF) and caring practices. The caregivers of children under two years of age are screened to identify needs for counseling and support by trained IYCF counselors.
Moreover, UNICEF supports community-based nutrition activities such as nutrition awareness sessions with mother-to-mother support groups, sensitizations, cooking demonstrations, and Mukhe Bhat sessions. Monthly growth monitoring and promotion (GMP) sessions are conducted in all the INFs. Community mobilization activities are conducted by trained volunteers including follow-up visits with an emphasis on absentees, defaulters, and non-responders from treatment. To further promote early stimulation and positive parenting, early childhood care and development (ECCD) activities are integrated targeting children 0-3 years old are integrated into the INFs. Iron folic supplementation (IFA) and nutrition education are provided to pregnant women and the adolescent for the prevention of anemia. Furthermore, homestead gardening is promoted to increase access to diversified and nutritious diets.
The existing Emergency Preparedness Response Plan (EPRP) will be revised to reflect the current needs, and partners trained to tackle any unanticipated weather incidents including fire outbreaks. All nutrition centers will be equipped with disability-friendly facilities including access during monsoon. The service providers will be trained on disability inclusion, child safeguarding, the prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse and messages displayed in the INFs. Furthermore, the existing comprehensive complaint and feedback mechanism and referral linkage will continue to be strengthened by the agency as per approved mapping in the camps.
Host Community: To ensure quality nutrition services, Upazila nutrition supervisors, counselors deployed in Upazila health complexes and the union health and family welfare centers as well as frontline service providers will be trained, and essential nutrition supplies provided for the prevention and treatment of malnutrition. Community engagement activities include support for mother/caregivers on improving the quality of complementary foods through cooking demonstrations and Mukhe bhaat ceremonies. Furthermore, UNICEF will continue collaborating with the Department of Agricultural Extension (DAE), the Department of Livestock Services (DLS), and local NGOs to build the capacities of households on homestead gardening and poultry rearing for improved access to diversified diets.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

Refugee Camp: Over the past five years (2017 – 2022) global acute malnutrition (GAM) in the Rohingya refugee camps has reduced by 6.9 percent from 19.3 percent in 2017 to 12.4 percent in 2022. While this progress has been, the GAM prevalence of 12.4 percent among children under five years is considered high according to WHO-UNICEF standard thresholds. Furthermore, the prevalence of SAM is at 0.7 percent and that of stunting remains very high (over 30 percent). Similarly, nearly half of children under five years are anemic (50.5 percent) and it is significantly higher among younger children aged under 2 years (72.8 percent). In addition, 40.3 percent of women of reproductive age are anemic. The main drivers of undernutrition among the Rohingya population are inadequate infant and young child feeding and caring practices (IYCF), low household food diversity, vulnerability to diseases and infections such as diarrhea and fever, inadequate access to water, sanitation, and hygiene, and mother’s age, early marriage, and poor childbirth spacing. (SMART/SENS, Link NCA 2017-2022)On IYCF, only 62.3 percent of children under five are exclusively breastfed and one out of three children aged 6 to 23 months are consuming 5 out of 8 recommended food groups (IYCF survey 2022). Overall vulnerability levels among the Rohingyas are alarmingly high. With limited access to regular income and livelihood opportunities, 97 percent of households are vulnerable and depend on humanitarian assistance and 78 percent (increasing from 44 percent in 2022) have insufficient food consumption (mini REVA, WFP, May-June 2023). Moreover, one in five households (19 percent) were reportedly not using an improved water source as their main source of drinking water, and not all households perceived always having had access to enough water. Nearly four in ten households reported problems related to latrines (J-MSNA-2022). The General Food Assistance (GFA) provided by WFP to the Rohingyas was initially set at $12 per person equivalent to 2100 Kcal and was reduced to $10 in March 2023 and to $8 in June 2023. These GFA cuts are anticipated to have negative consequences on the nutritional status of the most vulnerable populations within the Rohingya refugee community, particularly children under five years of age and pregnant and lactating women (PLW). Host Community: The overall GAM prevalence among children aged 6-59 months is 9.3 percent (Ukhiya-9.9 percent, Teknaf-8.9 percent), falling within the "Medium" public health emergency category but close to the "High" threshold. The overall SAM prevalence is 0.7 percent, with 0.9 percent in Ukhiya and 0.5 percent in Teknaf. The overall stunting prevalence among children aged 6-59 months is 23.7 percent with 20.7 percent in Ukhiya and 25.8 percent in Teknaf. This prevalence of stunting is categorized as "High" according to WHO/UNICEF classification. (SMART Survey 2021)Diets for children aged 6 to 23 months are very poor where only 26 percent consume nutrient-dense diversified foods in the right amount and frequency (IYCF survey 2022). Furthermore, Cox’s Bazar district has been experiencing an increase in food insecurity (from 21 percent in 2019 to 34 percent in 2022) and hence increasing the risk to undernutrition (REVA-6, 2022)The nutrition situation in both Rohingya camps and host communities is of great concern and may lead to growth, developmental, and health consequences if not addressed. For example, children with severe wasting are 11 times more likely to die than non-wasted children, stunted children have 5.2 times lower average IQ than non-stunted children, 35 percent of the overall disease burden in children under five years is due to malnutrition and maternal short stature and iron deficiency anaemia increases the risk of death of the mother at delivery accounting for at least 20 percent of maternal mortality . (Lancet, 2013).

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

The project will follow the principle of the rationalization exercise carried out by the Nutrition Sector. To avoid duplication and optimize resources, one implementing partner (IP) will be the only nutrition services provider offered through INFs located in each of the targeted camps and supported by UNICEF and WFP. For the implementation of CMAM and IYCF activities, costs will be shared equally between UNICEF and WFP. To implement the activities, the selected IP will maintain good communication and effective collaboration with different agencies and other local, national, and international organizations. Linkages with other sectors such as Health, Food Security, WASH, Education and Child protection will be ensured and referral paths will be established. Furthermore, UNICEF and its IPs will work very closely with Health Sector to ensure the referral of SAM children with complications and without complications and the promotion of optimal IYCF among pregnant and lactation women visiting health facilities. Moreover, as the lead agency in the sector, UNICEF will actively participate in key nutrition sector meetings, including TWGs, SAG, and others, providing valuable technical insights.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

UNICEF will work with children, families, and communities to prevent and address a wide range of protection issues especially focusing on children through mainstreaming protection across all components. All staff will receive training on the prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA), gender-based violence (GBV), and child protection. UNICEF will work to strengthen protection systems to prevent and respond to abuse and exploitation in humanitarian situations, including by supporting families, equipping community-based groups to monitor child protection concerns, and strengthening the social service workforce and case management services. UNICEF will also address gender-based violence by ensuring quality, multi-sectoral response services are accessible for all survivors of gender-based violence and improving the safety and resilience of women and girls, including by identifying and mitigating GBV risks across all sectors. Linkage and referral pathways between nutrition and protection in the camps will be established to access services in an appropriate manner. Besides that, UNICEF will promote mental health and psychosocial support, by providing community-led, multi-layered services focused on mobilizing family, social, and community support and strengthening MHPSS systems

3.3 - Age & Disability

Diversity and Inclusion is fundamental to achieving UNICEF's vision. UNICEF is for every child without discrimination whatever their ethnicity, sex, religion, language, abilities, or any other status. UNICEF will ensure that sex-, age-, disability- and other context-specific disaggregated data are collected and analyzed to monitor service accessibility and make changes to ensure everyone is included. UNICEF will also ensure humanitarian responses are based on a gender and diversity analysis and recognize and respond to the specific vulnerabilities due to diversity and gender norms and cultural practices. Nutrition project beneficiaries will be screened to identify disabilities and provide a higher level of care if required through establishing a cross-referral pathway among health, nutrition and other relevant sector.

3.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

UNICEF will reinforce the complaints response mechanism at all INFs and communities in line with core humanitarian standards (CHS). AAP sessions will be integrated in all the training curriculums, community sessions, and sensitization meetings to ensure knowledge of AAP principles. Each INF will maintain a designated helpdesk where beneficiaries can make a complaint, seek information, or share their views on topics of concern. Moreover, UNICEF will continue to train its implementing partners on Accountability to Affected Population (AAP) principles to ensure the availability of a functional, efficient and accessible complaints system. UNICEF will ensure that partners establish feedback and complaints mechanisms in all the centers with a clear plan to address any issues that arise in an integrated manner. Additionally, female Rohingya volunteers will approach mothers/women for feedback, and a hotline number will be posted at INFs through which mother/women can provide feedback anonymously. Complaints and concerns related to service provision will be addressed promptly. Furthermore, UNICEF will ensure that its implementing partners demonstrate their capacity to analyze the feedback routinely and integrate AAP aspects into the design and implementation of their activities.

3.5.1 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

UNICEF will ensure that the right of all children are protected, nurtured and free from all forms of violence, abuse, neglect, maltreatment, and exploitation as set out in the Convention on the Rights of the Child. This will be ensured by enforcing Child Safeguarding /Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) measures which must be adhered to by all UNICEF and partner staff. There will be zero-tolerance for violation of this. UNICEF will ensure that all frontline workers will be trained on gender equality and PSEA. Any PSEA-related concerns found in the complaints boxes or brought to the help desks at the INFs, will immediately be referred to the PSEA network and/or protection sector for prompt action. To ensure that partners will follow their approved PSEA action plans, UNICEF will follow up the implementation of the action plan systematically and timely through regular field monitoring visits and required reporting. Influential community and religious leaders will receive gender sensitization messages related to health and nutrition through awareness sessions to challenge harmful gender norms that inhibit adolescent girls and women to access nutrition services and taking action.

3.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Yes.Cox’s Bazar District is vulnerable to natural disasters, including cyclones, landslides and flooding during the monsoon season and fire during the dry season. To mitigate this, UNICEF and partners will employ disaster risk reduction measures as guided by a Multi-hazard Preparedness and Response Plan. This includes the pre-positioning of a buffer stock of supplies and medicines in the nutrition facilities for rapid deployment in the event of an emergency due to floods and cyclones. UNICEF is also dedicated to mitigating the adverse environmental effects of certain aspects of its nutrition programs by raising awareness and providing training to nutrition staff and volunteers on climate change principles. Emphasis will be placed on the importance of proper disposal of nutrition packaging (i.e RUTP empty sachets) to minimize pollution and prevent improper disposal within the camps.
