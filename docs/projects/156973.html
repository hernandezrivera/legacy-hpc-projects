Needs

According to the HNO 2019 sector overview, 8 years into the crisis, the main drivers to food insecurity are prolonged displacements, loss and lack of livelihoods and reduced production capacity which in turn resulted in limited physical and financial access to food, high prices and inflation contributing to reduced purchasing power and continuous depletion of livelihoods assets of the most vulnerable populations. As per HNO 2019, 6.5 million people in Syria are food insecure, while 2.5 million people are at risk of becoming acutely food insecure. A monthly ration with staple items costs at least 80 percent of an unskilled laborer’s monthly salary and 50-80 percent of a public service employee’s monthly salary thus demonstrating the existence of “working poor” in Syria. With an estimated 83% to 89% of Syrians living below poverty lines, and with a cumulative GDP loss of $226 billion, between 2011-2016 out of which $16 billion are in losses and damages attributable to the agriculture sector alone, the relationship between poverty and food security is inseparable. According to WFP's Syria Snapshot, food production has hit an all time low, according to the latest Crop and Food Security Assessment Mission. The worst drought in 30 years has affected cereal production in the 2017/18 winter season. There is a shortfall of wheat to meet domestic requirements (food use) of 3.4 million metric tons. Drought, increased cost of agricultural inputs including fuel for irrigation, and disruption to value chains means that people are more vulnerable to shocks and stresses and more likely to resort to negative coping mechanisms. Recent post project monitoring data has shown that vulnerable individuals have sold agricultural assets including trucks (to bring products to market) and livestock. Farmers are forced to leave their arable lands due to increased prices of agricultural inputs and the lack of access to farmland, while keeping animals is becoming ever more difficult and costly, forcing herding families to sell or slaughter their sheep, goats and poultry. Insufficient food production, combined with reduced government subsidies and currency depreciation, has led to continuous and sharp increases in food prices since the beginning of the crisis. Even when individuals harvest and sell surplus product, profit is low since agricultural inputs increase in price but product selling prices remain low due to widespread poverty. Most participants did not report earning enough income to meet their monthly basic needs. Women have particular barriers to income as reported by multiple assessments by the IRC and other actors. While women are more active in the labor market now compared with before the war due to the need to supplement household income. 90% of women are unemployed (compared with 25% of men) and societal barriers to seeking employment outside the home, including transportation issues, still exist. Women more often rely on agricultural and livestock activities. Individuals report that the necessary farming inputs for them to restart their farming activities exist in the market, but they do not have the financial resources to access them. In a recent post distribution monitoring of cash for wheat input distribution, almost 80% of participants used the cash for agricultural cultivation. Sources:FAS - HRP 2019 Circulation IRC's project data (assessments, PDMs, etc.)

Implementation of activities

From the HRP 2019 FSS sector overview, under the sector’s objectives related to agriculture and livelihoods, the IRC's intervention focuses on quick impact responses through appropriate cash for agricultural inputs or agricultural inputs at household level to promote the value chain between production, processing and markets. It is expected that inputs in rural areas will facilitate greater availability of food in markets in order to stabilize food prices. Livelihood and agricultural activities aim to restore and strengthen productive assets and create livelihood opportunities for the targeted populations, thus contributing to the overall food production and subsequently to food availability in Syria." Further, " both food and agriculture/livelihoods assistance as contributing to all three SO's as both aspects are lifesaving, life sustaining, reduce negative coping strategies thus contributing to protection, while having a strong resilience approach by contributing to the reduction in dependencies. Sector’s integration strategy further improves the cross sectorial linkages and promote early recovery." As IRC's activities aim to support livelihoods income and asset generation of targeted households, cash and livelihoods assistance help vulnerable households meet their shelter and NFI needs with links to the early recovery sector by helping households shift from assistance to self reliance without jeopardizing their food security. This is aligned with resilience oriented programming and early recovery to address humanitarian needs and reduce dependencies on assistance. Activity 1: Cash for Agricultural Inputs and in kind agricultural distribution including seeds, fertilizer, livestock fodder and irrigation to vulnerable rural households. Unconditional cash or in kind items will be available for small and medium sized farming operations, as well as household/kitchen gardens (typically managed by women). Distributions will target critical planting and harvesting periods of the year. Follow up cash distributions for irrigation will be provided to households which received cash for seed/planting inputs to ensure they are able to successfully plant as well as harvest their crop. All activities will be based on market assessments. Ongoing price monitoring will measure any project impact on local markets. Vulnerable households and individuals will be targeted, including PwD, female headed households, economically vulnerable households, and the elderly. Activity 2: Vaccination Campaigns. The IRC will provide veterinary extension service vaccination campaigns targeting livestock (cattle) with two or three different types of vaccines, in collaboration and coordination with other actors. Based on a risk analysis and learnings from a similar project in 2017, the campaign will target all livestock in targeted locations

Protection Risk Analysis

Based on the 2017 Do No Harm analysis workshop for the Syria Resilience Consortium’s North hub, as well as the IRC’s internal project design risk analyses, the IRC has identified the following protection risks and mitigation strategies: 1. Individuals are marginalized due to self exclusion from projects; for example, people with disabilities assume they cannot participate and therefore do not register with agricultural projects. In order to mitigate this issue, the IRC will ensure access and inclusion for all by improving project announcements within the community to detail that, for example, people with disabilities (PwD) will be supported to participate. Announcements will be both via posters and also verbally to ensure messaging reaches as many people as necessary, adjusted for people who are hard of hearing or sight. Outreach staff will spread the messaging further. IRC staff will be trained on inclusive livelihoods methodologies. Finally, by partnering with Handicap International (HI) which will provide inclusive livelihoods training to community members, the IRC will encourage PwDs to enroll in all projects with HI staff following up and providing adaptive tools when needed to ensure their inclusion. This collaboration will ensure identification, safe access and inclusion of PwD in the below activities with case follow up provided by HI staff. 2. Cash distributions contribute to household dynamic shifts which may negatively affect women and contribute to violence in the home. To mitigate this concern, the IRC asks beneficiaries about household power shifts during post distribution/end line data collection but will also complement this data with planned research by the Syria Resilience Consortium that will investigate socio economic consequences of the conflict on gender, age, and development. The IRC will ensure a proportionate number of women, men, elderly and people with disabilities in post project evaluations to ensure feedback on these harms is captured to measure the potential issue and effect program design. The IRC ensures that female staff are on the agricultural team and that all types of community members, including women and people with disabilities, are included. The IRC has also designed integrated livelihoods programming that includes specializes protection components (case management, psychosocial support and cash for protection cases) and is seeking funding for this approach. 3. Risks identified in the Protection Risk Analysis (PRA) for objectives 2.1 and 2.2 include Community tension arising from the beneficiary selection and Diversion of assets. The IRC will seek to mitigate these risks by ensuring strong community buy in and support; including community members in the project design and beneficiary selection criteria; setting clear eligibility criteria and a transparent beneficiary selection process; clearly articulated expectations of beneficiaries and expected outcomes for beneficiaries participating; and closely monitoring all activities by an independent team. Finally, protection mainstreaming is included throughout IRC’s program cycle, which is aligned to the FSS recommended response package for a harmonization. The IRC's programmatic approach focuses on inclusive livelihoods which identifies and conducts activities to reach women, and uses gender appropriate targeting strategies. Further, IRC's vulnerability criteria prioritizes female headed households for support. The IRC’s proposed programming contributes to improving conflict affect individuals' resilience and capacity to respond to shocks and food security by providing agricultural support for men and women including PWDs.
