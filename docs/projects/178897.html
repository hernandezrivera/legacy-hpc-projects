Project Summary

NRC’s project will contribute to Education sector HRP objectives 1 and 2. NRC will deliver this project through a combination of direct, partner-led and hybrid modalities. Partner names have not been included in this submission for reasons of confidentiality.
The Education intervention forms part of NRC Syria’s integrated response. The Education team will continue working in close collaboration with the ICLA, Shelter/WASH, and Livelihoods and Food Security (LFS) programmes to enable conflict and displacement affected populations to meet their basic needs, enjoy their rights, and benefit from pathways to durable solutions. We must ensure pathways back to learning and integration into formal education and acknowledgement of previous learning through supportive policies (accreditation, certification, examinations, and documentation). NRC will respond to the diverse problems and needs faced by vulnerable children and youth across Syria through the delivery of its three-pronged Child Education Pathways (CEP) and Youth Education Pathways (YEP) programmes. Each response modality (Emergency, Transition, and Recovery) is designed to ensure that conflict and displacement-affected children and youth are provided with age and context-specific educational pathways. All three modalities have been designed to support all out of school children and youth in entering the formal education system and succeed in their learning pathways, prioritizing those that have been displaced by the conflict and are returning home from both inside and outside Syria. All three modalities include capacity development opportunities for teachers, trainers, facilitators and coaches and will be implemented in cooperation with the ICLA, Shelter/ WASH and LFS programmes and partners. The teacher training packages include NRC Better Learning Programme.
The target group of the Education Programme in Syria is defined as conflict and displacement-affected children (aged 6-17) and youth (aged 15-18), including Syrian IDPs, IDP and refugee returnees, host community members, and Iraqi refugees. The exact target group definition will vary for the different response options:
• Emergency Response activities will target newly displaced and returnee children (6-
14years) and youth (15-18 years);
• Transitional Response will target out of school children and youth in contexts of long-term displacement and prolonged absence from school with activities tailored on their specifi c context (see more below);
• Recovery activities will target vulnerable populations living in post-confl ict and/or stabilised non-camp settings (see more below).
Teachers, facilitators and trainers will also be targeted across the 3 response options, working in NRC programmes in formal and non-formal education settings. For all NRC Education activities, civil documentation will not be a pre-requisite to receive assistance.

4 - Needs

10 years into the Syrian crisis, 7 million school-aged children and education personnel are in need of support. 2.6 million children are internally displaced and an estimated 2.45 million children aged 5 to 17 are out of school. It is estimated that the lack of education opportunities will cost Syria more than USD 11 billion in lost GDP. A cost that will be felt by the so called ‘lost generation. It will take decades for the economy to recover. The crisis has had a devastating impact, leaving a generation of children and youth unprotected, disconnected and unable to access their right to learn. 1 in 3 schools are destroyed or damaged. The conflict has forced young people to assume adult responsibilities due to the inability of households to meet basic needs. Instability continues to underpin crippling levels of poverty across the country, with 87% of the population living below the poverty line and unemployment at 55%. Compared to the overall Syrian unemployment rate, youth unemployment is significantly higher. Female youth reflect the highest rate of unemployment with 84% unemployed, and an already low labor force participation rate. 227,740 teachers and other education personnel are estimated to need assistance and over 140,000 teachers are no longer in service, leaving schools in Syria led and managed by unqualified teachers and understaffed. Improving teachers’ capacity is reported as the top priority for improving education services. The C-19 pandemic has increased the numbers of students out of school. The disruption to the formal education systems had a particularly dramatic impact on learning opportunities and well-being of children and young people already facing displacement.

5 - Implementation of activities

CEP & YEP Emergency: designed to provide safe/welcoming spaces for children/youth when newly displaced. Includes life skills, psychosocial & recreational skills & a space for connection. CEP-Transition: non formal learning opportunity for out-of-school children, preparing them to access education in formal settings, where available. SLP: A non-formal programme for children who have been out of school for over a year to catch up on lost learning. Unconditional Cash for Education will be used to respond to financial barriers to enrollment in formal education. CEP in Camps: A non-formal programme targeting children without access to formal schools in camp communities. YEP-Mobile Vocational Skills: 5-10 day competency-based training modules provide opportunity for unemployed male & female youth who have not completed basic education to develop new or strengthen existing skills identified through market assessments. Creating Welcoming Schools: Rehabilitation of learning spaces, teacher professional development, in kind materials, Student Councils & Parent Teacher Associations, NFI kits. YEP 3+3: participation in a 3-month competency-based training, followed by a 3-month coaching & mentoring programme, for unemployed male & female youth to develop knowledge, skills & attitudes for safe & sustainable livelihood options. CEP –Remedial: a 6 month programme for children who recently returned to school and are at risk of dropout. YEP Transition : a programme for youth who are preparing for grade 9 &12 exams. PSS activities integrated throughout all programs. NRC pathways are available for distance learning. M&E is an integral part of NRC, SOVs and indicators are standardized across hubs. Integrated programming approaches are used.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

Protection Risk (1): Exclusion of Children/Youth with disabilities who cannot access education centres and/or are unable to use relevant education tools. Likelihood: Medium Impact: High Mitigation Measures: 1) Learning facilities improved to ensure ease of access to PwD. 2) Staff and teachers are trained to be able to accommodate PwD. 3) Coordination with specialised actors and training to teams on inclusion. 4) Complaints/Feedback and Feedback mechanisms for students to voice their recommendations or concerns. Protection Risk (2): Children and Female Youth might be exposed to the risk of, or experience, physical harm, bullying or harassment by teachers, facilitators, staff or other students. Likelihood: Medium Impact: High Mitigation Measures:1) PSEA policies in place. 2) Staff and teachers aware of PSEA policies and Code of Conduct. 3) Children/Youth have access to reporting systems. 4) Field monitoring systems in place. 5) Children/Youth learning sessions divided based on different age groups. 6) Youth learning sessions divided as culturally appropriate. 7) Female Youth attend morning sessions. 8) Complaints/Feedback and Feedback mechanisms for students to voice their recommendations or concerns. Protection Risk (3): Learning facilities and surroundings, including sanitation facilities, might be structurally unsafe or do not provide sufficient protection. Likelihood: Medium Impact: High Mitigation Measures: 1) Facilities are improved by specialised technical staff (e.g. Shelter) to ensure that buildings and grounds are safe and protected. 2) Sufficient WASH facilities provided and maintained, gender and age segregated. 3) Complaints/Feedback and Feedback mechanisms for students.

7.1 - Please list any risks related to sexual exploitation and abuse that have been identified during the project design phase and any risk mitigation and prevention measures that have been adopted and built into the project

All NRC staff and non-staff are bound by NRC’s Code of Conduct, protection from sexual exploitation and abuse, and child safeguarding policies. Several sessions took place in 2020 to raise awareness of PSEA and child safeguarding to staff, in addition in sessions for contractors. All NRC teams have to complete online PSEA and safeguarding essentials e-courses as part of their induction. NRC participates in PSEA network meetings, regularly reports and shares information on good practice and challenges related to PSEA. NRC’s Complaints and Feedback Mechanism (CFM) is open and sensitive to SEA and child safeguarding reports, which are managed by a dedicated team in Head Office with support from regional and country office teams. In addition to inquiring during post-delivery monitoring (e.g. PDMs) whether people were asked to exchange anything for aid. Attempts by non-traditional humanitarian actors (e.g. local councils and authorities) to interfere in beneficiary selection and eligibility are reported and addressed as they come. In order to reduce perceptions of power, NRC asks who recipients of aid think provided the assistance, in order to identify possible areas of concern. Efforts in 2021 to strengthen child-friendly CFM have started and are expected to conclude by mid 2021. Core-competency risks: Education: Issues related to imbalance of power between students, parents and facilitators or staff.

8 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Threats to health, safety and security of staff, partners and beneficiaries linked to conflict or criminal activities: dedicated staff to follow-up and implement NRC Security policy, regular risk assessments, updated security and contingency plans, security and safety training for all staff. Limitations by local authorities preventing NRC to access areas and to independently select beneficiary or staff: networking with peer organisations to advocate for united policy positions, regular meeting with local key stakeholders to reinforce humanitarian principles, transparent recruitment policy and criteria for selection of beneficiaries, clear red lines identified, careful liaison work. Border closure: Market analysis for local procurement, Advocacy at UN level for humanitarian exemptions, Stockpiling of basic needs items when possible and relevant Fraud/Corruption/Bribery: Enforcing NRC’s policy of zero tolerance through strengthening of procurement and operating procedures, promote the “whistle-blowing” policy, corruption risk mapping, ensure partners implement anti-corruption measures. Loss of access in targeted areas due to context developments, legal restrictions or loss of acceptance: good understanding of context, contextual analysis, security plans and procedures, contingency planning, community acceptance & good relationship with local authorities. C-19 Restrictions: SOPs for safe implementation during C-19; adapted implementation modalities. NRC engages with education & camp authorities, parents, teachers, students/ youth, and other actors important to developing educational pathways. NRC organizes FGDs and workshops to collect feedback on curriculum from teachers to incorporate suggestions based on best practice.
