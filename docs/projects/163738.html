Project Summary

In all countries, PHC services are the backbone of preventive, promotive and curative health care. In Libya, strengthening PHC services is a priority. Current efforts to respond only to humanitarian health needs are yielding sub-optimal results because the health care system is weak and fragmented. Many of the leading causes of mortality and morbidity in Libya could be prevented or treated through a high-quality, people-centered health care system that pivots around PHC. According to international standards, three family physicians are required per 10 000 people. Currently, there are only 47 family physicians in Libya, almost all of whom are working in the private sector.
In December 2017, WHO began implementing a two-year project to improve access to PHC services in all regions of the country. The project, developed in collaboration with the Libyan Ministry of Health (MOH) and the United Kingdom’s Department for International Development (DfID) aims to build the capacity of PHC service providers and managers and thereby improve people’s access to quality PHC services. Three interventions were designed to build the managerial, service delivery and community-care capacities of the MOH. Six PHC centers (two each in south, west and east Libya) were selected to implement the project. In line with WHO’s Regional Framework for Action on Advancing Family Medicine, a comprehensive family medicine model was introduced in each center. A total of 716 health professionals (63% female and 37% male) were trained on the 13 core elements of introducing family practice into PHC. This training allowed each center to respond more effectively to the routine and humanitarian health care needs of host populations and internally displaced people (IDPs). WHO procured essential medicines and supplies and distributed them to all six centers. In parallel, WHO worked with the MOH to strengthen central and regional planning and management. Links were forged between the centers and the communities they served through a network of trained facilitators and volunteers who played an instrumental role in promoting the importance of integrated health care services within their communities.
Building on the accomplishments of the mentioned interventions and in line with the basic premise of humanitarian and development (H&D) nexus, the proposed project aims to strengthen MoH capacity at both developmental and humanitarian fronts involving strategic interventions at national and sub-national level through the following measures. The project will target 60 PHC facilities, (20 in each of the West, South and East region) across Libya. The selection of PHC facilities will be based on the findings of the recent assessment which was conducted by WHO in preparation for HRP 2020

1 - Needs

Libya is suffering from the impact of persistent political instability, insecurity, conflict and badly disrupted public sector services. Years of crisis and oil blockades have ravaged the economy and severely limited the capacity of the national authorities to provide health care and other public services. Over 800 000 Libyans – 12% of the population - need humanitarian health assistance. Primary health care (PHC) facilities and hospitals have been badly affected. An assessment conducted by WHO in 2017 showed that 18% of hospitals and 20% of primary health care (PHC) facilities were closed. There were acute shortages of medical specialists, midwives and nurses . There were huge gaps in coverage due to the uneven distribution of general internists, most of whom were working in urban areas. In many remote and hard-to-reach locations, poor and vulnerable communities had very limited access to health care. The health sector’s recent joint needs analysis, conducted in preparation for the 2020 Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP) for Libya, shows that, only 59% of PHC facilities are providing curative and preventive services for children under five years of age, 42 % of PHC facilities are providing NCD care and one third of the PHC facilities has none of 20 essential medicines. In addition, only 63% of hospitals have emergency services and only 52 % have capacity to perform major surgery. Only 9% of the hospitals have between 16-20 essential medicines in stock but have frequent stock outs of those medicines. Planning, managing and monitoring the delivery of health care services are seriously inadequate at all levels of the health system. PHC facilities are not required to provide a standard package of services or maintain essential medicines, equipment and laboratory services to support the delivery of high-quality care. In many facilities, doctors are either not available full time or they are young and inexperienced. There are frequent stock-outs of essential medicines and there are no electronic or paper medical records that allow different physicians to monitor individual patients over time. Many facilities remain open only three to four hours a day. Lastly, many PHC facilities are thought by patients to be unclean and unsanitary.As a result, Libyans tend to bypass PHC services and go directly to the outpatient clinics or emergency services in secondary or tertiary care hospitals in the belief that they are likely to be referred to these facilities anyway.

4 - Protection risk analysis

The most important precondition is a security situation that is stable enough to allow health care staff to implement and monitor programme activities and train essential medical staff to allow the project’s expansion to other facilities. A stable political and security situation is also essential to ensure the timely delivery of medical supplies under this project. Likewise, strong support from government and community leaders is needed to implement and expand services to address the needs of host and IDPs population. A further deterioration of the security situation and the eruption of armed conflict and internal strife among the various tribal leaders in the targeted areas may hamper the continuing functioning of the health facilities and hamper population access. In the event of changes in political control, UN agencies may be asked to leave the country or reduce their activities. The closure of main roads may prevent the timely organization of training events and the delivery of supplies to support project activities.Assumptions:• Medical staff are available and willing to work in the targeted districts• All the needed medicines and medical supplies arrive on time and the delivery and distribution process is not interrupted by various malicious acts• Health care facilities remain open and are not affected by attacks on health care • Target populations’ access to health care facilities is not hampered by the ongoing insecurity.• Community leaders are willing to support the project interventions.

5 - Mitigation measures

Contingency measures:If the conflict escalates and access to the project sites is compromised, supplies will be pre-positioned in Tripoli until such time as the roads re-open and the security situation allows for the safe delivery of supplies to project sites. Some of the important clinical trainings that require the involvement of WHO technical staff involvement from regional office or other offices in the region will be conducted in Tunis.
