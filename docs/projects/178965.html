Project Summary

Around 12.2 million people in Syria are in need of WASH assistance in 2021 (OCHA: HNO Summary, February 2021). The combination of impoverishment, poor water quality and reduced access to hygiene items would lead to increases in water-borne diseases, COVID-19, and child malnutrition. For years, both host communities and IDPs in NES have had limited access to WASH services, and this has been compounded by the COVID-19 pandemic. The limited water supply across in NES is a major obstacle to critical steps in prevention, such as handwashing, particularly for pregnant and lactating women and children. Sub-standard WASH facilities in IDP camps, health facilities, and in schools can expose children and their families to high risk of Acute Watery Diarrhea (AWD), lowering their immunity and ability to fight other infections, which may further contribute to malnutrition. WASH facilities lacking basic protection measures, including gender segregation, can increase the risk of GBV particularly for girls and women, and pose a risk to people living with disability as well.
Due to the needs and urgency of supporting already extremely vulnerable families, along with the negative impact of the COVID-19 pandemic coupled with inadequacy of WASH facilities, SC will continue to implement a multi-level approach, to ensure increased access to sufficient and clean water and sanitation facilities. The intervention will consider the specific needs and context-based perspective of children, men, and women through participative community consultation and mobilization.
SC will continue the “Hygiene 5” campaign, carried out by trained hygiene promoters, with a focus on five key messages: Hand-washing, consume safe water, use and maintain latrine, social distancing, use mask and avoid touching face. The messaging will also include the provision of information and learning opportunities regarding aspects of drainage, solid waste disposal and vector control.
In coordination with WASH WG and Water Department, the project team currently identify gaps in water supply service in the host communities and in the camps, and conduct technical feasibility studies to select community water systems (CWSs) for support (installment, repair or rehabilitation). The studies will continue to incorporate a lens of conflict sensitivity in the planning and implementation of emergency water supply activities in terms of source location, accessibility maximization in the community, consideration of any tensions between camp and community populations, etc.
During the implementation, SC will support existing or/and establish new water user committees, with appropriate representation from gender, people with disabilities, teachers, isolated community.
SC will make sure to conduct regular water quality monitoring in all targeted area and at all levels, including at the water source, water points, and user-level.
In line with the WASH Working Group’s guidance on the content, the above activities will be complemented by the distribution of COVID-19 kits. Additionally, SC will provide personal protective equipment to hospitals to ensure that protection measurements against COVID-19 at hospitals are in place, too.

4 - Needs

The Daily Emergency Needs Tracking by REACH in NWS highlighted that between November and December 2020, 4% of newly arrived IDP households did not have access to a sufficient quantity of drinking water, while 6% did not have access to a sufficient quantity of water for other purposes such as cooking, bathing, and washing. 21% reported the lack of functioning hand-washing facilities; and 4% of did not have access to a functioning toilet. Many of the respondents reported that soap and hygiene items are too expensive for newly arrived IDP families. A SC KAP survey conducted in mid-2020 reported that there was a severe gap in IDP camps in access to hygiene items primarily due to lack of purchasing power. General hygiene conditions are extremely low, especially for affected communities without safe shelter and sufficient water supply and sanitation facilities, leading to poor practices of personal hygiene. Without safe handwashing, the camp residents have limited ways to mitigate the risks of COVID-19 transmission.In North East Syria, only 38% of key informants reported access to piped water networks and 58% of surveyed household reported insufficient access to water (REACH: HSOS NES, February 2021). The limited water supply across in NES is a major obstacle to critical steps in prevention, such as handwashing. Sub-standard WASH facilities in IDP camps, health facilities, and in schools can expose children, families and communities to high risk of Acute Watery Diarrhea, lowering their immunity and ability to fight other infections. Open defecation poses a serious health risk for the IDPs and the host community. WASH facilities lacking basic protection measures, including gender segregation, can increase the risk of GBV.

5 - Implementation of activities

SC will work at three levels when conducting WASH activities:Community/institution level: Water trucking along with the consideration of sustainable water supply (where possible), clean-up activities in both communities and mobile institution including debris clearing and solid waste hauling to prevent harborages of and breeding sites for vectors, and disinfection.Household level: Installation of latrines for household clusters (4 latrines for 10-20 families), distribution of COVID-19 kits along with sensitization sessions on improved hygiene practices mainly targeting children and caregivers, with special attention to children living with disabilities.Individual level: Regular hygiene promotion in communities and at facilities (health care facilities/learning spaces/child-friendly spaces) in order to increase the knowledge on basic hygiene practices and mitigating the risk of COVID-19 transmission, and also establish and strengthen locally relevant ‘change agent’ groups such as child clubs and champions; child, mothers and caregivers peer-to-peer groups; community health workers etc.The integrated three level approach will enable SC to address the most urgent needs of the beneficiaries and at the same time reduce the risk of affected families to engage in negative coping mechanisms due to exacerbation of their financial capacity.

Mitigation measure: SC will provide 35 liter of clean drinking water/person/day through water trucking, or community water systems, in line with the Cluster standards. SC will support the WASH committees to ensure that the quality of water always meets the cluster standards by conducting regular water testing especially using ‘pool tester’ in water trucks, water taps, family size water containers, family-size water storages.Impact: High Likelihood: High Toilets present GBV and/or health, safety risk; Access to toilet is not available to all 24/7; Access to toilets not available for all. Mitigation measures: Women and children will be consulted on the design of all communal latrines to ensure the facility is adapted to their needs. The WASH team will take into account several physical and cultural factors.Impact: High Likelihood: High Water distribution not safe to access or not accessible, especially for women, girls (exposure to GBV risk) and children elderly and people with disabilities; inequality in distribution. Mitigation measures: All water tanks will be safe to use and accessible for men, women, boys and girls, including those living with disability. The locations will be identified through a consultation process with community members. This process of consultation will be led by the WASH committees. Social distancing measures will be considered. 7.1 - Please list any risks related to sexual exploitation and abuse that have been identified during the project design phase and any risk mitigation and prevention measures that have been adopted and built into the project

SC is invested in the Centrality of Protection and is developing an action plan that focuses on capacity building of staff and partners, embedding of conflict sensitivity and protection analysis, strengthening safe programming and child safeguarding approaches, improving risk management and integrating protection into advocacy work. In addition, SC integrates protection mainstreaming across its programs, and these principles guide the implementation team to ensure safe and quality programming. Building on the foundation of all staff members being trained on Child Safeguarding, protection against sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA) and Safe Programming, SC is also expanding capacity building of staff in conflict sensitivity, protection analysis and participatory approaches. A child safeguarding risk assessment, gender and GBV audit, and conflict sensitivity analysis are conducted for each program. These identify gaps, inform the project design, and mitigate protection risks for children and their families while accessing SC facilities and using SC services. In addition, SC has prioritized more sustained capacity-building for staff related to disability and disability inclusion, as well as on gender mainstreaming and GBV.

8 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Unstable security situation: conflict escalation, poor acceptance from local stakeholders and/or change in lines of control can prevent access to different program areas, either temporarily or permanently. Inappropriate project design can exacerbate tensions between communities and lead to unintended negative or harmful outcomes during implementation including suspension of activities. In order to manage and mitigate this risk, SC regularly monitors the local context, and SC employs security focal points. SC staff complete mandatory security trainings. Context-specific Safety and Security Management Plans are regularly reviewed and updated.Failure of money transfer mechanisms or change in banking regulation restricts movement of funds; and currency fluctuations: SC may be unable to deliver program activities due to lack of funds or program staff may be put at risk due to a lack of personal funds to hibernate or due to deterioration in local acceptance as a result of unpaid balances to other staff, suppliers etc. SC mitigates this risk by using multiple money transfer channels and agents who have strong reputations and have been able to deliver funds in almost all circumstances inside Syria. All SC agreements are denominated in donor currency.Risks due to the COVID-19 pandemic: The situation may result in risks to children and their caregivers due to lack of access to programming sites; further exposure to protection concerns, including violence; and health risks to beneficiaries and SC staff members. Relevant programme adaptations have been developed and are currently in place. SC has also introduced Standard Operation Procedures for staff working remotely and to identify COVID-19 focal points in each programme area.
