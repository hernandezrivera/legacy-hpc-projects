Project Summary

IRC’s proposed project aligns with JRP 2025 objectives and adheres to Interagency GBV minimum standards, addressing the protection needs of Rohingya refugees and host communities while reducing GBV risks in the short, medium, and long term, aiding recovery, and promoting resilience. The project will coordinate with other sectors, targeting vulnerable groups at risk of GBV, including women, youth, children, persons with disabilities, Gender-diverse communities and elderly.
IRC’s project will address the full cycle of GBV through:
a) Improving Quality and Timely Response Services: Implement a comprehensive GBV case management approach at 3 WGSS with integrated GBV and SRH services, 9 MPCCs offering integrated GBV, CP, legal counseling, women’s economic empowerment, and DRR services under one roof, and 6 nutrition facilities with GBV services. Integrated GBV case management will also be provided at 6 health facilities, ensuring coordinated, timely responses for survivors of all genders, including male and GDP survivors, and survivors of sexual violence. Project will enhance the quality, accessibility, and inclusiveness of survivor-centered services by linking PSS, medical care, safety, and legal services. Capacity-building components cover staff training on case management, GBVIMS, confidential referrals, MHPSS, supervision, and PSEA and GBV prevention approaches based on the capacity gap. GDP will receive an orientation on leadership diversity and inclusion. Project will distribute dignity kits and lifesaving assistance to at-risk women and girls, including GBV survivors and Solar lights to elderly individuals, girls, young women, and PWDs to reduce GBV risks. Specialized case management will support survivors of child sexual abuse, with GBV data managed through GBVIMS+/Primero. IRC’s “Women Rise” curriculum will provide structured PSS to women and girls, fostering resilience in GBV response efforts, including during climate crises.
b) Promoting GBV Risk Mitigation and PSEA: Implement a community-based approach to GBV risk mitigation, focusing on capacity-building for women and girls through (1) community engagement, (2) IRC’s system-wide approach, and (3) cross-sectoral coordination. 3 community-based groups in each location will be trained using IRC’s 'Learn to Lead' module to foster leadership and empowerment, while yearly safety audit reports will support GBV risk mapping, identifying risks, and developing & implementing action plans. District and national advocacy events will emphasize access to GBV services and support. Awareness sessions will cover GBV, PSEA, and available support services through the SBCC framework and content (audio and video). IRC’s 'Safe Space to Learn Digital Literacy Curriculum' will train women and girls on online safety. Community volunteers and non-GBV actors, including APBN and health staff will receive training on GBV and risk mitigation. Project will invest in AAP through community-based feedback mechanisms, SEA risk mitigation, and protection mainstreaming across sectors.
c) Preventing GBV by Shifting Social Norms: Project will implement evidence-based programs to transform social norms, tackle discrimination, and promote gender equality. Initiatives through the 'Safe at Home' module will reduce IPV and child abuse, while the 'EA$E' will address women’s economic empowerment ,'Girl Shine' and 'Early Marriage Curriculum' for at-risk girls and youth, as well as 'EMAP Plus' for men and boys to challenge norms perpetuating GBV. 'SASA! Together' approach will drive social change. IRC will engage religious leaders, youth, and community leaders in peacebuilding activities to promote coexistence within camps through dialogues and PSS sessions.
IRC leads the case management working group and coordinates with GBVSS, GBVIMS TT, GBV Prevention WG, GiHA WG, PSEA Network, AGD Working Group, and relevant government ministries (e.g., MoWC, DSS) to ensure harmonized and collaborative GBV services.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

Now in its eighth year, the protracted crisis has evolved into a protection crisis, compounded by climate change. Bangladesh currently hosts over 1,010,298 refugees, including new arrivals (51% women, 52% children, and 4% elderly) across 33 overcrowded camps in the Ukhiya and Teknaf subdistricts of Cox's Bazar District (GoB/UNHCR, October 2024). 95% of the Rohingya refugee population is moderately to highly vulnerable and remains entirely dependent on humanitarian assistance (REVA-6, June 2023). The crisis also impacts an estimated 568,298 host community people in the surrounding Teknaf and Ukhiya subdistricts. 2023 AGD Inclusion Needs Assessment found that 12% of refugees have a disability, a trend also reflected in the 2024 ISNA, where 11% of female and 13% of male respondents reported having at least one disability. Persons living with disabilities experience higher rates of abuse and violence due to dependency, isolation, and stigma (GBVIMS Q2 2024 factsheet). Women, children, and vulnerable groups, including persons with disabilities and gender-diverse populations, face heightened risks of GBV. Adolescent girls and female- or child-headed households are particularly vulnerable to harassment (GBVIMS Q2 2024 factsheet). These risks are exacerbated by overcrowded living conditions, a lack of safe spaces, and barriers to accessing services. Protection needs for these groups are often underreported and worsened by an increase in security-related incidents in the camps and host communities. The stigma surrounding GBV renders survivors voiceless and unable to seek remedy or redress. The 2024 Q2 GBVSS factsheet showed physical assault (50.6%) as the most common GBV type followed by emotional abuse (22.5%) and denial of resources (16.2%). Sexual violence made up 10.4% of incidents (5.8% rape, 4.6% sexual assault), slightly higher than Q1 (9.9%). Intimate partners perpetrated 74.3% of incidents in Q2, similar to Q1 (74.8%), reflecting a persistent cycle of violence among clients, even after accessing GBV services. Child/early and forced marriage remains a significant concern, though underreported to case management. Child sexual abuse cases continue, comprising 3.5% of GBV cases. Anecdotal evidence suggests child marriage is a coping mechanism for struggling families. While women and girls are disproportionately affected by GBV, studies show men and boys are also significantly impacted, including sexual violence (Women's Refugee Commission, 2023).The 2024 ISNA report revealed that food insecurity is a major factor contributing to all types of GBV. Additionally, 14.01% of households (3.3% female-headed, 9.98% male-headed) are cutting back on essential health, hygiene, and education expenditures, and selling household assets to meet basic needs, indicating economic strain. Furthermore, 46% of respondents reported increased safety concerns for women and girls in the camps, primarily related to sexual assault, harassment, and domestic violence in both public and private spaces. Delayed reporting of violence can significantly limit the effectiveness of support, increasing health risks such as STI infections, and unwanted pregnancies. ISNA data highlights the need for further integration of GBV and SRH services, both within GBV facilities and across all health programs. The 2024 ISNA report also revealed that 33% of camps households reported psychological distress, with 55% experiencing increased symptoms due to compromised security, including excessive worry (67%) and sleep difficulties (53%). Similarly, the 2023 J-MSNA report found 33% of host community households experiencing psychosocial distress, with 51% in households with a member with a disability. The camps face varied risks with limited preparedness: 19% of areas are prone to landslides, 8% of shelters face flood risk, and 97% of households are vulnerable to fires and cyclones.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

The project is firmly centered on protection mainstreaming, ensuring humanitarian interventions prioritize the safety, dignity, and rights of affected populations. By embedding a protection-focused approach, IRC aims to prevent, reduce, and address risks and vulnerabilities throughout Project Cycle Management. This approach involves designing and implementing activities that tackle protection risks and potential violations while addressing the structural factors that create vulnerabilities, amplifying the protective benefits of humanitarian aid. A key element is building the capacity of both IRC staff and external partners to adhere to protection principles, ensuring activities do no harm, promote safety and dignity, and enhance meaningful access, accountability, participation, and empowerment across all phases of the project. Protection Mainstreaming Action Plan (PMAP) will be incorporated into ongoing monitoring and evaluation, allowing for consistent assessment of activities, services, and outcomes through a protection lens. Regular PMAP reviews will track progress, adjust to emerging risks, and identify new opportunities, ensuring the project can proactively manage unintended impacts, mitigate negative outcomes, and strengthen positive effects. Grounded in a rights-based framework, this approach ensures programming is inclusive, reliable, and of high quality, considering age, gender, disability, cultural appropriateness, and specific community needs. The project will ensure safe, confidential feedback mechanisms. Therefore, IRC will use the PM Scorecard for periodic evaluations, ensuring remedial measures address gaps identified. Community consultations and participatory risk assessments will engage diverse groups—ensuring that interventions are inclusive and meet community-specific needs. Analysis of power dynamics and cultural norms will guide interventions to promote equity and strengthen community resources, focusing on including the most vulnerable people.

2.3 - Age & Disability

IRC is committed to inclusive, rights-based programming, prioritizing gender equality and social inclusion for clients' well-being and empowerment. By integrating AGD principles, IRC ensures all clients can fully participate in decision-making and exercise their rights equally. The project engages diverse identities, including persons with disabilities (PWD) and the elderly, to address unique risks and needs. Special attention is given to the safety of older women and adolescent girls, ensuring meaningful consultations and integrating safeguarding policies throughout each phase.Elderly individuals and PWD often face barriers, including misconceptions about disability and stigma. To address these, the project will apply Washington Group Questions (WGQs) for data collection, aiming for at least 3% PWD participation. Information will be accessible through audio, images, and multiple channels. Porter services and door-to-door support will be provided for elderly individuals and PWD, with priority queuing as needed. Accessibility audits will also be conducted at facilities and service points.IRC, with CDD, will deliver training on Disability Inclusion in Humanitarian Action, covering disability basics, inclusive communication, and guidelines for inclusive GBV prevention and response. Technical support will ensure accessibility at MPCCs and WGSS, adapting partner tools and materials. Age-sensitive psychosocial support (PSS) and referral mechanisms will be developed based on consultations to enhance access for all.IRC will integrate DI sessions in case worker training and programs like SAFE at Home trough ‘’Rainbow Within Us All’ inclusion module. Community awareness sessions will promote understanding of PWD and elderly individuals’ rights and needs, fostering inclusive practices. Dignity kits will be customized for PWD and elderly individuals, while CDD will provide assistive devices and conduct hearing and visual support camps at WGSS and MPCCs.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

IRC and its implementing partners are committed to a robust Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) Framework, aligned with the IASC’s AAP commitments. This framework ensures meaningful participation of affected populations, especially marginalized groups, in all phases of program design and implementation, reinforcing IRC’s dedication to client responsiveness and community engagement as core organizational priorities.The project incorporates an accessible Community Feedback and Response Mechanism (CFRM), allowing community members to provide feedback, raise complaints, and foster two-way communication between IRC, its partners, and the community. This CFRM is open to all, regardless of disability, sex, caste, age, race, or color, ensuring safe and equitable access. IRC’s primary goal is to understand community satisfaction, identify needs, and integrate these insights to improve service relevance and quality.IRC and partners maintain a Zero-Tolerance Policy on Sexual Exploitation, Abuse, and Harassment (PSEAH) and strong child safeguarding measures. IRC’s Code of Conduct and organizational agreements require implementing partners to adopt SOPs for capacity building, prevention, reporting, and response to PSEA, child protection, and CFRM policies. To support this, IRC will provide orientation and refresher training on PSEAH, CFRM, and child safeguarding policies for all staff, volunteers, and community steering groups to enhance awareness and utilization of feedback channels.IRC’s Accountability team promotes active community involvement through in-depth research, regular consultations, and frequent feedback collection (e.g., satisfaction surveys). These mechanisms ensure program relevance and adaptability based on community input. Feedback channels include boxes, emails, helplines, and in-person engagement, with focal points at each project location providing support, monitoring, and safe referrals as needed.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

IRC is committed to integrating PSEA into the Rohingya Response in Bangladesh by following a comprehensive framework, including the IRC’s safeguarding policy, the Core Humanitarian Standards, and the Minimum Operating Standards for PSEA (MOS-PSEA). IRC will ensure 100% staff, partners and stakeholders are informed of and comply with the organization’s codes of conduct and safeguarding policies. A rigorous hiring process will screen for any history of SEA, including self-declarations by candidates. The Senior Management Team will lead policy implementation, fostering a zero-tolerance culture with ongoing PSEA training. Dedicated Safeguarding staff and PSEA Focal Points will collaborate with the project team and PSEA Network to align PSEA. IRC will offer tailored, context-specific PSEA training for staff and partners, especially frontline workers, to build capacity in a “do no harm” approach and confidentiality. IRC will develop complaint and feedback mechanisms that are accessible, safe, and clear to beneficiaries at service points. A survivor-centered approach ensures survivors' safety, confidentiality, and access to support services without the obligation to formally report. Feedback mechanisms, including in-person reports, hotlines, and complaint boxes, will allow for reporting across genders and ages. IRC maintains a mandatory reporting policy for SEA involving minors under 18. As reporting SEA is mandatory, IRC is committed to notifying the donor, PSEA network and relevant stakeholders of any SEA concerns. High-quality investigations will be conducted to support survivors, hold perpetrators accountable, and strengthen prevention. Regular field visits, interviews, and focus groups will monitor implementation, share best practices, and improve program quality. IRC will also offer continuous technical support to implementing partners to reinforce PSEA standards and ensure PSEA awareness. PSEA activities are integrated into every phase of the project cycle.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

As the climate crisis intensifies, Rohingya refugees face heightened risks, worsening already vulnerable conditions. Limited access to Liquid Petroleum Gas (LPG) in camps forces many refugees, especially women and children, to gather firewood for cooking, accelerating deforestation and exposing them to significant GBV risks, including harassment, abuse, exploitation, and health hazards like respiratory issues and burns. Inadequate lighting around shelters, latrines, and pathways further endangers safety, raising risks of violence, including sexual assault, and the presence of armed groups, undermining community security.In response, IRC integrates climate adaptation and mitigation strategies to address these resource and safety challenges. Addressing the LPG shortage is a priority; IRC will conduct a protection analysis to support advocacy aimed at increasing sustainable energy provisions, reducing environmental degradation, and minimizing risks of violence and exploitation. IRC will also work with camp management, community volunteers, and other sectors to improve lighting in high-risk areas by advocating for solar lighting solutions that enhance safety while preserving the environment. Through community outreach, IRC will disseminate climate-related information, strengthening community awareness and resilience to climate impacts. To support an environmentally sustainable approach, IRC incorporates Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) and Disaster Risk Management (DRM) elements across operations, recognizing that climate-related vulnerabilities and protection risks are particularly severe for women, girls, and children.The IRC responds to climate-driven GBV risks by building local leadership and emergency preparedness through targeted training, gender-inclusive rapid needs assessments, and integrated disaster planning with GBV. IRC also conducts research to understand climate impacts on GBV, using findings to advocate for safer, accessible GBV services.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

IRC’s active engagement in the Protection Emergency Response Unit (PERU) and ISCG's Emergency Preparedness and Response Working Group and District Disaster Management Committee aims to address GBV and protection concerns during disasters, such as monsoons and cyclones by collaborating closely with the protection sector and IRC’s Emergency Preparedness and Response (EPR) team. This unit is dedicated to safeguarding vulnerable populations—especially women, girls, children, and persons with disabilities—through strengthened community-based groups, such as self-help groups, Women and Girls Safe Spaces (WGSS) committees, and community watch groups, which play a pivotal role in enhancing resilience and disaster preparedness.IRC’s approach integrates GBV risk mitigation directly into emergency preparedness efforts by empowering communities, particularly women and girls, to take an active role in disaster risk management. WGSS women’s committees and community-based groups will receive training in GBV risk mapping and mitigation during disasters, enabling them to engage actively with local disaster committees to ensure that GBV concerns are prioritized in emergency contexts. IRC builds the capacity of community-based groups and volunteers through targeted training, dissemination of multi-hazard awareness messages, and provision of essential emergency response equipment and personal protective gear, all of which contribute to a safer, more effective response. Activities will include fire safety assessments and simulation drills to ensure communities are well-prepared for emergencies.IRC emphasises business continuity planning, emergency response protocols, community preparedness, and capacity building by embedding Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) within GBV, Multi-Purpose Community Centers (MPCC), and WGSS initiatives. The provision of essential equipment is central to enhancing the safety and well-being of vulnerable individuals. Additionally, IRC’s collaboration with energy and environmental networks supports climate mitigation and adaptation efforts, reducing risks and promoting community resilience in the face of disasters.To ensure that GBV-related activities within the EPR plan align with national disaster management protocols, IRC will integrate ISCG’s Multi-Hazard Response Plan and standard operating procedures (SOPs) for emergencies into its activities. Data on GBV risks and vulnerabilities will be systematically collected, with outcomes evaluated to refine and improve response approaches continually. This ongoing commitment enables IRC to respond effectively to evolving protection challenges during disasters, ensuring safety, resilience, and dignity for affected communities.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

IRC and implementing partners will establish and support youth groups and safe spaces as central hubs for engaging young people in GBV prevention, mental health, and life skills development. Youth participants aged 15-24 will receive targeted training to lead peer support sessions where they can openly discuss challenges and build positive coping mechanisms. Through Girl Shine, older adolescents will be engaged in initiatives that promote protection from GBV and foster essential life skills. youth will also participate in psychosocial support sessions, focusing on conflict resolution, stress management, and leadership skills. As trained advocates, these youth groups will raise awareness within their communities on issues such as child marriage, intimate partner violence, and harmful social norms, motivating their peers and families to adopt healthier, more supportive behaviors. Youth volunteers will organize community outreach activities, including public information campaigns and interactive theater performances, to educate communities on GBV risks and protective strategies. Furthermore, the EMAP Plus program will engage both adolescent boys and girls in early GBV prevention, encouraging a collective approach to promoting healthier, more equitable communities.Youth-led (including women) initiatives will promote peacebuilding and conflict resolution through community dialogues and recreational activities that strengthen connections among young refugees and between refugees and host communities. By participating in climate change and disaster preparedness assessments, youth will gain valuable skills and contribute to resilience planning. The project will also offer targeted GBV mainstreaming training to youth, equipping them with knowledge on safety, dignity, and accountability in service delivery. The community outreach will support youth engagement by sharing relevant, life-saving information and providing channels for youth feedback.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

The project will foster self-reliance and resilience for refugees by combining GBV prevention and response with community-led efforts. A key focus will be strengthening community systems to mitigate GBV and enable survivors and at-risk individuals to access care. IRC will work with refugees to build capacity in community-based groups, empowering them to exercise their rights and engage youth in conflict resolution. Through GBV case management, PSS, and life skills and leadership training for women, girls, and vulnerable groups, the project will strengthen resilience, helping participants recover from trauma. It will also emphasize self-reliance through partnerships with the Livelihood and Food Security sectors. Refugees will gain skills to protect themselves, especially during climate crises. IRC’s feedback mechanisms and safety audits will foster accountability, empowering refugees to voice concerns and access timely support. The project will collaborate with ministries like MoWCA and DSS to ensure coordinated, survivor-centered services.

4.2 Do project activities in the host community link to development initiatives, and/or align with development priorities in the Cox’s Bazar district? If yes, briefly describe.If yes, briefly describe.

Yes, the project activities will align with development initiatives and district priorities in Cox’s Bazar. It will strengthen community-based systems and enhance government capacity to prevent GBV, with specific actions such as revitalizing Sexual Harassment Prevention and Child Marriage Prevention Committees at the upazila and union levels. These activities will directly support the district's development priorities by addressing GBV, child marriage, and social security. IRC’s capacity-building efforts with local government representatives, religious leaders, and key stakeholders will align with district goals to improve social cohesion and community resilience.Additionally, the project will support gender-responsive budgeting at national and local levels, engaging with government ministries and CSO coalitions to promote gender equality. IRC’s work on , case management, and coordination with local justice actors will also align with the district’s priorities of improving GBV response , justice services and reducing GBV risks.
