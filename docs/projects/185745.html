Project Summary

BRAC targets a total of 7,000 girls and boys aged 4 to 24 years in the host community and 28,600 girls and boys aged 3 to 24 years in the Rohingya camps of which at least 51% girls and 2% are children with disabilities.
In line with Education Sector Objective 1, BRAC will reconstruct 300 existing learning centers for the Rohingya learners to access quality education. To provide inclusive and quality learning opportunities 350 gender-segregated WASH facilities including MHM facilities will be set up for the adolescents. A total of 28,000 beneficiaries will have the access to use these. Besides, BRAC will open and operate 10 adolescent centres in the camps in which 600 adolescents, girls and youth have the access to basic literacy numeracy and life skill-based education training. BRAC will also continue to provide learning opportunities for the host community children through 190 pre-primary schools targeting 5,700 learners, lifelong learning opportunities for 300 adolescents and youth through 5 adolescent centers and 1,000 members from 3 multi-purpose community learning centers (MCLC). This will contribute to increasing the number of school-going children and developing income-generating skills among adolescents. Involving the host community children in different learning activities will help to ease the tensions and contribute to social cohesion between the communities. BRAC aims to identify and provide 400 differently-abled children with assistive devices as per their needs in both host and camp communities. All learning centers will be made disabled-friendly with support structures.
To contribute to Sector’s objective 2, as a part of alternative pathway for education, BRAC aims to train up 400 adolescents and youths in basic computer literacy and 300 children, adolescents and youths in performing fine arts in the host communities. Staffs and volunteers’ capacity development is also prioritized. For this BRAC aims to build up the capacity of 981 persons in the camps in Teachers’ basic training, refresher training, subject-based training in LCFA-GiEP, pedagogical training and training on Myanmar Curriculum Piloting, Teachers Learning Circle (TLC) and other capacity-building activities. For the host community teachers and staff BRAC will ensure the basic training, refreshers training, subject based training for the 190 preprimary teachers’, 5 adolescent leaders, 3 librarians of MCLC. In addition training on PSS, PSEA, and inclusiveness will be carried out for the teachers and volunteers.
In line with Sector’s objective 3, All (100%) of community stakeholders (SMC, LCMC, and other education committees) will be actively engaged in capacity building and mobilization activities in education response supporting inclusive education initiatives and promoting return to school/learning centres. BRAC will sensitize the community on different issues such as gender, environment, social issues, increase girls’ attendance, safe back to school, awareness raising on COVID-19, disaster risk reduction management, roles and responsibilities of LCMC members etc. to minimize pre-existing socio-cultural norms hindering children’s education. BRAC will emphasize rigorous monitoring and the Sector’s contingency plan to prepare for disaster and emergencies for man-made and natural disasters. For this BRAC plans to reconstruct 200 LCs among the 350 LCs to be made safe against disasters.
BRAC will coordinate with all stakeholders, including the RRRC office, associated government agencies and other implementing organizations for smooth implementation of project activities. For localization and smooth operation of the project, BRAC will involve the local implementing partners. They will be supported and monitored by BRAC according to the project and BRAC standard operating procedures.

1 - Identified Needs

Needs in Rohingya communities: Due to the extended lockdown period during COVID-19, learners were not able to access the learning centers in the camps and were supported with home-based learning. However, due to the limited movement in camps, it was not as effective as planned, since the staff and volunteers could attend. Further challenges have arisen also, e.g. children who had not been enrolled in pre-COVID period; marriage of girls and/or pregnancy; lack of guidance from learning facilitators, and children entering an age group whereby they have lost interest in education. According to the report of JMSNA 2021, the main concerns of education are: Children not having accessed home-based learning (44%), Children not having been enrolled in learning facilities pre-COVID (40%), Children not returning to learning (35%). The learning centers have opened in 2018. However, being of bamboo structure, their lifespan is about 2 years and most of BRAC learning centers were constructed in 2018 ie three years ago and have not been adequately maintained because of the lockdown during COVID-19 when there was limited access in the camp. These structures need to be reinforced to ensure safety of the learners now attending. 8% girls of age group 3-24 are still out of reach (JMSNA 2021), especially due to patriarchal attitudes and social stigma against girl’s education and the lack of menstrual-friendly WASH segregated facilities in the learning centers. Children with disabilities have additional barriers of accessibility (camp, road, and geographical terrain) and lack of inclusive teaching-learning materials and are less likely to attend learning facilities.Needs of host communities: The refugee crisis has had an adverse impact on the host community schools of Ukhiya and Teknaf upazila, where enrolment and retention are among the lowest in the country. Further, countrywide lockdown due to the pandemic directly impacted children’s retention or enrolment. The quality of education has also deteriorated due to community teachers seeking higher-paid jobs in the camps with the humanitarian agencies. 37% of households with school-aged (6-18) children (girls/boys) reported at least one school-aged child as not having been enrolled in formal schools pre-COVID. Though during the pandemic, learners were supported with home-based education, JMSNA 2021 finds that that 50% of households reported at least one school-aged child as not having regularly accessed home-based education. The community report the barriers include: inadequate technological devices, teaching materials and poor mobile networks. Other problems include children not being able to concentrate at home, and home-based learning has been perceived as ineffective. Besides this the proportion of children aged 15-18 that will reportedly be sent back to schools once they will re-open were slightly lower than the proportions of children aged 15-18 reported as having been enrolled pre-COVID, indicating that not all those that were previously enrolled will also be sent back. Households with persons with disabilities were significantly more likely than households without persons with disabilities to report at least one girl as not having been enrolled. They were also significantly more likely to report at least one child, and in particular girls, that will not be sent back. Reduction in income was the second most commonly reported reason – other than not having been enrolled pre-COVID for not sending children back to schools, and the most commonly reported problem dictating the decision of sending children back to school. The fear of infection with COVID-19 also remains a major concern. Moreover, almost one fifth are using unimproved sanitation facilities and there is need for gender segregated WASH facilities and this will also help raise awareness and the habit of using WASH facilities among the families as well as the community people.
