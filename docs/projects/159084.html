Project Summary

The project aims to provide children, adolescents, and their families in Gaza with mild to moderate and moderate to serious mental health problems with quality and effective mental health services and practical support. War Child Holland and partner will work with the Ministry of Health’s Community Mental Health Teams (CMHT), primary health care, and UNRWA and NGO health and mental health service providers to strengthen the protection and social safety net around children who have been adversely affected by chronic conflict and violence in Gaza. This will be done by developing a cadre of semi-professional MHPSS community support workers to support the CMHTs in providing quality mental health care and social support to children, adolescents, and families with mild to moderate and moderate to serious mental health problems.

1 - Needs

Chronic exposure to occupation-related violence and trauma, including large scale military assaults, the 12-year blockade, and maiming and killings of Great March of Return protesters are all contributing to increasing levels of high psychological distress, especially among children and adolescents in the Gaza Strip. According to the recent needs assessment for the HRP in Gaza (2019), over half of Gazan children may be affected by PTSD, and 68% of school children in areas close to the perimeter fence show indications of psychosocial distress. Reported symptoms of psychological distress of children and adolescents include uncontrollable fear, hopelessness, fatigue, depression, sleeplessness, shaking episodes, and crying episodes or enuresis; prevelant common mental health disorders include depressive and anxiety disorders, and generalized anxiety. The main drivers of poor mental health are poverty, unemployment and conflict, and can be further exacerbated by GBV, child abuse and neglect, bullying, social exclusion and loneliness, disability, parental mental illness (including post-natal depression), and chronic diseases. The need for MHPSS services for children and adults with moderate to serious mental health problems is rising in Gaza – with an estimated 22% of the population with moderate mental disorders and 9% with serious disorders, according to a recent study by WHO on the prevalence of moderate mental health disorders at any point of time in a conflict-affected population. However, for children and adults with moderate to serious mental health problems, there is a gap in services. The challenges facing Gaza’s mental health system are immense. Community Mental Health Teams (CMHTs) in Gaza are overloaded. According to the MOH 2018 Annual report, there has been a significant increase in the number of new MH cases registered every year at the CMHTs (16.5% in 2018 vs 1.2% in 2017). CMHTs have an average of 5,000 patients (3000 active and 2000 inactive) per team of 10 people, according to War Child meetings with the MoH Mental Health Department and visit to the Community MH Centre in Shujaieh. Each staff member carries an average of 300-400 clients, which is many times the average for more developed mental health systems, and is seriously affecting the quality of care. Given their capacity and the high needs, they are unable to follow up with patients in the community, and due to lack of resources and capacity are not able to reach patients living in more remote areas. This has resulted in unfortunate tragedies and loss including most recently a patient who starved to death due to lack of follow-up. The main treatment of choice is medication, not therapy, even though the supply of medication is disrupted. In such a ‘disease model’ services that can reach larger numbers, such as group work, self-help, peer support and mental health promotion is rare to non-existent. This project addresses the MHPSS needs of children and adults with mild to moderate and moderate to severe mental health problems. It fills gaps in service provision by developing a cadre of MHPSS community support officers to support the over-burdened CMHTs. Community MHPSS workers will be recruited, trained, and receive on the job coaching and mentoring to provide practical and focused outreach support and care to those with mild to moderate to severe mental health problems. This will reduce the caseload on CMHTs and allow them to focus their efforts on providing specialized mental health services to those in need. The gaps, needs, and project interventions were identified through War Child’s review of the community mental health services in 2019, including discussions with the MOH Mental Health Department and members of CMHTs; the UNICEF CPWG Review of the Humanitarian MHPSS Needs and gaps in the State of Palestine (2019); OCHA HNO 2019; and discussions with Health Cluster partners including the MOH, UNRWA, GCMHP, WHO, MDM Spain and France, and UNICEF.

2 - Activities

Under Health Cluster Objective 1, HRP Activity “Establish a multi-level support in MHPSS by engaging communities to include marginalized people, increased self-help and social support…”, War Child will develop a cadre of semi-professional "MHPSS community support officers" to support CMHTs by providing focused, non-specialized care to children and adults with mild to moderate and moderate to serious mental health problems, allowing CMHTs to focus their efforts on providing specialized mental health services. To accomplish this, the following activities include:1. Recruit and train 30 semi-professionals to support 3 community mental health teams (10 per team) A committee made up of War Child, partner NGO, and MoH will recruit and train 30 semi-professionals as "MHPSS community support officers" to provide full-time support to the CMHTs in in Gaza East, Deir El Baleh, and Rafah. A TOR, recruitment process, and selection criteria will be jointly drafted with the MOH. The recruitment committee will interview and select the candidates based upon the selection criteria, and in-line with War Child’s recruitment policies. Once selected, the MHPSS community officers will be trained by War Child and partner’s experts on low-intensity non-specialized MHPSS interventions for children, adolescents, and adults, which may include: MhGAP and mhGAP-HIG for children and adolescents and WHO’s PM+. They will also receive training on safe identification, support and referral for GBV and CP cases, child safeguarding, inclusion, and data protection and confidentiality. The community support officers will be integrated as part of the CMHT, reporting to designated staff, who will also be trained by War Child experts to supervise its group of officers. Supervisors will be responsible for the clinical and technical supervision of the workers, while War Child and its partner will be responsible for the administrative and logistical supervision. War Child expert consultants (international and local) and partner will provide continuous on-the-job training, coaching, mentoring and group supervision to the 3 community mental health teams on task-shifting to the MHPSS community support workers, case prioritization, and referrals back to primary health care. 2. Given the stigma of MH problems in the targeted communities, the project will raise awareness of the need to seek services and strengthen the role of community members and organizations in the protection of children. War Child and the CMHTs will hold sensitization workshops for local CBOs, community members, and stakeholders on MHPSS and child protection. This will include sessions on self-help, psychosocial wellbeing and support, protecting children from harm including abuse, neglect, and exploitation; child safeguarding; minimum standards for child protection (MSIP), and core principles of child protection (survival and development, best interest of the child, non-discrimination, participation, etc.). The role of the community workers will also be introduced at these workshops to ensure community buy-in, feedback, and engagement. 4. Individual and group counseling and support provided through outreach to children and adults by the MHPSS community officers. Each trained community support officer will provide outreach services in CBOs and homes including individual and group support to children and adult based on need. MHPSS community support officers will: consult with family and others on mental health and psychosocial needs; prioritize children and parents with ongoing problems; provide regular emotional and practical support; promote good family relations, activate social networks and community traditional supports; help people gain access to resources/allowances; provide information and health promotion; help identify early signs of more serious problems and those who need referral; coordinate with and refer when needed; and facilitate group peer support groups for children and adults.

3 - Indicators

War Child has an established global monitoring and reporting framework and uses standardized and globally validated means of verification to assess program effects on the wellbeing of participating children and families, and participant satisfaction. Through WCH’s monitoring and evaluation methodology, quality implementation of programming and delivery of care will be ensured. Regular field visits to and supervision of all activity sites will be conducted. Activities and interventions will be further refined and adapted to meet the specific needs of the communities through engagement of CBOs and community facilitators who will regularly assess needs and participant satisfaction through quarterly focus group discussions and using WCH tools for measuring participant satisfaction and through the establishment of a complaints and feedback mechanism with support from WCH. An end-of-project evaluation with participants through focus group discussions will also give children, caregivers, and community volunteers the opportunity to evaluate the various activities. Community-based and child-friendly complaints and feedback mechanisms will be established for all services, and participant satisfaction assessments using WCH tools and through quarterly focus group discussions with women, men, girls and boys will regularly inform the project of what’s working and what needs improvement and to be further tailored. War Child and partner team members will every month to analyse and reflect upon feedback and complaints received to ensure regular reflection on the issues raised, solutions proposed and who reported them help us to address problems, and feed into strategic decisions and help to the program as needed. 9 - Protection mainstreaming
9.1 - Do No Harm

WCH aims at being accountable to the children and families of this project. One key measure is the implementation of WCH Child Safeguarding Policy, along with a reporting mechanism. All our staff are trained on the policy upon signing their contract, and are supported to prevent harm to children we work with. WCH also commits to support local organizations, NGOs and partners to be aware of the importance of a child safeguarding policy and supports them to develop their own. Within WCH programs, children are regularly informed, at start of any project, of what they can expect from our organization and staff, as well as being provided with clear information on details about how they can give feedback and complain if they experience or witness misconduct by representatives of WCH. Staff and partners will also be trained on Safe identification and referral, and the new updated version of the CPMS Child Protection Minimum Standard and IASC MHPSS in emergencies.War Child also ensures adherence to information management and data protection protocols that uphold the privacy and confidentiality of cases. Access to reports and case files will be kept limited to the professionals working on the cases and other professional organizations whose work require seeing these reports. War Child will ensure the confidentiality through safety measures outlined in the information sharing agreement that will be developed and signed by the project partners, and War Child will have data protection protocol starting from the beginning of the project.

9.2 - Equal and impartial access to aid

The nature of this project is to ensure that the most vulnerable children and adolescents, with moderate to serious mental health problems, including those with disabilities, are able to access care and practical support. To ensure meaningful access vulnerable children and caregivers will also be identified through outreach (including home visits), awareness raising sessions in the community, and through War Child's already ongoing structured PSS programs and case management services. The MHPSS community support officers will be based in the community where we work, making it easier to identify vulnerable children and caregivers. Community support officers will also coordinate with the CPN officers in their governorates who oversee protection cases for children, including those with disabilities, to ensure that children are referred to relevant MHPSS interventions as needed. Coordination will also be closely done with the available services for children with disabilities provided by Health and Protection Cluster partners such as Humanity and Inclusion and governorate-based networks for persons with disabilities. Community support officers and project staff will also receive an orientation training by War Child on inclusion principles in order to skillfully adapt interventions to ensure inclusivity, including for children with disabilities. WCH strives that all programs should meet the eight criteria for meaningful participation. These criteria measure how children perceive the quality of their participation in our programs. This indicator is measured through a group exercise or survey, and the exercise introduces children to each standard for meaningful participation. Children share their opinions through an anonymous vote. The survey allows children to provide feedback on different aspects of each standard for meaningful participation.
