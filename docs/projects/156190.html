Project Summary

The purpose of this project is to reduce the specific vulnerability of pregnant and lactating women and children under five years through improved access to quality, safe and dignified Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, Child and Adolescence (RMNCH+A) services and Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) in three governorates in Syria (Al-Hassakeh, Rural Damascus, and Aleppo). Access to primary healthcare services, including RMNCH+A and MHPSS, is a key priority within the health sector where at least 5.2 million women of reproductive age need RMNCH+A services alone. In addition, recent evidence of 137 low and middle-income countries across the world suggests that the cost of neglecting maternal psychosocial risk factors is huge and that addressing psychosocial factors among perinatal women can have a significant impact on child growth and well-being (Fawzi, et al., 2019). Given the distance and safety and displacement risks and the disrupted services, many women were unable to seek medical support for their RMNCH+A needs. Besides, physical hardship and mental trauma often complicate the pregnancy and the delivery, which is also exacerbated by inadequate access to MHPSS. Ensuring a large and safe physical access to those services is key to reducing the risks on pregnant and lactating women and children under five years of age. Overall, there remains a lack of available, qualified and trained staff, inadequate medical referral systems to address the needs for women who develop complications or require emergency obstetric and newborn care and limited financial means for supporting health education and public health awareness about topics related to these services. Therefore, the project aims to improve access to quality healthcare by strengthening the local institutional primary healthcare capacity in which at least one primary healthcare centre will be rehabilitated, and at least one mobile medical service will be mobilized to reach 10,000 beneficiaries in pockets of vulnerable populations in Rural Damascus, Aleppo and Hassakeh. A total of 30 healthcare staff (of which 15 are males and 15 are females) will be trained and mentored on-the-job to ensure implementation of national protocols and guidelines consistent with quality and resilient primary healthcare delivery. Additionally, prevention and treatment of RMNCH+A and MHPSS conditions targeting PLWs and children under five are provided in the targeted public healthcare facilities and mobile facilities where specific support is strengthened to deliver RMNCH+A and MHPSS services and referral to specialized care for children and women with urgent cases will be strengthened.

Needs

The 2019 Humanitarian Needs Overview confirmed that throughout 2018, health needs in Syria continued to be exacerbated by access constraints, damage to health facilities, and insufficient human resources. 46% of hospitals and primary health facilities in Syria as either partially functional or not functional. The number of people who need health assistance in Syria is estimated to be 13,2 million, where females make up 72% of the PiN (People in Need). Vulnerable groups include 5.2 million women of reproductive age, 2.2 million children under 5 years old, and 2.3 million people aged over 50 as at this age vulnerability to non-communicable diseases becomes highest. Syria is home to at least 3 million disabled people, and 6,183,919 million of IDPs. 8% of people in need are currently residing in UN declared hard-to-reach areas. According to information collected on health needs with reliable Key Informants (KI) and at community level, in Hassakeh, the health care is top priority need throughout the assessed communities. In 93% of communities, KIs reported that some residents faced barriers to accessing healthcare services, with 82% reporting that there were no functioning medical facilities in the community. Furthermore, in 67% of the assessed communities, KIs stated that none of the assessed medical items were available in their community in April 2018. Most of the communities stated that chronic disease and acute respiratory infections and severe diseases affecting children under five were the most commonly reported health issues faced by community members. In Aleppo, 67% of the assessed communities reported that no health facilities were available in their community in April. Moreover, 67% of the assessed communities reported that women primarily delivered babies in facilities in nearby communities, and only 6% of the assessed communities reported having functioning pre-conflict hospitals. The top three most common health problems reported were severe diseases affecting those younger than 5 (59% of communities), chronic diseases (52%) and acute respiratory infections (47%). In Rural Damascus, KIs reported healthcare and protection as two of the top priority needs in the assessed communities throughout Rural Damascus. This is significant as 75% of the assessed communities reported facing barriers to accessing healthcare, most commonly due to security concerns when travelling to facilities. The top three most needed healthcare services reported were rehabilitation services (55%), antenatal care (45%), and psychosocial support (40%).

Implementation of activities

Improved access to quality, safe and dignified healthcare will be achieved by strengthening the local institutional primary healthcare capacity in which at least one primary healthcare centre will be rehabilitated, and one mobile medical service will be mobilized to reach 10,000 beneficiaries with primary healthcare services in pockets of vulnerable populations. Emphasis will be placed on RMNCH+A and MHPSS services. Specific activities will include: coordination with Ministry of Health, sector related stakeholders and relevant partners, equipment and supplies of primary healthcare facilities and mobile medical clinics, and capacity building for health workforce on quality primary healthcare service delivery. A total of 50 healthcare staff (of which 25 are males and 25 are females) will be trained and mentored on-the-job to ensure implementation of national protocols and guidelines consistent with quality and resilient primary healthcare delivery. Additionally, prevention and treatment of RMNCH+A and MHPSS conditions targeting PLWs and children under five are provided in the targeted public healthcare facilities and mobile facilities where specific support is strengthened to deliver RMNCH+A and MHPSS services and referral to specialized care for children and women with urgent cases will be strengthened.

Protection Risk Analysis

Based on AAH’s internal Security Guidelines, Standard Operations Procedures and long experience in the field, possible risks have been identified based on context analysis, inputs from field staff and daily monitoring and observations. The possibility of any risk is constantly updated according to evolving situation and developments at the central and Governorate levels. The analysis takes into consideration several stakeholders that might influence the context as well as the planning and implementation of activities. AAH has a protection mainstreaming policy that all staff in the field sites have been trained in, and each field site has a protection focal point who is responsible for conducting continuous refresher trainings for the staff. AAH also mobilizes the expertise of a regional protection officer and a dedicated health coordinator in Syria who are specifically trained in mainstreaming protection and safe programming in health to support continuous revision and proactive identification of risks involved in the implementation of this project. Key risks include: violence against health care, gender-based violence (GBV), including early and forced marriage, sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA), discrimination against vulnerable groups like pregnant and lactating women and/or children under five years of age, and inequitable access to health services. More specifically, when it comes to access, there are various risks involved, including: lack of official documentation or legal status for beneficiaries to receive healthcare, beneficiaries reporting feeling unsafe during the journey to public healthcare centers, especially women and girls who are travelling alone, and women reporting lack of privacy, feeling unsafe, and waiting for long waiting hours and experiencing health concerns while waiting are reluctant to seek the necessary medical services. There are a limited number of protection experts who are experienced in identifying and responding to GBV and SEA-related protection risks. This situation leads to serious challenges in the provision of information, ability to identify high-risk cases and provision of referrals and services. Mitigation measures include: • Address the capacity gap by training health staff in static and mobile health facilities on providing first-line support, identification of GBV cases, safe referrals, and ensuring access of affected population to the Minimum Initial Service Package (MISP) for reproductive health, including clinical management rape.• Create and ensure safe, private and secure spaces for women and children that include spaces for confidential examination, availability of adequate female health staff to attend to the specific needs of women and girls, considerations for socio-cultural, age-specific, disability-specific and condition-specific needs (especially for MHPSS interventions), differentiate prevention or treatment methods for men and women, to the extent possible, and ensure that both men (including boys) and women (including girls) will be involved in decision-making activities relevant to healthcare interventions. • Establish protection mechanisms in targeted healthcare facilities and mobile clinics, including case management, functional referral pathways, provision of psychosocial first aid and enough female health workforce.• Apply the designated de-confliction mechanism for health facilities, continuous reporting on attacks against targeted public healthcare facilities and mobile clinics, and sustained advocacy. • Establish effective community-based complaints mechanisms.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risk 1: The security situation in targeted areas is unstable and AAH has no access to implement the activities; there are additional unforeseen administrative procedures imposed, and restrictions on access to beneficiaries. Likelihood: medium. Impact: highMitigation: AAH works and coordinates with authorities and Ministries to maintain access to target areas and beneficiaries, and the capacity to implement activities. AAH will reach out to local authorities and line ministries with trust building activities to further develop relationships and capacity to avoid or mitigate restrictions in AAH capacity to operate in Northern and Southern Syria. Advocacy and coordination with the humanitarian community and humanitarian diplomacy activities on the local level are also considered to contribute to this mitigation measure. Risk 2: Substantive devaluation of the EURO or USD against the Syrian pound; International bank transfers to Syria are fluid and timely.Likelihood: medium. Impact: highMitigation: The eventuality of significant changes in the value of the Syrian currency will be closely monitored and, if materialized, timely communicated to donors and options discussed. Risk 3: Aid diversion, fraud, and corruptionLikelihood: medium. Impact: highMitigation: diversification of supplier, triangulation of information, standard monitoring procedures in place, security plan and risks assessments tables updated and followed. Risk 4: Information gathering activities hampered by suspicion or misunderstandingLikelihood: low. Impact: mediumMitigation: Transparency, trust-building, developing relationships with municipalities, district authorities and facilities managers
