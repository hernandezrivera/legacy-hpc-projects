Start Date

01/01/2023

Project Summary

To provide access to inclusive, quality, and protective basic education to vulnerable and marginalised children, Out of School Children and youths aged (3-18) in Akobo, Nyirol, and Bor South of Jonglei State; Kapoeta East of Eastern Equatoria, Rumbek East of Lakes and Tonj East in Warrap. The programme will implement interventions that lead to increased access to education for children. The programme will ensure that access to basic education meets the quality standards as set in the INEE minimum standards of Education in Emergencies. Activities will promote enrolment, retention and transition of children to the next school year while minimizing the possibility of dropouts. The learning infrastructure and the school environment will be disability friendly and gender sensitive to learners. School environments will provide intentional MHPSS activities and have clear referral pathways including to CP to ensure school environments are protective. Teachers will receive CPD and volunteer teachers incentivized. The project will target 49,870 total beneficiaries desegregated into 48,000 children and youths (26,790 B & 21,210 G), 1,122 teachers (617 M, & 505 W), 748 PTAs/SMCs (411 M, & 337 W); with 3% comprising of persons with disability, host communities and IDPs.
The project will aim at improving equitable access to safe and protective education services by enrolling 48,000 children and youths (26,790 B & 21,210 G) in pre-primary, primary and secondary schools and in learning centers. 30 Temporary Learning Spaces will be established, rehabilitation of 40 Temporary Learning Spaces, establishment of 20 gender segregated latrines, water points. Additionally, the project will be rehabilitating 25 gender segregated latrines and water points; and distributing or provide sanitation and hygiene materials to 108 schools (hand washing soap, sanitizers, masks) and PPE for students and teachers.
The project will improve the quality of education, gender and disability-inclusive education services and teachers' capacity to protect the wellbeing of all children by providing 4,128 (2,270 B & 1,858 G) emergency-affected children quality education recreational materials; providing teaching and learning materials for 96 schools in ECD, primary and secondary school education. The project will equally be conducting training 522 (304 M & 248 F) teachers on EiE pedagogical skills and child-centered methodologies to improve teaching and learning process for emergency-affected children; update 522 (304 M & 248 F) teachers and learners on PSS, CP, and referral mechanisms including CP/GBV, health and nutrition. Providing life-skills and basic vocational training to 1,000 (550 G & 450 G) youth and adolescents provide of dignity kits to 6,480 girls in schools; provide customized support to 840 young mothers in schools and to conduct 1-disability assessment. All learning centres will have their referral pathways strengthened or established, with teachers trained on how to identify and refer cases through a child helpdesk.
In order to strengthen/enhance the education system’s response capacity of the school community and national/local education actors the project will: capacity build 1,104 (607 M, & 497 W) SMCs/PTAs and education authorities on school management, governance, PSS and DRR. The project will support prevention and mitigation of communicable diseases through training school communities by providing appropriate Information and Education and Communication (IEC) materials (i.e. posters, leaflets); conducting 5 back to learning campaigns. SCI will implement directly and with national partners, supporting technical, operational and institutional capacity strengthening of 5 national actors.
The projects will ensure that the following will be considered in the project design and implementation: gender and social inclusion, feedback & accountability mechanisms, crisis modifiers, nexus programming and community-based sustainability measures.

1 - Needs

SSEC ranked and classified Akobo, Nyirol, Kapoeta East, Rumbek East and Tonj East as high risk areas that require urgent interventions (SSEC Prioritization, 2022). Children in South Sudan said hunger was forcing more young people to drop out of school, putting girls at risk of early marriage and sexual exploitation, while also forcing some boys into crime (Kapoeta Hunger Report, SCI 2021). Akobo is an area of great conflict and climate sensitivity; while Tonj East is composed of high numbers of IDPs (OOSC Report, 2018). In 2022, 3.6 million people, 97 per cent of whom are children are estimated to need education services. Following COVID19, the closure of schools and broader socio-economic hardship exacerbated existing education challenges and inequities. The current estimated number of out of school children is 2.8 million after schools re-open after 14 months closure due to Covid-19. Additionally, gross enrolment decreased by roughly 7 per cent between 2020 (2,727,209) and 2021 (2,523,204). (South Sudan HNO, 2022).Insecurity is a significant challenge to school functionality. In the targeted project locations, safety and security incidents in and around education facilities, as well as destruction or occupation was mentioned as barriers to access education facilities (FSNMS+ Publication, 2021).Learners cited insecurity in and around schools as a major reason for learner absenteeism and dropout in 2021. School functionality has been severely impacted by flooding in 2021 including in Jonglei. (Education Needs Assessment (ENA), 2022). Lack of and delayed salaries and incentives result in teacher absenteeism, high turnover and poor-quality teaching.In the ENA, fees associated with education, such as school contributions, volunteer teacher payments, uniforms and examination fees, are most significant barriers to regular attendance and dropout. Distance to school, inadequate learning spaces, lack of school supplies and untrained teachers are other barriers to learners accessing education. Barriers are amplified for marginalized groups. CwDs face numerous challenges in accessing quality education, most schools having no mobility or assistive devices & over half of schools were inaccessible for CwDs. Teachers are not trained to foster or deliver inclusive education and school communities were not sensitized on the need to create enabling environments for CwDs.One in three schools reported having no access to a functioning safe water source and less than half of schools have clean water and soap available. A lack of menstrual health management is also a significant barrier for female learner retention and progression. Over 70 per cent of schools reported having a washroom for girls and half said they had never received dignity kits for female learners. Over 80 per cent of schools reported a rise in child protection cases during COVID-19 school closures. Over a third of schools assessed said they had no referral mechanisms in place and less than a quarter of schools having a general protection referral mechanism. Teen pregnancy is both a cause and consequence of being out of school; parents often forced children into marriage as girls got pregnant during school closures to alleviate household economic stress, leading them to dropout of schools. A significant proportion of teachers (26%) indicated teen pregnancy as the reason for which girls could not return to schools. (Covid-19 Impact Assessment Report, 2021).South Sudanese women and girls face extreme levels of gender-based violence, exacerbated by armed conflict, organized violence and forced displacement due to flooding. Much of the violence goes underreported, with limited response services available to survivors. This creates conditions for conflict-related sexual violence and barriers for survivors to access gender-based violence response services. (Global Humanitarian Overview, 2022).

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

The project will ensure feedback from the most vulnerable by ensuring that: a) The feedback and complaint mechanism design and implementation is appropriate for all members of the community including those who are not literate, the elderly, women and girls and individuals living with disabilities;b) Ensure that the most vulnerable have equal access to the feedback and complaint mechanisms;c) Ensure that there is sufficient time, resources and flexibility to implement a feedback mechanism and respond to feedback once the mechanism is in place and to actively solicit feedback from individuals representing marginalized groups;d) Conducting a thorough context analysis to ensure that all the targeted groups/ vulnerable groups are considered before deciding on a particular feedback mechanism and ensuring the mechanism has different ways to report into it, including methods which are without cost and which are suitable for persons who are not literate; e) Sensitise beneficiaries including the most vulnerable to the purpose and process of giving feedback, both at the start of the project and on an ongoing basis, and allow time to build trust in the mechanism and to be able to adapt the mechanism if all or part of it is not working well, or if feedback isn’t being received;f) Engage with external stakeholders including representatives of the most vulnerable groups about the feedback mechanism and establish referral protocols;g) Ensure that there is sufficient scope in the programme design to make changes and respond to requests to increase or reallocate resources;h) Ensure those with ‘first contact’ with beneficiaries (often project staff) understand the purpose of the feedback mechanism and the processes and timelines for responding to feedback;i) The project will careful give consideration to how feedback will be analysed and how the feedback and any required responses will be shared back with the community in a manner which is inclusive and age-appropriate. j) SCI staff members receive annual training on the response mechanism to ensure staff’s capacity to manage and oversee the system appropriately.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

The project will ensure that there is a functional feedback and accountability mechanism established within the project locations. Needs assessments will be conducted before starting new activities and the affected population will be asked how they would prefer to share feedback with the programme and receive assistance. The s targeted schools will be installed with the accessible suggestion boxes, to allow teachers and learners to share their feedback, requests & concerns about the project. The project will conduct a stakeholders workshop prior to the project launch in all the project locations, this will provided an opportunity for various stakeholders to learn in details about various components of the project, including the design, mode of implementation, targeted beneficiaries and about PSEAH and how to report this confidentially. The project team will be jointly conducting a periodic review of the program with the stakeholders to track the progress and offer any control measures, this will equally provide an opportunity to the stakeholders to feedback into the implementation process and track the progress. Community meetings which include vulnerable community members will be held termly with adaptations made to the projects where possible based on the feedback received.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

Engagement with local populationsMost of the work in protection from sexual exploitation and abuse will be done at community, payam and county level. Engaging local populations and having support locally is a crucial element in succeeding in this work. This will involve, raising awareness in local communities of what PSEA is, that there is zero tolerance for PSEA, the options for reporting suspected PSEA cases, how the reporting mechanism works and where they go access further support and referrals. Raising awarenessKey to prevention and response is raising understanding among local populations about their rights, for example entitlement to aid without sexual exploitation), the prohibition against humanitarian, development and any other personnel engaging in such acts and how to report it when it occurs. Specific tasks will include the development and delivery of information sheets, posters, and having community based training sessions to properly inform local staff and local populations. Building Effective Complaints MechanismsA major element in engaging with the local population will be based on building effective complaints mechanisms. In order for the project to establish a safe, accessible and confidential complaints mechanism (i.e. system for receiving allegations or rumours of SEA), the local community in each geographical area will be engaged in designing it to ensure its usability.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The project will ensure that there is a feedback mechanism from the schools which is accessible to all.: On a termly basis, the feedback from the complaints mechanism (received via phone), the school information boxes, and from termly community meetings and from implementing staff and volunteers will be consolidated and reviewed.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

The project will create awareness among the learners and teachers on the risk to UXOs, they will be trained on how to identify possible UXOs and how to report by an agency specializing in Mine Education. The learners will also be sensitised on the areas to avoid while playing or moving within the community to avoid possible exposure to the UXOs. 12 - Contribution to Collective Outcomes
12.1. Does your project contribute to any of the three Collective Outcomes, i.e. Improved Food security; Enhanced support for prevention and response to needs of survivors of Gender-Based Violence; and Flood displacement?

Yes

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

The project is going to contribute to outcome 3 by strengthening/enhancing the education system. This is going to be achieved by training SMCs/PTAs and education authorities on Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR), climate change, school management, governance, psychosocial support (PSS) and the creation of school safety and emergency preparedness plans. Construction of new TLS’s will be in locations of higher-ground, to prevent their flooding and rehabilitation of damaged TLS’s and gender-segregated latrines will seek to use materials and construction methods to reduce their exposure to future flooding.

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

The project will contribute to collective outcome 2 by ensuring that there is improved equitable access to safe education in schools in which CP and GBV awareness sessions will be held with girls, boys, their teachers and PTAs. This will include community and school-based dialogues on gender equality to raise communities (men and women’s) understanding of gendered norms and behaviours and the impact this has on their girls. All schools will have existing referral mechanisms checked for functionality and having up-to-date information and schools without referral mechanisms will have these established. The referral mechanisms will include for GBV referrals to specialized actors including NGOs and to the Ministry of Gender, Child and Social Welfare and to female police units if they are trained and have the capacity to respond to GBV cases. Save the Children implements education activities alongside our Child Protection programmes which ensures child helpdesks are set up per school, led by one female and one male staff member who receive comprehensive training. The helpdesks help to identify and refer children requiring case management and psychosocial services. SCI’s CP teams include qualified caseworkers in each project location who lead case management processes. At the same time, community-based child protection networks are established and strengthened as a community-led initiative to support the protection, identification and referral of vulnerable children and they are empowered to advocate and implement activities directly which contributes to the protection of children. SCI co-chairs the Case Management Technical Working Group in South Sudan and leads the UASC Technical Working Group. SCI has the mandate to train all CPIMS+ partners on comprehensive case management and the use of CPIMS+. SCI also co-chairs the Child Protection Area of Responsibility for South Sudan.
