Project Summary

The protracted crisis in South Sudan has led to the internal displacement of 1.5 million people, widespread human rights abuses and acute protection needs arising for much of the population, particularly vulnerable groups such as persons with disabilities. The protection cluster estimates that in 2020, 4.49 million people including 674,000 persons with disabilities will be in need of protection services. HI's proposed intervention aims to address both the immediate and long-term protection and MHPSS needs of vulnerable groups (with and without disabilities) in Juba, Yei, Morobo and Kejo Keji through a combination of static and roving response modalities. The project is comprised of two core components – the first to identify and address immediate protection risks and needs in target locations to support risk mitigation, and the second to promote resilience among vulnerable population through community-based MHPSS and rehabilitation service-delivery. In the first component, regular specialized protection assessment and monitoring activities will be critical to identify protection risks, specific needs and to inform suitable responses in target locations. HI will conduct detailed protection assessments, which will integrate focuses on MHPSS and disability inclusion (i.e. barriers and facilitators to the inclusion of persons with disabilities in service delivery, disability-specific risk factors, etc.) in order to develop comprehensive recommendations to inform humanitarian programming. HI will also conduct on-going protection monitoring and vulnerability screening activities to identify individuals with specific needs and support them through comprehensive case management activities, including possible accompanied referrals and donation of individual protection assistance in the form of mobility devices. Such interventions will be complimented by the second component of the project, which will include MHPSS support services to address high-levels of trauma, post-traumatic stress and other mental health conditions of beneficiaries through psychological first aid, peer support groups, and psychoeducation. Overall, the project will seek to support resilience, social cohesion, and address the exclusion of vulnerable groups (with and without disabilities) in humanitarian response efforts. Participatory mapping exercises will help to identify and elaborate beneficiary-led responses to protection risks and barriers to services, which will be supported through local actors – NNGOs, disabled people’s organizations (DPOs), local leaders, and community and religious groups. Community-based protection and MHPSS interventions will allow women and girls, and vulnerable groups with and without disabilities to become more resilient to the effects of adversity, stigmatization and violence in their communities, while addressing exclusion mechanisms in the humanitarian response. Such groups will also promote positive coping mechanisms among sub-sections of the population, and promote social cohesion, understanding and social integration through activities which link groups from different ethnicities, genders and persons with/without disabilities. Such activities will also promote safe returns and the social integration of newly settled groups particularly in Kajo Keji, Yei and Morobo. Lastly, the intervention will include a strong cross-cutting element aimed at promoting disability inclusion and the safe and dignified access to essential humanitarian services for all. This will include joint-response efforts with INGOs, NNGOs and DPOs to provide guidance and support, but also direct capacity building, monitoring and follow-up activities to embed inclusion. As a result, an increased number of people in the intervention areas will be able to access improved quality and disability-inclusive material assistance based on their specific needs.

1 - Needs

Across South Sudan, people with disabilities are disproportionately affected by the crises with limited access to services and material assistance. In addition, the country has a high prevalence rate of post-traumatic stress disorder among the population – given the linkages between protection and mental health needs, an integrated response is required to support the most vulnerable in target locations. In Juba POCs and IDP settlements of Mahad, Don Bosco, and Mangateen, HI’s participatory protection monitoring activities have identified substantial protection needs among the population of people with specific needs (PSNs) residing at the sites, including but not limited to persons with disabilities. These common protection risks include: lack of early identification of persons with disabilities among mainstream humanitarian service providers; low levels of participation of PSNs in camp coordination/leadership structures; theft, bribery, coercion, and physical violence targeting PSNs at higher rates; inaccessibility of humanitarian services, such as activity sites that lack physical accommodation for people with mobility impairments and food distribution agencies not allowing for prioritized distribution to people at risk; lack of livelihood opportunities and insufficient material assistance—particularly for new arrivals—forcing women and girls to seek dangerous work in the informal economy; and low levels of social acceptance of disability and mental health issues, leading to discrimination even extending to service providers. As such, there is need for on-going protection monitoring and assessment, comprehensive case management support to individuals with heightened protection risks/needs, psychosocial support particularly aimed at fostering social connections and community cohesion, as well as diverse forms of awareness raising on mental health, disability, services and referral pathways. Assessments by HI and partners in Yei, Morobo, and Kajo Keji have identified similar heightened protection and MHPSS needs. In Kajo Keji, an October 2019 assessment by HI identified common MHPSS and protection concerns, including high rates of trauma resulting in nightmares, loss of sleep or appetite, and deliria; feelings of idleness and hopeless, particularly among youth; anxiety and fear arising from high numbers of revenge killings; feelings of social anxiety among people with disabilities and the elderly, who felt they had been abandoned and neglected during the recent periods of displacement; and inaccessibility or overall lack of services, which confounds vulnerability. The assessment also identified large populations of people with disabilities, with notable gaps in service provision and a clear need for targeted assistance. A Protection Cluster assessment in Morobo county, in which HI took part, found that movement restrictions due to ongoing insecurity were common, thus cutting people off from accessing vital and essential services; there was a noticeable lack of educational, mental health, and protection activities or services; there are high rates of disability—mainly caused directly by violence or a lack of health services to respond to war-related injuries and wounds—but physical infrastructure and services are not accessible; discrimination and social isolation is common against persons with disabilities and mental health issues. Similarly, a March 2019 study commissioned by HI in Yei found that very few respondents (3%) were able to access mental health services. Those that could not access services reported lack of acceptance of mental health issues and lack of community-based support structures for accessing mental health were the primary challenges. The assessment findings from Yei, Morobo, and Kajo Keji clearly indicate more is needed to be done to lessen the stigma about mental health, disability, and vulnerability and rebuild community-based structures to support people with specific needs.

2 - Activities

The proposed project activities directly align with all protection clusters HRP strategic objectives for 2020, specifically SO1, SO2 and SO4. Activities are designed to mitigate the risks and address the immediate and long-term protection and mental health needs of acutely vulnerable populations (with and without disabilities), whilst mainstreaming inclusion in the wider protection response. Target locations have been selected based on HIs existing presence, but also tailored to ensure specialized and integrated services are available in prioritized and high-need locations: Juba, Yei, Kajo Keji and Morobo. Activities linked to SO1: 1.1 Identification of vulnerable populations including persons with disabilities, 1.2 Comprehensive case management including the identification, assessment, referral and follow-up of vulnerable individuals to support access to protection and essential services, 1.3 Individual protection assistance (in-kind) including assistive devices for persons with disabilities, 1.4 Emergency mental health and psychosocial support services (MHPSS) for vulnerable individuals and at-risk individuals including GBV survivors, 1.5 Establishment / strengthening of referral pathways for protection, MHPSS and essential service provision. Activities linked to SO2: 2.1 Protection, MHPSS and disability inclusion awareness raising, and risk prevention messaging, 2.2 Establishment / strengthening of community-based protection &MHPSS support groups for preparedness and risk mitigation, 2.3 Trainings to CBOs, local leadership and protection partners on MHPSS and disability inclusion to promote resilience and inclusion. Activities linked to SO3: 3.1 Conduct MHPSS and disability inclusion training to livelihoods and micro-finance partners to support integration of persons with disabilities and those living with trauma in communities, SO4: 4.1 Conduct integrated protection & MHPSS assessments and monitoring in target locations, to identify the risks and needs of vulnerable groups and persons with disabilities, 4.2 Conduct trainings to protection partners on the collection, synthesis and analysis of disability disaggregated data, 4.3 Advocate for the integration of Washington Group questions, and disability disaggregation in all protection monitoring activities.

8.18 - Please indicate how the risks selected above will be mitigated.

Given that some of the target locations are heavily contaminated with cluster munitions, ERW and UXOs, it is critical that due diligence and coordination with mine-action partners is completed before conducting activities. The presence of explosive hazards in communities would heavily impact the delivery of key activities, but also factor into the types of risk-mitigation programming introduced. There is also a critical link between communities affected by mines, ERWs and physical impairments – therefore creating linkages with HI's own functional rehabilitation programming would be considered. In all locations, disputes over housing and land ownership are to be expected, particularly given the nature of the land rights system and legislation in South Sudan. The nature of conflict and forced displacement has also complicated land rights claims, and led to expropriation of displaced populations' land and property. To mitigate this effectively, HI teams will play a key role in monitoring HLP issues in communities – particularly in areas with high expected numbers of returns such as Yei, Morobo and Kajo Keji. Strong coordination with HLP partners in each location, as well as government ministries is required to mediate issues and sensitize communities about how to manage disputes effectively. Priority will also be given to particularly vulnerable sub-sets of the IDP population, such as single-female households, persons with disabilities and the elderly, who may face greater challenges in addressing their property rights. In all target locations there are high numbers of female IDPs living in insecure and unsafe settlements. In formal IDP sites such as Juba PoC sites, HI will continue to coordinate closely with GBV partners, camp management and health providers to ensure post-incident support to survivors, as well as to take necessary steps to address risks long term. Protection monitoring and reporting is critical to identify trends and prominent risks.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

HI has a robust feedback and complaints mechanism for beneficiaries that combines both unsolicited communication channels such as suggestion boxes, text and phone-line as well as an anonymous reporting email service, with solicited forms such as monthly focus group discussions, interviews and post-intervention monitoring surveys. Detailed sensitization of communities on when, how and why to report is critical for the functioning of the system, as is HIs internal review, response and feedback protocols – designed to close the loop.
