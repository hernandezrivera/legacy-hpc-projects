Project Summary

The project capitalizes on previous CRS education projects in Dohuk, Kirkuk, Ninewa and Baghdad and continues to implement all project activities in all areas. The project will upgrade/repair physical infrastructure and provide necessary school materials and furniture to ensure an adequate physical learning environment. The project will foster safe and protective environments that promote social and emotional learning through training teachers in child protection, socio-emotional learning, and psychosocial support in the classroom, and will provide follow up to these trainings through monthly Peer Learning Circles and regular observation of selected teachers. The project will provide remedial summer schools for students who failed the first round of examinations, giving them an opportunity to prepare for and pass the second round of examinations and continue their education. The project will enable vulnerable IDP and returnee children to remain on the pathway of formal education, and strengthen caregiver engagement in their children’s education. The proposed intervention is designed with strong consideration of protection concerns, as well as the unique needs of girls and boys, persons with disabilities, and diverse groups, across the IDP, returnee and host community students it serves. Drawing from global best practice, the project is integrated within the existing formal school system, effectively utilizing limited resources to increase its sustainability, expand the number of beneficiaries served, and promote self-reliance. The project targets 7,000 primary and secondary students. Project activities also support the teachers and school administration with trainings, teaching materials, and professional support. Parents and caregivers benefit indirectly from caregiver events and activities held as part of the project, and from support provided to the Parent Teacher Associations.

Needs

Physical structures are often in poor condition and inadequately equipped. In areas of displacement, schools that already lack adequate physical space, including a sufficient number of classrooms, as well as furniture and teaching materials are further strained following the enrollment of IDP students. In areas of return, school buildings have been damaged by the conflict and had materials and furniture looted. School staff lack the time and skills to meet the psycho-social and emotional needs of conflict-affected students. Having experienced conflict and displacement, many students exhibit fear, anger, distress, and difficulty concentrating. School staff in IDP and returnee areas lack the knowledge and skills to create a safe environment that is responsive to the unique psycho-social and emotional needs of students. Further, insufficient numbers of formal teachers have led to inconsistent attendance of school staff, creating disruptions in students’ learning and resulting in reduced ability for teachers to address psycho-social needs of their students. Displaced and returnee students are at increased risk of dropping out. To accommodate the influx of IDP students, many schools in areas of displacement have had to reduce instructional hours for the multiple shifts now sharing one school building. In areas of return, damage to or destruction of school buildings also has contributed to shorter school days. As a result, both IDP and returnee students are at risk of failing year-end exams, a critical contributor to drop out in Iraq. Other key factors include financial pressures on caregivers, resulting in boys dropping out to support the family (12% of male dropouts) and girls getting married (10% of female dropouts). Any displaced or returnee students in Iraq who have been out of school require supports in order to re-enroll. Lesson-learned: Caregivers must be engaged to reinforce quality learning. CRS globally has found that engaging caregivers reinforces project investments in education, including the role of caregivers in supporting students’ positive learning environment and retention. CRS has found that utilizing the existing but nascent Parent-Teacher Association (PTA) structures has been an effective means of engaging caregivers.

Activities

The project will support targeted schools to meet minimum physical standards including student-teacher ratios; safe access for persons with disabilities to latrines, school entrances, and where possible, classrooms; latrines for students and teachers that are sex segregated and lockable from the inside; and sinks and water tanks. The project will provide furniture to outfit repaired/upgraded classrooms and teaching and learning materials for each school. In order to ensure schools supporting displaced students are able to meet DoE/MoE student-teacher ratios, the project will support stipends for lecturers and transportation for Head Teachers (HTs) and Head Teacher Assistants (HTAs). Furthermore, the project will ensure that school staff receive training on child protection; administrators, teachers, and lecturers will receive training and support to further establish an atmosphere conducive to Social Emotional Learning (SEL) in the classroom and school. Both IDPs and returnees are at risk of falling behind in their studies – whether linked to the quality of education or number of instructional hours per week, or not being able to attend school for a period of time due to population movement. The project is designed to provide early warning for students at risk of dropping out, supplemented with non-formal educational opportunities including remedial summer school to keep school-age children on track to complete their education. Intentional attention to the involvement of parents and other caregivers is a critical contributor to increased well-being of students, especially if this involvement is responsive to the unique social and emotional needs of displaced students. Greater support will be provided to strengthen the Parent Teacher Association (PTA) structure at all schools, so this critical Iraqi MoE institution can continue to support the school and students beyond the life of the project. Resulting activities will bring parents and other caregivers to the school in constructive ways that focus on positive family interactions with education. To promote project sustainability, CRS maintains strong partnerships with the Baghdad Ministry of Education (MoE) and the Dohuk, and Ninewa DoEs. CRS further develops good relationships and promotes capacity building with Head Teachers at targeted schools. Trainings, PLCs and continual school visits will include skills transfers to the Head Teachers, when possible. DoE staff are invited to every training and are encouraged to visit the schools to receive beneficiary feedback and observe activities. The nature of these interventions advances solutions that go beyond serving the direct beneficiaries (i.e. students). The project seeks to realize behavior change in the teachers and school administrators, so that they continue to approach children’s education from a mindset of developing the whole child and their social and emotional needs as well as cognitive needs after the project ends. To further ensure the sustainability of activities, CRS proposes to provide lecturer stipends only in schools hosting IDPs in Dohuk and KRG-administered Ninewa, where unpaid lecturers are at risk of not attending work due to financial barriers. In order to promote a sustainable re-establishment of the education system in returnee schools, CRS will not provide these stipends in areas of return. Finally, the CRS-Caritas partnership model is a pillar of sustainability and promotion of local ownership. CRS views partnership as an opportunity for subsidiarity and solidarity with Iraqis; it is CRS’ obligation as an international actor to develop local capacity that will carry on with the same high-quality work after the project ends.

Protection Risk Analysis

CRS has identified the following as potential protection risks: 1- Project beneficiaries may be exposed to physical risks (including injury) during school upgrades. 2- Students may be exposed to protection risks such as inappropriate contact (physical or verbal), physical or emotional harm through their interactions with adults during project activity implementation. Adults can include school staff and visitors to the school, including NGO staff, implementing partner staff, and contracted workers (e.g. contractors working on physical upgrades). 3- Humanitarians may be exposed to threats affecting their safety and security in the target project implementation areas.

Mitigation Measures

As is true across all CRS programming, protection is mainstreamed throughout project activities following CRS Global guidance and best practices. CRS monitors protection issues through both its feedback mechanisms as well as through ongoing beneficiary follow-up and program monitoring; CRS will regularly review this information and make program adjustments to reduce existing and newly identified risks. Specific mitigation measures for those risks identified above are as follows: 1- School upgrades are timed and planned in a collaborative process with CRS staff, CRS engineer project managers, contractors, and DoE representatives to ensure that a schedule for upgrades is put in place that will minimize interference with daily activities in the school. To the extent possible, upgrades are planned for holidays and non-school hours, and protective barriers are required on all work sites to prevent beneficiaries from accessing them. School administrators and teachers are instructed to assist in keeping children away from the work sites, and CRS staff monitor the upgrade work on a daily basis to ensure security and protection measures are being followed, and to confirm that no child labor is employed. 2- CRS has robust child protection policies in place, including training for all CRS and partner staff, and orientations to all contracted workers (field workers, hired contractors, other day laborers) on child protection and safeguarding. Clauses are included in all contracts issued by CRS with stipulations against child labor and inappropriate contact or behavior with minors. In addition, the project will ensure that all school staff receive training on child protection as a preventative measure to safe guard children from harm or abuse in school. Key messages will include prevention of and proper response to violence, abuse, neglect, and exploitation of children; positive discipline methods; and identifying protection needs and providing appropriate follow-up. Monthly Peer Learning Circles will also be held for the teachers to review and discuss topics related to Child Protection. As is true across CRS programming, CRS monitors protection issues through both its feedback mechanisms as well as through ongoing beneficiary follow-up and program monitoring; CRS will regularly review this information and make program adjustments to reduce existing and newly identified risks. Any critical protection cases will be referred to the Child Protection Sub-Cluster. Additional information regarding accountability to the affected population is described below. 3- The current security situation in the targeted areas is relatively stable. CRS Iraq regularly updates its field safety and security plan, which details potential security risks and instruction for risk mitigation and reporting. CRS Iraq provides security training for staff, including security strategies, situational awareness, context and actor mapping, risk assessment, and personal safety. Additionally, CRS is in regular contact with local authorities, the UN, and other humanitarian partners to identify security risks and monitor the security situation. In the event of deterioration of the security situation, CRS will analyze the specific changes in risks to CRS staff and beneficiaries, and if necessary put in place any measures needed to mitigate new or heightened risks.

How is your project accountable to affected population

The CRS Education project is designed based on review of secondary data sources, coupled with primary data collection during which the affected population is directly engaged to identify priority needs within their community. Based on the priority needs identified by the affected population, this project has been tailored to address the unique needs of conflict-affected Iraqis while also ensuring the design is responsive to the identified needs of vulnerable populations especially women, girls, youth, the elderly, and those with disabilities. CRS-Iraq takes a client-oriented, active approach to accountability. In addition to the feedback collected during regular program monitoring, CRS Iraq has developed inclusive feedback mechanisms (responsive to gender and other vulnerability considerations) based on input provided by affected communities on preferred methods of communication. In addition to operating a community feedback hotline, CRS also engages community ambassadors in each project location to liaise between project staff and beneficiaries. CRS ensures that 100% of feedback is addressed either real-time when the feedback is received, or through appropriate follow-up by CRS staff; most cases are resolved within one week of receipt. This two-way communication ensures transparency throughout project design and implementation. The MEAL Technical Support Unit (TSU) is responsible for managing all feedback mechanism inclusive of activities implemented directly by CRS or by local partner Caritas Iraq. CRS utilizes stakeholder feedback to support adaptive program management. The CRS MEAL TSU shares all feedback to program staff on a weekly basis and aggregate information is compiled into a feedback dashboard to identify trends across projects and activities. During quarterly programmatic reviews aggregated community feedback from the quarter are analyzed, informing a “learning-to-action” discussion for programmatic design and implementation adjustments. CRS and partner staff are responsible to adhere to the CRS-Iraq Data Protection Standards, detailing the data management from collection (including requirements for consent), processing, and retention of personal data for persons of concern in Iraq. Sensitive feedback, including PSEA-related issues, is communicated directly with the CRS Country Representative and addressed according to CRS policy and procedures. All staff and partners participate in an orientation on accountability within one month of hire. In an effort to increase accountability to child beneficiaries, CRS includes data on students’ assessment of the safety, protection, and quality of their learning environment. CRS also has extensive child protection policies in place, including training for all CRS and partner staff, and orientations to all contracted workers (especially project and MEAL Field Workers) on child protection and safeguarding. These policies including best practices and guidelines for child-sensitive data collection approaches when engaging with and/or interviewing children as part of regular project and monitoring activities.
