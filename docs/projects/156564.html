Project Summary

Preventing &responding to child protection(CP) is lifesaving and continues to be an essential component of the humanitarian response in Syria.As the largest CP agency in Syria, UNICEF has technical&operational capacity, global expertise &responsibilities to lead on the delivery of quality, innovative&scalable programming efforts.UNICEF’s CP programming is critical not only as a standalone intervention, but also as an essential part of UNICEF’s health,education,nutrition,WASH,Adolescent Development &Participation &other programming efforts.
This project aims to contribute to the reinforcement of CP systems by expanding the availability &quality CP services for girls&boys in targeted locations.In line with the strategic priorities of the 2019 CPAoR Humanitarian Response Plan(HRP)&the No Lost Generation initiative, UNICEF will focus on 3intervention areas:1)improving the quality of community-based CP interventions;2)expanding the reach &improving the quality of specialized CP services for children most at-risk &survivors of violence, exploitation&abuse; and3)strengthening the CP workforce.Advocacy&evidence generation will underpin these intervention areas as a strategy to achieve results for children.
CP:UNICEF&partners will continue to support community-based CP interventions such awareness raising on critical CP risks,including risk education.Parenting programmes,structured &sustained PSS interventions will be scaled up to reduce vulnerabilities of children&strengthen individual resilience,as well as support the identification of children in need of additional support.UNICEF will build on the investment of previous years in case management systems to expand access to specialized services, including FTR,for children in need &children living with disabilities.Centre-based,mobile teams & CP networks will serve as delivery mechanisms for reaching girls&boys.Additional focus will be directed to multi-sectorial collaboration to jointly address CP concerns, as well to build the CP workforce.
GBV:Contribute to expanding the mainstreaming of GBV responses in other thematic areas &the response through case management&referrals for women,girls&boys in targeted locations.UNICEF’s GBV programming is critical not only as a standalone intervention,but also as mainstreaming into other essential part of UNICEF’s response including CP,health,education&other programming efforts.In line with the GBVAoR HRP results framework,UNICEF will adopt a holistic approach to: i) assist &support survivors to address the consequences of GBV through case management &referrals; ii) mitigate the risks of GBV &build safer environments for women,girls &boys and; iii) implement preventive measures to tackle the underlying causes of GBV.A core package of interventions will build a minimum response within UNICEF’s existing CP interventions, &serve as a strategic entry point to promote further GBV-specific programming.PSEA,as a core corporate responsibility, to reduce the risk of sexual exploitation&abuse of people of concern; therefore, UNICEF will work on building the capacity of partners&humanitarian actors to address&prevent SEA.

Needs

The scope and range of protection issues affecting children and families in Syria is ever widening and deepening due to prolonged nature of the conflict. Widespread and systematic humanitarian law and human rights violations by parties to the conflict continue to heighten protection concerns. This is taking a toll on children and causing multiple deprivations and violations of rights, including in their gravest forms: Secretary General's 2018 Report on Children and Armed Conflict (based on 2017 data) verified 2,896 grave violations against children, which included 961 cases of recruitment of children by parties including through abduction, arrest and coercion;1,271 incidences of killing, maiming; and 175 attacks against schools and hospitals. Exposure to persistent and extreme violence, continuous displacement and socio-economic collapse has exacerbated existing child protection risks and has contributed to the propagation of other forms of vulnerabilities such as family separation, recruitment and use by parties to the conflict. Families affected by economic pressure are coping negatively through child labour and child marriage. The lack of civil documentation to prove identity, lack of economic and educational opportunities, profound and prolonged distress plus weakened family coping mechanisms continue to be major drivers of vulnerability. The safety and wellbeing of girls and boys is equally threatened by exposure to violence; while both are exposed to child labour, girls are more likely face child marriage, harassment and sexual violence. While quantitative data continues to be hard to collect given the complexities and sensitivities around these issues, there is substantive qualitative evidence on the nature and the impact of GBV. The length of the crisis, in conjunction with deep-rooted patriarchal structures underpinning the most traditional parts of the Syrian society that are affected by the crisis, are normalizing and deepening this violence. Child marriage, sexual violence, abuse, harassment and domestic violence. Face girls and boys. Women and girls face heightened vulnerabilities in conflict settings and are often systematically exposed to GBV risks including due to both gender and age-based power relations. In certain areas of Syria, girls face increased restrictions on their mobility due to insecurity, rules imposed on them by extremist groups and lack of family members to accompany them to services. There is evidence of boys being victims of sexual violence in formal and informal detention facilities documented since the beginning of the conflict. Moreover, children with disabilities are at risk of social exclusion, poverty and violence due to limited mobility, discrimination and lack of inclusive services. As a direct consequence of years of conflict, many girls and boys are estimated to be living in areas affected by explosive hazards in Syria. The presence of explosive hazards continues to threaten lives of children and families, affecting the livelihoods and endangering humanitarian actors. According to the 2019 HNO some communities reported the presence of explosive hazards and members of the communities reported known cases of injury or death because of explosive hazard contamination. Data found as well that children are exposed to threats while playing, mistaking explosive weapons for toys, hence children are direct victims of injury by explosive hazards and mine contaminated inhabited areas or return sites which limits access to basic services. Explosive hazards continue to cause risk of death and injury to children even after conflict ended hence needs risk education prioritized intervention

Implementation of activities

UNICEF’s CP response will focus on three intervention areas: 1) improving the quality of community-based CP interventions; 2) expanding the reach and improving the quality of specialized CP services for children most at-risk and survivors of violence, exploitation and abuse; and 3) strengthening the CP workforce. Advocacy and evidence generation will underpin these intervention areas including through MRM reports or assessments and surveys. Additional focus cross-sectoral collaboration (e.g. livelihoods, education) to accelerate results for children. UNICEF and partners will continue to support community-based CP interventions such as awareness raising on CP risks, parenting programmes, structured and sustained PSS activities to reduce vulnerabilities and strengthen individual resilience, as well as to identify children in need of additional support. UNICEF will build on the investment of previous years in case management systems to expand access to specialized services. Centre-based and mobile teams will serve as entry-points for girls and boys at risk and survivors with trained case managers to support identification, assessment and management of cases with severe protection needs. While in some areas case management work will continue to be pursued through strengthening the capacity of statutory service providers, in other areas UNICEF will work with community-based actors to handle cases of children with specific protection needs in line with minimum standards. Community-based and first line responses will be targeted in IDP shelters and camps, hard to reach areas, including host communities and in recently accessible areas. Urban areas and locations with large IDP concentrations, where CP risks are vulnerabilities are exacerbated will be targeted with prevention and response strategies, including case management. Special attention will be given to children in need of specific CP responses such as children recruited/used by parties to the conflict and unaccompanied and separated from caregivers. UNICEF will integrate elements of gender sensitivity to ensure partners are equipped to design and deliver programmes that address the specific needs of girls and boys of different ages. RE: Under this activity, UNICEF and partners will provide risk education to children and their families through school,- community and media modalities. Curriculum-based risk education (using the national risk education curriculum developed by UNICEF in partnership with Ministry of Education) will be reinforced with messaging through mass media broadcasts. UNICEF will also seek to disseminate RE messaging through large-scale interventions, such as health campaigns and WASH activities. In newly accessible areas, UNICEF and partners will establish information centers at relevant entrances or access points to provide awareness on explosive hazards in full coordination and collaboration with UNMAS on national level and through the RE working group. UNICEF will strengthen the capacity of range of risk education providers (humanitarian and public service personnel) to provide quality risk education. GBV: UNICEF and partners will deliver on these activities through supporting the provision of quality, coordinated and age-appropriate psychosocial and protection services to address GBV risks. UNICEF will support service providers by improving coordination with other key stakeholders to ensure children including adolescent survivors have access to essential services to help them heal, recover and cope with the harmful after-effects of GBV through existing child protection programming and entry points (including centre and mobile service delivery modalities). UNICEF will strengthen capacity of CP actors and service providers to provide survivor-centered, age-appropriate quality care for child survivors through targeted and ongoing trainings and follow up, as well as rolling out the IASC guidelines in UNICEF-led sectors.

Protection Risk Analysis

With close reference to the Protection Sector PRA, the following risks have been identified with the activities described in this project sheet:1) limited access to affected population due to security situation; 2) direct exposure of children to violent attacks while on their way to, during and directly after service provision; 3) risks of exploitation or abuse by service providers; and 4)services causing unintended harm. Assumptions: In carrying out community-based CP services, CP actors need to devote attention to the choice of locations and targeting of beneficiaries, to avoid perceptions that assistance is delivered in a discriminatory or non-equitable manner. As a risk mitigation measure, CP actors should be guided by the sector severity analysis; by an analysis of exiting services through mapping tools at the disposal of the sector partners; by maintaining a transparent, inclusive and adequate communication with communities on available services; and by consulting communities in the planning, selection of services, feedback. GBV actors may need to overcome cultural resistance from parts of the communities to engage and accept the direct engagement of and provision of services to women and girls, particularly those who may have limited access to facilities. Mobile resources to reach out and overcome mobility challenges, as well as a gender-balanced staffing able to overcome cultural and other barriers will be critical for implementation. We assume that engagement of men and boys in awareness and sensitization initiatives on GBV topics will also be pursued. GBV AoR will continue to provide services through an integrated GBV/RH approach and will advocate for increasingly integrated GBV/RH services to expand entry points for survivors and improve overall access to GBV services without fear of stigma from communities or family members. Assume coordination with other sectors, particularly Health is critical. Managing expectations through clear communication with communities; by strengthening referral pathways to immediately support cases emerging during the assessment and through proper coordination across partners to avoid assessment-fatigue. The quality of service provision may be impacted by turnover of staff, lack of adequate technical capacities, particularly in specialized protection interventions such as case management, restoration of family links, specialized assistance to persons with disabilities, certain topics of legal assistance. Maintaining and upgrading training initiatives, harmonizing content, engaging expert resources will continue to be central in the capacity building of protection partners on the ground. Mitigation measures include:1) working through community-based modalities including mobile services; 2) ongoing consideration to conflict patterns to inform the delivery of services; 3) diversification of delivery channels (media, XL convoys, range of tools etc) and training community representatives; 4) ongoing training, mentoring and coaching to staff, clear policies ensuring do no harm principles including PSEA and Safeguarding

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

In several parts of the country, civilians continue to be directly exposed to the effect of the hostilities, resulting in casualties, forced displacement, trauma and distress. Protection risks stems from loss of livelihoods, property and assets, and sub-standard and undignified living conditions in makeshift settlements or overcrowded sites, particularly in certain areas of the country. The widespread use of several types of weaponry has left behind a range of explosive hazards including improvised explosive devices, landmines, and explosive remnants of war across the country. An estimated 10.2 m civilians live in the 1,980 communities reporting contamination by explosive hazards. Each hazard poses its own risks, contributing to civilian casualties, exacerbating vulnerabilities, hindering safe access to humanitarian support, basic services and livelihoods, and ultimately compounding the humanitarian crisis. The protracted crisis has disrupted social services and weakened institutions; strained family coping mechanisms and community safety nets, legacy of psychological distress. Most affected are displaced, returnees, hosting communities and Palestine refugees. Lack and loss of civil status documentation continue to emerge as a key need for all affected people, where national institutions are non-functional, where confiscations are applied, notably for returnees or for populations residing in areas that have shifted control. Civil documentation remains essential to ensure freedom of movement, to access basic services, to register vital events and land transactions and to prove ownership. This may results in multiple protection risks and violations for children and their basic rights. Continuous displacement, exposure to violence, deepening poverty and the persistent lack of access to services and even the most necessities, Grave child rights violations remain a critical concern. Recruitment and use of children remains widespread. Child labor is common, begging and scavenging for scrap metal. Separation from caregivers reflects patterns of both voluntary and involuntary separation, leaving some children without safe care arrangements and underscoring the need to strengthen prevention and family tracing and reunification mechanisms. Girls and boys, children with disabilities, are also at risk of forms of violence at homes, schools, workplaces and communities. Gender-based violence remains also a major protection risk, particularly in forms such as domestic and family violence, child marriage and the fear of sexual violence and harassment. The lack of understanding for the need for GBV services in some communities remains high, indicating a need to strengthen GBV awareness, referral pathways, as well as outreach and prevention activities.
