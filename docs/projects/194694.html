Project Summary

This project seeks to improve health, promote dignity and well-being of communities in South Sudan by providing appropriate water and sanitation infrastructure, and educate people on good hygiene practices. The project aims to achieve this through a) increasing access to sustainable, safe water for poor and vulnerable communities; b) reducing the prevalence of WASH-related diseases, through the promotion of personal hygiene and environmental practices and; c) through an integrated approach with nutrition and health and fostering institutional synergy.
The project will directly contribute toward the achievement of the WASH Cluster objectives and thereby to the overall humanitarian response plan. Specifically, the project will contribute to WASH cluster objectives CO1, CO2, and CO4 which include: (CO1) “Contribute to reducing WASH-related morbidity and mortality in priority counties through WASH integrated programming with Nutrition”, CO2 “provide access to basic WASH services in emergencies and mitigate WASH GBV” and CO4 “Community members’ exposure to environmental hazards is reduced through the construction of disaster-resistant structures and infrastructures”.
The project will address the WASH needs of 74,000 people facing crisis in 5 Counties across four States of South Sudan. Specifically, 36,500 people in Jonglei State, 12,500 in Northern Bahr el Ghazal, 12,500 in Unity, and 12,500 people in Warrap State. Project sites and communities will be targeted through the proper application of WASH Cluster guidelines giving priority to communities with high rates of malnutrition, those severely impacted by flooding, refugees and IDP groups, or those settling in new locations.

The project will help communities obtain adequate supplies of safe water and sanitation facilities by developing new wells, repairing existing wells, constructing household latrines, and hygiene education for children and adults, including proper hand and face washing to prevent diseases. To ensure these initiatives become sustainable, the project will train community members to repair and maintain water sources and serve on water and sanitation committees that oversee community improvements and build local ownership.
The project will be integrated with JAM’s ongoing FSL and nutrition programming in the targeted locations. In particular, in Pibor county, the WASH activities will be integrated with JAM’s ongoing Integrated Management of Acute Malnutrition service being provided through 22 Outpatient Therapeutic Program (OTP) centers. The WASH services will target SAM children and their caretakers as well as Pregnant and Lactating women at nutrition facilities to reduce disease transmission at the household level. The nutrition centers are also targeted with a basic water and sanitation package to reduce the risk of Hospital Acquired Infection (HAIs) transmission due to poor WASH conditions.
To guarantee accountability to the affected population, the project will employ participatory and transparent processes as well as put in place a grievance redress mechanism to allow targeted community members to voice their needs, and preferences and demand inclusive processes. Throughout the response, the project will continue to mainstream protection and mitigate WASH Gender-Based Violence to guarantee that beneficiaries of WASH services, in particular women and children can access facilities in a safe manner.

1 - Needs

Access to water and sanitation facilities in South Sudan remains very low. According to South Sudan’s WASH Cluster Humanitarian Need Overview (updated HNO 2022-2023), an estimated 6 million people (excluding refugees) will not have access to adequate water, sanitation, and hygiene in 2023. To survive, families may be forced to drink dirty water, putting them at risk of waterborne diseases such as cholera and diarrhea, which remain the leading causes of death among children in South Sudan (UNICEF 2021). Among the states with the highest WASH needs are Unity, Jonglei, Warrap, and Northern Bahr el Ghazal state. Weak water supply infrastructure coupled with episodes of severe weather conditions such as flooding and drought and internal displacement of people all contribute to limited access to WASH. Due to limited water infrastructure, only 39 percent of the country’s population has access to safe drinking water. The remaining population is required to travel long distances to fetch water or is reliant on surface water or unprotected water sources. Inadequate access to water is a very common problem in key local institutions such as nutrition treatment centers where only 40 percent of the centers have access to safe water (HNO 2019). The situation is further aggravated by poor sanitation facilities and hygiene practices. Over 80 percent of the households lack access to improved latrines (HNO South Sudan 2022). As a result, open defecation is widespread. While lack of access to latrine is a key driver for open defecation, many still practice it as they are not accustomed to using basic sanitation facilities, highlighting the huge need for awareness-raising and behavioral change communication on WASH. The situation is even worse in some States like Warrap, Northern Bahr el Ghazal and Jonglei where only less than 10 percent of their population have access to a latrine. Inadequate access to WASH services has a major impact on people’s health and nutrition conditions. In addition to WASH infrastructure, the need for WASH non-food items such as buckets and soap is also high. Only 14 percent of households in South Sudan reported having at least one of such items (HNO 2022). Further, the need for WASH NFI will continue to be crucial to maintain the ongoing momentum in COVID-19 prevention. Sporadic sub-national localized episodes of conflicts will continue to drive WASH needs in South Sudan in 2023 without any major transformations in the socio-political fabric at the community level. Conflicts often result in reduced access or destruction of WASH infrastructure as well as limited access to WASH commodities among the affected and displaced persons. About 18% of the households reported unsafe conditions during collecting water in the two weeks prior to data collection (HNO 2022). Women and girls face an increased risk of harassment, assault, and sexual violence when collecting water and using communal latrines, and access to menstrual hygiene products. Appropriate and dignified washing locations remain their key need. The project will target 5 counties all of which are in states with the highest WASH needs (WSC Phase 3 & 4) according to projected HNO for 2023 by the WASH Cluster. These include Pibor and Twic East Counties from Jonglei State; Aweil South from Northern Bahr el Ghazal State and Guit County from Unity state and Twic from Warrap State. These counties are frequently affected by recurrent conflicts and flooding as well as high levels of malnutrition which places an extra burden on existing WASH infrastructure in displacement sites, presenting an extreme WASH vulnerability.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

The project will strictly ensure all vulnerable groups including people with disabilities, women, and girls are fairly represented in all community mobilization events and local targeting committees related to the project. This is done to help the most vulnerable people have influence over the local decision-making processes and ensure their perspectives and needs are properly considered in the project’s implementation. The project will ensure all information communicated to the target community is appropriate and tailored to local specific circumstances. The information will be delivered in local languages and will be culturally and gender-sensitive. Printed information will take into account the high level of illiteracy rate and will be presented in pictorial forms. Telephone helpline numbers will be provided for the greater community to report any cases of concern and this will be supported by the helpdesk instituted at every activity forum. Other complaints and response mechanisms will be available alongside the above inclusive channels.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Because of information gaps, fear, or limited understanding of the procedures for complaints, a significant number of the beneficiaries may not use complaint feedback mechanisms. The project will take the following actions:a) Establish or strengthen WASH committees at community level to act as the facilitators for sharing information about the projectb) Conduct adequate sensitization to all beneficiaries to ensure they are aware of the availability of complaint and feedback mechanism, how it works, where it will be located, and what kinds of complaints can be reported; b) Strengthen existing or establish a new safe and secure feedback mechanism that encourages beneficiaries to feel free to express their views, for example, toll-free lines, c) put in place clear and simple complaint and feedback mechanisms so as to make it easier for beneficiaries to give feedback; d) ensure beneficiaries get responses to and provide feedback for their complaints on a timely basis. This will build trust in the CRM system and encourage continued use of the platform in the long run.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

The project will use various channels to inform the affected population. the project will organize wider community sensitization events before the start of the implementation to inform the target communities about the objective of the project, the specific activities involved, and other support services available. For every activity, the pre-address checklist will be developed and used to ensure it provides information on PSEA and channels of reporting. The project will assign sufficient field monitors and closely work with local institutions such as project Management Committees (PMCs) to reach the community at regular intervals with key messages and information including on targeting criteria, entitlements, distribution place and times, protection against sexual exploitation, the importance of using the complaint mechanisms including and the channels available for reporting any abuses. The project will take advantage of all community mobilization platforms to sensitize the community on PSEA including during targeting, training sessions, participation in asset creation among other key aspects of the project. Printed materials such as posters and banners will be used to disseminate information on entitlements and other key messages on protection against sexual exploitation and abuse in a manner that is appropriate and understandable to a specific community. Where opportunities exist, the project will use local media channels, in particular, radio broadcasts about the project and its strategy regarding PSEA.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The project will carefully review feedback or complaints collected from beneficiaries for course correction. Depending on their nature, the feedback will be categorized into non-sensitive and sensitive feedback or complaints. The non-sensitive feedback commonly concerned with the implementation of activities/dissatisfaction with activities are commonly collected through the feedback helpdesks, project review meetings, stakeholder’s meetings and are analyzed by a team of M and E, Gender, Protection and Accountability team, technical officers, and project manager and immediate actions are taken in consecutive activities for course correction and future programmatic consideration. Beneficiary feedback or complaints on fraud and corruption, abusive behavior, and sexual exploitation and abuse are considered sensitive cases and handled at the country office level and are reviewed through the higher management organ. Where required an investigation team is commissioned by the Country Director to investigate and recommend actions to management.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

To Mitigate risks from violence, the project will:a) conduct specific risk analysis regarding the security situation of the targeted areas before deciding on the specific location and period of key project’s activities; b) Set up registration/ distribution sites closer to settlement areas so as to reduce distance travel and exposure to the risk;c)schedule registration/distribution early enough in the day so as to avoid traveling at nightfall and; d) coordinate with local protection partners and the UN system to obtain regularly updated information on security situations and forage protection support when necessary. To mitigate the risk of sexual exploitation and abuse, the project will: a) provide regular training to JAM field staff on PSEA, GBV, and gender equality to enhance their awareness of prevention and protection from sexual exploitation and abuse and other forms of sexual violence so as to reduce and protect from the risk of being a perpetrator; and awareness on human rights protection.b) conduct a specific risk analysis to identify factors that expose women and girls to sexual violence and take the necessary protection measures. For instance, project sites (water sources) as far as possible be set up closer to settlement areas to reduce distance travel and exposure to the risk. Priority will be given to vulnerable groups such as women and girls who often bear the burden of securing water for the household so as to avoid traveling at nightfall which may expose them to SGBV and SEA;c) JAM will put in place complaint and feedback mechanisms- suggestion boxes, helpdesk, at distribution sites to provide safe and secure avenues for women and girls such as the use of telephone lines to encourage them to report on any act of abuse and seek assistance. Confidentiality will be maintained at all times to ensure the reporter's anonymity. To mitigate the risk of non-participation of marginalized groups in the consultation and decision-making process, the project will strictly ensure all vulnerable groups including people with disability and women are represented in key local community processes and decision-making structures. Particular attention will be given to the local community based participatory planning (CBPP) exercise, Project Management Committees(PMCs) andBoma targeting committees. Ensuring gender balance and equal opportunities for marginalized groups such as key local community institutions will empower them to influence local decision-making processes related to the project’s resource allocation. The risk of beneficiaries not accessing/using complaint and feedback mechanisms will be addressed by the provision of user-friendly channels depending on the forms of vulnerabilities. Because of information gaps, fear, and partial understanding of the procedures for complaints, a significant number of the beneficiaries may not use complaint feedback mechanisms. To mitigate such risk, the project will: a) conduct proper sensitization to all beneficiaries to ensure they are aware of the availability of complaint and feedback mechanisms and what kinds of complaints can be reported;b) strengthen and establish a new safe and secure feedback mechanism that encourages beneficiaries to feel free to express their views; c) put in place clear and simple complaint and feedback mechanisms so as to make it easier for beneficiaries to give feedback; d) ensure beneficiaries get a response to and feedback for their complaints on a timely basis so that they have trust in the system and continue to use the platform in the long run.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

By increasing access to water, sanitation, and hygiene services, the project will contribute to the overall health of the targeted population, enabling them to engage in more productive activities such as food production and other livelihood activities. This directly contributes to the collective Outcome 1 focused on the reduction of the number of people in IPC Phase 4 and 5 by 2023. HNO 2022 aptly summarizes the interconnectedness of WASH and food security, indicating that poor access to WASH services and goods combined with high levels of food insecurity has a detrimental impact on the health of the most vulnerable people, as seen through the high prevalence of malnutrition. At the national level (GAM) prevalence for children, 0-59 months was 11.3 percent while Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) was at 1.7 percent. The presence of water-borne diseases in children under 5, for example, diarrhea, is also indicative of poor WASH conditions (HNO 2022). The linkage with nutrition outcomes is also central to food insecurity, making WASH an integral part of the intersectoral contribution to Collective outcome 1. Without access to adequate water for domestic use, food productivity at the household level will be negatively affected, and the risk of water-related illnesses due to poor hygiene and sanitary conditions becomes heightened resulting in negative nutrition and health outcomes. Further, this reduces people’s ability to become productive leading to a deterioration in their food security status. When livestock has no access to water, their livelihoods are affected. Lack of water infrastructure hampers JAM’s ability to implement resilient food security programming linked to agriculture-based livelihoods such as communal food gardens which rely on irrigation to thrive. In water deficit parts of South Sudan such as Jonglei and Warrap states, improvement in food security is unattainable without access to a sustainable water supply. Hence, JAM considers access to water as an important entry point for augmenting farm productivity and improving food security. This project will target highly food-insecure communities faced with chronic water scarcity. The project will be implemented in partnership with relevant government ministries at both county and state levels as well as community-level project management committees. JAM will work closely with WASH and FSL clusters to ensure technical alignment and ensure harmonization of all cross-sectoral interventions.
