Project Summary

IRC intervention will improve access to essential health services and psychosocial support for approximately 508,000 individuals in northwest Syria by supporting 19 healthcare facilities operated either directly by IRC or jointly working with implementing partners. The intervention will contribute to improving access to comprehensive and inclusive healthcare services and increase the well-being of the conflict-affected population in Idlib and Aleppo Governorate, focusing on areas densely populated with IDPs.

IRC and its partners maintain a flexible approach and adapt intervention according to priority needs defined in coordination with the Health Cluster. Emergency response to the most vulnerable groups will be prioritized.
Between January and December 2023, IRC aims to provide a total of 671,398 consultations, including 534,468 PHC consultations, 51,300 SRH consultations, and 11,246MHPSS consultations.
In both Idlib and Aleppo, it is estimated that about 75% of beneficiaries reached by the project are displaced individuals (living either in camps or out of camps) while 25% are part of the host population. This is due in part to the fact that certain health facilities are serving IDP camps or urban areas with a strong presence of IDPs. IRC recognizes the specific needs of certain groups who face higher health risks or might have limited access to health services. In line with HNO 2023, these populations are identified as children under five, pregnant and lactating women, at-risk pregnancies, individuals living with war-related injuries and disabilities, older people, people with functional difficulties, people living with chronic conditions, and people affected psychologically by the conflict. IRC has a proven track record in providing emergency and long-term medical care to conflict-affected populations in the northwest. IRC has been responding to the Syrian crisis since 2014, progressively increasing funding dedicated to the response. The intervention is in line with the Humanitarian and Do No Harm principles and is adapted to comply with the EPHC Package developed by WHO and the Health Cluster.

3 - Needs

According to UNOCHA, Oct 22, number of people in need of humanitarian assistance in NWS reached 4.1m out of 4.6 m. Cholera is an emerging challenge. Urgent resource mobilization is needed to ramp up preparedness and ensure timely treatment, including the procurement of treatment kits and medical supplies, provision of support to labs, and training to health workers and communities. Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs) and Communicable Diseases remain the most prevalent health issues and demand for treatment and follow-up of chronic diseases is growing. According to the Multi-sectoral needs assessment by IRC in Oct 22, 35% of the assessed population reported at least one of their HH members suffers from a chronic illness of which 61.25% reported occasionally not being able to find the medications necessary for managing their illness. According to HNO 2023, It is estimated that 25% of the population experience one or more limitations in functioning, a significantly higher portion than the global figure of 15%.A clear need was highlighted in 2023 HNO specifically for reproductive, maternal, and child health. The limited availability of clinical management of rape was also highlighted as a major service gap. High-risk groups include pregnant adolescent girls and young mothers with their new-born, with early marriage being common in Syria and adolescent pregnant girls representing an alarming percentage of deliveries at the health facility level.

4 - Implementation of activities

The IRC will deliver integrated primary and secondary health care through its existing presence across NWS through static and mobile PHCs and Hospitals. IRC health response is in line with the EPHC Package developed by WHO and the Health Cluster, with a specific focus on NCD, Mother and Childcare, and MHPSS. IRC will provide a comprehensive package of PHC, sexual and reproductive health, trauma and MHPSS services to the conflict-affected population in 19 HFs. Mainstreamed across its programming are COVID-risk mitigation measures, Cholera mitigation measures, and IPC. The provision of life-saving pharmaceuticals, PPE, and medical supplies will be maintained in all IRC-supported facilities. IRC will provide health promotion activities by trained CHWs. IRC will also provide emergency referrals to patients with life-threatening conditions and ensure these patients reach hospitals by transportation and life-saving/first-aid medical care through the local ambulance service, supported by IRC in Harim district. IRC and its partners provide key information in 4W reporting to the cluster coordination. Monitoring is central to quality control and continual improvement of the services provided. Regular monitoring will be carried out by IRC staff in Gaziantep and Syria to ensure activities are implemented per agreed objectives and that activities have a positive impact on beneficiaries as well as to monitor and adjust (if necessary) activities to ensure quality of services provided.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

Security incidents: attacks on healthcare continue to be a major issue in NWS and mitigating the risk will be by developing a contingency plan, strengthen incidence emergency report, and conducting refresher training. GBV is a high protection and safety risk: IRC staff provide safe referrals of GBV through inter-agency referral pathways to relevant protection actors. Moreover, regarding assistance for GBV survivors’ coordination on protection response, capacity building with the Protection Cluster and sub-clusters.Gender Sensitivity and privacy is needed in service (data protection):- Health facilities include private rooms with an adequate level of privacy for examination and counselling and a confidential data recording system.- Gender and cultural sensitivities are considered priorities. Orientation sessions on GBV were provided to staff, and selected staff were trained on the Clinical Management of Rape. - A multi-stage transportation of hard/soft copies and disposal of the unnecessary document strategy is available.- Beneficiaries have access to complaints mechanisms information which is available in the waiting areas and can be processed safely through the Complaints and Feedback Mechanism. Equal and impartial access to assistance and services:- Community-based outreach visit activities will take place to increase the reach of intervention beyond the clinics and enhance meaningful access to health care. - Rehabilitation of health facilities for accessibility


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

Risk of sexual exploitation and abuse and sexual harassment of women and girls’ clients by staff and volunteers. Likelihood: Medium/Low Impact: Very High Mitigation: IEC materials for beneficiaries explaining reporting channels for beneficiaries to report PSEA and ASH. IRC adheres to a strong internal code of conduct related to prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA) and safeguarding beneficiaries, especially children, as well as a safe environment for reporting that allows for anonymity. Each year, all IRC staff attend a full day IRC Way Code of Conduct Training that includes discussions on IRC’s commitments to beneficiaries and IRC’s zero tolerance for sexual misconduct. IRC will work with partners utilizing their existing beneficiary mechanisms to establish common standards for beneficiary feedback and ensure protection from sexual exploitation and abuse and accountability to beneficiaries and other stakeholders. This will include capacity building activities with partners to reach these standards, as necessary. The IRC is a member of PSEA networks inside Syria and provides regular updates on the number of staff receiving PSEA training and number of complaints received through feedback and complaint channels


9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Clients' confidentiality is being maintained, and their data security and ownership being respected by providing means and training for data protection protocols. Service provider’s safety and security because of their role and potential impacts of secondary/vicarious trauma (eg. PTSD) by providing self-care and build the capacity of supervisors on staff care and wellbeing and maintaining a duty of care policy in place. Lack of quality services and referral expose clients to harm, enhancing the risk of stigmatization reprisal and further trauma, which can be mitigated by updated service mapping and liaising with coordination forums on new services. Security situation deterioration which may limit IRC and partners ability to respond, which can mitigated by regular context analysis and program adaptation maintaining flexibility to move activities where access allows and ensure teams are ready to shift from static to mobile service provision where needed.
