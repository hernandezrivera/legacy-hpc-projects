Project Summary

The proposed action has been developed in the frame of the EU Trust Fund, based on the lesson learnt of the previous two years intervention that Cesvi has implemented, as part of the Consortium led by DRC, under EUTF.
The new action is designed, in partnership with IMC, to give continuity to the in-depth work already undertaken in addressing the protection needs of vulnerable migrants, refugees and host communities (including Internal Displaced Persons (IDPs) and Returnees), in detention centers (DCs) and urban communities located in Misrata and/or its surroundings.
The expected outcome of the project is the improvement of the protection environment of the targeted vulnerable population by (a) providing psychosocial, and protection emergency assistance services to mixed migrants in DCs; (b) improving access of vulnerable Libyans and non-Libyans living in urban areas to humanitarian assistance and specialized protection and GBV services; (c) enhancing the knowledge of local stakeholders on protection approaches, norms, and humanitarian and human rights laws. The estimated period of intervention is 24 months, starting in January 2020. The targets and the budget included in Cesvi's HRP submission takes into account only 2020 intervention.
Furthermore, given the humanitarian imperative to address the human sufferance and given the current security instability in the country, Cesvi is willing to expand in Sirte. The aim is to address the immediate needs of conflict affected population, targeting specifically vulnerable girls and women at risk of protection and GBV incidents. The emergency response in Sirte is not part of the EUTF submitted action.
Cesvi is a key protection actor in Libya since 2011. Its continuous involvement in protection response is further enhanced by its efforts to contribute to Libyan crisis coordination architecture through the active participation in the relevant fora and sectors (PS, CP and GBV AoRs, MRP). Cesvi is currently coordinating, for the second time since its estalishment, the GBV sub-sector. It has co-lead the CPWG form January to December 2020.

1 - Needs

According to the Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP) 2020, It is estimated that xxxxx of the xxxx refugees and migrants currently in Libya are in need of humanitarian assistance, with many facing severe protection risks. Almost 4800 refugees and migrants are arbitrarily detained in over 20 officially recognized DCs across Libya, run by the Directorate for Combating Illegal Migration (DCIM), under the Ministry of Interior. Number fluctuates and there is no proper registration system to record movements in and out of DCs. Moreover, armed groups can gain access and deal with refugees and migrants as financial assets, through forced labour or by threatening or torturing them in order to extort money.Living conditions in DCs are sub-standard. Migrants and refugees in overcrowded DCs are reported to suffer from various abuses and deprivation, including forced labour, torture, Gender-Based Violence (GBV) , and lack of adequate food, water and healthcare. Moreover, some of them are in areas affected by the ongoing conflict, including in or nearby military facilities, with permanent presence of armed personnel and military assets, posing an extra risk for the detainees. The severity of the situation is exemplified by the recent targeted attacks on the refugee and migrant DCs in Tajoura15 on the 2nd of July 2019, which has stressed on the inability of the authorities to ensure the safety and wellbeing of persons under their control, highlighting the need for a coordinated humanitarian action to explore safe alternatives to detention.During the regular visits that Cesvi have conducted during the past two years in the DCs under the responsibility of DCIM, it has been consistently observed severe overcrowding, lack of proper ventilation and lighting, inadequate access to toilets or washing facilities, food or clean water. Moreover, reports from detainees demonstrate a consistent and widespread pattern of violence, humiliation and extortion operated by DCIM authorities and guards, resulting in high incidence of physical harm, severe psychological distress.In the urban areas, migrants and vulnerable Libyans live in extremely poor conditions; they are particularly marginalized, due to their lack of access to basic services, which drive them into situations of isolation, lack of hope, and radicalization, exposing them to the risk of being forced or coerced into joining armed groups or militias . Therefore, the outbreak of conflict and deteriorated instability within the country have caused new displacements among Libyans and non-Libyans who have been forced to move from their homes, with highly exposure to serious protection and health risks, and challenges to access basic goods and commodities.Finding proper housing for refugees and migrants is extremely challenging with the vast majority of migrants and refugees experiencing difficulties to afford safe, dignified and private settings. Moreover, refugees and migrants displaced due to the hostilities in and out Tripoli, including those released or escaped from official DCs, have mainly found refuge with fellow migrants and refugees in communities, adding to the burden of the hosts, and increasing the need for financial and shelter assistance.According to a Cesvi/REACH joint study on refugees and migrants conducted in August 2019 in Misrata, 70% of respondents reported having only a rental verbal agreement with the landlord. Moreover, the 10% declared of having been recently evicted or threatened with eviction.In this pattern of violence, women and girls are particularly exposed to risks, vulnerable to sexual abuse and other forms of violence.In a recent joint protection sector needs assessment conducted in May and lead by the Protection Sector , more than half of Libyan and non-Libyan key informants reported that 57% of women had experienced sexual violence, mostly non-Libyans from Chad, Eritrea and Iraq,

4 - Protection risk analysis

Following the eruption of clashes on April 3rd, 2019 in Tripoli and the escalation of the conflict between the Libyan National Army (LNA) and the Government of National Accord (GNA), Libya has spiraled into further disarray, causing a consequent scaling-up of the humanitarian crisis. Heavy clashes and indiscriminate use of explosive weapons have caused the displacement of approximately 250,000 individuals, with hundreds of casualties among civilians. Hundreds of thousands of people across the country are living in unsafe conditions and highly-risky environments, with reduced or no access to basic services.The current migration crisis has been exacerbated by the continued and deepening lack of governance and wavering internal conflicts. Estimates of migrants in Libya range between 700,000 and 1 million, most of which are living in urban centres. As of June 2019, UNHCR had registered in Libya 46,422 asylum seekers (63% male and 37% female) and 6,497 refugees (53% male and 47% female).Many migrants and refugees have reported human rights violations and abuses.They are subjected to torture, other ill-treatment, unlawful killings, human trafficking and sexual exploitation, as well as unlawful detention.In Libya, sexual violence continues to be perpetrated with impunity by traffickers and smugglers along migration routes, in DCs, judicial police prisons, and against urban migrants by militants and armed groups. Furthermore, the existence and application of article 424 of the penal code for Libyans, which exonerates perpetrators of rape if they agree to marry the survivor, exacerbates the problem.The current Libyan legislation sheds light on the widespread stigma associated with being a survivor of GBV, who often become rejected by their family and by their communities. Moreover, rules and practice related to mandatory reporting for health practitioners in Libya is increasing the barrier for survivors to seek timely support, including life-saving medical assistance.

5 - Mitigation measures

The action aims to provide emergency and specialized protection services to vulnerable groups and community members, libyans and non libyans. Based on the experience in the country and in the target location, Cesvi intends to ensure an integrated approach that entails a set of activities seeking to reduce the risk of exposure to protection incidents and threats, while providing quality support to survivors of violence, abuse and exploitation. To guarantee such approach, the action has been designed using the socio-ecological model, where different layers are taken into account in all steps of the project cycle: the individual, the family, the community, the society. The socio-ecological model is thus entails in both prevention and response related activities envisaged through:1.Analysis and monitoring of the impact of the humanitarian crisis on risk and resilience factors at the four levels; 2.Consultations and involvement in the activities with individuals, families, communities, local authorities; 3. Awareness raising on protection and principles at the four levels; 4.Advocate to improve laws, policies, procedures and their implementation to prevent harm; 5. implementation of capacity-building initiatives that include prevention actions led by families, communities and service providers (such as local authorities and CSOs); 6. Provision of specialized protection services, including case management and psychosocial support, at all levels of the socio-ecological model.Moreover, the response strategy aims to ensure a comprehensive package of activities to be tailored to the needs and profile of the affected individuals/groups: Individual Protection Assistance (IPA), Cash for rent, community based care arrangements (hosting families), distribution of CRIs are meant to complement the provision of specialized services, enhancing their effectiveness.

6.1 - Other accountability mechanisms

Cesvi is currently rolling-out a Complaint and Feedback Mechanism in order to to receive and handle feedback and complaints for all services provided.Cesvi Complaints and Feedback Mechanism targets 3 kinds of communication regarding organization, staff and project, namely: feedback, operational complaints and serious complaints, applying the following categorization: • Feedback: it refers to positive, neutral or minor negative communications. They can be positive comments, suggestions, requests for information, requests for assessment, etc. but also negative comments, which do not fall under the categories of operational and serious complaints, as described below. This positive, neutral or minor attribute implies the absence of an investigation phase, while keeps on ensuring a response to the feedback provider.• Operational Complaints: communication characterized by a negative connotation, which may refer to project, staff or organization. Operational complaints are limited to issues linked with operations, such as delay in activities, low quality of provided products or services, low quality of staff performance, etc. • Serious Complaints: communication characterized by a negative connotation, which may regard project, staff or organization and refers to a breach of the principles and rules established by Cesvi’s policies and prescriptive documents. These three categories of communication are received and managed through the same CFM, but applying different Handling Procedures (person in charge, maximum time to provide a response, investigation, etc.).All feedback received will be recorded in a database that will be handled by one focal point channelling the feedback to relevant staff in order to provide the correct answer to beneficiaries. The CFM is also expected to contribute the lessons learned approach, allowing the project identify concerns, needs and opinions of the community.
