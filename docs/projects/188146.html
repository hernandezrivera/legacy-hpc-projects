4 - Needs

GBV continues to be a dominant feature of the Syrian humanitarian crisis, affecting the lives of millions of women and girls across the country. Women and girls continue to survive various forms of GBV, most commonly perpetrated by their male partners, male family members, and other men and boys. Women and girls faced compounded forms of violence when exposed to overlapping and mutually reinforcing forms of discrimination and social exclusion, such as for divorced and widowed women, women and girls with disabilities, older women. Virtually every space that women and girls access is marred with experiences of GBV, including online. The combination of the prolonged humanitarian crisis; the Syrian economy’s swift deterioration and the lasting effects of the COVID-19 pandemic have exacerbated women’s and girls’ risks of GBV.The impact of GBV on women and girls is tremendous, frequently aggravated by forms of victim blaming, social stigma, and isolation. Despite seeking protection and health services, family and community support, women and girls are increasingly engaging in harmful and life-threatening coping mechanisms such as self-harm and suicide. GBV service providers and Women and Girls Safe Spaces (WGSS) continue to be key allies of Syrian women and girls in their daily struggle against GBV. The impact of GBV services is also demonstrated in women’s and girls’ demands for their increased number, improved accessibility and long-term sustainability. (Voices from Syria 2022)

5 - Implementation of activities

The project will increase availability of and access to specialized GBV services through static facilities, mobile teams and remote services. UNFPA will also continue to increase its support and services, including through CVA and dignity kits distributions, to female headed households, adolescent girls, widows, divorcees and women and girls with disabilities and focus on empowering them through women and girls safe spaces (WGSS), community wellbeing centers (CWC), safe spaces within health facilities, integrated RH/GBV outreach services and through remote/on-line services. UNFPA will continue to lead the GBV coordination and enhance the quality of the GBV response through building the capacity of member organizations, strengthening and updating referral pathways and SOPs. 1.1 / 1.2 Provide lifesaving and quality GBV response services. This will translate in an anticipated 767 community reached with GBV response services and 464,500 GBV response services provided. 1.3 The capacity of 694 service providers will be built to ensure quality GBV services 2.1 UNFPA will engage in strategies to empower women and girls and prevent GBV, with a focus on changing negative attitudes that foster harmful social norms and implementing the WoS adolescent girls strategy. UNFPA aims to reach 1,110,388 beneﬁciaries (444,155 women, 333,116 men, 222,078 girls and 111,039 boys) 3.1 UNFPA will train up to 792 humanitarian actors in GBV risk mitigation.

9 - Protection Risk Analysis

Providing GBV services is critical to ensure survivors are able to recover and heal, if these are not conﬁdential, they may put survivors at further risk of stigmatization or even death. Beyond confidentiality, if services are not survivor centered, this will have a negative impact on help-seeking behavior with dramatic consequences on survivor's health. Communities may be resistant to engaging around topics of GBV and ultimately prevent women and girls from attending. To mitigate these risks, UNFPA carries out thorough risks analysis of its interventions and works with communities to reduce risks of GBV. All services will aim to be conﬁdential and survivor centered by building the capacity of partners. UNFPA continuously conducts assessments on barriers to access services. Women and girls are at the centre of the decision making at every phase of the project implementation. UNFPA organizes and facilitates trainings for a variety of sectors on GBV risk mitigation to ensure that actions to prevent and mitigate risks of GBV are included in their responses. UNFPA has established strong M&E systems. In addition to UNFPA’s regular M&E standard reporting practices such as onsite visits, monthly and quarterly partner meetings, UNFPA conducts quarterly assessments using client feedback forms at health facilities and WGSSs, KIIs with service providers, and FGDs at WGSSs and an annual impact assessment. Third party monitors are used to verify program quality and implementation


10 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

UNFPA enhances the prevention and mitigation of protection risks through mainstreaming the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) into all activities, where UNFPA’s partners integrated PSEA policy into the code of conduct for the facilities staff.Risks: - The COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated PSEA risks for displaced populations. - The deteriorating economic situation inside Syria has led to more human rights abuses, including SEA. - Ongoing conflict enables a context where SEA cases are inadequately addressed. Mitigation: - UNFPA organizes specific trainings on PSEA for staff and IPs- UNFPA conducts PSEA assessments of all its IPs and supports them in developing and validating a PSEA policy. - UNFPA has established a hotline to report any SEA related incidents. - UNFPA has a zero tolerance policy with regards to SEA. UNFPA ensures that implementing partners apply the following:1. Embed PSEA into policies, contracts, and code of conduct; in the health facilities UNFPA ensures that all HFs staff are aware and signed the codes of conduct.2. Embed PSEA into MEAL toolkits; UNFPA is committed to integrating the PSEA and protection mainstreaming components into the design of all monitoring tools, which is applied by the TPM.3. Enhance the awareness of both GBV and other staff and the beneficiaries regarding the prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse through displaying PSEA posters in the supported facilities.


11 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Access, permissions and bureaucratic hurdles may slow the implementation however, UNFPA actively works with its national partners to over come these challenges. Furthermore the possible non renewal of the SCR will have a tragic impact on women and girls’ life, not being able to access life-saving services anymore. Security issues and shifting lines of control will lead to a hampered access or even material damage. This is mitigated by a detailed contingency plan for every segment of the UNFPA intervention. Assumption that communities will continue to engage in preventing GBV. While GBV remains a diﬃcult subject in many communities, this can be overcome by actively engaging with communities. Another risk associated with the project is mainstreaming fatigue as various projects aim to mainstream GBV, gender, disability and other protection issues. The GBV AoR assumes that this fatigue will occur and has prepared to provide tools, checklists and technical support (Skype calls, email, meetings) to facilitate technical support in developing and implementing mitigating measures across sectors. The GBV AoR, as a mitigating measure, will work with colleagues addressing gender, disability and protection risks in order to synthesize and streamline processes e.g. conduct a FGD that addresses the GBV risks associated with person(s) with disabilities or pilot an indicator that addresses GBV and disability for women, men, boys and girls.
