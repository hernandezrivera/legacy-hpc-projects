Project Summary

This project seeks to strengthen the protective environment for vulnerable conflict-affected
persons in Libya through multi-purpose cash assistance. Without access to hard
currency due to the liquidity crisis, a decline in purchasing power due to inflation and covid-19 or no income available to meet their basic needs, vulnerable households suffer the most
from the effects of the protracted crisis, increasing their reliance on negative coping
mechanisms and exposure to protection threats. Therefore, DRC will provide multipurpose,
unrestricted, and unconditional Multi-Purpose Cash Assistance (MPCA) to the
most vulnerable households to meet their basic needs empowering household choice on
the response most suitable for them, while respecting their dignity and reducing their
reliance on negative coping strategies such assistance will be delivered in areas where
markets are functioning, accessible, and safe delivery of cash will be possible.

1 - Needs

Libya has been adversely affected by conflict and protracted political crisis since 2011. According to the 2020 HNO, out of 6.7 million people in Libya, 1.8 (26%) million people have been identified as people affected. About 50% of the affected population around 897,000 are identified as people in need of humanitarian assistance, and more than 342,000 are identified as acutely in need of humanitarian assistance including 102,600 children. According to OCHA's humanitarian bulletin of October 2020, It is estimated that for the beginning of 2021, 1.3 million people will be in need of some form of humanitarian assistance, an increase of 40 percent, compared to 2020. The IOM DTM Round 32 of August 2020 reported 392,241 IDP and 493,716 returnees. The need for humanitarian assistance is due to instability, conflict andinsecurity, the absence of rule of law, disrespect of IHL by armed actors, a deterioratingpublic sector and a dysfunctional economy. Since the suspension of hostilities in southern Tripoli in June 2020, some 36,000 people have returned home. However, across the country, more than 392,000 people remain displaced. The rate of these returns has been constrained by a lack of basic services, which were damaged during the conflict and the presence of booby traps, including IEDs, landmines and explosive remnants of war that continue to present grave protection risks until they can be safely cleared. As the broader conflict has cooled, inter-group violence between GNA-aligned armed groups in the West has increased. This has included, in some instances, the use of heavy weapons in urban areas. There have been clashes reported in Tajoura in late September, between rival groups in Tripoli in early October and most recently in Tarhuna (source OCHA humanitarian bulletin October 2020). According to MSNA 2020 initial findings, about 20% of IDPs/Libyan HHs with members working reported disruption of work as a result of Covid-19. The loss of income continues to impact people’s ability to cover their basic needs and pay rent. Similarly, MSNA 2020 reported 50% of the interviewed sample reported their inability to cover their basic needs. MSNA 2020 reported IDPs communities tend to resort at the beginning to selling none-productive items they own (TV, furniture, household appliances, etc.), spend savings, borrow money from friends or relatives and/or reduce expenditures on essential none-food items and this is found mainly in Tripoli and Zwara mantikas. When these strategies are exhausted and the financial status deteriorates further, communities start to sell productive household items (sewing machine, car, etc.), reduce expenses on health to prioritize food, rent and/or take an additional job. As last resort, adults and minors may get involved in degrading activities such as theft or smuggling. Sometimes, they beg for money from strangers in the streets and/or sell their house or land to survive. In a multisector household assessment that DRC conducted in both Tripoli and Aljabal Algharbi (1521 HH interviewed), more than 60% of respondents reported limitations of access to services due to financial and economic barriers, while 48% reported being delayed on rent payments, 69% of participants reported inability to cover most pressing survival needs unless resorting to negative coping mechanisms; reducing expenses on non-food items was reported as the most common coping mechanisms used (82%), followed by borrowing money to cover basic needs (57%).

2 - Activities

1. Identification of the 1356 most vulnerable households by contacting the relevant authorities in the municipalities, crisis communities, and ministry of social affairs to obtain lists of the most vulnerable HHs. This could be substituted or complemented with information obtained in the field by DRC field teams, referrals either internally or externally, and self-referral. 2. Interviewing by phone, due to covid-19, the identified households using a questionnaire that integrates the socio-economic and other criteria endorsed by the CMWG in December 2019. 3. The vulnerabilities of the households will be analyzed and scored based on the vulnerability scorecard. The HH with the highest scores will be considered as the most vulnerable and will be enrolled in the project to receive MPCA over three months that will be calculated based on the size of the households as endorsed by the CMWG. 4. Once the final selection is finalized, each household will be assigned a specific DRC code which will be the only data shared with the FSP. 5. Payment instructions to the financial service provider (FSP) will be initiated following the validation of the final list of selected recipients and the calculation of the transfer value per household.6. The selected households will be informed of their enrollment in the MPCA project and information on the distribution.7. A suitable distribution site will be identified by the DRC EcRec unit jointly with the safety that is close and accessible by the beneficiaries and offers safety and security with Covid-19 measures respected during the distributions as per the DRC MPCA SOP. 8. The cash transfers will be performed in a timely and orderly manner, following a strict control mechanism and full capacity to track the use of cash assistance.9. DRC will use a contracted FSP with a positive experience in cash transfers for this activity. 10. DRC will continue to coordinate with other actors implementing MPCA to avoid duplications.

5 - Mitigation measures
5.1 - For each risk mentioned under 4 above, please describe the ways you plan to mitigate, prevent and/or address these.

4.1 The beneficiary could use the common feedback mechanism but also the DRC complaint and feedback mechanism that is shared with the beneficiaries on regular basis. DRC MEAL team is also present during the cash distribution and a complaint box is available. 4.4 DRC has developed a distribution SOP with all the necessary precautions especially during the distribution and therefore provides to the beneficiaries hand washing stands, masks, and gloves that are available for them. Social distancing is also respected and the number of beneficiaries at the distribution site is limited and controlled. 4.21 The CMWG monitors the fluctuations of the prices in the market on monthly basis through the Joint Market Monitoring Initiative. 4.23 The number of installments of the cash assistance has been harmonized by the CMWG and is limited to three installments reducing the risk of increased dependency on aid.

6.2 - Other accountability mechanisms

DRC is currently in discussion with WFP on signing the CFM agreement. Parallel to the CFM, DRC has put in place a strong internal complaint and feedback mechanism accessible in all areas of intervention, allowing for the participation of the targeted group in the services and assistance delivery.The DRC CFM is managed by the MEAL department and includes a helpline of which the number is systematically communicated to targeted populations. DRC registers and addresses all requests and regularly reviews and highlight learnings from received feedback throughout the project cycle. Learnings inform current and future programming and allow for increased engagement with the supported groups.In addition, an internal accountability framework is under development, to allow the increased participation and engagement with beneficiaries and community groups into the design, implementation, and feedback on proposed activities. These include regular assessments, focus group discussions, and key informant interviews, throughout the project cycle.
