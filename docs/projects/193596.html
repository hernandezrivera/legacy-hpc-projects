Project Summary

Palestinians in the West Bank and East Jerusalem are subject to an extreme coercive environment, worsened by the ongoing tensions and conflicts, resulting in a wide range of humanitarian needs. The frequent escalations in the WB and EJ further exacerbate the situation of protracted violence and increased the protection needs of Palestinians living in vulnerable communities. More specifically, the use of live ammunition and of force by the Israeli Forces, the settler´s attacks, and the demolition of Palestinian houses, have drastically risen in the last years in East Jerusalem and the West Bank, which affects, in a devastating way, mental health wellbeing of the victims, but also of the communities to which they belong. Anxiety is the most common symptom developed by the ones affected by these incidents, and it´s often translated in physical symptoms such as headache and stomach pain impacting everyday capacities of many members of the population. It also has consequences on the academic and social development of children. In addition, the stigma around Mental Health disorders hinders the access to proper MHPSS services.

MdM-CH & YMCA have been working together to increase the quality of the MHPSS services provided to Palestinian population, building on previous individual experience:

a) MdM: MHPSS support to ex-detained children and their families, peer-to-peer education and developing support groups in areas considered as most vulnerable.

b) YMCA: academic rehabilitation for students who released from Israeli detention, vocational training or alternative education for children not able to return to school.

The action comes as a continuation to MdM's work with YMCA with educational professionals, students and caregivers from East Jerusalem, Bethlehem and Hebron (including Masafer Yatta and H2).

In line with the strategic objectives, priority needs, and most vulnerable populations established by the Protection Cluster and HNO, and considering the regular SWOT analysis conducted as part of MdM-CH’s standard project cycle management procedures, as well as the periodic need assessments and risk analysis conducted by our staff and its experience in the area of operation, in addition to the feedback of the beneficiaries of our intervention and our partners, this action is aimed at delivering timely, coordinated and principled assistance in order to prevent and mitigate the negative impacts of conflict related violence in children, their caregivers, counselors and teachers through protection, MHPSS and educational activities in East Jerusalem and areas C and H2 in Bethlehem and Hebron. Consideration on ex-detainees children, those ones presenting suicidal ideations or behaviours, and PWD needs will be given and the project will follow a gender-mainstreaming approach with a particular attention to GBV. The project will also enhance effective coordination with other humanitarian actors- especially within the WBPC, CBOs and local authorities. The following result is expected: Mental health and psychosocial well-being of students, parents and educators is improved through the provision of MHPSS support, capacity building on emergency response and extracurricular interventions.

1 - Needs

Palestinians live in a permanent state of extreme tension due largely to the encroachment by settlements and the occupation policy promoted by the government of Israel. Palestinians are subjected to military occupation, as well as to generalized poverty and lack of services. Socioeconomically, the PA’s fragility remains unchanged and has even worsened. Surrounded and controlled by a strong military presence, Palestinians are submitted to regular requisitions at checkpoints, and random limitations in movement and access permissions, destruction of crops and other livelihood means, limited control over natural resources, daily violence and harassment from settlers, repeated home demolitions, and the revocation of their residency rights, facing daily violence and harassment from settlers and intensified settlement activity in East Jerusalem, H2 of Hebron, and the West Bank’s Area C. According to OCHA, 35 children were killed in the West Bank, and in August 2022, there were 56 outstanding demolition orders against schools where at least 6,400 children are taught in the West Bank including East Jerusalem. Children and schools are facing threats such as direct or indirect firing of tear gas, stun grenades, and/or rubber-coated bullets, intimidating military and settler presences at schools, detentions, and movement restrictions preventing students from reaching their classes (Statement of Lynn Hastings, OCHA, 28.08.2022). In the first half of 2022 in the West Bank, the United Nations recorded 115 of those education-related violations, nearly 8,000 students have been impacted. These actions promoted by the occupation policies in both West Bank including East Jerusalem and Gaza Strip have created a coercive environment which takes a toll on health of Palestinians (especially mental health). In recent research conducted by Médecins du Monde and AIDA (No Peace of Mind, 2022), 80% of respondents indicated they experienced feelings of extreme fear and stress, with some explicitly saying they feared for their lives. General and chronic exposure to trauma and violence related to the military occupation of Palestine may also explain the high burden of mental disorders. This violence leaves the Palestinian population with chronic mental health disorders such as sleeping problems, anxiety, physical illness, depression, and psychosomatic disorders —especially those who experience severe traumatizing incidents such as arrest, excessive use of force, and self-demolitions. This context also generates a web of complex protection threats to women and girls, including heightened risks of GBV (UN WOMEN-Gender alert). The stigma around mental health disorders is preventing access to MHPSS, consequently this project targets to pursue MdM wide work on awareness raising on mental health in the West Bank. Children are particularly vulnerable to experiencing high rates of mental distress, including significant risks of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, anxiety, behavioural problems, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, as well as functional impairment (HRP 2022), inhering their academic and social development. Further, child detention remains an issue of high concern given the fragility of children who undergo such traumatizing experience. Each year, between 500 and 700 Palestinian children (12-17 years old) are detained and prosecuted by the Israeli court system. According to info provided by Save the Children, 8 in 10 child detainees were physically beaten while 9 in 10 experienced verbal abuse. They face treatment such as beatings, strip searches, psychological abuse, weeks in solitary confinement, and being denied access to a lawyer during interrogations. Ex-detained children come out of prison with various MH related problems which also maximise the need for academic and MH support. This difficult socio-economic-political-security context hinders the risks of dropping out of schools, especially among ex detainees' children. The 2023 MSNA reported that 19% of school aged children (5-17 years old) dropped out of school during 2021-2022. The main reason (for 32,8% of them) was due to the protection and political risks, including violence or harassment from Israeli soldiers, forces, or settlers. In conflict environments, good parenting is also affected by trauma and toxic stress and children are therefore also victim of the cycle of violence. There is a high level of violence at home, with 90% facing some form of violent discipline at home (boys 92.3 per cent girls 97.9 per cent) in the oPt. This includes 20,1 per cent of children subject to severe physical violence from their caregivers (boys 23.3 per cent, girls 16.9 per cent), with the rate of 13,7% in the West Bank (HRP 2022).

2 - Activities

In cooperation with local CBOs and the MoE, the project aims to conduct the following activities: Capacity building for counselors to improve the quality of emergency protection services provided to students at-risk within the areas of intervention. Coaching and mentoring for counselors and/or teachers. Provision of remedial education and catch-up learning support to vulnerable school aged children. Vocational guidance and support for dropped-out children or at-risk-of drop-out students. Recreational activities for children, parents, counselors and teachers from acutely vulnerable schools. Individual and/or group psychosocial counseling and PSS for children affected by occupation-related violence, with special attention to ex-detainee children. Family counseling for caregivers of children affected by occupation-related violence, with special attention to caregivers of ex-detainee children. Group & community psycho-educational sessions on the impacts of traumatic events on mental health and connection to available resources. All the activities will consider the needs of the most vulnerable groups identified before, including specific actions for each one of them. MdM-CH MHPSS team is made up of men and women psychologists and social workers to ensure a gender-sensitive provision of the sessions. Special attention is given to women, girls and boys during counselling sessions to prospect if they are victims of GBV or other intrafamilial violence. Necessary support and referrals are made according to the needs of the survivors. Family counselling sessions or peer-support groups with caregivers are notably implemented in order to break the circle of violence inside the family. Boys, girls, men and women are trained to identify their feelings, talk about them and identify and use their resources to feel better. The activities are notably made to tackle the taboo of mental health and an effort is made on sensitizing men, the most reluctant, on the importance of MH and PSS. This starts also with ex-detainee children (mostly boys), often perceived as heroes by the community, on their acceptance of feelings and pain. Peer counselling sessions are encouraged to tackle the feeling of isolation resulting from their individual experience. Every counselling session is held in safe spaces and child-friendly spaces. People in-need are referred to other actors such as WBPC members for different types of protection services (shelter, livelihood, financial, medical assistance, WASH, and/or advanced MHPSS). Counsellors and teachers in the schools, our local partners and our MHPSS team are trained to detect and to do appropriate referrals. The referrals pathways are based on FRM Inter-agency referral mechanism and are considering high protective procedures regarding the data by using the national referral system. Regarding partnerships: Local CBOs facilitate our access to the targeted population and are referring us and YMCA beneficiaries. They also provide us safe spaces for sessions. Our local partner YMCA has an agreement with the MoE to provide remedial education and vocational training inside the schools in the targeted areas, as well as for the training and monitoring of the counsellors and teachers. Meetings will be conducted with the MoE to choose the schools in the targeted areas and to agree on the main needs of the counsellors and teachers (assessment of the needs).

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

MdM-CH’s Feedback and complaints mechanism are in place in Palestine as part of the M&E process, following WHO globally recognized complain mechanisms, procedures, and standards (rules of conduct for abusive behavior as part of MdM prevention alert and management standardized system since 2018). Feedback & complaint channels are handled exclusively by designated office staff. Response is provided regardless of whether corrective measures are feasible to be implemented or not. Complaint & feedback is handled on the basis of sensitivity: - Less sensitive complaints & feedback are handled on country level, with support from our focal point at HQ. - Sensitive complaints (PSEA or corruption) are handled on an HQ level, with support from field staff. MdM CH is part of the oPt´s PSEA network. As part of the process to be a member, our procedures were reviewed and validated. 2 staff members have received TOT training on PSEA from the PSEA International Coordinator, and one staff member was designated officially as PSEA focal point. She handles all the F&C channels, to guarantee full confidentiality. All complaints and feedback are taken seriously and, if further investigation is needed, other professionals might be involved in handling the case depending on the matter raised. Since 2020, one of our main partners in the West Bank, Sawa Helpline, is one of the main reporting channel for PSEA for the entire oPt, meaning that any beneficiary from the West Bank, East Jerusalem or Gaza can use the phone number 121 to report misconduct. The results of MdM-CH’s community surveys and the results of its regular Beneficiary Satisfaction Surveys (BSS) for MHPSS ER, have informed the design and strategy of the proposed intervention.

9.1d - Explain how you have ensured that mechanism are accessible to all population groups?

All our beneficiaries and partners receive a card with information on how to provide feedback & complaints using different mechanisms (email, phone number, orally to the field staff, complaint boxes) to guarantee accessibility to provide feedback or complaints for all of them, including people with disabilities, with or without internet access. In addition to this card, our field staff make sure to provide the information about complaints and feedback mechanisms also orally, in an accessible way, in order to include people who cannot read. After the end an intervention, feedbacks are collected by phone, through satisfaction surveys, by a member of our administrative staff and on the field by the staff, giving the possibility to provide feedbacks orally and by written. MdM-CH focal point then review all the feedback and adapt our approach according to them. Our MHPSS field staff is made only of local men and women professionals familiar with the context in the targeted location, ensuring a higher trust of the beneficiaries. MdM-CH is also very close from local CBOS and DBOs, also increasing this trust and channel for everyone to the FCM, including children, survivors of GBV, victims of occupation related violence and people with disabilities. As mentioned above, F&C are dealt with sensitivity and confidentiality, according to the procedures and SOPs established by MdM CH, as part of the PSEA network. Regarding MdM-CH staff, all of them sign a code of conduct document, and are aware of the feedback & complaint mechanism. There is also a special email where MdM employees and volunteers can report to. Our staff receive refreshment training once a year to ensure principled assistance is always maintained. Our partners’ offices are also equipped with letter boxes and posters / cards to inform the beneficiaries about the feedback and complaint mechanism.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

At all stages of the Health Project Cycle management: Diagnosis, Implementation and M&E, protection mainstreaming is a cross-cutting theme for MdM-CH’s programs and projects. MdM-CH understand and fully adhere to the core principle of “Do No Harm” as a humanitarian imperative, a cornerstone in all programs followed by the pyramid of IASC minimum standards regarding MHPSS & PSEA. We follow strong PSEA policies to protect beneficiaries and staff, following international standards under the umbrella of the PSEA network. MdM CH and the partners remain vigilant throughout project implementation to identify and prevent unintended negative consequences of their actions. In this regard a number of steps and measures are adopted to ensure that its interventions maximize positive impacts for community stability, mainly by strengthening PIN capacities and resilience, while minimizing harm. Moreover, to further enhance protection mainstreaming the project will ensure that beneficiaries of the intervention will be capacitated on the following topics, according to their level of expertise: MHPSS IASC minimum standards: Guidelines on Mental Health and Psychosocial Support in Emergency Settings (WHO guidelines). MdM-CH is indeed an active members of the relevant clusters and work in cooperation with MoH and MoE, we contribute to the evaluation of the needs and advocate on them, the M&E includes the beneficiaries and CBOs, appropriate referrals are made according to the guidelines, our staff is local and aware of cultural sensitivity to provide safe, dignify, culturally and socially appropriate assistance, they regularly participate to trainings and supervision is provided for them, resilience building is emphasized, as well as access to supportive education, etc. GBV: Minimum Standards for Prevention and Response to Gender-based Violence in Emergencies (following UNFPA standards). Child Protection Minimum standards in Humanitarian action (following Unicef and Sphere guidelines, as well as MdM guidelines). Disability mainstreaming and health: CBR (Community based rehabilitation guidelines) health component of WHO. PSEA IASC guide our feedback & complaint mechanisms. Further, YMCA has developed internal guidelines and policies that are regularly updated and improved to reflect the realities on the ground. In this respect it is important to mention that YMCA has direct contact with hundreds of beneficiaries each day.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

MdM-CH is basing their interventions on ethical and humanitarian principles. This project aligns with the principal approach that is widely accepted in the humanitarian community through such vehicles as the SPHERE Humanitarian Charter (and SPHERE MHPSS). Ethical principles in healthcare include respect for persons to advance human dignity and for individuals’ autonomous choices; beneﬁcence, the promotion of others’ well-being, commonly known as “do no harm,” as described above and justice, encompassing both the fair distribution of resources and fair processes for decision-making. Humanitarian principles is the second source of values and norms that animate both applicants’ actions. HP include humanity, requiring that suffering must be addressed wherever and for whomever it is found; neutrality, the duty of humanitarian actors not to take sides in a conﬂict; impartiality, which stipulates that humanitarian actors must not discriminate or give preference to any nationality, physical ability, race, religious belief, class, political opinion, or similar status; and independence, which demands that humanitarian actors retain their autonomy and remain independent of political or military objectives of other actors. YMCA also follows international standards and has specific guidelines and procedures for the equal and impartial access to aid, that have been reviewed by us before starting operations together.
