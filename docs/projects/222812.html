Project Summary

In 2025, IOM's Child Protection Program will focus on addressing, responding to, and preventing child protection concerns in Camps 9, 19 and 20. The program will proactively support vulnerable children, parents, and community members to empower families in safeguarding children. One key objective will be strengthening Community-Based Child Protection (CBCP) mechanisms to tackle protection and prevention issues within the community, through capacity building, engagement, and promoting participation via community-led activities. IOM has established 11 CBCP groups in camps 9 and 20 where we also support adolescent groups. IOM will establish more such groups as we move into Camp 19, an area with an identified need according to the CPSS.
IOM-CP focuses on community-based child protection, system strengthening, justice for children, case management, and cross-sector collaboration. Implementation involves four operational facilities: one child protection center, one multi-purpose community centers (MPCCs), one case management hub, and one adolescent hub, supported by mobile teams to ensure accessibility. A MPCC is also being established in Camp 19. IOM maintains a child-friendly approach, providing safe spaces and conducting awareness sessions to address child protection risks and promote mitigation strategies. IOM's child protection efforts include specialized services such as case management for at-risk children, as well as structured psychosocial support (PSS). Parents and caregivers receive PSS and positive parenting support through evidence-based group discussions. IOM supports child survivors of gender-based violence and trafficking, collaborating with partner organizations for legal aid. Our CP, GBV, and CT units work together to maximize expertise and fund efficiency. The program addresses the age, gender, and diversity needs of the population, with a focus on children with disabilities, to enhance their quality of life and reduce risks.
IOM collaborates with the government, including the Office of the RRRC and CiC authorities. Additionally, training is provided to law enforcement agencies, such as the APBN, to mainstream child protection. IOM adopts a comprehensive multi-sectoral approach, leveraging community-based initiatives that prioritize inclusivity across age, gender, disability, and diversity. A member of the CPSS SAG, IOM is also actively involved in the Children Associated with Armed Forces and Armed Groups (CAAFAG) task force, the Case Management Working Group, and the Best Interests Determination (BID) panel.
IOM’s Child Protection team collaborates with other IOM units, including Health/Mental Health, Shelter, WASH, Site Management/Communication with Communities, Nutrition, and Social Cohesion, to ensure child protection, child safeguarding, and considerations of age, gender and disability are integrated into all aspects of programming. The team also focuses on disaster preparedness and emergency response coordination.
To ensure accountability to the affected population, IOM CP provides timely and transparent information to program participants about processes that affect them. Program activities are designed to reflect participants' feedback and needs, ensuring they remain responsive and effective. IOM Child Protection also plays a vital role in preventing and responding to Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (SEA) and Sexual Harassment (SH).
IOM is an active member of the SAGs for the Protection Sector and GBV Sub-Sector and we co-chair the Anti-Trafficking Working Group to ensure trafficking issues affecting children are understood and addressed. As a technical member of the Age and Disability Technical Working Group (TWG), IOM ensures that child protection concerns are incorporated into the broader response. Additionally, IOM participates in the Gender Diverse Population Working Group and remains committed to supporting children of diverse SOGIESC.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

Due to ongoing security concerns and the country's unstable political situation, child protection issues in the camps have intensified. The recent Child Protection Services Coverage Assessment and Rationalization exercise (August 2024) highlighted significant gaps in the child protection response. According to the Joint Protection Monitoring Report (January–March 2024), the increasing conflict between the Myanmar Armed Forces (MAF) and the Arakan Army in Rakhine State has had a direct impact on the refugee camps, where organized groups are recruiting adults, youth, and children—including adolescents—into the conflict. This poses a serious concern for children and families. The report identifies adolescent girls, single women, widows, and people with disabilities as particularly at risk. Organized groups have been offering family’s money to recruit their children into the fighting inside Myanmar. Abductions have increased, with children being released only after paying a ransom.. Case analysis by CPSS (CPIMS+, January–September 2024) identified key child protection risks: 31.86% faced neglect, 11.57% psychological distress, 10.48% physical abuse, 7.84% emotional abuse, and 4.9% family separation. Additionally, 5.49% have physical disabilities, 4.53% face child marriage, 7.58% child labor, and 1.36% are at risk of trafficking. Child marriage is higher among girls (4.08% vs. 0.45% for boys), while boys are disproportionately affected by child labor (7.24% vs. 0.34% for girls). These findings emphasize the need for targeted, gender-sensitive interventions. According to the 2024 Child Protection Situation Monitoring Dashboard, 65.3% of Key Informants (KI) reported being aware of children experiencing behavioral and psychological distress. This number is expected to rise in 2025 as the political situation in Myanmar continues to drive prolonged displacement. Coupled with growing insecurity in the camps, this environment significantly impacts the well-being of children. The reduction in humanitarian assistance, combined with the worsening security situation, has exacerbated existing child protection concerns in the camps. Recent data from JMSNA, CPIMS+, and IOM's internal Protection Monitoring reveal a rise in child labor within the Rohingya refugee camps. This increase is driven by deteriorating living conditions, restricted movement, limited livelihood and education opportunities, rising violence, environmental hazards, and multiple ration reductions throughout 2023. According to IOM’s internal reports for Q3 2023, economic hardship and limited access to education have also led to more adolescent boys being recruited by organized groups. In 2024, IOM provided services in Camps 9 and 20 in coordination with the CPSS and our commitment under the 2023 rationalization process. Following the CPSS child protection gap analysis, IOM identified significant coverage gaps in Camp 19. This camp, managed by IOM’s site management team, is one where we serve as the Protection Focal Point and provide General Protection, GBV, and Counter-Trafficking services, as well as operate a Women and Girls Safe Space (WGSS). 2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

Incorporating a protection lens is fundamental to IOM’s operations, ensuring all programs are designed with attention to protection risks and potential violations. In Cox’s Bazar, IOM prioritizes protection as a cornerstone of its humanitarian response, integrating activities that safeguard the rights of crisis-affected individuals. IOM delivers specialized protection services—including general protection, counter-trafficking, child protection, and GBV support—through an integrated team of protection specialists. To ensure meaningful access, safety, and dignity, particularly for children with disabilities and other vulnerable groups, IOM incorporates inclusive programming that identifies and addresses specific barriers to participation. Ongoing protection monitoring, community consultations, safety audits, and risk mapping address the needs of vulnerable groups, including children with disabilities, those with diverse SOGIESC, and other marginalized communities. As part of its protection mainstreaming approach, IOM actively participates in technical working groups focused on age, disability, and gender diversity to ensure comprehensive support. The 'Do No Harm' principle guides all IOM activities, with capacity-building sessions for community volunteers, advocates, and support groups designed to foster child-friendly practices and accessible reporting mechanisms. Child-friendly and disability-inclusive communication tools are used to provide essential information and ensure informed decision-making for children with disabilities and their caregivers . Collaborating across sectors, IOM integrates protection mainstreaming into all initiatives, such as targeted Cash for Work programs for vulnerable families, accessible shelters, and safe spaces for gender-diverse individuals in livelihood programs. Through these comprehensive efforts, IOM remains committed to safeguarding the well-being and rights of those affected by the crisis.

2.3 - Age & Disability

Age and disability are cross-cutting components mainstreamed throughout all of IOM’s work with beneficiaries. Child safeguarding mechanisms and considerations are integral to every facet of IOM programming, from development through implementation and evaluation, with continuous emphasis on upholding the best interests of the child. For example, IOM's MBR (Mother’s and Sister’s Ways) program highlights age-appropriate, culturally sensitive two-way communication developed in consultation with women and girls. All IOM CP programming caters to diverse age groups, needs, and abilities, tailoring inclusive activities for both children and adolescents. The involvement of parents and caregivers ensures a protective home environment, raising awareness of child protection risks and encouraging their participation in identifying solutions.IOM’s protection work is aligned with the CRPD, advocating for the inclusion of persons with disabilities. As a technical partner in the Age and Disability Technical Working Group, IOM promotes age and disability inclusion. Although definitive data on disability prevalence is unavailable response wide, IOM's CP programming uses ISNA 2024's assessment, which applied the Washington Group Questionnaire (short set), finding that 12% of the population reported having at least one disability (11% female, 13% male), to aim for supporting at least 12% of children with disabilities. Collaboration with community-level Family Support Groups of Persons with Disabilities is key to providing this support.IOM’s facilities, including child protection spaces, are designed to be disability-friendly, with ongoing efforts to enhance inclusivity. Additionally, collaboration with the Gender Diverse Population Working Group ensures that CP services are inclusive of people with diverse SOGIESC. IOM remains committed to inclusive, age-sensitive programming and advocating for the rights and well-being of all individuals engaged in the response.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

Information-sharing on humanitarian aid, including child protection, is facilitated through the design, translation, testing, and dissemination of context-appropriate messages for awareness-raising and sensitization—empowering affected populations to make informed decisions. IOM-CP provides capacity-building to volunteers and community groups, ensuring they understand and promote child-friendly reporting mechanisms within the Community Feedback Mechanisms (CFM). IOM-CP collaborates with the IOM CwC team to enhance accessibility, inclusivity, and trust in feedback systems. Multimedia resources and materials are carefully tailored for accessibility and understanding.Two-way communication is maintained through regular, participatory community engagement, ensuring that diverse groups—including those marginalized and at-risk—have meaningful opportunities to access information and participate in decision-making. IOM’s CP program emphasizes community-based, participatory approaches that are responsive to varied needs and promote inclusive engagement. A standardized, multi-sectoral CFM operates across all IOM camps, allowing refugees to ask questions, request assistance, and submit sensitive complaints with data protection safeguards. This mechanism complements referral pathways and activates timely processes for internal and external referrals, enhancing trust by ensuring that feedback loops are consistently closed. CFM reports, disaggregated for effective response, are published to support transparency and strengthen interventions. IOM collaborates closely with coordination forums, leveraging its leadership in the AAP TWG to advance technical support and collective inter-agency commitments. Aid effectiveness is prioritized through rights-based, people-centered programming that upholds dignity and respect. Consultative evidence-generation enables IOM to track rumors and identify emerging issues to enhance advocacy and elevate the voices of affected persons.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

IOM maintains a Zero Tolerance Policy toward sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA), binding all staff, implementing partners, contractors, vendors, and service providers to high standards of behavior at all times. SEA against affected populations constitutes gross misconduct and can result in disciplinary action, including summary dismissal and referral for criminal prosecution. A dedicated PSEA Officer supports ongoing efforts to combat SEA, ensuring adherence to PSEA principles. IOM continues to roll out PSEA training and Training of Trainers sessions for PSEA Champions, aiming to equip all IOM staff, implementing partner staff, and volunteers with essential knowledge of PSEA guidelines and mandatory reporting systems. During the project’s initial implementation phase, SEA-related risks are screened, and mitigation measures are identified to enhance safe programming for affected populations. IEC materials are developed to raise awareness among communities about their rights to free assistance, reporting mechanisms, and available referral services. The PSEA Officer, in collaboration with the program team, conducts regular consultations and focus group discussions with beneficiaries to improve awareness and address concerns about SEA. IOM has an online reporting platform, WEAREALLIN.IOM.INT, for reporting SEA incidents. Recognizing potential access barriers, IOM has also established alternative complaint and feedback mechanisms to receive SEA-related concerns from beneficiaries and community members. IOM’s specialized investigation team ensures follow-up on SEA complaints, with access to protection and support services for survivors provided in coordination with its MPHSS-Protection team, the PSEA network, and existing GBV and Child Protection referral pathways.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

IOM prioritizes environmentally friendly approaches in its assistance, reducing paper and plastic waste, and minimizing usage in camps. IOM’s CP facilities use solar panels, which produce no air pollution or greenhouse gases, promoting sustainability. This environmentally friendly approach is replicated wherever appropriate to ensure long-term environmental responsibility. IOM is working to enable the nature based solution for the beneficiaries. To consider the climate change and it’s impacts the programme is doing plantation inside its centres and also motivate adolescents and caregivers for plantation where possible.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

The IOM Child Protection team works on early warning and preparedness systems to mitigate risks and enable more effective responses. IOM also raises awareness about Protection Key Messages, helping communities mitigate the impact of disasters before, during, and after they occur. This includes house-to-house visits by trained volunteers to ensure that children, including those with disabilities and other vulnerable populations, receive necessary support.IOM regularly provides capacity-building support to volunteers and staff responsible for working with beneficiaries during emergencies, such as cyclones or landslide-related concerns. The Child Protection (CP) team responds to the needs of children and families who may become vulnerable due to natural disasters. Awareness-raising messages about the risks of the monsoon season are disseminated to children and their families through both door-to-door outreach and center-based services. Further, the IOM CP team works closely with disaster management unit volunteers who assist the most vulnerable refugees, including the elderly, children, pregnant women, and persons with disabilities or special needs. The Child Protection team also supports families living in landslide-prone areas and provides direct assistance to those in need. IOM, alongside its implementing partners, is an integral part of the Rapid Protection Assessment team, Protection Emergency Response Unit (PERU), and Mobile Medical Team (MMT), ensuring a swift response to crises and disasters. Close coordination is maintained with Site Management, CiC offices, and the Disaster Management Unit (DMU) volunteers, who are trained in DRR, safe referrals for gender-based violence (GBV), and child protection, including response to child/family separation during disasters. IOM maintains a Rohingya Response Business Continuity Plan (BCP), and contingency stocks to support overall EPR efforts for host communities and refugees via relevant Sectors and ISCG.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

Engaging with youth is a priority for IOM as whole, and in particular for IOM’s Protection Unit. The Organization is committed to developing activities targeting youth and young people on the move, addressing their needs and vulnerabilities and promoting meaningful youth engagement. The Child Protection project applies IASC Guidelines on working with and for Young People in Humanitarian and Protracted Crises. IOM Child Protection has dedicated support groups for adolescents and coordinates programming with the General Protection, GBV, and Counter-Trafficking teams look at ensure that all Youth between the ages or 15 to 24 are supported, regardless of gender and ability. Through skill development training, the Child Protection program will empower youth aged 15 to 18 years. Young parents will be specifically targeted for positive parenting initiatives. Additionally, volunteers will be recruited, with 30% to 40% of those selected falling within the 18 to 24 age group.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

IOM’s Child Protection programming promotes self-reliance in through strategies that foster resilience and empowerment. Psychosocial support, provided through support groups and recreational activities, helps children overcome the trauma of displacement. The program encourages children’s participation in decision-making, instilling a sense of agency within their communities. Protection measures safeguard children from exploitation, trafficking, and illegal labor, ensuring safe environments conducive to learning and growth. Additionally, IOM strengthens families and communities, creating stable networks that support children’s development. By linking children and families to essential services like healthcare, legal aid, and social services, barriers to independence are reduced, enabling them to navigate challenges more effectively. Together, these elements support children’s immediate well-being and lay a foundation for self-reliance within their communities.
