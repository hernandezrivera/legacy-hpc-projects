Needs

The Refugee Influx Emergency Vulnerability Assessment (REVA) as well as the Nutrition Standardised Monitoring and Assessment of Relief and Transition (SMART) report conducted by WFP and partners in November 2018, noted that the camp situation has relatively stabilized due to continued assistance, increasing economic engagement between refugees and the host community, as well as the Rohingya refugees’ own level of resilience. However, socio-economic challenges such as poverty, illiteracy and limited livelihood opportunities persist and exacerbate significant protection, nutrition and food security concerns. . Besides, pre-existing gender inequalities, gendered isolation and restricted mobility of women and girls according to traditional norms of Rohingya are limiting their access to life-saving assistance, services and information as well as their participation in decision making.Research has identified economic vulnerability as the main driver of food insecurity. Over 88 percent of Rohingya refugees remain highly-to-entirely dependent on life-saving assistance provided by the humanitarian community. This is equivalent to approximately 713,000 Rohingya individuals’ classified as “vulnerable” or “highly vulnerable”. Further exacerbating the situation is the increasing adoption of negative and unsustainable coping strategies, which adversely affect the refugee population. The majority of refugee households have sold many of the assets and savings they brought with them from Myanmar to purchase other essential needs. An increasing number of families have now resorted to monetizing portions of their food assistance, reducing food consumption and contributing to worsening food consumption outcomes at the household level. Furthermore, a gender analysis to understand the cultural practices that affect food intake by gender showed that across both (Refugee and Host) communities, men (41 percent) and boys (14 percent) are more often prioritized to eat first in the family, ahead of women (8 percent) and girls (3 percent). This finding highlights the importance of addressing discriminatory practices to ensure food and nutrition security for women, men, girls and boys through gender transformative approaches. Despite the current assistance levels, REVA (2018) found that nearly 54 percent of all the refugees remain unable to meet the minimum essential needs captured through the Minimum Expenditure Basket (MEB). The MEB equates to the poverty line, as defined by national governments and the World Bank. If assistance were eliminated or reduced, approximately 85 percent of the refugee population’s consumption rates would drop below the MEB. The Nutrition SMART survey results indicate a similar trajectory in terms of the camps’ nutrition situation. There is evidence of an overall significant improvement in nutrition outcomes compared to Round 1 (R1 Oct-Nov 2017, R2 April-May 2018) of this assessment. The prevalence of GAM among children 6-59 months per WHZ was below the 15% WHO ‘Emergency’ threshold across the camps and is categorized as “Serious” and is still higher than the UNHCR 10% acceptable levels for camp populations. Anaemia in children 6-59 months has decreased significantly from Round 1 to Round 3 but also increased significantly from Round 2 to Round 3 (SMART Survey 2018). It consequently remains near the >40% WHO threshold for Public Health Significance. A two-week period of extensive cases of diarrhoea, acute respiratory infection, and fever indicate a considerable disease burden in children under five. This is primarily attributable to the crowded camp environment, which is the world’s largest. Despite considerable expansion of nutrition treatment centres, food assistance, WASH facilities, and health services, the overall findings among the Rohingya population constitute worrying levels of malnutrition in need of ongoing nutritional support.

Activities or outputs

WFP’s general food assistance intervention in 2020 will be a hybrid modality, which combines blanket food assistance to all refugee households and a targeted additional top-up for farmers market to extremely vulnerable households. The blanket food assistance will be about US$ 10 per person per month, meanwhile the targeted top up for farmers’ market will be another 3$ per person per month for the vulnerable households. Further, we plan to pre-position a contingency stock and response capacity to provide emergency food assistance to 200,000 refugees throughout the year. This emergency food assistance will cover new arrivals, displaced families due to both natural and man-made disasters, and protection referrals. Besides WFP is also augmenting targeted food assistance interventions next year. WFP’s self reliance and skills development interventions will scale throughout the camp to support another 30,000 participants, meanwhile livelihoods intervention will be streamlined to assist 20,000 most vulnerable households in Ukhia and Teknaf. These targeting criteria is based on pre-established vulnerability profile informed by REVA II. The targeted vulnerable women for the livelihoods intervention will go from relying on fragile income sources of their husbands (daily labourers) to starting their own business and contributing significantly to the income of their families. Lastly, school feeding programme will continue to support all learning centres in the refugee camps and primary schools throughout the two sub-districts. In addition, WFP also plans to pilot cooked meals in Teknaf. While WFP’s food assistance intervention will focus on increasing dietary diversity and choice of fresh food for refugees, and at the same time also improve economic opportunity for host communities by leveraging demand for fresh food from the camps. Importantly, the support to host communities not only derives from direct projects like livelihoods and school feeding programme, but also re-designing WFP’s supply chain and procurement processes to include smallholder farmers and micro retailers in refugee food supply chain through farmers market. As part of WFP’s programmatic strategy, WFP partners will also promote financial inclusion and support opening bank accounts for participating smallholders and retailers in host communities who are currently unbanked. WFP livelihoods interventions will also identify training needs, incentives and support services to foster market linkages and business development of smallholder farmers and livelihoods groups who participate in this project. All in all, WFP’s programme operations next year includes proportional focus on the vulnerability and needs of population in both refugee camps and host communities. WFP recognizes the need for a transparent and continuous two-way communication that captures, records and relays the expressed risks, needs, capacities and aspirations of communities. WFP actions and approaches have enabled communities to participate meaningfully in key decisions throughout the programme cycle: assessment and analysis, planning and design, resource mobilization, implementation, monitoring and evaluation. In addition, beneficiaries will be well informed about their rights, entitlements, and have access to complaint and feedback mechanisms. A beneficiary feedback and complaints mechanism which includes a toll-free call centre in the WFP Bangladesh Country Office will allow beneficiaries to access information about humanitarian services, provide feedback, and to lodge complaints. WFP has ensured that a broad spectrum of the community (men, women, boys, girls, vulnerable populations) is able to participate in decision-making in a way that is inclusive, non-discriminatory and has real influence in the humanitarian response.
