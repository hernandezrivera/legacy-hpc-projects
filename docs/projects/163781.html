Project Summary

The IRC will provide primary and reproductive healthcare services by deploying mobile medical teams (MMTs) to existing MoH Primary Healthcare Centers and will maintain referral pathways between different levels of care. MMTs will provide standardized packages of primary and reproductive health services, including antenatal and post-natal care, which are aligned with the EPHS for Libya using WHO protocols and guidelines. Clinical packages also include healthcare for children and the management of childhood illnesses as well as mental health services. SGBV case workers will be trained alongside the health staff on clinical care for sexual assault survivors (CCSAS) and mental health gap action plan (mhGAP) to promote comprehensive care.
MMT activities will be complemented by robust community outreach activities. This strong link between the community and the PHCs allows for better follow up of cases and successful referrals. In addition, the IRC will procure and supply medicine, medical equipment and supplies, laboratory equipment, nutritional supplies, necessary furniture, and other supplies such as toiletries, stationeries, etc. to all supported facilities. The IRC will work to strengthen its existing referral systems with local and international actors to maximize the number of people who receive appropriate next levels of healthcare, including specialized care for complicated pregnancies and birth.
The IRC will complete minor rehabilitations of healthcare facilities to increase their capabilities to deliver the full range of services outlined in the EPHS package and ensure compliance with MoH and WHO standards. Ensuring that all facilities meet the standards for infection prevention and control is a key priority and the IRC will support MoH to make sure that all healthcare workers have sufficient knowledge and skills to protect themselves and their patients. This will likely entail the development of SOPs and trainings for staff. All training materials will be available in Arabic and English from MOH & WHO, MSF, IMC.
The IRC will provide technical training and assistance to effectively respond to emerging health needs and improve general health seeking behaviors. Technical training will cover topics such as specific disease management and adherence to treatment protocols, standard training packages related to health in humanitarian settings and minimum standards of services in crises, as well as psychological and traditional first aid. Additional trainings related to project management, specifically related to management health information systems, emergency preparedness and response, reporting into and monitoring early warning systems, and best practices in pharmaceutical management will also be provided to staff to grow their capacity to manage complex health programming.

1 - Needs

Over the past six years, Libya has suffered economic collapse, general lawlessness, and the proliferation of violent militias. Civilians in Libya continue to suffer as a result of conflict, instability, political fragmentation and economic decline culminating in a collapse of essential services. Severe shortages of health staff, medicines, supplies and equipment have been compounded by years of under-investment in the health system and a lack of transparency and accountability. According to HNO 2020, the rapid evolution of the conflict since April 2019 has led to mounting health and protection needs. For 2020, the health sector estimates that 3,970,842 people will be in need of health assistance, as compared to 554,000 in 2019. The continued fighting has restricted the movements of health workers and people’s access to healthcare and the number of mobile teams is not sufficient to meet the needs. Additionally, in 2019, the casualty rate following many clashes was high and many trauma patients did not receive timely medical assistance due to the lack of ambulances and the disrupted emergency referral system. The conflict has taken a toll on local infrastructure and reduced availability of services for Libyans and non-Libyans alike. Simultaneously, the flow of migrants into Libya from Africa and the Middle East continues, while the number of migrants departing Libya for Italy has reduced by 75% due to increased scrutiny and policing by the Libyan Coastguard. The latest Mixed Migration Centre report shows the number of migrants and refugees in Libya (Jul-Sep 2019) increased compared to previous quarters. Tripoli: According to IOM, there are 655,144 migrants in Libya, of which 20% are estimated to be in urban Tripoli, and 4,933 in detainment, including 3,133 in conflict-affected areas around Tripoli. Migrants in Detention Centers (DC) are disproportionately vulnerable to abuse, restricted from accessing primary services, and affected by outbreaks of infectious disease, such as scabies and Tuberculosis (TB). Outside of DCs, urban migrants fearing imprisonment are discouraged from seeking critical public services like free healthcare and safe protection spaces. Health is the primary need of migrants in and out of DCs, with 86% having limited or no access to healthcare. Misrata: Even prior to the clashes, Misrata was the second city (after Tripoli) for the number of migrants and refugees hosted. These non-Libyans make up 77.3% of the total people in need in Misrata according to the 2019 Humanitarian Needs Overview. Based on IRC observations across three years of integrated health and protection activities in the area, migrants are exposed to higher discrimination in Misrata’s conservative society, especially those who come from more liberal countries/societies, and struggle to understand the codes and behaviors of their host community. This is even truer for women, in particular single mothers and widowers. Southern Region: According to a recent assessment of primary health facilities conducted by WHO , 20% of PHCs in the South are currently closed. The open PHCs do not consistently offer the whole package of essential services, with antenatal care not available in 80% of open PHC facilities, immunization services not available in 28% preventive and curative services for under 5 years old children not available in 75%, NCD services not available in 57% and surgical services not available in 55% of open PHC facilities. The greatest obstacle to service provision is the absence of essential medicines: less than 3% of the facilities assessed had at least half of the top 20 essential medicines. List of assessment: 1. 2 - Activities

Primary and reproductive healthcare: The IRC will ensure availability of primary healthcare services in line with WHO guidelines through the deployment of qualified medical personnel in selected public healthcare facilities. The team will be deployed to fill critical gaps identified by initial assessments and the MoH. The MMT will provide free primary healthcare consultations for all beneficiaries, including but not limited to: maternal, newborn and child healthcare, treatment of common complications, communicable diseases (including regular reporting to Early Warning, Alert and Response System, non-communicable diseases, integrated mental health and clinical care for survivors of sexual assault. Community Health: The IRC will complement the MMT activities with the delivery of health education and hygiene promotion by community health workers (CHWs) at the targeted health facilities and the surrounding communities. The CHWs will provide information about services available and how to safely access them, as well as health education on topics such as communicable diseases, NCD, danger signs, Infant and Young Child Feeding, RH topics, mental health promotion, and prevention of avoidable mental and substance use disorders. GBV: As an essential component of IRC’s health and protection integrated approach, the MMT will embed case workers to provide SGBV case management in collaboration with health providers trained on clinical care for sexual assault survivors. Teams of female CHWs will also receive training on basic psychological first aid, psychosocial support techniques, and women’s protection approaches, allowing for a tailored response to the needs of women and girls. CHWs will provide resources and referrals during private one on one educational sessions, reproductive and maternal health related follow up. The CHWs will also contribute to building referral pathways between communities, reproductive health clinics, and available SGBV services.

4 - Protection risk analysis

Access and availability of services and staff: In 2019, the rapidly evolving conflict, exacerbated by increasing numbers of migrants and internally displaced people, led to mounting health needs. The situation was exacerbated by attacks on healthcare facilities. As of December 2019, 75 people had been killed and 49 had been injured in 60 separate attacks. More than two thirds of these attacks occurred in and around Tripoli. The continued fighting has also restricted the movements of health workers and people’s access to healthcare locations. In 2019, the casualty rate following some clashes was high and many trauma patients did not receive timely medical assistance due to the lack of ambulances and the disrupted emergency referral system. Many of these patients did not survive and others are suffering with lifelong disabilities. There is also a severe shortage of physical rehabilitation services and limited information about those that remain available (source: HI). Urban migrants fearing imprisonment are discouraged from seeking critical public services like free healthcare and safe protection spaces. Health is the primary need of migrants in and out of DCs, with 86% having limited or no access to healthcare. ( Ibid. 2) From IRC experience working with migrants and refugees, most of them feel discouraged when referred to public health facilities due to concern of discrimination specially those coming from none Arab countries. In addition, most public hospital demands migrants and refugees in need of surgical procedures to undertake blood test for HIV, Hepatitis, and TB prior to performing any procedure. Most of these tests are not available in public health facilities, hence patients need to be referred to private clinics to do these tests which costs money and time while the patient is in need to emergency procedure. As many refugees and migrants don’t have legal documentations, they discourage to go to public facilities as they are afraid to be arrested.

5 - Mitigation measures

Access and availability of services and staff: The IRC will provide comprehensive primary and reproductive healthcare services through mobile medical teams, including referrals to secondary and tertiary level clinics. In addition, the IRC will partner with Handicap International and establish referral pathways for persons with disabilities for physical rehabilitation and psychosocial support services. The IRC will work with the MoH and partners on technical and operational capacity development, including HR processes, to increase skills and create a pool of qualified applicants who can fill gaps in the project. Furthermore, the IRC will support the health facilities to develop contingency plan to respond to emerging health needs. IRC will continue to support financial costs for referral of migrants and refugees to private clinic while advocating at central MOH to have access to public health facilities.Gender Based Violence (GBV): The IRC MMT will offer integrated Clinical Care for Sexual Assault Survivor (CCSAS) services through GBV case workers, who will be embedded in the MMT. Survivors will receive clinical care and PSS support through trained health and protection staff. In addition, both health and protection staff will be trained on CCSAS multi-media training tools. Health facilities will be supplied with necessary supplies and equipment, including post-rape kits, as per IRC global GBV facility checklist to ensure services provided with dignity, confidentiality and privacy. The IRC will use the service mapping produced by the protection sector to refer and receive GBV cases to/from other actors.Mental Health and Psychosocial Support: The IRC will recruit a full-time psychologist and part-time psychiatrist as part of the MMT to provide MHPSS. General Practitioners will receive mhGAP training to provide a wide range of MH treatments together with the psychologist.

6.1 - Other accountability mechanisms

Direct beneficiaries of the Action will include vulnerable individuals such as women, girls, children, elderly, chronically-ill patients, persons with disabilities, and pregnant and lactating women. The IRC maintains a conflict-sensitive approach and provides services across statuses, including refugees, migrants, displaced Libyans, and returnees. In 2020, the IRC will focus its intervention on remote, hard-to-reach populations. The IRC’s 2020 strategy also prioritizes a needs-based, client-informed humanitarian response. This approach includes soliciting feedback from clients, and adapting projects to meet client-identified and evidence-based needs. At the onset of the Action, the IRC will conduct a series of KIIs and FGD to gather primary data about needs and access constraints for the general population and for acutely vulnerable groups such as women, people with disabilities, migrants, and ethnic minorities. IRC will conduct client exit interviews with beneficiaries, solicit feedback on services and medical teams’ behaviors, and create an open forum for additional feedback. IRC will also continue to use the feedback/complaint boxes, and feedback forms. Feedback will be analyzed and course-corrective measures taken. All new staff involved will receive training on confidentiality and information protection. Facility safety audit assessments will be conducted, and interviews and focus group discussions held with women and girls to identify and mitigate access barriers.
