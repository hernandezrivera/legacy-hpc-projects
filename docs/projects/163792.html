Project Summary

Provide access to education opportunities formal and non-formal for 50,000 vulnerable
children (Migrant, refugees, IDPs and returnees, children with special needs) affected by the
protracted crisis.
The project aims to provide educational opportunities for vulnerable children affected by
The protracted crisis. In particular, the project will target both access and quality of education,
including components related to psycho-social support needed to cope with the
consequences of the crisis. The objectives and activities proposed are in line with the
priorities of the Education Sector Strategy for 2020 for formal and non-formal education,
with a strong focus on capacity building of education personnel on child centered
pedagogies and education in emergencies. The project’s beneficiaries include Internally Displaced Persons (IDP) children, host community children, migrant and refugee children, teachers and education personnel.
The project includes the following activities: remedial classes, distribution of
learning/teaching material, school furniture, capacity building of education personnel on
psycho-social support, education in emergencies, rehabilitation of schools and WASH facilities. Furthermore, the project supports the improvement of education sector

1 - Needs

Among the total affected students, about 127,000 school aged children (including 38,500IDP, 12,500 returnees, 17,500 migrants and refugees, and 58,500 non-displaced students)are in need of support for access to quality formal and non-formal education in safe andprotective learning environments. According to the recent DTM report, there are over 230damaged schools (partially or totally damaged) whose classrooms and/or WASH facilities need to be rehabilitated.. Some schools are taking in students from other nonfunctionalschools and are operating through double-shifts- students attend school at different times (JENA 2019). Shortage of functionalclassrooms and schools are resulting in overcrowded classrooms, which has hinderedthe quality of teaching and learning and has had consequence on dropout rates andirregular attendance. School-aged children across Libya are exposed to violence in andoutside the classroom, ranging from bullying and corporal punishment to witnessing thehorrors of war. As a result of displacement, families have lost their documentation; datashows that 32 percent of displaced households (HH) have faced difficulties in their children’s access toeducation, due to loss of documentation (MSNA 2019). Despite efforts by authorities towaive the need of documentation for IDPs, in practice families still face difficulties enrolling their children in school. Migrants and refugee children’s right to access educationremains unmet; approximately 25 percent migrants and refugee children have noaccess to formal and non-formal education (MSNA 2019). The major reasons for migrant children’s low enrollment rate is due to their lack of documentation, language barriers,social discrimination, safety concerns on the way to school and in residence, longdistances to school, and school fees. To ensure that displaced and non-Libyan studentshave access to education, formal and non-formal education support is essential. Theconflict has already affected students’ school attendance, particularly adolescent boys(JENA 2019). Some boys have been found not attending schools and are working tosupport their household (among total 34 schools interviewed, 26 percent in the South and13 percent in the West reported so). Six percent of HH with children have observednegative behavior and emotional changes in their children because of conflict anddisplacement. Teachers require capacity building to teach in the emergency context andto identify and address the psycho-social needs for their students and colleagues. Mostof the school-aged children in need of education support are found in urban areas in theWestern, Eastern and Southern regions of the country. However, many of the most severeeducational needs are in the following Mantikas: Tripoli, Al Jabal AlGhabi, Benghazi, Misrata, Aljafra,Almargeb, Derna, Azzawaya, Ejdabia, Zwara and Sabha.

2 - Activities

1- Conduct remedial/ catch-up classes for 50,000 children in the affected area. 2-Conduct psychosocial support (PSS) and recreational activities for 20,000 children3-Capacity building of 1,500 teachers and education personnel on emergencypreparedness, EIE, child center approach, and PSS.4- Improve learning environment through the school rehabilitation including WASH facilities in 20 schools selected from the areas affected by conflict and displacement in Libya. 5- Distribute essential teaching learning supplies/materials, school furniture for 40,000 children, teachers or facilitators and schools in the targeted conflict affected areas. 6- Conduct Education Sector meetings, planning & review workshops, and need assessment.

4 - Protection risk analysis

While UNICEF has mitigation measures in place to reduce protection risks (see section 6) the current security situation throughout the country could potential put children at risk on their way to or during these activities. For example, children and teachers may experience risk in going to school in periods of conflict or while they are at school. Collateral damage is also putting children and teachers at risk.

5 - Mitigation measures

UNICEF operates under the principle ‘Do No Harm’ and continuously monitors activities and situations through national and international staff, national and international NGO partners as well as through contracted consultants. UNICEF provides capacity building for all teachers and partners on the prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse- PSEA and Gender Based Violence .

6.1 - Other accountability mechanisms

Field monitoring is key for timely and informed decision-making, and UNICEF is committed to its accountability and transparency to the affected populations, government and donors. The volatile situation, constraints posed by security and lack of access, however, prevent UNICEF staff from undertaking regular field monitoring visits. UNICEF Libya, therefore, engages the services of a third-party monitoring organization (ELKA organization) to conduct programmatic visits. In addition, UNICEF Libya maintains regular communication with its implementing partners and governmental actors to ensure timely and updated information on the situation of children and women inside the country.To assure quality delivery of programs and mitigate financial risks by implementing partners, UNICEF Libya conducts regular programme and financial assessments to review risks, to check assurance systems are in place and to address capacity gaps of implementing partners through the Harmonized Approach to Cash Transfers (HACT). UNICEF Libya contracts an international firm to conduct micro assessments and financial spot checks of the accounts of its partners, based on UNICEF’s partnership management. HACT implementation remains a top priority and current partners have been duly trained. A comprehensive assurance plan that incorporates activities for spot checks, programme visits, and scheduled audits is in place and monitored; it also sets the risk level for each of UNICEF Libya’s implementing partners
