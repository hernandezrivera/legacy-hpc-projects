Project Summary

This project will seek to build on gains made through programming in 2015–2018, meeting the needs of approximately 115,813 conflict and famine-affected individuals in South Sudan through an integrated Nutrition, Food Security and Livelihoods (FSL), and Water, Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) response. Under FSL programming, Agricultural Training Groups (ATGs) will be formed, seeds and tools will be distributed to beneficiaries, tree nurseries will be established, and fishing kits with training on best practices will be provided to improve future food security.
The areas of South Sudan targeted for this project include: Mayendit, Northern Bahr el Ghazal (NBeG), Abiemnhom and Mayom Counties. According to the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC), it is expected that between June and September of 2018, Mayendit, Abiemnhom and Mayom Counties will reach Crisis levels (Phase 3) for acute food insecurity; NBeG is expected to reach Emergency levels (Phase 4) classification. In addition to their levels of food insecurity, each area has been identified due to a lack of access to clean water, high rates of malnutrition, or overall poor health and hygiene practices. According to the Famine Early Warning Systems Network (FEWS NET), insecurity in South Sudan has negatively impacted markets by disrupting staple crop production and access, and increasing prices.

Needs

It is estimated that 5.4 million South Sudanese will require food and livelihoods support in 2018. According to the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC), which analyzes and categorizes food insecurity rates based on five phases of acute food insecurity, Phase 5 being Catastrophic, 48% of the population of South Sudan is facing at least Crisis levels (Phase 3) of acute food insecurity – a 40% increase in comparison with last year. This includes one million people facing Emergency (Phase 4) levels. Prolonged conflict, insecurity, and ongoing displacements have had a profound impact on crop production, allowing only an anticipated 61% of the needed cereal to be produced in 2018. In an effort to mitigate these levels of food insecurity, SP, in partnership with OFDA, is currently providing Agriculture and Food Security activities in NBeG, Abiemnhom and Mayom, and Mayendit (i.e. distribution of seeds and farm tools/equipment; trainings on vegetable farming and pest and disease control; cooking demonstrations; distribution of fishing kits; and trainings on fishing and preservation). According to a baseline assessment conducted by SP in 2017, findings indicated that over 90% of HHs in all three areas (NBeG, Mayendit, Abiemnhom and Mayom) responded that they planted staple crops, and 91% of respondents in NBeG planted vegetable crops, in comparison with 93% in Mayendit, and 87% in Abiemnhom and Mayom. Even though most HHs participated in planting crops, many of them continued to struggle with overall food security. Only 41% of HHs in Mayendit were considered food secure, another 40% were borderline, and 16% were severely food insecure. In NBeG, 47% of HHs were food secure, 29% were borderline, and 24% were severely food insecure. In Abiemnhom and Mayom, 50% of HHs indicated they were food secure, 15% were borderline, and 34% were severely food insecure. With regard to on-hand food supply, 24% of HHs in Mayendit indicated that their food stock would last less than two weeks, compared with 34% in Abiemnhom and Mayom. In NBeG, 37% of HHs indicated they had sufficient food stocks to last two to four months. When respondents were asked about fishing, 40% of HHs in Mayendit indicated they practiced fishing, compared with 27% in Abiemnhom and 19% in Mayom.

Activities

Training progressive farmers on basic vegetable and staple crop production practices namely nursery establishment and management, transplanting, and harvesting. Food production and agricultural training will build on prior work completed in 2017-2018 for Mayendit with programming in new areas (new target areas in northern Mayendit are the following: Mirnyal, Leah parts of Thaker payams) informed from previous lessons learned. A total of 500 progressive farmers (50 in Abiemnhom, 50 in Mayom, 300 in Mayendit, 100 in NBeG) will be selected to each lead a group of 30 members, forming ATGs. Tree seedling distribution through the establishment of two tree nurseries, one each in southern and northern Mayendit. Distributing vegetable and staple crop seeds to target beneficiaries. Using the progressive farmer’s farm as the distribution center for the neighboring farmers. Purchasing and distributing watering cans and treadle pumps for irrigating the farms to boost crop production during the dry season, and provide nutrition training to farmers, promoting the benefits of dietary diversity. Building capacity for extension workers and county support staff in order to promote ownership and sustainability of the program in the community and in order to community resources and collaborate with their institutions. Providing selected farmers with basic business skills training to support the marketing of their grain harvest. Additionally, SP will continue to take the following steps to ensure protection of seeds and crops: (1) promoting safe utilization of organic fertilizers, including organic manure; (2) encouraging crop rotation to mitigate the buildup of pathogens and pests that often occurs when one species is continuously cropped; (3) endorsing inter-cropping to limit outbreaks of crop pests by increasing predator biodiversity; (4) increasing crop diversity by planting various crops on one plot; and (5) utilizing organic pesticides prepared traditionally by the farmers on an as needed basis.

How does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

The agricultural technical groups (ATGs) are a participatory approach, bringing together groups of farmers to disseminate best farming practices. The ATGs will be formed by consulting community leaders and neighboring members, to designate the lead farmer’s plot in a central location to help facilitate trainings. Lead farmers will be selected in consultation with community leaders based on (1) accessibility of the farm, (2) willingness of the farmer to share knowledge with others, (3) ability to influence other farmers, and (4) use of their resources, i.e. labor on the farm. In certain counties where there is access to permanent water sources (river, pond, lake, or ground source water), farmers will also be selected based on proximity to water sources, extending the planting season and opportunity for ATGs to learn farming practices. Farmers will similarly be selected in consultation with the payam chiefs and be registered by field monitors based on their willingness to learn from other farmers and replicate the acquired knowledge on their own farms, with factors such as socio-economic status, gender sensitivity and vulnerability taken into consideration.

Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse

Yes

Briefly describe how

Since women are traditionally responsible for agricultural activities, this intervention will primarily target women as the progressive farmers for each group. Women face the triple burden of leading production for remuneration, managing child rearing and HH duties, and engaging in community and religious meetings. Enabling women to engage in a more productive means of supplying food to their families will decrease the overall time spent in searching for food, such as gathering water lilies from swamps that may be far away or walking long distances to a neighboring area/county to carry back food rations from relatives. Time spent in receiving inputs and trainings has decreased with SP scheduling distributions at the appropriate time and season. In support of this, SP will ensure that trainings and other program activities are scheduled around inputs and training, allowing women to choose the most convenient time for them. It is also anticipated that increased agricultural yields will contribute towards reducing the prevalence of gender-based violence within the HH: communities in NBeG have previously noted that violence within the HH is more prevalent during periods of food insecurity and poverty. In certain target locations, SP will encourage men to share the burden women bear in agricultural production, through gender sensitization workshops. The workshops will create awareness on gender inequalities in terms of different roles and decision-making processes within the HH with regards to food usage and control of productive assets. In 2015-16, SP piloted such an approach as part of its Global Affairs Canada (GAC)-funded project in NBeG, which met with some success in increased male assistance to women in activities related to agricultural production.

Community Engagement?

Yes

Briefly describe how

AAP will be operationalized through integration of IASC commitments including reporting and coordinating with the cluster system. SP will enhance the capacity of its staff, beneficiaries and community committees in accountability and participatory monitoring and evaluation. Community participation at all stages of the project cycle will be encouraged and ensured through community meetings with local leaders (Village elders, water management committees, and health committees). This will ensure that beneficiaries provide feedback on their level of satisfaction with the quality and effectiveness of the assistance received and project activities. Samaritan’s Purse will use qualitative methods during bi-annual assessments to understand the community’s opinions, feelings, beliefs and perspectives on how to improve the program. Each sector will develop a dissemination strategy for sharing lessons learned internally as well as externally. PMs will also forward evaluation findings to local authorities and beneficiaries’ representatives like lead farmers and lead mothers to enable findings to be synthesized and linked to program planning adjustments. For all these purpose, the evaluation findings and lessons will be presented in a brief, reader friendly summary formats tailored for the targeted groups. Stakeholder´s workshops will also be held during the program opening and closing to discuss the project plans, roles of partners and expectations. Project managers and beneficiaries will meet quarterly to discuss the progress of the project. SP has a policy and system of addressing complaints which enables beneficiaries and staff to report complaints and seek redress safely. The policy is open to all program stakeholders including suppliers, staff and beneficiaries through direct open-door contact with project staff and management.
