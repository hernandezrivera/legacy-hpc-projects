Project Summary

Cyclical climate shocks, including drought and flooding, which is challenging traditional rural livelihoods, as well as ongoing conflict, together contribute to growing internal displacement in Somalia, with an estimated 2.6 million women, men, girls and boys currently displaced. Between January and August 2020, conflict, flooding and lack of livelihoods contributed to the displacement of 893,000 people across the country. IDPs comprise 30% of Somalis in IPC 3 and 4 requiring lifesaving assistance in 2021.
FSNAU and FEWS NET projected in May 2020 that around 3.5 million Somalis would be in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) and Emergency (IPC Phase 4) through at least September 2020. An additional 3 million Somalis were anticipated to be Stressed (IPC Phase 2) and engaging in negative coping strategies to meet their food and non-food needs.
Desert Locust affected rural livelihoods in Northern and Central regions and presents a risk (serious) to the South. The 2020 Gu seasonal flooding (river flooding) contributed to loss and damage to property, housing, farmland and infrastructure. The riverine areas of Hiran, Middle Shabelle, Lower Shabelle, Gedo, Middle Juba and Lower Juba were some of the worst-affected areas. The COVID-19 pandemic and associated socio-economic impacts also contributed to the food insecurity situation in Somalia. Conflict/insecurity led to further population displacement and disruption to 2020 Gu season agriculture in some areas, including Lower Shabelle and Middle Juba.
Many of the IDPs rely on relief aid and have no skills or capital to start alternative livelihoods. In Mogadishu (Deynille, Dharkeynley and Kaxda), Jubaland (Kismayo, Dolow, Bardere and Belet-Hawa), South West (Biadoa, Afgoye and Wanlaweyn), Galmadug (Abudwak and Dhusamareb) currently lead in the number of IDPs hosted.
Targeting will be carried out according to clear and transparent targeting criteria involving all stakeholders. Systematic respect of the “do no harm” principle will also be adhered to. Protection principles wil be mainstreamed to ensure respect for the rights of all individuals, regardless of their age, gender, or social, ethnic, national, religious or other background, in accordance with the relevant bodies of law (e.g. human rights law, international humanitarian law and refugee law).
The proposed project will target 3590 households representing 21,540 beneficiaries (5,385 women, 6,462 girls, 5816 boys, and 3,877 men) including 2,154 people with disabilities.
The overall objective of the project is to improve access to food and protection of livelihood assets through 1) Provision of agricultural skills training for 800 IDPs to eventually increase household income Gedo, Lower Shabelle and Banadir Regions. 2) Provision of unconditional cash transfers for 2,790 IDPs, urban and rural destitute households in Gedo, Lower Shabelle and Banadir regions giving priority to women and child-headed households. 3) Provision of support with appropriate agricultural farm inputs (e.g. quality seed, farm tools, land preparation, and irrigation support); for 500 River Juba and Shabelle riverine farmers Gedo and Lower Shabelle Regions. 4) Provision of support training to 500 farmers on modern crop husbandry to help increase the production of crops to all regions. 5) Undertaking fodder cultivation support for 500 riverine farmers and agro-pastoralists in Gedo and Lower Shabelle Regions. 6) Provision of biopesticides and training to 500 farmers to control armyworm, and other crop pests to eliminate them from farmer’s ﬁelds in Gedo, Lower Shabelle Regions.
CoDHNet will maintain regular communication, and coordination mechanisms with all the project stakeholders including the community, local authority, regional food security cluster, cluster partners, and will submit monthly reports through 4W Matrix to FSC and OCHA will receive monthly financial and narrative reports for accountability purposes.

What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

Flexibility and choice: One size does not ﬁt all! Using cash or vouchers recognizes that beneficiaries are best placed to decide what their households need, and gives them the flexibility to choose according to their own diverse needs and priorities. – Dignity: In allowing the beneficiaries to control what goods and services their households need, cash transfers are considered by many as more dignified than receiving in kind, and recognize beneficiaries as active participants in providing for the welfare of their families after a disaster. Empowerment: The availability of cash gives households a sense of restored power over their immediate situation. In addition, there is evidence that receiving cash can at least temporarily empower women within the household. Access to financial structures such as bank accounts may empower beneficiaries, in particular women, in the longer-term as well. Supports or revitalizes local trade: Injections of cash or the use of commodity vouchers with local traders benefits the recovery of trade and the local economy. Cash-based support for livelihood rehabilitation can potentially also provide a link between relief and longer term economic recovery and may offer the opportunity for beneficiaries to increase income and create savings. Reduces negative coping strategies: When aid is given in kind, households are often forced to seek cash in ways that may be detrimental to longer term livelihoods, for example selling productive assets, or giving children to the care of relatives. Evidence shows that cash transfers reduce the negative coping strategies used by beneﬁciaries. Multiplier effects: By stimulating local markets, cash-based interventions may beneﬁt more than the direct recipients of cash. Local traders and service providers, transport workers, and suppliers and transporters of goods may all beneﬁt from households having money to spend. Time and cost-efficient for agencies and participants: When goods are available and accessible locally, cash or voucher-based interventions have the potential to be more rapid than bringing supply from out of the country. When beneﬁciaries use local markets, agencies also eliminate logistical and import costs. However, the additional staff, security, and logistical resources sometimes required to distribute cash or manage a voucher program must also be factored in.

Mitigation Measures

o Keep lists of beneﬁciaries and personal data conﬁdential and safe from unintended use - apply effective data protection measures. o Ensure the location of and access routes to distribution points and services are safe with enough space for orderly distribution. o Identify central locations - beneﬁciaries should not have to traverse long distances or pass through conﬂict zones to access distribution points. o Distributions should take place in daylight hours giving beneﬁciaries suﬃcient time to travel home before nightfall. o Identify groups at risk of unsafe livelihoods practices (eg. child labour survival/transactional sex) and prioritize provision of alternatives for these groups. • Develop livelihoods programmes in a way that protects and supports household caring responsibilities and promotes family unity. Participation in livelihoods opportunities should not undermine child protection or other caring responsibilities. Consider employing care providers, providing care facilities (nursery) or supporting community care mechanisms to enable women’s participation in such programs. • Promote equitable and impartial access to all • Apply a systematic outreach process in registering beneﬁciaries – otherwise vulnerable persons, such as older people, may easily be excluded as other more mobile and vocal population groups are registered. • Ensure lack of documentation does not exclude individuals from accessing services Use this information to inform monitoring activities and identify any barriers to access, discrimination against particular groups or whether assistance is being diverted. Take this into consideration when determining locations for food security and livelihood interventions. • Ensure the location, distance and access routes to the distribution points is accessible to all members of the community, including the most vulnerable groups. • Separate male and female queuing areas and ensure female staff presence at distributions so that they are accessible to women and girls. • Introduce special arrangements for persons who have diﬃculty accessing the distribution point or unable to carry the rations (such as persons with restricted mobility or marginalised groups) community mobilisation, home/mobile distributions, youth groups assisting older or chronically ill persons. It should not be assumed that friends and familywilldo it. • Ensure that both men and women have equal and fair access to work opportunities (including cash/foodfor-work schemes), micro-credit, cash transfers, agricultural inputs and skills training and that they receive the same beneﬁts for their input and work. Risk that beneﬁciaries are not accessing/using complaint and feedback mechanisms • Set up accessible, conﬁdential and well understood feedback mechanisms for suggestions and complaints with a view to improve programming, understand community perceptions, promote beneﬁciary empowerment and assist in detecting misconduct. Eg. place feedback surveys in food baskets, feedback box at distribution site, phone lines • Set up mechanisms for submitting feedback that do not require the beneﬁciary exposing themselves to project staff. • RESPOND to complaints, regardless of whether corrective measures can/need to be put in place. • Consider a joint feedback mechanism with other sectors to minimize confusion. Risk of explosive hazard contamination in project target area • Avoid placing distribution points near military installations or threats such as attacks, stray bullets, crossﬁre or violence.• Weapon bearers should not be present inside distribution points.
