Project Summary

IRC’s Bangladesh aim is to contribute to the safety of children and adolescents at home and in their communities. As a CPSS partner IRC will adopt the child ecological model specifically targeting Children, Care giver/parents (family) and the community to strengthen the protective environment for children and adolescents through prevention, response and restorative interventions. The project purpose is; 1)Promoting a community-based approach to the response, support community self-protection mechanisms and facilitate meaningful access to specialized services for persons at heightened protection risk, including girls, boys, women and men of all ages and with diverse needs and vulnerabilities, with the ultimate aim of mitigating exposure to protection risks, strengthen the resilience of affected communities, and place communities at the center of the response in line with AAP principles and contribute to 2)Boys and girls including adolescents facing life-threatening risks of abuse, neglect, violence, exploitation, and severe distress have access to well-coordinated and gender-sensitive quality child protection services by 2022.
IRC will achieve the objectives by improving access to psycho-social support for children, adolescents and their caregivers by applying Safe Healing and Learning Spaces (SHLS) model that has a structured Social-emotional learning (SEL) curriculum for Children aged 6- 11 and a structured parenting education curriculum for care givers of the same children. SEL curriculum is a conducted for a period of 36 weeks per cycle and addresses the stresses refugee children to support the regulate their emotions, ultimately lowering signs of distress and giving them a predictable future. IRC will also support adolescent girls and boys aged 12 -19 through SAFE (Supporting Adolescent and their Families in Emergency) curriculum. The structured SAFE curriculum aims to increase adolescent safety and participation, strengthen family and peer support, and equip them with positive coping strategies and reduce their vulnerability to risky behaviors such as substance misuse, violence or crime among others. The curriculum is implemented for a period of 12 weeks while the caregivers of adolescents will be trained for a period of 8 weeks. IRC will also provide comprehensive case management services to children at risk and strengthen community based child protection mechanisms (CBCBM) to enable communities protect their own children. A review of existing Child Protection Committees (CPC) will be done to enhance their capacity in executing their roles and responsibilities. CPC’s will be supported in identification, design and implementation of CP activities which guarantees ownership of CP interventions and sustainability of actions beyond IRC presence.
IRC will conduct a results-based study in partnership with Columbia University focused on SHLS to measure the impact the curriculums on child outcomes. IRC will sample CPSS partner CFS facilities as a control group to understand what is working and what is not working.
IRC target 2532 children aged 6-11 and adolescents aged 12- 19 in 5 refugee camps and 1 Upazila in Nhilla Union of Teknaf. 1344 care givers will benefit through parenting sessions and 6 CBCPC’s will be operationalized. IRC will require 455,400 US dollars.
IRC will empower children and their families in disaster risk reductions conduct safety walks, risk mapping and contingency planning. IRC will promote inclusive service provision and accountability through client feedback and equitable service provision and collect data in sex, age and diversity. Linkages with other such as GBV and education Sector and promote children’s participation, child safeguarding policy and promote do no harm. CCCCP project produces sustainable impact at child level, family level and community level and CBCPC’s will linked with formal system to provide protective environment after the project ends for sustainability.

Needs

Boys and girls represent 55% of all refugees, the majority of whom need child protection (CP) services, especially due to the emergence of new protection risks including refugee children reporting feeling unsafe and vulnerable to violence, including sexual violence as reported by CPSS. The unaccompanied and separated children continue to be vulnerable to sexual violence, child labor, child marriage and trafficking. These vulnerability factors continue to increase rendering children in adversity psychosocial distress, lack of adequate services and spaces to promote child development needs. The congested camp settings pause challenges like traffic accidents, injuries, drowning and landslides. Furthermore, children in the camps and in the surrounding host communities are extremely vulnerable to forced labor, trafficking, and hazardous child labor. From the CPSS cluster assessed needs, child marriage continues to be a major concern for girls and two-thirds of these households consider it a normal practice, while approximately one-third use it as a coping strategy to either protect girls or reduce economic burdens on the household. Parents/ caregivers are not exempt from the effects of this crisis as the context has continued to constrain their livelihood opportunities impacting their ability to provide a nurturing care and protection for children wellbeing outcomes. The community safety nets and protections systems have also disintegrated leading to poor community social support system and networks. It is therefore urgent that children, caregivers, families and communities become the major focus of any child protection interventions. Host Community; The host communities also suffer under the weight of the refugee influx which causes economic insecurity driven by the ecological degradation of over 4,000 acres of forest removed to settle the Rohingya, which was traditionally the main source of livelihood opportunities. children in host communities face pressure on limited space attributed by some host community members to the large number of refugees has been reported as a factor contributing to inter-community tensions. In December 2018 reported the presence of at-risk children, with 5% of households reporting the presence of children who were already married/about to get married and 32% of women and 3% of men aged 20-25 who married before reaching 18 years of age.24, 8% reporting the presence of at least one child working; and 52% of working children reportedly engaged in hazardous work namely working more than 40 hours per week and working with machinery/lifting heavy objects. Research; Child friendly spaces (CFS) including SHLS facilities are widely used approaches to provide psychosocial support to children in emergencies however, little evidence is documented on their outcomes and impacts. As the Rohingya crisis becomes protracted and the resources are dwindling, it’s imperative that all programing are more evidence based for efficiency and effectiveness. IRC will partner with Columbia university to highlight significant benefit and gains made through the implementation of the SHLS approach and sample CPSS partner facilities to understand specifically what is working and what is not working to inform facility based programing and the structured PSS response as a high priority intervention in child protection.

Activities or outputs

1. Improving the quality of CP services for boys and girls most at-risk and survivors of violence, exploitation and abuse (including those recruited into criminal groups, child labor, unaccompanied and separated children, children with disabilities and those exhibiting severe signs of distress; • Enrollment and registration of 960 children for SEL session in five camps • Facilitate positive parenting skills sessions for 480 parents/caregivers of children 6-11 in 5 SHLS facilities• Recruitment and training of 10 SEL facilitators on CP basic concept and SEL curriculum• Maintenance of 5 SHLS in camps 1E,1W, 8E, 22 and 23 • Establish mobile psychosocial support for 160 children and 80 care givers using the SEL curriculum in the Nhilla Union in Teknaf • Strengthening evidence based programing for IRC and CPSS partners on structured Safe Healing and Learning Spaces (SHLS)/ CFS through research with Columbia university 2. Case management and alternative care will be harmonized and extended to other key line ministries while efforts will be made to engage all stakeholders to address CP concerns based on global learning and field-evidence on core child protection issues; • Recruitment and orientation of 12 case workers in 5 camps and host community on basic CP and GBV concepts among others• Capacity building of case workers on particular vulnerabilities such as caring for child survivors including boy’s survivors of sexual abuse through CPSS partners and capacity of case management supervisors on coaching and supervision• Establish a case management fund to support vulnerable children to access services including medical care and other referral services. 3. Increasing the capacity of community-based child protection mechanisms (CBCPM) to prevent and mitigate CP risks through multi-layered, cross sectoral psychosocial support interventions IRC will work in 5 refugee camps and Nhilla Union of Teknaf on the following:• Capacity building of 6 CBCPC’s on how to identify children exhibiting signs of abuse and link them with Case management for specialized services• Train CBCPC on child protection, their roles and responsibilities and develop action plans • Support 12 community awareness/mobilization to advocate against harmful practices and emerging child protection trends and risks using CBCPC’s • Facilitate quarterly and monthly reflection and planning workshops for CBCPC respectively• Establish community accountability and feedback mechanism on client satisfaction on services provided4. Continue to work with the Education Sector and selected partners to ensure that adolescent boys and girls have access to life-skills, peace-building initiatives, individual case management services, Adolescents Sexual Reproductive Health (ASRH), specialized MHPSS and;• Provide training for 960 adolescent girls and boys using SAFE curriculum for adolescents aged 12-19 in camp 1E,1W,8E,22 and 23. • Facilitate mobile SAFE training for 120 adolescents in Nhilla Union in Teknaf. • Facilitate adolescents who graduate from SAFE training to establish peer clubs • Provide specialized caregiver’s parenting session for 720 parents of adolescent five refugee camps and 120 caregivers in Nhilla union.• Facilitate quarterly adolescent talent exhibition and competition aimed at reaching 100 in 5 refugee camps through existing IRC SHLS facilities.
