This emergency response project aims to serve 55,000 individuals from disaster-affected households in IPC 4/5 in Jonglei state with cash/voucher assistance to meet their immediate food and nutrition needs, and also inputs and training to help them to recover their livelihoods.

1 - Needs

In 2023, the greater Jonglei area of South Sudan will face a dire food security situation, requiring scaled-up and sustained humanitarian assistance to save lives and prevent suffering from hunger, malnutrition, and disease. South Sudan's populations in need of humanitarian assistance, particularly those in food security crisis or emergency are predicted to be among the worst figures since the country’s independence 11 years ago. Jonglei State is among the most-affected by the current food crisis: Per the most recent IPC data (October 2022): 1,510,000 people (74% of the population) in Jonglei state is predicted to face food security crisis or emergency (IPC 3+) from Apr-Jul 2023, with 690,000 predicted to face emergency (IPC4) and 69,000 facing catastrophe (IPC5). For HHs whose 2022 harvests are destroyed by flooding, meaningful food security improvement is not feasible until at least the 2023 harvest of cereal crops, approximately a year away. These populations are in urgent need of sustained humanitarian assistance to access basic meals and avoid malnutrition and disease, as well as support to rebuild livelihoods for the future.The current food crisis is driven by multiple factors including protracted sub-national conflict and violence, political instability, national economic crisis, climate-change driven shocks which include both drought and flooding and the COVID-19 pandemic. At the same time, local currency value has depreciated, and the price of staple foods have continued to soar in line with regional and global commodity price trends driven by the global food crisis and conflict in Ukraine, as well as localized market supply disruption due to flooding. Following three previous years of unprecedented flooding, 2022 is turning out to be a fourth successive year of severe flooding, forcing many families to abandon their homes and flee to safer areas. Jonglei state is among the most severely affected both in severity and extent of flooding. The majority of the population in Jonglei State are agro-pastoralists relying on subsistence farming. However, three years of successive catastrophic flooding (2019-2022) have destroyed croplands, pastures, and productive assets like livestock, contributing to low agricultural production, and forcing some to abandon their homes and villages or isolated them from others. Severe flooding has cut off the over-land access to these communities for the better parts of 2019 -2022. Families affected by this emergency are unable to secure even basic meals and are completely dependent on humanitarian food assistance, due to lack of livelihoods and diminished purchasing power. Vulnerable populations, especially those that have been displaced or are isolated by floodwaters and destroyed roads, face not only hunger but also dramatically elevated risks of malnutrition and communicable diseases. Children, vulnerable adults such as pregnant or lactating women, and persons with disabilities are disproportionately affected. At a time of increased needs, limited donor resources have led to the scale-down in lifesaving humanitarian programs, including reduced food distribution funded by WFP. This leaves vulnerable populations, in particular women and children, with limited or no access to life-saving services. CRS has been implementing the USAID/BHA-funded Responding to Emergency Nutrition, Health and WASH (RENHW) and Resilience and Food Security Program (RFSP) in several counties of Jonglei state, but these have recently ended, resulting in a lack of access to essential life-saving services for communities that previously depended on these programs. CRS will continue implementing the USAID funded Pathways to Resilience (P2R) in counties including Duk and Akobo, and those activities are reflected in this plan.

2 - Project activities

The project will prioritize targeting of HHs who are most vulnerable or at highest risk of malnutrition, including displaced families. CRS will provide unconditional cash transfers to HHs in IPC 4+ during lean season to help families purchase food or other essential needs during most severe months of food insecurity. Where appropriate, CRS will consider coupling cash assistance with beneficiary participation in inclusive community activities (following the WFP GFD+ model) that aim to improve community assets or expand access to physical, livelihood and social assets and markets - (e.g. water pans, feeder roads, community infrastructure), or which strengthen HH and community knowledge (e.g. training on climate-adaptive and nutrition sensitive agriculture practices, GBV awareness training, hygiene or nutrition sensitization, social cohesion messaging); participation will not be held strictly as a condition for cash assistance, but will give beneficiary HHs and communities opportunities to strengthen foundations of self-reliance, while increasing buy-in and agency, and promoting social cohesion. (Est 4 months @ $30/HH/month; final values will be coordinated with FSL Cluster, CWG, and WFP, based on markets) As families are being supported by emergency cash assistance, the program will also provide training and inputs to help them rebuild sustainable livelihoods. CRS will link with FAO for provision of seeds and tools. During dry season (Jan – May), CRS will support these HHs with small-scale vegetable farming kits they can use with easy hand-irrigation on land or in container gardens, helping provide nutritious meals throughout dry season. During the same period, CRS will provide small livestock in suitable locations, or fishery kits where land has been flooded and there is fishing potential. As the next planting season approaches, CRS will provide seeds/tools to plant staple cereal crops and vegetables ahead of 2023 rains, ensuring families harvest and supporting early recovery of livelihoods. In the short term the program will ensure families and communities avoid sickness, hunger, and negative coping mechanisms like selling assets, and will ultimately help get HHs on track to re-build sustainable food security and resilience to future shocks. WFP-funded general food distribution is ongoing in all most areas of Jonglei (CRS currently implements WFP GFD / GFD+ in Ayod, Duk, Pibor, Akobo); however this food distribution is not designed to cover 100% of a family’s food needs, and CRS field teams indicate that due to pipeline and funding challenges, distributions can be inconsistent, while reach is not universal across the counties. This means that the food security assistance available from WFP is likely to be inadequate during the current emergency, especially for those with elevated vulnerability, and many HHs will likely turn to negative coping mechanisms or face the loss of precious livelihood assets. CRS’ will respond to the urgent life-saving needs arising from the current food security emergency by complementing food assistance available from WFP in close coordination with WFP (either complementing geographically to reach communities not included in food distribution, or complementing for most-vulnerable HHs especially those at high risk of malnutrition with top-up cash transfers)to ensure sustained access to food during the lean season when vulnerability and risk is highest. PROJECT GOALS AND OBJECTIVES This emergency response project aims to serve disaster-affected households with assistance to meet their immediate food and nutrition needs, and also help them to recover their livelihoods. 5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

Rapid response analysis including market assessment and protection risk analysis will be conducted by CRS teams working in each county prior to implementation; Based on recent response analysis by CRS in Jonglei counties, as a generalization, cash is viewed as the most appropriate modality because: 1) Information from CRS field teams implementing cash indicate that markets in many locations are functional and interconnected with main supplies in the key market of Juba for re-supply (by road or boat). CRS will take into account seasonality of access and its effects on market functionality before programming cash in any location. 2. Cash is strongly preferred by local project participants as it provided more ability in decision making on their needs 3. The local government supports the use of cash for aid in the counties 4. Financial Service Providers were available and have capacity to safely deliver cash in the targeted areas. 6. It is much more cost effective and more logistically realistic to conduct cash transfers rather than in kind assistance in greater Jonglei, and any organizations are now implementing cash-based assistance.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Among feedback and complaints channels, CRS will ensure that there is at least one channel that gives community members an opportunity to share sensitive complaints related to safeguarding and fraud issues in a confidential manner. CRS projects also actively seeks feedback from beneficiaries through informal discussions with all the projects beneficiaries who include the most vulnerable members.All sensitive feedback (feedback regarding suspicions of fraud, protection violations, or exploitation, abuse, and harassment) will be handled by the Country Representatives of the respective organizations or CRS’ online reporting mechanism. Focal points assigned to each field location will train staff on confidential reporting using CRS’ online FRM. A fair and impartial FRM will be set up in collaboration with the communities. Throughout the project cycle, CRS and partners will create awareness on the project’s feedback and complaint channels (two channels in each target community). These may include channels such as suggestion boxes, focus group discussions, and in-person reporting to trusted focal points.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

CRS consults beneficiaries on their preferred feedback channels and engages key stakeholders at different levels at the appropriate targeting of individuals. CRS disseminates knowledge on services provided through IEC, mass messaging, and community meetings. Projects also conduct education during pre-registration and distribution, which highlights the existence of a response and feedback mechanism and how the mechanism can be accessed.Beneficiaries and other community members are sensitized, particularly during distributions, on the available CFRMs and how to report any concerns.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

The project will comply with CRS Protection and Safeguarding policy. CRS mainstreams protection into its programming, including through the following: 1) CRS incorporates risk assessment into project design and implementation, including considering risks around GBV and intra-household conflict/violence that may be elevated by provision of cash or assets. 2) The MEAL system, and particularly PDMs, gather information about decision-making within HHs around cash (there is a question about who makes decisions), providing the project team with data which is triangulated with field monitoring visits and feedback received through CFRMs, to understand any unintended consequences of the intervention and adapt programming accordingly. 3) CRS livelihood interventions commonly target women per vulnerability criteria, and in this case provide assets that are culturally appropriate for women to own and govern (e.g. small livestock vs. cattle). 4) All CRS staff as well as local authorities and community leaders are trained on reporting and referral systems in place for GBV, alongside the code of conduct, the free assistance they should expect, the targeting/inclusion method. 5) Distribution points are located to ensure vulnerable beneficiaries can access with safety and dignity, and without traveling long distances. 6) Beneficiaries and other community members are sensitized, particularly during distributions, on the available CFRMs and how to report any concerns.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The project has a database for feedback. CRS also conducts regular review of feedback received and consider any changes to the project. All feedback will be responded to individually or in groups, as appropriate. CRS policy requires feedback is responded to or referred within 90 days from when it was received, and that feedback is analyzed to inform project decisions. Feedback will be managed by dedicated gender and protection staff in collaboration with the MEAL team to ensure communities are aware of how to submit feedback and expectations regarding responses.

Yes

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

By providing cash/voucher assistance, and interventions aimed at increasing crop and livestock production as well as promoting dietary diversity, they project will reduce the number of food insecure HHs in targeted areas. Where implemented, improvement of community assets such as feeder roads and water pans will help communities strengthen climate-adapted and nutrition-focused food production, build resilience to future shocks through preparedness and increased adaptive and absorptive capacities, and strengthen their access to markets leading to increased access to opportunities for income generation and strengthened access to food as well. The project prioritizes community engagement and buy-in of diverse groups within the community, building on strengths and capacities existing in communities, while being trauma-informed and conflict sensitive. All of this will strengthen HH and community capacity to ensure food security even during future shocks, reducing food-related displacement.

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

CRS will train project staff, community leaders, and other key stakeholders on GBV; CRS will ensure all project staff are trained to refer survivors of GBV with safety and dignity; CRS will ensure project monitoring and evaluation and accountability to affected populations system (complaints and feedback mechanisms) are designed to capture information about GBV from diverse project participants and community members, and to systematically reflect on this data and feedback to inform adaptive action. The projects deliver safe programming that ensures activities do not exacerbate established risk and vulnerability factors and seek to challenge gender inequity in a safe, dignified, and respectful manner. CRS will deliver clear statements of zero tolerance of inaction on sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) and routinely share information with staff, partners, and communities on CRS’ Code of Conduct policy and expected behaviors.
