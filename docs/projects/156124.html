Project Summary

UNHCR is a lead agency coordinating the shelter responses and implementing shelter programme from inside Syria, as well as from Gaziantep (Turkey). UNHCR shelter project is designed to ensure the affected populations and returnees shelter needs are met and to continue the support of IDPs and returnees returning to their houses.
Particular specific and vulnerable groups including female-headed households, youth, and persons with disabilities, elderly persons at risk and large-size families will be particularly considered within the response.
The shelter program will further distinguish between shelter needs of short-term and long-term solutions. Therefore, in 2019, the shelter assistance will continue to provide an emergency response through life-saving, life-sustaining quick impact solutions, such as rehabilitation of collective shelters, upgrading of private collective structures, distribution and installation of shelter kits and tents along with provision of technical assistance. Equally, the shelter program will enhance sustainable, recovery-oriented shelter solutions through support to IDPs returning and returnees to their original homes, contribute to rehabilitation of buildings in newly accessible areas, and support urbanization projects in various stages of completion. UNHCR’ shelter response will also contribute to public infrastructure rehabilitation to ensure sustainable community development. The shelter program will be aligned with the Syrian Humanitarian Response Plan as well as the Shelter Sector Strategy and coordinated with the different stakeholders and partners such as MoLAE and national/international NGOs.
UNHCR’s shelter programme aims to address shelter needs of 26,000 vulnerable households/130,500 individuals through basic house repairs and to improve the shelter conditions of 20,000 households/110,000 individuals by providing 20,000 emergency shelters kits. As well as providing emergency shelter to 100,000 individual /20,000 households through tents distribution, camp construction, collective shelter and unfinished buildings, to provide safe and proper access to shelter for affected population in returnee areas. UNHCR will support the returnee communities by removing 250,000 cubic meter of debris that will support 500,000 individuals/100,000 households and install 21,000 solar street light column with device in 30 neighbourhoods to support 750,000 individuals /150,000 returnee families and host community. To ensure our program will be implemented with adequate project management and technical skills 225 engineers from local government will be trained over the course of the project.
Objective 1: Provide life-saving and life-sustaining shelter
Objective 2: Contribute towards the resilience and cohesion of communities and households by improving housing and related community/public infrastructure

Needs

After eight years of protracted crisis in Syria, the shelter and infrastructure situation continues to worsen, in terms of available shelter capacity, adequacy of shelter conditions, ability of people to repair or rehabilitate their own damaged shelter, and affordability of rent. Shelter needs remain high and are one of the most important factors causing the deterioration of population’s living condition as well as socio economic vulnerability. Despite the improvement of the security situation in most of the governorates, the increase in shelter related need is particularly acute in more densely populated urban areas (Idleb and western Aleppo) including in areas with high level of returns, with an increase in the number of people in need of shelter assistance of almost 14% to 4.7 million people over the last year (as per 2019 HNO).While the crisis continues to induce new displacements requiring an emergency response in North East Syria, Idleb and Westren Aleppo, there is also a need to address the shelter needs of the populations who returned to their original houses and areas in Rural Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, Hama, Dara’a and Al Hassakeh governorates as the security situation in these governorates has generally improved over the last year.The range of needs is diverse across different groups, including newly displaced families, those suffering long term and multiple displacements, families in substandard collective shelters, families in makeshift camps, those trying to remain in their own damaged homes, and those who returned to their original houses and areas. There is a lack of adequate housing and limited access to existing housing. The continuous increase in prices of construction material has significantly reduced the ability of the affected populations and returning populations to repair their houses. Specific requirements vary widely between groups and locations with different experiences of and exposure to the crisis, requiring flexibility and adaptability of approaches and modalities. Damaged houses, coupled with non-functional public service networks in return governorates such as Aleppo, Dara’a, Al Qunitra, Hama, Ar Raqqa, Deir Azzor and Rural Damascus, form the main challenges for the population to settle back in their original areas. The complexity of the shelter and housing situation is increased due to housing land and property (HLP) issues. Repair and rehabilitation of housing is only pursued following confirmation of HLP rights; however, availability of documentation is a pervasive issue, and the processes and infrastructure to formalize or recuperate documents are not uniformly functional, accessible or recognized. There is an increased risk of eviction and/or exploitation with major protection implications, while there are also risks of rights violations (either of owners or occupants) and potential disputes due to difficulties in demonstrating ownership. While hosting can be a good solution, the pressures on host communities are significant. The financial burden experienced by the host communities and the social obligations felt by the displaced families are exacerbated by overcrowding, placing a strain on already weakened facilities and infrastructures.

Implementation of activities

In its role of a lead agency coordinating the shelter responses from inside Syria, as well as from Gaziantep (Turkey), UNHCR will continue to provide an emergency response through life saving, life sustaining quick impact solutions, such as rehabilitation of public and private collective shelters and distribution and installation of shelter kits, as well as establishment of infrastructure and distribution of tents in tented settlements. Activities will be implemented through partners, in addition to direct procurement of shelter kits conducted by UNHCR. For the long term shelter assistance, the program will reinforce and upscale the sustainable, recovery oriented shelter solutions through support to those returning to their original homes. The shelter response will also contribute to public infrastructure rehabilitation, including, but not limited to, debris removal, rehabilitation and upgrading of water utilities networks, distribution of solid waste bins and to ensure sustainable community development. The implementation modalities of the shelter interventions will follow established Shelter Sector guidelines. The following main activities will form the core of UNHCR’s shelter program: 1) Provision of life saving and life sustaining shelter, e.g. tent installation in camps, transit sites and spontaneous settlements with associated infrastructure, and distribution of emergency shelter materials and kits. 2) Rehabilitation/upgrading of life saving and life sustaining shelter spaces in collective centres, unfinished buildings, spontaneous settlements, and other emergency spaces. 3) Support to sustainably repair and rehabilitate damaged houses for returnees settled in their original houses, through distribution of shelter materials, physical repair of their houses. 4) Upgrade and rehabilitation of public infrastructures covering return areas, including debris removal, distribution of solid waste bins, and construction/rehabilitation of water and electricity networks. 5) Training of stakeholders on resilience oriented shelter skills.

Protection Risk Analysis

Risk 1: During the provision of emergency shelter, there is a risk of increased community tension (host communities, IDPs and returnees) due to potential different assistance packages, and sometime unclear beneficiary selection criteria leading to a feeling of discrimination. Mitigating measure: This can be mitigated through standardized assistance packages with guidelines followed by all sector partners and ensuring beneficiary selection criteria are transparent, uniform and agreed among sector members and with the Government. These risks occur in all above mentioned activities. UNHCR and partners will engage with communities and leaders to raise awareness, ensure clear selection criteria to prioritize female headed households, people with specific needs, older persons and communicating them to the community, and conducting regular monitoring, consultation, post distribution monitoring and adjustments to the program to prevent and manage any tensions which may arise. Risk 2: For the return population, there is an increased risk of eviction and/or exploitation with major protection implications, while there are also risks of rights violations, either of owners or occupants, and future disputes due to the difficulties in demonstrating ownership documentation (HLP). Mitigating measure: UNHCR and its partners will only pursue repair and rehabilitation of housing following due diligence assessments to gain a solid understanding of the relevant HLP issues. Noting that unavailability of documentation is a pervasive issue, and the processes and infrastructure to formalize or recuperate documents are not uniformly functional, accessible or recognized, it is nevertheless possible to gather available information locally through a structured due diligence assessment that would help to identify and prevent/mitigate HLP risks specific to each intervention. Risk 3: The current selection criteria of the shelter beneficiaries may be influenced by abuse of local authorities which may contribute to nepotism, tension in the community and risk of the most vulnerable being left out (GBV, discrimination, monetary coercion). Mitigating measure: UNHCR will clearly inform community on beneficiary selection criteria and ensure that community leaders, local council, elderly people are engaged in community sensitization and beneficiary selection process. An active and informed complaint mechanism will be in place. In addition to partner post distribution monitoring, UNHCR contracted third party monitoring service provider will monitor beneficiary selection process and also conduct post distribution monitoring. UNHCR together with the sector partners will have Code of Conduct and PSEA in place, conduct refreshing sessions and advocate to establish clear selection criteria taking into consideration the population vulnerabilities and prioritizing the areas with immediate shelter needs.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Assumption: The potential risk of underfunding will likely impact the scope of UNHCR shelter response, with a consequence of assistance principally targeted at maintaining life-saving and life-sustaining humanitarian activities for those most vulnerable. The reduced scope of the response will preclude from undertaking activities intended to provide more durable shelter solutions. 1)Risk: Access restrictions to geographical areas due to active fighting and/or shifting of front lines and interference of different armed groups would prevent the humanitarian actors to provide timely emergency relief to the affected population. Beneficiaries might be exposed to risks if attending badly planned, organized and conducted distributions in connection with poor safety and security planning and/or lack of adequate consideration for vulnerable groups. Mitigating measure: UNHCR Shelter program must take into account safety, security and access to services in the implementation location and must include an understanding of the local context and dynamics, so as to avoid or prevent any potential issue and constraints. There are open communication lines between UNHCR and its partners and the contextual changes and security threats are closely monitored and analysed. Stocks are being propositioned inside Syria to be able to respond to humanitarian needs even when access is blocked. Standard operating procedures are in place for beneficiary selection and distribution process in emergency situations. Incidents of interference are reported at the inter-agency level to ensure collective action and messaging (to armed opposition groups). 2)Risk: Shelter implementation risks include the potential for aid diversion, corruption and abuse of power, which leads to reduced assistance to the persons of concern to UNHCR and a decline in donor funding.Mitigating measure: SOPs are jointly developed between UNHCR and its partners for the beneficiary selection and distribution process which should be addressed by UNHCR through his intervention design, management and monitoring system. Bulk procurement of shelter kits is conducted by UNHCR and distributed to partners for installation. Third party monitoring is in place to confirm the delivery of assistance in accordance to the SOPs, and to take into account the feedback of beneficiaries. The findings and recommendations in partners’ monthly and quarterly reports, post-distribution monitoring reports, and the third party monitoring reports are carefully reviewed and implemented.
