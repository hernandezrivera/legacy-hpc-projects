Project Summary

"The project adopts a comprehensive household-based food security strategy, strategically enhancing technical skills for climate-smart food production and boosting income to meet fundamental food requirements. This initiative spans both Rohingya camps and nearby host communities.
The youth-focused methodology involves a meticulous analysis of income and agricultural production trends within each household. Subsequently, a Family Development Plan and Business Development Plan will be crafted, empowering youth and women to fortify their food security. This includes initiating alternative on-farm and/or off-farm-based income-generating activities, with a deliberate focus on the active participation of 80% female members or young women. The project aims to introduce additional income sources by engaging women household members in Cash for work, enabling them to produce safe and nutritious foods and sell surplus products after family consumption.
To address vulnerability and high-protection risk families, such as those affected by child marriage, gender-based violence, and human trafficking, the project will provide minimum complementary food support. Persons with disabilities will be highly prioritized with meaningful participation. This will be done through consultation and coordination with relevant sectors, camp management, and local administration.
The project employs a two-step approach, combining household-based food production through year-round vertical gardening, microgreens and mushroom production, aquaculture, poultry and small livestock rearing, and increased income-generating opportunities. A strong contextual analysis underpins the project's commitment to sustainable food security at the household level.
Target individuals will undergo capacity-building training in climate-smart agriculture, safe vegetable production, aquaculture, livestock, and poultry rearing as well as post-harvest loss management of Agri products. Business capital will be transferred to each household to acquire productive assets for climate-smart food production (vegetable, poultry and fish culture, mushroom) both in host and Rohingya communities. A collective approach of homestead-level food production and marketing, with a special emphasis on engaging 80% women will be formed to recognize women's contributions to agriculture and to enhance their negotiation capabilities and market access in the host communities.
A collaborative youth-led and women-focused market linkage will be strengthened with all forward and backward market system actors. Regular meetings, workshops, and dialogue among market actors, private sectors, and target participants will facilitate smooth access to the agro-based value chain. A youth-led and women-focused digital marketing platform or system (e-commerce and f-commerce) will be established in the community. A household and farm-based contingency plan will be developed for the security against damage of food production interventions.
In addition, the project will integrate various issues and approaches, such as Natural Resource Management, paper voucher system in agriculture inputs distribution, Climate change adaptation, environmental conservation, post-harvest loss management, Food processing, Social Cohesion, Disaster Risk Reduction, Cash For Work and many more. These interventions will be strategically implemented to create a comprehensive and sustainable impact on the targeted communities. Total project participants will be 1670 for Rohingya camp of camp 21,22,24,25 an 26 and total 2200 from Ukhia and Teknaf host community.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

Bangladesh now hosts 967,765 Rohingya refugees in Cox’s Bazar (of which 52% are children), in some of the largest and most densely populated refugee camps in the world. With limited movements, access to regular income, and livelihood/educational opportunities in camps, it is estimated that 97% of the Rohingya refugee population is moderate to highly vulnerable and remains entirely dependent on humanitarian assistance. In the 2023 J-MSNA, the majority of refugee households (75%) reported that food was their first priority, which can be a direct consequence of the food ration cuts in March and June 2023. REVA-7 (p. 1) reports that “for the first time since 2017, 10 percent of Rohingya households were classified as having poor diets, and 70 percent were in poor to borderline conditions. Additionally, 24 percent of Rohingya households could not afford the minimum food basket, remaining below the food Minimum Expenditure Basket (MEB) despite current humanitarian assistance levels. However, simulations indicated that if assistance were removed entirely, 83 percent of Rohingya households would fall below the food MEB line, highlighting their significant dependence on humanitarian aid for survival.” With the refugee population being almost double the host community population in Ukhiya and Teknaf, the massive increase in population density following the influx, coupled with the pre-existing lack of livelihoods and levels of poverty and vulnerability among the host community population, has led to tensions over labor competition, falling wages and price hikes of daily essentials. It is reported that 69% of Bangladeshi people are moderate to highly vulnerable, reflecting a gradual increase since 2017.3 Most of the agricultural land in Ukhiya and Teknaf is no longer available due to the establishment of refugee camps, and overcrowding has also heightened the risk of landslides and fires. The perceived increases in crime and security concerns, and high stress over environmental resources leading to deforestation and the depletion of water sources, have been reported as sources of tensions between host communities and refugees. Similar to that of Rohingyas, food was identified as the most priority need (33%) - signifying the need for food security- in the host communities. In the Bangladeshi community, inadequate food consumption (poor and borderline) increased from 34 to 45 percent from 2022 to 2023, underscoring their struggle to recover from COVID-19-related job and livelihood losses, natural hazards, high food prices, increased cost of living, and low wage rates, which hinder a return to earlier food consumption and livelihood levels (REVA-7, p.13). Both Rohingya and Bangladeshi households are struggling to secure sufficient food and are frequently resorting to negative coping strategies to meet their food needs. About two-thirds of households in the camps (67 percent) and half of the households in the Bangladeshi community 2 (50 percent) relied on less preferred or less expensive food as their most frequently used coping strategy (REVA-7, pp. 1-2). There is a gendered dimension of this situation as well; discrepancies were found in food consumption between female and male-headed households among Rohingyas, despite standardized assistance from WFP. A higher proportion (49%) of women-headed households reported inadequate and poor food consumption compared to male-headed households (41%). Similarly, in the Bangladeshi community, food consumption discrepancies were evident (41 percent versus 33 percent, respectively), though overall food consumption was comparatively better (REVA-7, p. 13). In terms of vulnerability, the disaggregated data for male and female-headed households showed a significant difference in the Bangladeshi community (REVA-7, p. 5), with female-headed households being more vulnerable (72%) than male-headed households (67%), signifying the need for targeted interventions for females.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Considering the heightened climate risk, the project will emphasize on Climate smart agriculture technologies and will introduce contextualized Climate Risk Insurance (CRI) to the farmers as a tool to protect them from sudden climatic shocks. As a new concept, CRI will be initiated with learnings captured regularly to suggest improvements for future programming. To strengthen disaster readiness in the local communities, the project will emphasize strengthening early warning systems and forecast-based early action, as well as capacity strengthening of local institutional DRM actors like DMCs and CPP volunteers. The project will facilitate the development of a localized early warning (EW) dissemination mechanism through rigorous and inclusive community participation. All these efforts are expected to strengthen disaster readiness and climate change resilience at the community level.
