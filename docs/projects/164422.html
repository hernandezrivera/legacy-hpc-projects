Project Summary

Shafak, in line with education cluster’s objectives and in coordination with involved authorities as well as targeted communities, will focus on achieving two major goals:
- scaling up access to formal and non-formal education for Syrian crisis-affected children
- enhancing the quality of formal and non-formal education within a protective environment
Access strategies will include small-scale rehabilitation of damaged schools and establishment of other safe temporary/alternative learning spaces through a gender-sensitive and disability-friendly approach. It is critical to improve the access to quality education services for IDPs in camps and within communities and ensure that these services are sufficiently linked to Child Protection interventions including the integration of mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) and other specialized protection services. It is increasingly critical to expand access to psychosocial support and recreational activities for vulnerable children, particularly in areas of high severity of need.
Shafak will support formal education in 22 schools targeting approximately 7920 students (4196 boys and 3724 girls) where this intervention will minimize the risk of students dropping out, where monthly incentives to teachers will be provided, as they don’t have any alternative fund in addition to provision of education materials for students and teachers and providing schools with necessary operational costs (including heating for winter).
As education is one of the very basic children’s rights, Shafak is planning to provide access to education for children with disabilities by providing transportation services and improve school environment by provide targeted assistance within the schools in order to encourage these children to continue their education and integrate them with their peers.
Non-formal education opportunities and pathways back to inclusive education at age appropriate levels are increasingly required to address the complex learning needs of children.
Thus, non-formal education will target school girls and boys (aged 8-12 and gender disaggregated) to fill in existing needs, where 16 Non-Formal Education centres will be supported targeting approximately 4400 children (2000 boys/2400 girls, 8-12 years old) to provide literacy and numeracy along with PSS sessions; the PSS activities will be selected from I-Deal curriculum; the Curriculum used for Non-Formal Activities will be Curriculum B, that combines 2 academic years in one or, the Self Learning Programme (SLP) and will run in one cycle in summer time.
Children and youth accessing formal and non-formal education are not experiencing quality teaching, and there are growing concerns that the toll of the crisis in its many forms is preventing children from attaining foundational literacy and numeracy skills as well as those skills relevant to cognitive, social and economic empowerment. Hence, teachers will be equipped with the skills in non-formal education modalities, child-centred pedagogy, and positive classroom management to cope with overcrowded classrooms, address and accommodate host community, displaced and returnee children within their classes.
Stakeholders in the project implementation site, including children, adolescents and older persons, including PwDs and their opinions are taken into account while developing the project design, implementation of activities so that they have better access to humanitarian services and information in accordance to their specific situation.
The project will make sure that children engaging in child labour or recruited for military purposes, or living with older or disabled caregivers or adolescent girls at risk of early or forced marriage are given special attention and have their own space to participate in the project activities and are properly referred as per Protection cluster guidelines.

4 - Needs

Civilians living in northwest Syria have been enduring a drastically worsening humanitarian situation as hostilities continue to intensify since November 2019. From 1 December 2019 to 27 January 2020, nearly 390,000 people – around 80% of whom are women and children – were displaced in northwest Syria, mostly from large population centers such as Saraqab and Ma’arrat An Nu’man, as well as smaller towns and villages in other parts of southern Idleb governorate. The dire situation of civilians in northwest Syria is further compounded by unprecedented devaluation of the Syrian Pound, with the USD exchange rate ranging between 1,100 and 1,200 SYP per USD in January 2020, compared to around 640 SYP per USD in mid-October 2019. (Situation Report No. 7 - As of 29 January 2020) In Aleppo and Al-Raqqa, education was reported as a priority need in 30% of all assessed communities. While children were able to access schools in 81% of all communities assessed, KIs in 31% of IDP hosting communities reported that less than half of all IDP children (5-11 years old) attended school in the two weeks prior to data collection. (REACH Syria | Northeast Syria Rapid Needs Assessments | December 2019). As of 28 January, due to the unfolding situation in Ma’arrat An Nu’man, Saraqab, Ehsem and Mahambl, education have been suspended, impacting the learning of 59,231 school-aged children and showing a 36% increase of displaced school-aged children in impacted areas. A total of 199 schools are out of operation, impacting 59,231 children and 3,703 teachers: 135 schools in Ma’arrat An Nu’man and neighboring communities, impacting 48,649 children and 2,704 teachers; 23 schools in the communities of Ariha, impacting 5,681 children and 384 teachers; and 41 schools in the communities of Saraqab, impacting 4,901 children and 615 teachers. (Situation Report No. 7 - As of 29 Jan. 2020)Locations have been identified based on accessibility, however, this might change due to situation on the ground.

5 - Implementation of activities

The project is designed to provide quality education services to Syrian conflict-affected children in NW Syria, by proposing activities within areas with high density of IDPs and HCs. To do so, Shafak will make sure to involve stakeholders, including children, adolescents and older persons, including PwDs and their opinions in the implementation plan and that their voices are heard and taken into account while specific needs assessments are conducted and while implementing activities, so that they have better access to humanitarian services and information in accordance to their specific situation.Through these FGD, Shafak looks to identify particular needs as highlighted by the various groups and look at addressing these needs in the project design and implementation phases as possible. Other crosscutting issues such as GBV protection, child protection from violence, exploitation and abuse, and rights of vulnerable groups will be mainstreamed in the project. Shafak will start with validating the needs in the target schools through field surveys to reassess the needs. Shafak will conduct the procurement though open and transparent tender process as per Shafak procurement policy and procedures. The rehabilitation of schools and WASH facilities will be implemented by local contractors selected through open tender processes and supervision of Shafak technical WASH team to make sure the process is done in line with WASH cluster standards. The same will be implemented for schools’ furniture distribution. After each distribution round, the MEAL teams will do PDM for the kits’ distribution. Moreover, Shafak will organize community awareness sessions and back-to-school campaigns in the communities surrounding the schools and community education stakeholders will be engaged in the process in order to facilitate the communication among all education stakeholders in the targeted communities.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk (1): Sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA), including harassment, against women and girls by distributors and male beneficiaries while queuing and before/after distributions Risk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure: - recruit male and female volunteers who are respected in the community and independent of armed groups. No children to be employed (under 18 years old) - make sure staff are already trained on Do No Harm, protection from SEA (PSEA), and child safeguarding; and sign and adhere to code of conduct that respects and uphold the rights of beneficiaries and PSEA - set up communication and complaints mechanisms or desks that are clearly marked; face interviews with beneficiaries, including women and children Risk (2): Tension between host community and IDPs (who were evacuated) Risk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure: - clearly communicate selection criteria to the community, community leaders and beneficiaries prior to the announcement of the activity. - harmonize the assistance delivered as per Cluster instructions and guidelines. - coordinate with local council in the targeted area as well as being active in field coordination Risk (3): Difficult access for women, unaccompanied children, elderly, people with disabilities (PWD) Risk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure: - Register all adult women, unaccompanied children, elderly, people with disabilities as main recipients of Kits distributed - During post -distribution follow-up, understand how women, children, and other groups accessed the distributed goods and their feedback on the process and materials Risk (4): Child safety incidents in school due to bullying and overburdened teachers Risk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure: - Training of partners and local teams on Child safety, development of schools’ specific child safety policies; establishment of complaint mechanisms in school;- Security/safety training for organization, students and school staff.

7 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1. Risk: Target areas are inaccessible or insecure because of advance of armed groups and/or aerial shelling, or that staff or beneficiaries may be injured, killed or kidnapped by armed groups during implementation of activities Mitigation: - Monitoring of the security situation; extensive acceptance measures to sustain good relations with communities for continuous understanding of local context and to facilitate unimpeded access to target areas; coordination for programmatic contingency (shifting resources/funds to other areas) - Minimal transfer of cash through staff to reduce exposure. 2. Risk: borders are temporarily closed, or humanitarian access restricted, between Syria and its neighbouring countries because of national regulations, of advances by GoS towards neighbouring countries' borders. Mitigation: - ongoing advocacy with relevant authorities for humanitarian access to vulnerable populations in Syria. Dividing stock of commodities between Syria and neighbouring countries in anticipation of risk; prioritizing local procurements (inside Syria); 3. Risk: Existing traditional practices and community structures conflict with child protection principles (e.g. corporal punishment, child rights messaging is perceived as a threat, etc.). Mitigation: - thorough contextual analysis to be undertaken and ensure safe access as part of the project design; - teachers are trained on positive discipline techniques as part of their basic training and sign a code of conduct in the school; - teachers and classrooms are regularly monitored; a child safe-guarding focal point is identified in the school; - Parent-Teacher Committees are consulted on any reports from their homes of children being mistreated.
