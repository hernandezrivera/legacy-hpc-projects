Project Summary

According the 2023 HNO, the number of people in need of life saving services increased to 5.9 million. 15 million people in Syria - more than half the population – currently face acute food insecurity which may further accelerate all forms of under-nutrition among vulnerable children and women. The rising food insecurity will negatively impact on the nutritional status of an existing 2.1 million mothers who are already consuming a poor maternal diet, with an estimated 544,752 PLW currently in need of treatment for acute malnutrition with 460,124 of them being anemic. The already concerning levels of maternal malnutrition is anticipated to increase among displaced populations. Children with malnutrition are at an elevated risk of mortality and morbidity. 363,556 young children who are currently suffering from acute malnutrition will require appropriate, timely and sustainable treatment interventions, with almost one million of them being in dire need of robust anemia treatment and prevention interventions.
SMART surveys conducted in NES and NWS indicate that there is an increment of malnutrition for both U5 children and women of childbearing age. The stunting levels show an increasing trend: GAM levels were found as high as 5.5% in NES. The result indicated an alert level of chronic malnutrition (stunting) close to exceeding the 30% critical threshold. The results for malnourished pregnant and lactating women (PLW) based on Mid Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) (<230 mm) reached 20.5%. According to the survey findings, the IMDD is below 50% (45%) and MAD is at an average of 20%. The challenges and the existing situation on the ground needs a great focus and effort to address the problems both in NES and NWS. SC will focus on responding in Idleb, Al-Hassakeh, Aleppo, and Ar-Raqqa governorates.
Over the last 2 years, food prices across Syria have hugely increased and the value of the Syrian Pound plummeted. This has left communities vulnerable to food insecurity amongst many other risks. Food insecurity is directly related with the risk of malnutrition, highest for PLWs and children under 5 year who have an increased nutritional need (SC Nutrition SMART Survey 2021). Caregivers of children under 2 years and pregnant and lactating persons will be provided with MIYCN services in the target areas. CU5 will be screened for acute malnutrition and treatment will be provided at the facility or through referral. SC will follow-up all cases of malnourished children until they complete their treatment and achieve the desired nutritional outcomes. Gender and disability considerations will be integrated into the design of activities.
The nutrition teams will work with the health teams to provide integrated, inclusive services – inclusive of screening, treatment, IYCF awareness, early initiation of breastfeeding, delivering nutrition supplies, and applying CMAM to treat malnutrition cases and referral for complicated cases and acutely malnourished infants below 6 months for inpatient treatment services. All PLWs and caregivers of CU-5 within the intervention population will be linked with MPCA support to enable improved dietary practices; particularly, improved dietary diversity for reduction of micronutrient deficiencies amongst infants and young children and PLW, and improved child growth and development. Mothers requiring PSS and GBV support will be referred to the protection teams for additional assistance.
The target population will include mothers, children (aged 0-23 and 24-59 months) as well as children with disability. The HIMS will be used for collecting and compiling all intervention data both internally and also for 4Ws reporting at the sector level. MEAL teams will enable program beneficiaries to share feedback on quality of services, accessibility and available services. The M&E team and health team will ensure protection of health data and control accessibility to only authorized personnel.

3 - Needs

The 2023 HNO highlights that wasting prevalence was at 5% in NWS and parts of Damascus while North East Syria recorded a high of 12% which is classified as critical in nutrition severity ranking scale, especially in the presence of aggravating factors. Maternal malnutrition is also extremely concerning with acute malnutrition prevalence ranging from 11% NWS and parts of Damascus to 25% in NES. Approximately 544,752 Pregnant and Lactating Women (PLW) suffer from MAM, including 460,124 PLW suffering from anaemia. Similarly, an approximate 25% CU5 suffer from anaemia, compounding to the burden of malnutrition.According to the 2022 SMART Survey for NES, stunting prevalence of around 23.3% (12.5% to 28.5%), while the 2022 SMART Survey for NWS the prevalence of stunting was 22.3% (17.7 – 27.6). GAM prevalence in NWS stood at 4.3% and ranged from 2.9% to 9.9% in NES. MDD stood at 8.09% in NWS, and over 44% in NES for children 6 – 23 months.As per the 2023 HNO, resource mobilization and supply chain constraints have been hindering ongoing emergency responses, such as health and nutrition activities. Primary care, immunization, and nutrition supply availability are negative impacted by these challenges.The lack of integration of malnutrition management at PHCs due to underfunded nutrition services, limited access of humanitarian service providers, and difficulty delivering supplies through cross-border access points, have contributed to poor coverage of treatment and surveillance.

4 - Implementation of activities

The MIYCN program will focus on early initiation of breastfeeding, exclusive breastfeeding, timely initiation of complementary feeding and MDD. Community-based house to house case identification will strengthen early identification and treatment of acute malnutrition by empowering mothers to use MUAC screenings.SC will coordinate the clusters, as appropriate.The nutrition program will improve access to quality life-saving nutrition services through integration of nutrition services in static health facilities in hard-to-reach areas and contribute to the reduction of morbidity and mortality of U5 children. The facilities will receive necessary supplies which enable them to render quality services. In addition, health workers, community workers, and volunteers will receive refresher trainings so on current SOPs developed following the pandemic. The team will reach the community through the integrated nutrition programs. SC will establish and strengthen referrals between the outreach program with health facilities and also coordinate with other implementing partners and CBO to establish referral systems. The nutrition program will use community volunteers, MtMSGs and father groups to systematically prevent, identify, treat, and refer cases, where required. Children with SAM will be screened and admitted to OTP centres while children and PLWs with MAM will be admitted to TSEP program. The referral path way will follow the CMAM guidelines and follow-up will be conducted.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk 1: Lack of or inadequate information provision, feedback and participation of affected communities due to access challenges.Likelihood: high, Impact: medium. Mitigation: Establish/strengthen local partnerships, build the capacity of partners to effectively engage communities, strengthen community-based health and nutrition services, ensure beneficiary feedback included in TPMs. Risk 2: Barriers to access or exclusion of some vulnerable and minority from accessing health and nutrition services for different reasons such as children and adults with disabilities and denial of access to certain areas that have shifted lines of control. Likelihood: medium, Impact: high. Mitigation: Analyze and disaggregate all data by sex, age and disability. Analyze the needs of vulnerable and minority groups throughout the program cycle with identification of risk factors and rights violations impacting service provision for beneficiaries. Risk 3: Breach or leakage of sensitive data and information containing patient names, medical conditions and health care provider names into the wrong hands maybe can put people and communities at risk of targeting, shame, abuse and even death. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: Electronic data and information should be properly protected and backed up.


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

Risks: Women and girls not able to access services or participate in income generating activities due to sexual harassment; exploitation and abuse by aid workers; female heads of HHs and adolescent girls in particular risk of sexual exploitation in exchange to services (Voices from Syria, 2022); risk of not reporting SEA for fear of retaliation; lack of awareness on reporting channels; stigma and shame and fear of losing on the services making it difficult for SC to hold staff/partners involved accountable. Mitigations: SC has a Child Safe Guarding policy which includes PSEA. To mitigate the risk of PSEA during the assistance delivery, the following will be included: mandatory training and regular refreshers on Child Safeguarding, Safe programming and PSEA policy to SC and partner staff; working closely with the WoS PSEA TF to strengthen feedback, reporting and response mechanisms for SEA; conducting community awareness and sensitization sessions on PSEA with emphasis on reporting and ensuring safety of survivors through referrals; regular participatory safety audit to identify protection and safety concerns arising as a result of project implementation and developing action plans to mitigate identified risks collaboratively with the community; including PSEA messages and topics in all IEC materials and training sessions conducted under the project; ensuring that centrality of protection is embedded in all projects to mitigate risks and prevent potential harm.


9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Access and security: SC regularly reviews and updates contingency plans for its operations inside Syria to mitigate against context changes (particularly for NWS, on the cross-border resolution). This has recently been updated for NES and NWS based, including multiple potential scenarios regarding changes in territorial control, continuation or interruption of cross-border access and access to locations of implementation. Fraud Prevention: SC has zero tolerance for interference and/or diversion of aid to any parties to the conflict. All SC staff complete mandatory trainings, which include Fraud, Bribery, and Corruption and Aid Diversion and Financing of Terrorism. Financial, procurement and supply chain procedures further eliminate the risk through multiple vetting processes and checks, including the partners. Risks due to the disease outbreaks: The situation may result in risks to beneficiaries due to lack of access to programming sites and spread of infection amidst beneficiaries and health staff members. Relevant program adaptations have been developed and health and nutrition sector SoPs are being implemented at all sites. Weak logistics capacity: Field staff have received technical and systems trainings on processes and management and contingency plans are in place in case of loss of access. SC has signed framework agreements with local suppliers in Turkey to be able to deliver aid across Syria in a timely manner.
