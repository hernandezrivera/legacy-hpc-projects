Project Summary

Severe food insecurity has continued to undermine and nearly collapsed Livelihood in areas where the populations are classified in Catastrophic(IPC 5), Emergency (IPC Phase 4) and Crisis (IPC Phase 3) and has increased over years in Jonglei state; Bor south county, Lakes state; Cuiebet, Rumbek East, Rumbek Center and Rumbek North; Central Equatorial; Yei, Lainya, Kajo keji, Kapoeta South targeting the host vulnerable households, IDPs and returnee’s communities with severe food and nutrition needs in the counties. With heavy local presence, with ongoing projects and as static in these Locations; TITI Foundation aims to target 18,000 most vulnerable HH with Nutrition, Food Security and Livelihood response to increase agricultural production and build community resilience for the vulnerable food insecure households through a multi-sector integrated response to prevent catastrophic consequences.
TITI Foundation in response to the ongoing emergency will improve the general availability of food and strengthen vulnerable communities’ ability to withstand future threats associated with massive displacements. The project will continue to promote alternative farming techniques through the capacity building of contact farmers. working with CAD (County Agricultural Departments, training of VAC (Village agricultural Committees), farmers groups on conservation agriculture and value chain addition to increase crop production coverage and minimize production costs. Farmers' groups will be organized and supported to continue developing community food banks, gunny bags gardening, kitchen gardens and post-harvest handling practices to enhance food security for vulnerable households. This will assist them to have the capacity to process farm produce and identify potential markets for their farm produce. TITI Foundation will work with strategic partners to provide inputs including early maturing vegetable seeds and tools to farmers in the target locations with an aim to restore food security, production of vegetable variety, fishing to enhance nutritional diversification and production capacity while maintaining high levels of accountability to affected persons through participatory engagement. In addition, TITI Foundation will support mechanized agricultural practices; Value addition to increasing employment among the youths and women; improve agronomics practices; integrate fishing and apiary practices; Provide food assistance, this will limit household food expenses and ease the burden. TITI Foundation will also ease pressure on the elderly, women, Girls, PWDs to acquire Psychosocial support, child-headed household protection and GBV.
During the project implementation, TITI Foundation (MEAL) teams will conduct field visits, PDMs after every distribution and progress reports in the selected locations. Strengthen Complaint and Feedback Mechanism to enhance accountability for the affected communities. This will involve key informants' interviews in the various communities and focus group discussions to determine the efficiency and effectiveness of the intervention.

1 - Needs

An estimated 8.2 million people, including refugees and Abyei, are likely to experience severe food insecurity at the peak of the 2023 lean season (May – July). This is comparable with the situation described in the FSL sectoral analysis (December 2021) and IPC analysis (March 2022) where food security needs remained large and severe. Food insecurity is highest in Jonglei where 79% of the population will likely face severe acute food insecurity, followed by Unity (75%), Warrap (70%), Northern Bahr El Ghazal (66%), Lakes (61%) and Upper Nile (60%) with the highest levels of severe acute food insecurity (IPC Phase 3 or worse) at the peak of the lean season 2023. The main drivers continue to be: (1) covariate climatic, conflict and economic shocks (such as floods and dry spells, inflation and rising food prices, and the continued disruption of livelihoods due to conflict and insecurity); and (2) household-level idiosyncratic stressors (such as the death of breadwinners, gender-based violence, morbidity and mortality due to poor hygiene, and lack of access to essential basic services) which are greatly amplified by the compounded effect of shocks. Food insecurity is greatest when these shocks and stressors are compounded by the persistently high levels of vulnerability and poverty associated with the protracted crisis that has existed since December 2013 and progressively worsened over time reaching peaks in 2022 and projected to continue into 2023. The analysis of the stratified data revealed that displaced people are more likely to be severely food insecure (IPC 3 or worse) with 61.1% of total IDPs being severely food insecure. This is particularly significant in Western Bahr el Ghazal (69.9% of the total IDPs), Central Equatoria (67.3%), and Unity (61.2%) States. In Eastern Equatoria, returnees are relatively (80% of the total number of returnees) more food secure compared to another residential group. The residents tend to be the most food insecure among residential groups in Eastern Equatoria, Jonglei, Northern Bahr el Ghazal and Upper Nile States.FSNMS results at thstate level show that the age of the head of household has a strong link to food security. Households headed by an adult, aged between 18 to 60 years, are more. The Food Security and Livelihoods (FSL) sectoral analysis identified an increase in the severity and extent of food insecurity through the use of Food Security and Nutrition Monitoring System Plus (FSNMS+) outcome indicator data, FSL assessment reports (including over 100 pieces of evidence), and other information and analysis by colleagues from WFP, FAO, FEWSNET, REACH, IRNA's and the Food Security Cluster. In total, 19 counties have seen significant or minor improvements, while 22 counties have remained stable, and 37 counties have experienced significant or minor deteriorations in their food security level. In terms of food insecurity severity (refer to FSL HNO severity map), 31 counties are described as being of extreme or severe concern, 36 counties are described as of very high or high concern and 11 counties are of moderate concern. Five urban locations were assessed as being of very high concern. In response to the needs, beneficiary selection and registration will be done in participation with displaced & host communities and will be carried out by TF in joint committees to strengthen feedback and accountability mechanisms. This will be verified and information provided to the community at every point. TF will work closely with the financial service provider (FSP) to carry out the distributions; procurement of tools and seeds; carry out agronomics and mechanized agricultural practices; establish a complaints desk to analyze all risks carefully prior to, during and after distributions; conduct post-distribution monitoring exercises. TF will strengthen support for PSS, protection and GBV services, FES (fuel-efficient stoves and briquettes), extension services, VDCs, CAD and referral pathways.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

TF will work with the local authorities, County Agriculture Department and Village Agriculture Committees to support and establish markets in locations where markets were destroyed; The beneficiaries will be supported through Cash Voucher assistance to enhance cash flow and increase purchasing power that will strengthen the existing market. TF will continue to Link households to local, national, regional market with increased availability of food or sufficient food production, access to food and ability to purchase food, sufficiency in terms of nutrition including energy, proteins and micronutrients as well as safety, and the stability and foreseeability of these conditions.

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

CVA interventions will be based on functional and accessible markets through conducting rapid market assessments, needs analysis among the beneficiaries and provision of unconditional cash support. Using cash or vouchers recognizes that beneficiaries are best placed to decide what their households need, and gives them the flexibility to choose according to their own diverse needs and priorities based on the nature of their needs.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Titi Foundation will use varied needs assessment reports, information and data received from stakeholders through the different IRNA conducted, PDMs, and other internal programme assessments to design this project. At the initiation of the project, project inception meetings will be held with a cross-section of stakeholders including local authorities and community leaders. During the course of implementation, project review meetings will be conducted to enable stakeholders to provide feedback and raise any concerns regarding the project. Beneficiaries will be involved and their voices enhanced during all processes of the project from the beneficiary selection, to locations and sites of the different activities and infrastructure. From time to time the different assessments will be conducted to provide platforms for beneficiary participation and involvement. Titi Foundation will ensure there is equity in targeting and access to the project, transparency with project information and platforms for feedback and response mechanisms (FRM) will be put in place. Each project will complete the pre-address checklist to provide as much information as possible regarding targeting, beneficiaries’ rights and entitlements, protection from sexual exploitation, abuse, and harassment (PSEAH), Gender-based violence, and reporting channels for raising complaints. Channels for reporting and sharing feedback will provide TF with real-time data on the feedback being received and this will inform programming. Through the utilization of a harmonized FRM/CRM approach, feedback will be recorded using the available feedback trackers and aggregated on a monthly basis to spot trends in the amount, type and nature of feedback and complaints being received. This analysis will be used to verify that project participants’ questions, concerns and suggestions are being addressed and followed up on. At the same time, project staff will continue to regularly meet and communicate with key stakeholders, and beneficiaries regarding project successes and challenges, reception by the community and to communicate plans and results with community leaders to maintain a transparent and open relationship with the communities the project is serving. To ensure beneficiaries are protected from harm, all staff, volunteers, and community resource persons working on the project will be trained on PSEA and GBV commitments. All persons contracted will be required to sign the PSEA declaration including contractors, suppliers, and consultants. TF will ensure beneficiary and child safeguarding policies and approaches are adhered to at each level of engagement. AAP committees, CAD, VDC and Project Management Committees (PMCs) will be put in place to share information, receive, and raise concerns and address any issues arising that require clarity.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

On a broader base, participatory evaluations will be used by including the various authorities and group representatives through project implementation phases to collect complaints, advice and resentments. This will be used for accountability to the grassroots and staff using the FSL cluster AAP checklist to improve the engagement process. TF has internal regulations on PSEA, with all employees signed commitment. TF is also a member of the protection cluster and understands the modalities to ensure protection is mainstreamed within our activities. Key staff employed at the field level (enumerators, crowd controllers, and others dealing directly with beneficiaries) will sign/sign the safety code of conduct. Training is continuously provided with clarification to the beneficiaries' abuse, reporting abuses, confidentiality and risk mitigation. Through Titi Foundations' existing staff code of conduct inscribed with clauses on zero tolerance to the prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA). TF is able to prevent staff from indulging in activities that can lead to protection threats during project implementation. Titi Foundation has readily available hotlines, help desks, and complaint committees which consist of men, women, youth, child-headed households, people living with disabilities (PwD) and local authorities to address gender equality; through the various means which beneficiaries can reach out to higher authorities of the organization to report any possible protection threats during the cycle of project implementation. In conclusion, allow the representatives and Titi Foundation staff to be more accountable to affected populations so as to mitigate any protection threats. The project will guarantee the dignity and safety of beneficiaries by ensuring the Do no-harm approach. Staff, volunteers, and community resource persons will be trained on aspects of PSEA, GBV, and safeguards to ensure they understand how to work with an affected population with the knowledge that they are entitled to all the project beneficiaries.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

At the inception of the project Titi will provide sensitizations explaining details of the programs, community involvement in the targeting process, what assistance beneficiaries are entitled to and the CRM. Following beneficiary selection, beneficiaries will be consulted on the design of Nutrition and FSL kits to ensure materials are appropriate to the needs. Stakeholders and community feedback meetings and sessions will provide avenues for sharing of the findings and development of solutions that are long-lasting, this will be used as learning avenues that will strengthen the relationship of the program at the community level. Possible courses of action for resolving disputes and complaints will include: Apologies and acceptance of the complaints when genuine and consider all relevant information and arguments; Refer/forward to alternative agencies if the complaint/issue raised is not within TF mandate and is unable to assist with services; Explain the complaint process and the steps that will be followed after complaint submission, if possible, resolve the complaint immediately or commit to a timeframe for investigating the complaint further; Refer if the complaint requires additional information and further investigation/analysis by management for decision making; Referral to government body if the complaint requires government intervention.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

Risks that beneficiaries are exposed to violence during registration/distribution as a result of the distance to or location for humanitarian assistance and services will be conducted. Protection assessments and risk analysis, stakeholders mapping, and needs verification with clear AGD data to understand context dynamics, protection risks and vulnerability level will be done through focus group discussions, key informant interviews, meetings and HH surveys with women, men, children, institution workers, community leaders, within IDPs, host community and returnee population to inform the WASH, S/NFI, FSL, Nutrition and Education responses plan based on needs, access, safety, and gap findings. Risks of women, girls and boys without and with disabilities exposed to sexual violence, including sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA), when accessing humanitarian assistance: TITI has integrated the SEA in its HR manual and an orientation/ sensitization will be done to each staff, PwD, Volunteers and suppliers. Where the rights have been violated an immediate action will be taken to ensure the beneficiaries are protected throughout. Risks that the project does not have sufficient internal mechanisms to combat and prevent child safeguarding and PSEA: Titi Foundation is committed to the principles of quality of service delivery and responsibilities of the beneficiaries on our project. This means beneficiaries are fully consulted and informed about the benefits of such projects. TF allows room to discuss, speak and analyze what short and long term benefits such a project may offer to allow the targeted beneficiaries and communities to be able to own the project throughout the project cycle. Titi Foundation projects are guided by the humanitarian principles of ‘Do No-Harm’ and this allows for the application of the Human Resource policies guiding staff behavior, PSEA code of conduct which has been signed by all staff.Risk of no access to or awareness of protection assistance or referral services for survivors of protection incidents (i.e. GBV, child protection, evictions, explosive hazards, etc.). Based on the considerable protection risks in the program locations, protection will be considered at all stages of the program cycle. South Sudan ranks 187/189 countries in the; 2017 HDI. The country loses 36.3% when the value is discounted for inequality (HDI reduced from 0.388 to 0.247 ). This figure reflects a strong patriarchal society and culture in South Sudan; and traditional gender roles subjected to women play secondary roles in the community. This places them at a disadvantage with household decision-making and this disempowerment exposes women and girls to several risks including early/forced marriage and sexual and domestic violence. While the laws in South Sudan provide for the protection of women and girls against discrimination, PwD, abuse and GBV, they are often misinterpreted or ignored. There are often large numbers of female headed households where women have to take additional roles and their needs will need to be considered such as whether as per social norms they can access land for farming and markets for products.
