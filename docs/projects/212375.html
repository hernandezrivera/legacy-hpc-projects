Project Summary

An estimated 16.7 million people are in need of humanitarian assistance (HA) in 2024, an increase of 1.3 million compared to 2023. This includes 8.4 million women and girls, with 4.1 million of reproductive age. Over 500,000 women are expected to become pregnant and give birth in 2024, of which 380,000 are in need of HA including ANC, safe delivery, PNC, and FP services. Among them, 56,000 are likely to face complications and will require EmONC. Additionally, there are 2 million adolescent girls across Syria in need of HA. An estimated 7.2 million people are internally displaced and approximately 90% of the population live below the poverty line. These structural and economic realities reduce women’s access to SRH services, as does the partial or complete damage of 40% of health facilities and severe HRH shortages.
UNFPA will continue to reduce maternal and newborn morbidity and mortality; support the EPHS; provide FP commodities, and provide medical assistance and care to GBV survivors. This will be achieved by 1) ensuring the availability of and access to integrated quality SRH services and information; 2) building health worker capacity; 3) increase access to services where no SRH services exist; 4) ensuring availability of essential medicines and supplies; 5) delivery of complementary services, including MHPSS and NCDs; and 6) strengthening referral mechanisms and improving SRH coordination.
UNFPA will follow the Whole of Syria approach, implementing in all governorates from Damascus (including to NES), and cross-border from Türkiye. Vulnerable populations prioritized include WRA, IDPs, host communities, returnees, adolescents, and PwDs. Through December 2023, UNFPA reached 1,147,345 people with SRH services through 77 static SRH clinics, 19 EmONCs, and 108 mobile clinics and 4 satellite points in government-controlled areas and 175,443 people with SRH services through 11 facilities including 9 EmONC facilities and 2 mobile clinics.
Key 2024 objectives include:
Increasing access to: (1) lifesaving health services for the most vulnerable and in need persons, including >57,000 deliveries and >6,000 C-Sections; (2) to secondary and tertiary (in SCO) healthcare for high risk pregnancy, malnourished PLW, EmONC cases, and complex cases which cannot access treatment in their area, including > 95,000 referrals for delivery, including referrals out of Al-Hol and Areesah camps; (3) to primary healthcare by providing EPHS to cover RMNH needs; (4) to vulnerable rural areas and camps with service gaps through mobile teams and the community midwifery networks throughout NWS, NES and GoS.
Supporting (1) SRH and GBV integration to ensure access for GBV survivors to SRH services, 117 health facility under integration initiative, in addition to 1500 health staff to be trained; (2) essential infrastructure maintenance, equipment repair and supply to health facilities, including expansion of maternity clinic in Al-Hol camp; (3) health sector coordination and HIS, emphasizing enhancing protection, access to health services, and coordination within and across hubs, and joint contingency and preparedness planning; (4) continuity of care, strengthening community resilience, responding to IDP movements and shifts in control, and beneficiaries’ participation in programming; (5) resilience and early recovery through one midwifery school in NWS and 2 in GOS/NES; (6) leadership of women to be able to lead the SRH program in NW Syria (7) voucher assistance to enhance access to FP and PNC, including transportation and value vouchers.
Strengthening and consolidating medical supply chains, ensuring availability of quality supplies and medicines for SRH services. Pre-positioning emergency stocks of RH kits, in line with contingency scenarios for reduced access and increased hostilities.
Adapting, updating, and adopting national RH guidelines, including on CMR and Health and Nutrition of PLW in 2024, with MoH, and developing training packages.

0 - Project Summary (extended)

Thirteen years since the crisis started, the SRH response is expanding beyond the MISP. For women to have sufficient coverage and quality of SRH services the responses must be implemented within equipped and functioning health care facilities, skilled HCWs, referral networks, and surveillance and monitoring. The objectives above show how UNFPA is continuing to strengthen health systems. However, the ongoing conflict and deteriorating economic situation inside Syria has led to more human rights abuses, including SEA. UNFPA is actively working to mitigate risks related to violence, sexual exploitation and abuse and have adopted prevention measures that are built into the project. UNFPA enhances the prevention and mitigation of protection risks through mainstreaming the PSEA into all activities, conducts trainings, developed a hotline and zero tolerance policy and undertakes PSEA assessments of all partners and support in validating their PSEA policies. UNFPA has embedded PSEA into policies, contracts, code of conduct, MEAL toolkits; all health facility staff are aware and signed the codes of conduct. PSEA and protection mainstreaming are integrated into the design of all monitoring tools, which is applied by the TPM. To mitigate and prevent violence against health facilities, health workers, UNFPA, and IP staff, UNFPA (with the health sector) will increase advocacy efforts on health protection and respect of international humanitarian law. UNFPA works to prevent and support the management of GBV, including ensuring the MISP SRH services are part of the EHP. UNFPA trains specialized health staff to provide first-line support, CMR, identification of GBV, and safe referrals. Risk assessments have been done and risks are known including increased targeting of health facilities, increasing displacement and limiting access to services. Movements are restricted, relying on authorities’ approval. Service gaps and monitoring challenges remain due to insecurity and movement restrictions. UNFPA and IP staff have restricted movement and higher risk in insecure areas, especially NES, NWS, and in camps. Female patients face limited access to services due to insecurity, social norms, and economic situation. Limited staff from delayed visa/long recruitment processes, high turnover of staff results in delay of activities. Sanctions make local procurement difficult, and international procurements are long. Uncertainty on the cross-border resolution renewal will affect services in NWS. Reduced donor funding would reduce UNFPA’s ability to support life saving services. UNFPA has strong contingencies for these risks and leads contingency planning within RH/GBV coordination groups, including access changes, Continuous monitoring of implementation based on the five AAP principles and UNFPA’s global results-based monitoring framework. UNFPA or third party staff and IPs will conduct joint monthly supervision visits and quarterly third party monitoring.
