Project Summary

The primary aim of this project is to usher in a transformative change by bolstering access to a secure, inclusive, and top-tier education system for school-aged children residing in crisis-affected regions, notably targeting vulnerable demographics like internally displaced persons (IDPs), returning communities, and areas hosting affected populations. With a specific focus on 12,500 children aged between six and eighteen, this initiative is geared toward dismantling multifaceted barriers hindering education access, ranging from societal norms and economic constraints to gender disparities and geographical challenges.
This comprehensive project endeavors to achieve several pivotal outcomes, notably bolstering both access to and continuity of education, fostering learning environments that are not only conducive but also tailored to meet the diverse needs of children. Furthermore, it seeks to elevate the overall quality of learning experiences while specifically addressing the educational gaps of children who previously dropped out. To accomplish these objectives, the project integrates essential psychosocial support into teacher training, champions the establishment of inclusive, disability-friendly learning spaces, and adopts a holistic approach by intertwining gender-sensitive and conflict mitigation perspectives.
Through seamless collaboration with the basic education services in Borno and Adamawa States, this project actively promotes and embeds crucial protection principles. These principles revolve around ensuring the fundamental rights of safety, dignity, and active participation for every child. This collaboration seeks to create an educational environment that not only safeguards these principles but also fosters an atmosphere where children feel empowered and valued participants in their own educational journey.
Understanding the pivotal role education plays in steering sustainable development goals, this project acknowledges its broader impact. It strives not only to provide high-quality education but also to impart essential knowledge on climate literacy among the youth. By equipping them with the necessary skills, values, and attitudes, the project aims to empower young minds to spearhead positive changes for sustainable development. Education becomes the conduit through which young individuals comprehend and respond to global issues like climate change, encouraging them to make informed decisions and champion necessary adaptations to mitigate its effects.
In essence, this project transcends the traditional realms of education provision, aiming to instigate a holistic transformation that empowers children not just academically but also equips them as conscientious global citizens ready to tackle pressing challenges and contribute meaningfully to a sustainable future.

1 - Needs

Humanitarian need overview (HNO) indicates that 1.3 million people in need of education services/ assistance in 2023. An estimated 959,642 school-age children have limited access to learning. Around 42 per cent of displaced children do not attend school (DTM 2022). The conflict, along with cultural norms and financial hardship, has a disproportionate impact on school-aged girls. They are among the most vulnerable groups and are at risk of child marriage and dropping out of school.Children with disabilities are also disadvantaged. Although the majority (67 per cent) of non-specialized schools have at least one child with special needs, only 15 per cent reported having additional provisions for their needs.According to UNICEF, some 2,295 teachers have been killed across the BAY states since the beginning of the conflict and many have been displaced. This has led to a shortage in the supply of qualified teachers. According to JENA 2021, the average pupil to teacher ratio across the BAY states is 68 pupils to 1 teacher. Yobe has the highest (92:1), followed by Borno (66:1) and Adamawa (41:1).108JENA found that the average number of students per classroom is 124 and only 60 per cent of teachers have some sort of qualification. In Borno State, for example, there is one classroom per 122 students. In Yobe the ratio is 1:177, and Adamawa 1:66. Infrastructure for WASH and disabled access is severely lacking as well. Only 46 per cent of schools have access to adequate and safe drinking water. Most schools have no functioning handwashing facilities, or water or soap. There is an average of 264 students per latrine. JENA also found that only 6 per cent of schools in the BAY states have adapted their premises to allow access for children with physical disabilities. The BAY states crisis severely hampers displaced children's education, with 31% of schools struggling to enroll students. Conflict has damaged 1,400 schools, hindering access and learning materials. Furniture shortages (47% in Borno) and financial constraints impede attendance. Absence of gender-segregated facilities in 71% of schools and teacher qualification gaps amplify challenges, demanding urgent interventions for a secure learning environment.In 2022, the volatile humanitarian situation is projected to lead to an increase in unmet needs for children and youth, their families, teachers and communities. Insecurity, internal displacement and camp closures have deepened existing vulnerabilities and depleted the coping mechanisms of affected populations. Other issues such as communal clashes and flooding have disrupted learning as schools were used for non-educational purposes. In Adamawa State, following severe flooding during the 2022 rainy season, 120 schools were used as shelters for affected communities. As a result, an estimated 40,000 students were unable to resume the new academic year.Windstorms and flooding are a recurrent issue, and can be expected in 2023, once again impacting access to some schools. In JENA 2021, when children were asked about the specific risks they faced when coming to school, 20 per cent referred to "crossing rivers/ flooding". Inflation and the cost-of-living crisis, and the consequent reduction in purchasing power, are likely to have a significant impact on teachers and parents. This is especially relevant as JENA 2021 revealed that financial factors are the prime cause of teacher absenteeism in about 50 per cent of the assessed schools, particularly low salaries (35 per cent) or non-payment of salaries (14 per cent).

2 - Activities

Through this project, Salient aims to ensure that both children and teachers in supported classes have access to sufficient teaching and learning materials, directly impacting the quality of education and learning outcomes. Teachers will receive training to enhance their knowledge and skills, including the integration of psychosocial support into their teaching methods for the social and emotional well-being of learners. A comprehensive assessment, involving communities and authorities, will identify barriers and needs for education access, including protection analysis. Support for schools will include additional Transitional Learning Spaces (TLS), WASH facilities, teaching materials, dignity kits for girls, emergency teacher training, and the integration of out-of-school children. The project will also involve recruiting, contracting, and training teachers and facilitators, providing catch-up classes and remedial support, supporting extremely vulnerable children through Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA), and collaborating with education authorities and teacher training institutes to develop curriculum and teacher training for catch-up and Alternative Education Programme (AEP). Community engagement, including Parents-Teacher Associations (PTAs), is an integral component of the project.

8.6 - Please describe briefly the modalities your project will put in place to ensure protection mainstreaming (particularly referring to the above questions).

SHO will integrate humanitarian protection principles into crisis responses, ensuring actions prevent unintended harm, align with needs, prioritize safety and dignity, engage local empowerment, and hold actors accountable. The focus is on addressing community needs, upholding human rights, emphasizing Protection Mainstreaming and Centrality. Assistance will be impartial, disregarding legal status, as all affected individuals merit equal humanitarian aid and protection.

9.5 - How will your project ensure feedback is collected from the most vulnerable community members?

SHO will initiate a project inception workshop engaging all stakeholders, presenting comprehensive project details, including the budget. Ensuring accountability to the affected population at every project stage, SHO will actively involve the targeted community in needs assessment, emphasizing women, youth, and persons with disabilities. Community involvement includes mapping structures and conducting focus group discussions with representatives, addressing the diverse needs of vulnerable groups. Regular feedback mechanisms during project implementation will be established, utilizing suggestion boxes and a community-based complaint and feedback system managed by the SHO Protection unit. Existing complaints and feedback mechanisms in Borno State ensure effective two-way communication with the community, shared with relevant agencies. SHO emphasizes accountability through beneficiary surveys, incorporating findings into project design, and tailors interventions for continuous monitoring, including Participatory Design Monitoring (PDM), Post-Intervention Monitoring, and Focus Group Discussions (FGCs) throughout the project lifecycle.
