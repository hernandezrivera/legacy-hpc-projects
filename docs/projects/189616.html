Project Summary

To paint the picture of the existing WASH infrastructure in Leer, Mayendit and Panyijiar in Unity and Kajokeji county in CES are not able to provide adequate WASH services talk of water points for clean water, sanitation infrastructures and hygiene promotion that educates communities to improve their mindset and perception). The risks of the outbreaks of water borne diseases including acute watery diarrhea, cholera, and malnutrition are wide. Inadequate WASH services is a major cause of diseases in Leer, Mayendit and Panyijiar counties and improving sanitation through hygiene promotion is known to have a significant beneficial impact on health in households. The communities in these locations are lucking good sanitation because some were either destroyed by the community conflicts and or the continued floods. This is the same case with Kajokeji county where WASH facilities and infrastructure were vandalized. There is need for improved sanitation, safe water points for clean drinking water, waste disposal, and to raise awareness on water and sanitation related health/hygiene issues, distribute Homebase care kits, ( buckets, soap and water purifiers -water flocks sachets/Aquatabs) MHM kits to the community, in order to reduce the risk of cholera outbreak, and other sanitation related diseases. The project aims to repair 45 non-functioning boreholes, Construction of 5 protected shallow wells and 3 mini-water yard in several Payams of Leer, Mayendit and Panyijiar Counties so that the IDPs, the returnees and host communities can access safe and clean water at an average of 750 people (women, men, boys and girls) per borehole which is still below the UNHCR and SPHERE standards quality requirement but is far better than the current average usage of 1,250 per borehole and use of alternate unsafe sources according IRNA assessment report and FSNMS 2021 where only 34% of the community are accessing safe water. The displaced populations are forced to live within the host communities and IDP camps with very limited WASH infrastructure resulting to increase of open defecation, overuse of functional boreholes and mini-water yards, institutional and shared family latrines. The project intends to conduct hygiene promotion to influence behavior change from harmful practices that encourage spread of diseases, by involving women, men, boys and girls in the community through community hygiene promotion approaches, water and sanitation management committees, through involving all the stakeholders in the populations that includes people living with disabilities and the elderly. A total of 60,000 people (women, men, boys and girls) will benefit through provision of access to safe adequate water by repairing of boreholes and mini water yards, construction of protected shallow wells and improve sanitation through construction of gender sensitive latrines and hygiene promotion awareness creation. POH will conduct GBV safety audit before the construction and rehabilitation of water points, institutional latrines, creating awareness about hygiene and sanitation in advance of the implementation and distribution of WASH NFIs and constructions. This approach has always guaranteed a more enthusiastic participation of women, girls and boys, that has led to a higher level of acceptance and ownership by a larger group of community members and thus supports greater sustainability of the program by reinforcing the link between water, sanitation and hygiene. The project aims at enabling the community accessing safe and adequate drinking water, to promote construction and rehabilitation of WASH facilities while prioritizing the safety of the women, girls and boys accordingly.

1 - Needs

According to the latest WASH situation on South Sudan by unicef, it is recorded that, 59% of the population in South Sudan lacks access to safe water. And so in order to survive, families may be forced to drink dirty water, putting them at risk of waterborne diseases such as cholera and diarrhea, which remain the leading causes of death among in the County. The situation is further aggravated by poor hygiene practices. It is said that, just only 10 % of the population have access to improved sanitation.In this year 2022 alone, the humanitarian community in South Sudan has estimated that more than two-thirds of South Sudan's population, 8.9 million people will need humanitarian assistance which is an increase of 600,000 since 2021. This situation that is coupled with high levels of poverty, high returns of refugees (talk of Kajokeji county), destroyed infrastructure (kajokeji, Leer, Mayendit and Panyijiar counties), food insecurity and flooding (kajokeji, Leer, Mayendit and Panyijiar), has caused mass displacement of people and many people (women, men, boys, girls and with disabilities (PWDs)) are residing in IDP camps and within the receiving host communities. With such existing groups in the host communities, the WASH facilities and services are inadequate to support such a huge population and at such, becomes the source of conflict between them and the hosting community considering the SPHERE, WHO and UNHCR guidelines. This environmental, humanitarian, and political crisis has negatively impacted the access on basic water, sanitation and hygiene services. According to HNO 2021, communities were hit hard by the triple shock of intensified conflict and sub-national violence, including major flooding a situation that has kept on worsening. And this is why, an estimated 6,159,886 million people’s living standard and their well being will be impacted in 2022 as a result of lack of access to safe water and improved sanitation. According to the FSNMS, and IRNA 2021, it is recorded that, despite the fact that major waterways were replenished by the long rainy season, only 39% of the population reported having enough water to meet their household needs countrywide, dropping to 34% in rural areas In Unity, where 33 % of households travel more than 30 min to access any type of clean and safe water, an issue that is particularly problematic for persons with physical disabilities, women and the elderly. The low coverage of water infrastructure explains that 49% of households reported relying on surface or un-clean water as their main water source. Surface water is often untreated prior to consumption and this, along with the widespread practice of open defecation, a public health risk, when flooding. Limited access to water is also compounded by 14% households reporting having at least one container for safely water storage. Insecurity is also a factor that has affected water access in Leer, Mayendit and Panyijiar Counties. In Kajokeji County, most of such facilities or all of them were destroyed/stolen during the conflict three year down. Protection concerns are also issues when accessing water points, with 18% of households, reporting they felt unsafe while collecting water in the two weeks prior to data collection. Of those, 12% reported of women and girls reportedly avoiding water points due to GBV concerns. In certain states such as Unity, open defecation is a norm due to lack of sanitation facilities. Poor personal hygiene and poor access to WASH services and goods combined with high levels of food insecurity has a detrimental impact on the health of the most vulnerable people. In Leer the (GAM) prevalence for children 0-59 months was 19.2% while (SAM) reads at 6.1% and in Mayendit (GAM) prevalence was 14.6% while (SAM) was at 4.0 (WHO classification’s). The presence of water-borne diseases was also reported, with 16% of households reporting children under 5 had been affected by diarrhea in the two wks to data collection.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

POH has in place a tested system of yearly multi-sector surveys that through the employment of more than 300 questions (including question on WASH, FSL, nutrition, GBV, hazards, protection, disabilities, education, gender inclusion, etc.), a large number of interviews (more than 300 HHs) capable of providing detailed reference at the county and payam level, an analysis centred on specific groups (single mothers, families with members with disabilities, poor farmers, etc) and the participatory discussion of such findings with the staff and representatives of the communities ensures not only that projects are designed on the base of the specific needs of the different groups of the population, but also that such knowledge is interiorized by POH staff and becomes the base of an informed conversation and a common understanding with local communities, ensuring feedback from the most vulnerable within the community and such is aimed at monitoring the results of POH’s projects. Beside such surveys, POH has a tested system of detailed post distribution Monitoring which is used to gather precise feedbacks over the targeting, timing, transparency and overall implementation of activities oriented at the community level. During each field visit, POH personnel is required to fill apposite forms that include a section for open-ended feedback and complaints. Finally, the numerous permanent bases that POH has across the targeted communities are equipped with mail boxes, suggestion boxes; focal point protection help desks for unanimous and isolated reporting of abuses or complaints.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Concerning WASH, POH will work collaboratively with other actors (UN agencies, NGOS, FBOs and CBOs) involved in child protection issues to safeguard the delivery of WASH services in the schools and communities. POH will be accountable to the beneficiaries by enabling them to participate in the project at the planning, implementation, and evaluation phases. Moreover, appropriate mechanisms through which affected populations can measure the adequacy of interventions, and address concerns and complaints will be set up. On regularly basis, POH conducts community engagement meetings such as awareness campaigns, innovative youth events that allows open participation/discussion over the quality of progress of the activity conducted. POH embrace a last-mile approach through field-based personal and daily visits to the field and conducts impact analysis and post monitoring reviews, which are analyzed distinguishing relevant categories of people with the objective of providing detailed information about the targeting of the intervention and beneficiaries barriers to access services. Subsequent projects are designed to address such barrier and put in place a system of monitoring that puts special emphasis on monitoring the targeting of those groups of beneficiaries found to face particular barriers.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

POH will place protection and gender issues at the centre of the intervention by engaging directly with the IDPs, returnees and host communities’ children and CWDs so that their needs, priorities, rights are addressed within the scope of the project. POH will always create conducive and interactive environments that encourage them to express their needs, rights and priorities. Apart from the active involvement of the rights holders in the project, POH will ensure that the duty bearers (Government, PTAs/SMCS, institutions and non-state actors) play an effective role to promote the rights of the CWDs and girls. Activities will be developed with the active participation of women and parents in all stages of the implementation process, to ensure that girls’ priority needs are taken into consideration and that WaSH infrastructures meet the right gender standards (IASC, 2015, Guidelines for Integrating Gender-Based Violence Interventions in Humanitarian Action: Reducing risk, promoting resilience and aiding recovery). Moreover, prevention of GBV (Sexual Exploitation) is pursued by displaying SEA code of conducts at service access points (schools, health facilitates, distribution points and community). On the other hand, safety audits are conducted, and multiple locations selected to avoid far travel of beneficiaries and reducing risks. Community members, staff and stakeholder’s capacity will be strengthened through specific PSEA trainings.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

The capillary and continuous presence that POH has in the communities targeted by the intervention (POH has more than 80% of its staff, including senior staff), and the extensive use of staff recruited locally among people recognized by the communities, ensures that such fruitful cooperation with project beneficiaries continues throughout the implementation of the project. All project’s beneficiaries (female and male IDPs and returnees’ children, CWDs and host communities’ children) will be considered as capable and active resources rather than as passive recipients who do not have a say in the project. Thanks to a very localized approach with POH staff regularly present and usually coming from the same community of project locations, accountability is pursued also directly through its staff that can act as bridge between top management and beneficiaries. Furthermore, considering the purposely field-oriented management team (officers and also project managers are field based), beneficiaries and affected population at large as constant possibility to interact and file complain to staff at different level. Yearly, POH conducts surveys which results are discussed through open meetings with the community, the county and payam officials and traditional leaders (Boma and sub Boma Chiefs). During these events, POH explains to the community its plans for the incoming years as a means to an informed discussion among community members and receive solid suggestions. POH extension officers, AAP committees before selecting a person/group/community as beneficiaries, establish a case specific dialog where POHs expectations are compared with the one from the beneficiaries in order to evaluate possible challenges/ intervention failure chance. Beside the surveys, POH’s beneficiaries have access to information during participatory meetings which provided platform to address issues linked to power-relations whereby some strata of the population culturally marginalized can find the opportunity to express themselves, include the view of beneficiaries in programming and allow intervention in case of issues reported thus increase transparency and accountability through awareness of beneficiaries on protection-related issues as well as project-design.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

• Risks that beneficiaries are exposed to violence during registration/distribution as a result of the distance to or location of humanitarian assistance and services: distribution sites will be established equitably in all the Boma which is easily accessible by the local communities, and in Boma and the project will ensure vulnerable groups are reached through mobile services; Reducing waiting time of beneficiaries at service point by adhering to the effective flow in service provision as per the guideline and ensure every distribution sites has got adequate staffing (CHWs) attending promptly to beneficiaries. In areas where case of violence is high ration provided to beneficiaries will be adequate enough to reduce on frequency of follow up visits at distribution sites. Engagement of local and traditional leaders during mobilization to provide security in their area for their people accessing services and protection of service access points; Women are always more exposed than men, therefore service provider will make sure women are encouraged to be accompanied by their husbands while accessing services. • Risk of no access to or awareness of protection assistance/ referral services for survivors of protection incidents ( GBV, child protection, evictions, explosive hazards,): the project will ensure initial involvement of women and local women groups representatives in AAP committees and in planning activities implementation; the facilities will act as a valuable entry point for disseminating information about available GBV services and collecting feedback from women and girls about their needs/safety concerns; ensuring CNVs are trained/oriented on how to safely respond to a disclosure and that they have the most up-to-date referral pathway. With support and coordination with GBV specialists from available local actors, household nutrition screenings will be used as a modality to proactively disseminate information on available GBV services. Health and WASH workers/volunteers will be equipped with pocket cards containing relevant contact information; Representations of the GBV referral pathways will be posted in all nutrition sites. • Risk that beneficiaries are not accessing/using complaint and feedback mechanisms: POH has managed to install suggestion boxes in most parts of the nutrition sites however very few feedbacks were received majorly because majority of beneficiaries are illiterate (not able to write) especially in Unity. Beneficiaries access to information in form communicating complaints and receiving feedback will be enhanced through formation of AAP committees at Boma level with representatives from each village who will be in touch with beneficiaries to receive compliant and feedback. POH will meet with the AAP committee to discuss on the complaints and feedback from time to time (Monthly); On quarterly basis there will be a help desk at Nutrition sites attended by one personnel from POH at a higher position to receive complaints and feedback from beneficiaries. • Risk of interference from external stakeholders in the impartial and needs-based selection of beneficiaries thus negatively affecting social cohesion and sparking intercommunity conflict over access to resources: POH will ensure Local stakeholders are oriented on the program in term of its objective/purpose, targeted beneficiaries and justification of selection criteria such as those with acute malnutrition (children and PLW); Involvement of Local stakeholders and community organs/committees or groups and tradition leaders in selection procedures e.g. vulnerability assessment; Visual presentation of targeted beneficiaries and their entitlement at distribution points/service access points in form of charts, poster; Engagement of community committee/groups or local leaders to participate, supervise and monitor distribution; Distribution points should be centrally and equitably allocated accordingly.
