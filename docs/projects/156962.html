Project Summary

The program’s overall objective is the socio-economic well-being and adaptive capacities of people and communities in Syria are strengthened through increased income, assets and consumption. Through two livelihood centers in the Northeast, this project aims to help Syrians boost their income and find more regular work through business and life skills training, vocational training, apprenticeships and business start-up grants. Vocational training covers a range of sectors, including courses in tailoring, car repairs, mobile phone maintenance, hair dressing and make-up artistry.

Needs

At least 69% of the Syrian population lives in extreme poverty and 10.5 million people are food insecure. Of this number, 4 million people are at risk of becoming acutely food insecure. Livelihood opportunities are scarce in Syria and many households depend primarily on unsustainable livelihoods and negative coping strategies to generate income (sale of property and assets, use of savings, borrowing, remittances from families and friends, etc.). Some households also resort to extreme coping strategies, including child labor and early marriage of girls to reduce the economic burden. Conflict-related activities including joining armed groups and smuggling are also becoming viable livelihood alternatives. Large-scale displacement and urbanization have meant that many Syrians find themselves with livelihoods skills and work experience, which are not in demand locally. The agricultural sector has also been decimated, as fields have been left unattended, equipment has been looted, and power cuts and a lack of fuel have prevented farmers from planting and harvesting. Additionally, there is reduced access to land due to contamination with Explosive Remnants of War (ERW). It is estimated that 5.1 million people are living in areas highly contaminated with ERW. The conflict has resulted in large-scale disruption to livelihoods and an estimated 69 percent of Syrians are currently living in extreme poverty (less than $1.90 per day). Combined with a sharp increase in prices and currency depreciation, purchasing power of Syrian households has been greatly reduced. Decreased purchasing power inhibits vulnerable households in Northeast Syria from meeting basic needs, forcing households to resort to negative coping strategies to survive. IRC’s August 2018 livelihoods assessment in Deir-ez-Zor revealed that the majority of IDPs surveyed reported having insufficient ability to meet basic needs, including food (88.1%), NFIs (80.7%),%) and shelter provisions (78.3%). As a result of severe disruptions to households’ livelihoods, access to income and reduced purchasing power, households are relying on negative coping strategies to meet their essential basic needs. IRC’s household vulnerability assessments show a high reliance on coping mechanisms associated with lack of income and accumulated debt. The IRC’s baseline survey conducted in northeast Syria identified that 7.2% of households reported having no main income earner; in 27.7% of households the main income earner was elderly; in 24.1% of cases the main income earner had a chronic disease; and in 13.9% the main income earner had a disability. Approximately 63% of assessed households’ financial resources were spent to buy food, leaving only 37% of financial resources for non-food related basic needs. The average household dietary diversity scores were 5.3 out of 12 possible, indicating very low dietary diversity and reliance on cereals, oil and sugar, without proper sources of other required vitamins and nutrients. Measurement of household hunger score showed 46.4% of households in Ar Raqqa had moderate or severe hunger during the 30 days prior to measurement. The average Reduced Coping Strategies Index (rCSI) was reported as 20.7 out of 56 possible, indicating high reliance on coping strategies, in particular, relying on less preferred and less expensive food, reducing number of meals per day, as well as reducing portion size of the meals. 62.8% of households reported having to borrow money to ensure their households’ basic needs were met during the 30 days prior to survey, and 42.3% reported they had to withdraw children from school to send them to work.

Implementation of activities
Protection Risk Analysis

In households, women, children, and other at-risk household members (such as those with disabilities and older persons) may experience forms of violence by their intimate partners, caregivers, or other household members. “Safer in homes and communities” requires that people be protected from harm and are also able to seek and receive support to reduce their vulnerability to harm, as well as heal and recover from harm. Williams and Thompson (2011) found that collective action to re-establish community links is critical in helping individuals recover from trauma and promoting safe communities and contributes to a reduction in morbidity from the psychological impact of conflict-related trauma. When women, PWDs and other marginalized groups become part of decision-making processes by taking on leadership roles within decision-making bodies and institutions, these groups are then able to exert greater influence. The removal of barriers to full social, political and economic participation is important across all pathways and for all groups. The ability of women to exercise influence can be strengthened by supporting economic resource transfers targeting women with activities to intentionally engage men and gatekeepers to change attitudes, perceptions, and behaviors that act as a barrier for women. The lack of safe access can create a higher barrier to economic participation for marginalized community members such as women, the disabled, the elderly or youth. The safety of markets, community meeting rooms and other common spaces, banks and other financial institutions, as well as places of work should take into consideration structural integrity and accessibility, basic environmental safety and the maintenance of a civilian, neutral character. Accessing these locations should not pose safety hazards to community members. Safe access supports equal access by marginalized groups, including women and persons affected by conflict and displacement.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

i. Market stability: While the market in Northeast Syria remains vulnerable to contested governing authorities and/or possible closure of international and regional supply lines, the markets in the governorates of Ar Raqqa, Deir-ez-Zor, and Al Hassakeh governorates have remained functional, accessible, and resilient in terms of maintaining a supply of basic needs items and prices. It is unlikely that Ar Raqqa, Deir-ez-Zor, and Al Hassakeh will experience a wide-spread and total market collapse.ii. Security: While the operating environment in Northeast Syria is relatively stable, the programming is likely to face a higher level of instability. Therefore, there must be a close working relationships with other actors in Northeast to monitor the situation and coordinate on operational and programmatic responses. Additionally, IRC will work closely with the Mine Action Group and other actors to ensure safe programming and to prevent pull factors towards areas of potential harm.iii. Access: IRC endeavors to have unhindered access in all of the communities. However, in preparation for severe security deterioration or changes in the control in the areas where IRC manages programs, has developed Syria-specific remote management guidelines, in-line with IRC’s Global Remote Management Guidelines. Based on level of access and capacity of staff, IRC in NES is able to provide remote control over programs, where highly skilled national staff implement with decisions from international managers located apart from programs, or remote delegation, where there is partial or temporary delegation of authority to national staff at project sites while other staff are in a separate location. In case expatriate staff have to relocate to Iraq during a full military operation and manage remotely, our national staff are capable of implementing emergency programs in NES and will be supported with technical guidance from the expatriate staff working out of Iraq. Additionally, key positions such as of Field Coordinator (FC) have been nationalized and the FC will be the focal point for the emergency response. IRC reinvigorated its risk mitigation efforts in 2015 with the establishment of a dedicated Ethics and Compliance Unit (ECU). This unit is responsible for ensuring that the IRC maintains and enforces effective policies, procedures, controls, processes and systems to prevent, detect and investigate potential violations of law or regulation and/or material failures to meet its obligations under contracts, grants, or agreements as well as its own ethical and conduct standards. The ECU is charged with addressing not just IRC’s own activities, but those of related third parties that may be viewed as acting on the IRC’s behalf such as partners, sub-grantees, subcontractors, suppliers, incentive workers, and others.
