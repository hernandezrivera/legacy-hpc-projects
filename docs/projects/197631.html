Project Summary

Through the proposed intervention, SIF will contribute to achieving objectives of the 2023 Protection Sector’s Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP) for Syria through 1) providing psychosocial support (PSS) to children, 2) identifying cases facing protection risks, access to specialized services and case-management 3) supporting the establishment of Children committees in schools, 4) conducting awareness raising on a high range of protection concerns such as GBV, general protection, child protection (CP) and risk education (RE) toward explosive hazards and 5) implementing capacity building training on PSEA and child safeguarding.
Proposed locations were identified according to the severity of the needs, their level of accessibility, and the experience of SIF. The comprehensive, multisector and multiyear approach was the determining element in the selection of the locations for 2023, with the aim of promoting the sustainability of the interventions, community self-reliance and resilience.
In line with the results of consultations with the local community and relevant authorities, and taking into account sector priorities, the proposed intervention will focus on the age-group from 12-18, SIF will tailor awareness sessions aiming to meet their development needs specifically. Youth and adolescents will be involved in all project phases. Children with disabilities will be also involved in the activities that fit to their disabilities and specialized staff will be appointed for inclusion.
SIF has ongoing child protection and education programmes in the target locations with 10 schools in Dara’a governorate, 4 schools in Hama governorate, 4 schools and 1 informal settlement in Homs governorate. This project will aim to scale up these interventions, including in juvenile centers. SIF will consider and ensure gender-and-age aspects across all sub-sectors of focus – CP, GBV, and general protection issues. Cultural preferences and sensitivities will be considered through consultations with the staff – the majority of whom will be from the local community – and the affected populations. Specific needs of girls and boys, people with disabilities and other vulnerable groups will be considered in the design of the intervention, and will be continuously evaluated throughout the implementation.
Safeguarding and protection principles, including the Do No Harm approach, will guide SIF team throughout all the phases of the project cycle. SIF is fully committed to the SPHERE standards and humanitarian principles based on which SIF’s internal policies and procedures have been created to guide project implementation.
Accountability to affected population will be realized through strengthening child-friendly community-based complaint mechanisms, improved participatory assessments and enhanced outreach through SIF MEAL team.
SIF will implement this project in close coordination with various stakeholders on the ground for an effective response, including the Ministry of Education, Ministry of Social Affairs, Protection sector, I-NGOs, contractor, SARC, Clusters, local committees, refugee, IDP/returnee and host community leaders, in order to bridge gaps in available services and ensure better access of People in Needs to services.
4Ws to relevant sectors (Protection, Mine Action) and FTS reporting will be ensured.

3 - Needs

Syria is enduring a protracted humanitarian crisis as a result of ongoing conflict, insecurity, displacements and damaged infrastructure coupled with the deterioration of the economy. Significant air strikes targeting residential areas and vital facilities such as hospitals or schools resulted in mass displacements and destruction. It is estimated that 15.1 million PiN are in need of protection assistance and 6.7 million children face violence and protection concerns and have been exposed to protection risks, ranging from psychosocial distress, early marriage, GBV, physical disability, detention, kidnapping, injury or death (HNO, 2023). During SIF assessment, severe issues of GBV were reported, though cases remain under-reported and difficult to identify due to the absence of case management services and societal constraints. High prevalence of child labour is preventing school attendance. Psychosocial distress caused by children’s exposure to violence in their daily lives, community, schools and homes, along with repeated displacements, loss of or separation from family and lack of basic social services is affecting children’s well-being and development of girls and boys. Unexploded ordinance remains a serious problem.Particularly in Daraa, assessments show that there is high number of returnees in target localities, high numbers of out of school children and child labor, kidnapping as well as a concentration of explosive remnants of war and related incidents.

4 - Implementation of activities

Assistance will be provided through types of interventions: 1) structured and non-structured PSS 2) case management and referral to specialised services 3) child welfare committees 4) awareness raising sessions on CP and protection to children, parents, and education staff and 5) capacity building training on PSEA and child safeguarding; and three modalities: 1) CP activities will be conducted by a mobile team in public schools, in coordination with SARC, DoE and MoE 2) in community centers established by SIF 5 days per week in the absence of sufficient public facilities in targeted areas. Interventions will also target informal settlements and juvenile centers. In community centers, children will be provided with both non-formal education and community-based CP services 3) Outreach Volunteers (ORV) will be deployed to reach the population of surrounding villages. ORV will be trained by SIF on risk education, CP minimum standards and PSS. CP interventions will include provision of Mine Risk Education and other ERW educational activities seeking to address the risk of injury and promoting behavioral change, in coordination with UNMAS. SIF will ensure participation of children in all phases of the project and monitoring on the project progress to ensure that standards of implementation and fund resources are being effectively used. Participatory approach will be undertaken in all stage. Discussions will be gender-disaggregated and conducted by women facilitators if necessary.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk 1: Perceptions that assistance is delivered in a discriminatory or non-equitable manner leading to tensions. Likelihood: Medium Impact: High Mitigation: (1) selection of localities based on the Protection sector severity scale analysis, (2) community consultations to ensure risk of discrimination is minimized and processes are transparent and informed (3) coordination with other service providers (4) prioritization of services to groups considered particularly vulnerable, keeping the selection of beneficiary transparent (5) monitoring and feedback during and after service provision develop schedules Risk 2: Community reluctance towards protection services, stigmatization and violence against victims of GBV Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: (1) gender-balanced staffing; men and boys participating in awareness sessions on GBV (2) case management in coordination with other services; data protection policy ensuring confidentiality; child friendly room ensuring confidentiality and privacy during case management and counselling (3) careful assessment of risk before taking any decision always respecting the principles of do no harm and the best interests of the child (5) identify solutions that are seen as culturally acceptable to the family or community and that uphold the rights of children.


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

Risk: Sexual exploitation of community members, including children, in return of promise for assistance. Mitigation measures include the following: (1) SIF ensures that specific PSEA indicators are included in the monitoring framework.(2) Vetting process is conducted during the recruitment of a candidate at application and reference stages. (3) The code of conduct, which includes the strict prohibition of SEA, is signed by everyone working for and with SIF, including staff, SIF volunteers and contractors. After the selection of the contractors, as the same time of the signature of the purchase order, the contractor signs the SIF Code of conduct which is then attached as an annex to the contract/agreement.(4) SIF employees are trained on PSEA and a PSEA focal point is assigned in all governorates targeted by SIF. Awareness sessions of the community members on PSEA are conducted every year. Sessions includes the definition of the what is SEA and channels of reporting/whistleblowing. (5) SIF community-based complaint mechanisms is accessible to affected populations in all response locations. (6) A process, steps, and timeframe for the investigation of allegations of SEA has been developed by SIF.
