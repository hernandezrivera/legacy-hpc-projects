Project Summary

Boys and girls in post -conflict Iraq are affected by severe psychological distress, risk of arrest and detention due to perceived affiliation to extremist groups, denial of access to birth registration and civil documentation, social threats and stigma against boys and girls born of rape by extremist group. At the same time, sextual violence and exploitation, child marriage, child labour and children joining armed forces due to economic reasons are observed in post conflict Iraq.
This project aims to intervene on child protection issues and risk outlined above by providing child protection prevention and response services such as community based, structured psychosocial support activities, case management, legal assistance and support to obtain civil documentation, legal representation and release and reintegration for children in detention, family tracing and reunification of separated and unaccompanied children including children of foreign nationalities, advocacy with key government ministries to find solution for barriers affecting to provide service for critical child protection issues. This project targets boys and girls in IDPs (in camp and out of camp), returnees and host communities who have been most affected by conflict.

Needs

Four years of violence in Iraq has had a drastic impact on boys and girls; many have experienced unabated insecurity and violence and multiple displacements, either under Islamic State in Iraq and the Levant (ISIL) or during the long and violent campaign to oust the group from Iraqi territory. Small-scale conflict in 2018 led to new displacements and large numbers of people linger in camps and informal settlements. In Dahuk, ethnic minorities remain displaced until their security can be guaranteed, while in Erbil and Sulaymaniyah, large numbers of families have been internally displaced for more than 4 years. In 2017, 667 incidents were reported to the Iraq Monitoring and Reporting Mechanism (MRM) on grave violations of child rights, of which most were killing and maiming of children. Children associated with ISIL or who lived under its control, have a high likelihood of having witnessed, survived, and been forced to participate the most extreme forms of violence. In addition, many children were arrested for perceived affiliation with ISIL, detained for significant periods without charge and sentenced to years of imprisonment for affiliation and for acts they may have been coerced to commit. Although the number of violations has abated, a year after Mosul was retaken, children’s experiences of violence and displacement continue to negatively affect their wellbeing. A recent survey (MCNA-VI) revealed that psychosocial distress among returnee (6 times) and remainee (8 times) children was significantly higher than IDP children. The severe stress experienced by these children can have significant consequences for their long-term health. To stabilize and to allow recovery, children and families who fled conflict areas must have means by which to re-engage in patterns of normal life. While most children are resilient enough to recover with school and structured psychosocial support, some children will need specialised services to address their needs. Children who were separated or unaccompanied during the conflict continue to need alternative care, family tracing and reunification with their parents, or PSS to increase their personal reservoirs of resilience. In addition, many children who were born under ISIL have been deprived of their basic identity rights and services due to absence of adequate mechanisms for them to obtain civil documents. Though Iraq’s national coverage rate for birth registration is 98 percent, in Ninewa 16 percent of children under age 5 (out of camps only) do not have birth certificates (Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey, MICS, 2018). Government refusal to recognize documents issued by ISIL and the absence of other evidence of births and marriage ceremonies by recognised government departments has posed major challenges for children’s legal status. Apart from the conflict, Iraqi children have significant experiences of violence. Violent discipline at home is identified as a critical child protection issue through recent MCNA-IV survey and MICS 2018. According to MICS 2018, 80 per cent of children age 1 to 14 years experienced some form of violent discipline method during the last one month, while 7 pe rcent of children between 5 and 17 are working under hazardous condition, and 24.8 per cent of all women married before their 18th birthday, with significant numbers married under age 15. Finally, large scale contamination of return areas by Unexploded Ordnance (UXO), Explosive Remnants of War (ERW), and Improvised Explosive Devices (IED) continue to pose significant risks to children and families, which can be reduced through awareness and education.

Protection Risk Analysis

Due to the dynamic political and security situation in Iraq, particularly outside the Kurdistan Region of Iraq (KRI), the overall political and social environment is subject to change, and there are moderate risks of access constraints affecting parts of the country, the presence of security actors and armed groups, and sectarian tensions which may impact UNICEF and partners’ operational presence in some areas. Key protection risks for programme participants include: a) Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (SEA). UNICEF identified Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (SEA) as a substantial risk during project preparation. Women and girls in particular are at risk of SEA who are perceived to have affiliation with ISIL group. Child protection partner who provide legal assistance and finding whereabout arrested children may receive threat and protection risk. b) Retaliation and Collective Punishment. Boys and girls and their families with perceived affiliation to ISIL may under risk of arbitrary arrest and detained in undisclosed locations and may subject to torture and intimidation and social stigma. c) Children born of rape and their mothers. Existing social norms and cultural barrier among Yazidi, Kurdish and Arab communities may post threat to newly born children of rape. Communities including mothers deny the existence of the child. Mother don’t want to disclose their children born of rape. This categories of children and mothers may have significant protection risk by their society, and may be unwilling to approach for services. Child protection partners may also experience protection risk in dealing with this category of beneficiaries. d) Protection risk for CP actors and lawyers: Lawyers who represent boys and girls who perceived affiliation with extremist group may be arrested or threated. CP actors who try to assist of intervene with authorities on behalf of vulnerable populations or who report SEA, retaliation or intimidation may be at risk and be discouraged from assisting all populations on an equal basis.

Mitigation Measures

UNICEF takes a proactive approach to protection from sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA): a Code of Conduct, PSEA reporting mechanism, support to and guidance for the survivors in case any instances of SEA were to occur within the context of the project, establishing a survivor-centered approach with multiple entry points for anyone experiencing SEA to seek the help they need; and these measures are taken in close coordination with the Iraq PSEA Network and partner organizations. UNICEF partners are trained on the ‘Do No Harm’ Principle, PSEA, and Community-Based Complaints mechanism. As part of partnership monitoring, UNICEF conducts monitoring visits and holds partnership review meetings with implementing partners to review progress and help identify challenges and solutions, including protection risks. UNICEF requires that all staff working on the project sign the organisation’s code of conduct, which includes the obligation to report any abuse by staff, or suspicion thereof. UNICEF has strict protocols against SEA. UNICEF has established close working relationship with key government institutions, directorates, and ministries such as High Judicial Council in Baghdad, Ministry of Justice (MoJ), Ministry of Labour and Social Affairs (MoLSA) and periodically discusses matters affecting those categories of boys and girls and agrees on steps and approaches to address key issues. More importantly, before finalising implementing partners, UNICEF discuss with relevant ministries and get their agreement in a transparent manner, while avoiding undue influence of government over partnerships. This approach has created trust in UNICEF’s work and enables UNICEF to bring unresolved issues and critical problems to ministries and institutions to solve. MoLSA and MoJ have granted UNICEF and its partners to have access to detention facilities and other locations with children. Legal representation by lawyers are being provided. UNICEF is closely working with implementing partners to document any critical protection risk and challenges and address it with appropriate channel. UNICEF recognizes that families and communities have the greatest impact on child well-being. By using a community-based approach, using entry points such as schools to reach children, and by identifying and addressing social norms that constrain and affect positive outcomes for children, UNICEF works to lessen the stigma associated to experiences of violence, as well as to help-seeking.UNICEF has agreed a mechanism with MoLSA on the issues of birth registration and alternative care for unaccompanied children or children born of rape. A UNICEF partnership to work on this issue has been agreed with MoLSA.

How is your project accountable to affected population

UNICEF has a strong commitment to accountability and forward accountability to its key constituencies: children and the communities where they reside. UNICEF already has in place systems that make individual programmers more accountable and transparent to populations that it seeks to assist, both in situations of stability as well as in crisis. UNICEF monitoring and reporting system includes beneficiary feedback to ensure the voices of beneficiaries are reflected in the monitoring and evaluation. UNICEF ensures crisis-affected communities, authorities and donors will regularly receive information on the following: • UNICEF Iraq Humanitarian monthly sitrep is available to the public, including the donor community. The sitrep consist of achievement, progress and funding status etc.• During implementation: targeting criteria used based on need, code of conduct and feedback mechanisms if the code of conduct is not respected, mandate of the organisation, content and mode of assistance, intervention dates and frequency.• During monitoring and evaluation: process of reasons for, and results of the evaluation, as well as feedback mechanisms for course corrections.• UNICEF is in the process of engaging an institution for documenting feedback from affected populations for UNICEF-supported programmes.
