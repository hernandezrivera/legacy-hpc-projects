Project Summary

Save the Children (SC) has operated a large and integrated child protection programme in Cox’s Bazar since the start of the Rohingya influx, aimed at strengthening protective factors and reducing risk factors at every level of the child's environment. With funding through the 2020 JRP, Save the Children, in partnership with 3 local NGOs, will maintain critical interventions and services including case management, family tracing and reunification, alternative care, adolescent and PSS programming, with a focus on protecting the most vulnerable children, including UASC, child headed households, married and house bound girls and children with disabilities. Through maintaining and establishing services in both refugee and communities, critical gaps in service delivery will be addressed, and social cohesion strengthened, by targeting inequity of access to services which is a conflict driver in the current context. To reach the most vulnerable children, project interventions will be delivered through static safe spaces (multi-purpose child and adolescent spaces, adolescent friendly spaces, adolescent clubs) and through improved quality and reach of mobile services.

Needs

Refugee community:One-third of refugee families have at least one specific protection vulnerability that requires tailored community-based and specialized protection responses to ensure access to basic services for persons with specific needs (UNHCR 2019). Risk of kidnapping, trafficking and violence in the community are three perceived risks most reported after natural hazards (UNHCR 2019), while FGDs run by SC indicate that children prioritize kidnapping and sexual and physical abuse as protection risks due to an unstable and unsafe camp environment. 52% of assessed refugee households reported the presence of at least one sign of distress within their own family (MSNA, 2019). GBV is a constant threat to women and girls and includes reports of rape, physical assault, sexual harassment, early and forced marriage, and psychological abuse (GBVIMS, 2019). Approximately 5-10% of Rohingya households engage in child marriage and two-thirds of these households consider it a normal practice, while approximately one-third use it as a coping strategy to either protect girls or reduce economic burdens on the household (July 2019 UNHCR’s SPP 5 and MSNA, 2019). 57% of households are unable to identify any of the GBV service points, while 85% mentioned ‘Majihis’ as their preferred point of referral for cases of sexual violence, but who are criticized for being patriarchal and exploitative (MSNA, 2019). Upscaling awareness-raising on GBV services and safe referral points for boys and girls, and intensifying behavioural change programs to address socio-cultural barriers, remains a key priority. There is enormous potential for the civic engagement of adolescent boys and girls in the camp, to enhance their resilience and self-esteem, and to be engaged in peacebuilding activities with other adolescents within and between the refugee and host communities. 8,596 unaccompanied and separated children (UASC) have been identified (CPIMS, 2019) and are considered to be at heightened risk of violence and exploitation (CPSS strategy 2019). Girls, who represent a larger proportion (57%) of this vulnerable group, are particularly at risk of child marriage, sexual exploitation, abuse and neglect. Ongoing support to alternative care arrangements, referrals of child headed households to other services, and peer support of foster families, is needed. Despite a large number of children believing their primary caregiver to be deceased (SC-UASC report 2018), children need to be consistently consulted for referrals to TFL and FTR services. Host Community:15% of the 2,873 host community households surveyed in December 2018 reported the presence of at-risk children, with 5% of households reporting the presence of children who were already married/about to get married and 32% of women and 3% of men aged 20-25 who married before reaching 18 years of age. Recent assessment findings confirm the presence of serious child protection issues in host communities: 6% of assessed households report at least one member under the age of 18 is already married or about to get married; 8% report the presence of at least one child working; and 52% of working children report engaged in hazardous work, namely working more than 40 hours per week and working with machinery/lifting heavy objects. 7% of host community households report relying on children to collect firewood; exposing them to further protection risks (MSNA, 2019). As a result of deep-rooted patriarchal social norms, GBV is one of the most pervasive form of human rights violations to which Bangladeshi women and girls are exposed. 21% of assessed households reported safety concerns being a barrier to accessing services and facilities such as health, education and markets and in 15% of households, married women reported that they were not permitted to go to markets (20% for unmarried women). Recreational space for children in host communities is limited and the pressure on limited space attributed by some host community.

Activities or outputs

Case Management: SC will continue to directly provide quality case management services for children at risk of, or experiencing, violence, abuse, neglect and exploitation, prioritize mainstreaming GBV best practices and a cadre of para social workers to collaborate with case workers and child protection committees on supporting low risk cases. Strengthening protective home environments: Targeted parents and caregivers (child registered for case management services, including UASC) and caregivers of children at risk, will be supported to create a safe and supportive home environment.Mental health and psychosocial support interventions: Curriculum based structured and focused psychosocial support for children will be provided through adolescent spaces, multi-purpose child and adolescent spaces, and though mobile service delivery. Caregivers of children with moderate to severe signs of distress will be supported through MHPSS support groupsAdolescent resilience and empowerment: Targeted adolescent girls and boys will be identified and referred to gender specific life skills curriculums, resilience sessions, and protective behavior sessions. A network of adolescent clubs will also be supported.Community based protection: SC will continue to support a network of Community Based Child Protection Committees (adults with adolescent representatives), adolescent boys and girls’ clubs, and children’s clubs, in and around our protection spaces and targeted host communities. Disaster risk reduction: SC will continue to build awareness of weather related events and risks of family separation, psychosocial distress and trafficking, in partnership with a network of community based groups, volunteers and community mobilisers. Child friendly DRR messages and awareness raising will be mainstreamed through all protection spaces, and ‘Temporary Family Shelters’.Systems strengthening: Project activities will include capacity development/sharing with local partners, as well as contribution to capacity building initiatives led through the Child Protection Sub Sector. SC will offer to coach and mentor DSS social workers on best practices in GBV/child protection case management and supportive supervision. Sustainability: SC will promote community ownership, local partnership, capacity and responsibility for child protection in target locations. Accountability: In 2020, committees in each protection space, including children, will steer community level consultation and decision making. SC will also launch child friendly versions of quality benchmarks for each main CP service.PSEA and CSG: SC has prioritized maintaining a robust internal mechanism for PSEA and child safeguarding, consisting of codes of conduct, mandatory induction trainings of staff/volunteers/partners, refresher trainings, development and monitoring of risk registers, and capacity building of PSEA and CSG focal points.Coordination: SC will continue to be an active contributor to the child protection sub sector, and continue to lead/co-lead the development of SOPs and guidance notes and act as the CP focal point nominated camps (10 in 2019), engage in upazila and field level coordination meetings, and as a member of the case management working group.
