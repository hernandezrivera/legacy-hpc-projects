Project Summary

The project promotes the rights and empowerment of Rohingya and host community women, providing gender responsive, diversified livelihood services and support to increase women’s economic resilience and engagement in income generation. This is achieved through a combination of accelerated adult learning, technical and vocational training, entrepreneurship support, and evidence-informed advocacy.
Upgrades initiated in 2024 will be finalized to improve the training facilities and equipment of 8 Multi-Purpose Women’s Centers (MPWCs), 5 in the camps and 3 in host community locations, and the Women's Market (camp 5). The MPWCs offer integrated empowerment and protection services to adult women and adolescent girls, including women with disabilities and gender diverse persons (camp 4), applying feminist principles and best practice, ensuring competency-based learning opportunities and accessibility of training services and materials, sensitizing trainers on the concerns and needs of the most vulnerable women and girls, including those with disabilities.
Accelerated Adult Learning will be provided to a minimum 200 Rohingya women to enable them to progress to vocational training and engage in productive activities. Altogether 1,200 Rohingya women (primarily aged 18-35) will increase their skills through non-formal technical trainings. The service offer of MPWCs will expand to include one competency-based vocational training (360 hours) per MPWC (industrial sewing machine operation or bakery), training a total of 200 Rohingya women and adolescent girls (aged 18-19) in line with the framework on skills development and related guidance. Basic training at camp MPWCs will also include digital literacy and business skills development. 600 volunteers/trainees will benefit from stipends/cash grants/start-up materials and 200 trained women will benefit from production group activities, developing clothing and decorative items for sale, and female hygiene materials distributed through MPWC activities. Women vendors in both communities at the Women’s Market will also be supported with business planning and business exchange and promotional events will be implemented in support of women’s entrepreneurial activities.
Technical skills trainings and vocational trainings will be offered at MPWCs in 3 host community locations, including camp 25 (host community area) and in Ukhiya and Teknaf in areas of high poverty far from government training institutions. Nonetheless, market opportunities exist and will be leveraged, supporting IPs to introduce demand-driven vocational training (industrial sewing machine operation and bakery) for 150 young and adult women, partnering with government accredited organizations, alongside technical skills training for 300 women aged 18-35, including women with disabilities, and entrepreneurship-oriented training (business skills, soft skills, and digital skills), promoting opportunities to participate in government-accredited TVET. To address the high unemployment rates among women, UN Women will provide start-up grants to host community women to establish micro-business (target: 300) and support market linkages through advice, advocacy and events engaging the business community. The project will contribute analysis of women’s livelihoods and their support needs.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

Roughly 51 % of the Rohingya refugees are women and girls. Lack of education among adult refugees is at alarming levels, with 90 % in 2019 assessed as either never attended, or as not having completed their primary education while 7 % of adults are estimated to have unfinished secondary education and just 1 % as having completed secondary or higher education (World Bank, 2019) – statistics which can be assumed to be higher among women than men. This deprivation in education among Rohingya women hampers their entry into vocational and non-formal training and presents a long-term challenge to ensuring women’s access to livelihoods opportunities, including in Rakhine, in the event of return. The absence of post-secondary education for young Rohingya women (and youth in general), points to the need to ensure non-formal technical training and vocational training options are made available to them, as keeping young women in education increases the likelihood of them living healthy, productive lives. Among also host community women, poor education outcomes limit participation in vocational and post-secondary training and in income generation activities. Data from 2019 shows that 37 % of adult women in host community areas close to the camps had no schooling at all compared to 29 % among men, and only 52 % of adults could read with men twice as likely to have completed secondary school. The 2024 ILO skills demand and supply assessment, focused on five sectors in the Chattogram-Cox's Bazar region with high growth potential and employment opportunities, highlights several systematic constraints, which contribute to lower enrollment and higher dropout rates among female trainees in TVET institutes (ILO, 2023). These include a lack of female trainers and regressive gender norms constraining females from participation. Based on UN Women’s programmatic assessments, access to TVET institutes for host community women is also hampered by long distances to government training institutes and the need for transportation support.In both the refugee camps and host community, women’s access to livelihood opportunities is extremely limited and is restricted by regressive gender norms, which continue to pose significant challenges to improving access to livelihood services and support for vulnerable women in the Rohingya and host communities. The situation of violence in the camps has slowed women’s participation in livelihoods activities, further exacerbated by gender-based violence, including intimate partner violence, with GBV-IMS showing increased numbers of GBV reported in 2024. Among both Rohingya and host community women and girls, disabilities pose a further, significant barrier, hindering their participation in training and income-generating activities. Today, altogether 44 % of refugee women and men (24% of women) are of working age (18-59 years). ISNA 2024 data show only 46 % of female-headed households report income, compared to 75% of male-headed households. Notably, engagement of women in informal income generation in the camps is limited to only 8 per cent compared to 64 per cent among men. Income of the refugees is restricted to the informal economy, including casual or daily labor (47 per cent); self-employment (15 per cent); cash for work (15 per cent); other forms of informal employment (14 per cent); and volunteering (7 per cent). The disproportionate burden of unpaid care work further restricts women's choice, with 48% of women spending seven or more hours daily on caregiving versus 15.2% of men.In host communities, vulnerability increased by 13% since 2017 to reach 30% of households (REVA 7, 2024). Labor force participation is also lower than the national and divisional averages, with only 19% of females participated in the labor force compared to 71% among males, with about 60% of the adults unable to access skilled employment in 2019 (World Bank, 2022).

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

The project will ensure the safety, dignity, and rights of the targeted women, girls, and gender-diverse individuals, ensuring activities are designed and implemented in ways that Do No Harm, in line with the IASC protection principles. Protection monitoring and accountability mechanisms are built into IP reporting and continuous monitoring by UN Women ensure accountability. UN Women collaborates with UNFPA to undertake safety audits of MPWCs. All UN Women IPs are members of the PSEA Network and PSEA referrals and reporting mechanisms are in place in facilities (ref: 3.7). MPWCs are designed to provide a secure and welcoming environment exclusively for females. Protection is mainstreamed through a holistic approach, raising awareness among beneficiaries of their rights and supporting them to learn and to build their livelihoods skills. Mothers’ education has powerful intergenerational effects and is a strong determinant of child survival and education outcomes. The introduction of AAL is key to enhancing not only resilience of women, but also protection outcomes among them, and their children. Alongside education and livelihood services, MPWCs offer psychosocial counseling, GBV referrals, and awareness-raising sessions related to women’s rights issues including GBV, child marriage, and polygamy. These initiatives aim to mitigate the physical and psychological harm caused by violence, coercion, intentional deprivation, and other threats. In 2025, specialized legal aid services will be integrated in the MPWCs in the camps in collaboration with UNHCR. Each MPWC has a ‘Community Watch Group’ providing a platform for women to share feedback on, and influence decisions pertaining to the services and support provided by the MPWC. Project activities include promotion of women’s role in income generation and gender equal behaviors, a critical enabling factor, and under the WPHF, activities include conflict resolution training and social cohesion building activities.

2.3 - Age & Disability

Young women (18-35 years) are the primary beneficiary group and services and support under this project are designed to consider their age-specific needs, addressing the specific barriers that many in this group face, including gaps in their education that prevent them from accessing vocational training and present a challenge to their holistic empowerment and income generation capacity, with inter-generational effects. Trainings will address the growing needs among young women for education and training. This includes collaboration with UNICEF to provide education in the Myanmar Curriculum for young women. Childcare rooms with play areas and nursing facilities are provided, alongside integrated SRHR services within MPWCs supported by UNFPA. UN Women is dedicated to “leaving no one behind” and all UN Women staff and partners’ staff are trained in disability inclusion and on human rights-based approaches. Emphasis is placed on monitoring and guiding partners to ensure the specific challenges women and girls with disabilities face are identified and addressed. MPWC guidance developed by UN Women includes specific guidance and tools on how to ensure disability inclusion within MPWC activities, including at planning, implementation and monitoring stages. The project has a target of minimum 3 percent of beneficiaries who comprise women or adolescent girls with disabilities or with disabled family members. MPWCs are designed for safety and accessibility; ongoing renovations aim to further enhance accessibility for elderly women and those with disabilities, supported by accessible feedback mechanisms. UN Women will coordinate with organizations dedicated to persons with disabilities and use the Washington Questionnaire to select project participants, engaging volunteers to identify and support women with disabilities to access services, providing individualized and where possible peer-to-peer support, engaging as volunteers women with disabilities.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

Accountability to women and girls is central to UN Women's humanitarian response and all program planning includes needs assessments, client satisfaction surveys, consultations and FGDs with different stakeholders, especially women, in all their diversity. Throughout the program cycle, UN Women collaborates with communities to inform and seek community feedback on MPWC activities, emphasizing the active involvement of crisis-affected women and girls in decision-making throughout the project, in line with the IASC Revised Commitments on AAP and Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse. All project participants are oriented on PSEA to mitigate related risks. A community feedback mechanism is established within each MPWC and the Women’s Market in camp 5 with both written and verbal options available to ensure accessibility. UN Women supported MPWCs engage Community Outreach Volunteers, who serve as advocates for women’s livelihoods within their communities and for gender equal relations, promoting available MPWC services and support and gathering any feedback and complaints from their communities, which is shared with the MPWC Community Watch groups, which meet on a monthly basis to discuss and take action on any feedback or issues related to MPWC operation, to improve service provision. UN Women will also leverage its 6 Gender Field Officers in 2025 to monitor MPWC services, engaging directly with project beneficiaries to seek their feedback and to undertake regular consultations with community members to ensure that programming is tailored to the actual needs of beneficiaries, especially the most vulnerable women and girls, including women and girls with disabilities and female heads of households. All UN Women IPs have permits from government authorities to operate in Cox's Bazar (whether in refugee camps or host communities) to ensure accountability.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

"UN Women emphasizes the protection of all individuals from sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) through robust governance frameworks and operational strategies. Adopting a survivor-centered approach in line with the IASC’s core principles on SEA, UN Women advocates for adherence to humanitarian standards, gender equality, women's empowerment, and the protection of women's rights in the Rohingya crisis setting. As an active participant in the PSEA network, Protection Sector, GBV sub-sectors, and the GiHA WG, UN Women contributes to inter-agency discussions, planning and joint mechanisms and initiatives to foster accountability, with all partner organizations also actively involved in the PSEA Network. UN Women has established two PSEA focal points who participate in the inter-agency PSEA Network, with partners committing to PSEA through Partnership Agreements. Information about these focal points is clearly displayed in the UN Women sub-office and at the 9 MPWCs and Women’s Market, as well as in the offices of IPs. UN Women will continue to ensure, in coordination with the PSEA Network Coordinator, that all staff, partner personnel, and volunteers are trained on PSEA principles and codes of conduct, ensuring compliance with inter-agency standards. PSEA feedback and complaint mechanisms are established and regularly monitored at MPWCs. These are available in written and verbal forms through the box or the hotline number, with mechanisms in place to ensure client confidentiality and safety. UN Women ensures survivor-centered service assistance by providing MPWC-based Psychosocial and SRHR services for SEA survivors. Livelihoods services offered at MPWCs and the Women’s Market are designed with the most vulnerable women and girls in mind, including SEA survivors, enhancing their resilience and recovery in a safe, female-only space. MPWCs have also established service linkages with rehabilitation/safe shelter for survivors through inter-agency referral pathways.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

UN Women strives for a resilient and empowered community which takes into account the specific needs and concerns of women and girls, and emphasizes skills building, empowerment and protection of vulnerable groups of women and girls in disaster-prone areas. UN Women supported MPWCs and Women’s Market function as cyclone shelters for women, children and the elderly in times of disasters. In 2025, UN Women will strengthen the infrastructure of MPWCs and the Women’s Market to enhance disaster resilience and preparedness, applying disaster risk reduction (DRR) principles. This includes improving electricity connectivity; conducting assessments related to and procuring disaster preparedness equipment; and procuring material relief items to enhance availability of much needed items for disaster relief and preparedness in all the MPWCs and the Women’s Market. Further, in 2025, UN Women will train the MPWC Community Outreach Volunteers to enhance their skills in disaster risk reduction and emergency response, and train all MPWC IPs on gender-responsive disaster risk reduction and emergency response.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

UN Women implements business continuity planning to ensure continued operation of UN Women supported services during emergencies, and related procedures specific to the sub-office have been established with advice and support from the country and regional offices of UN Women. UN Women collaborates closely with women’s networks and groups to monitor, assess, and plan for emergencies, and coordinates with community leaders and local officials and other government stakeholders to plan and implement emergency response activities, in line with agreed procedures, when needed. As outlined in 3.8, in 2025, UN Women MPWCs and the Women’s Market function as cyclone shelters and in 2025, UN Women will further develop emergency preparedness and response capacities of the MPWCs and Women’s Market, renovating these with a specific focus on strengthening disaster resilience and emergency preparedness. Further, in 2025, UN Women will train the MPWC Community Outreach Volunteers to enhance their skills in emergency preparedness and response. All UN Women IPs operating MPWCs have a budget line from which to procure emergency response items, including dry foods for women, children and elderly taking shelter in MPWCs, and items such as clothing and towels procured on a need’s basis. In addition, UN Women has budget for and procures dignity kits for women and girls with each MPWC and the Women’s Market maintaining a stock of these kits. Furthermore, UN Women has allocated up to $ 5,000 in contingency funds to support 300 women and girls.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

As described above, with such a large contingency of the Rohingya refugees comprising youth (girls and boys) and with a small minority only – less girls than boys – participating in the Myanmar Curriculum Education at the higher levels, it is critical to ensure training opportunities for adolescent girls, to enhance their opportunities, but also to prevent child marriage and trafficking of girls, among other. Therefore, UN Women non-formal technical trainings and vocational trainings will also be offered to adolescent girls (18-19 year-olds). MPWC Community Outreach Volunteers specifically seek to identify such adolescent girls who would benefit from these activities, engaging with their family members to seek their support to participation of the adolescent girls in the MPWC activities. Of note, specific attention is placed, in collaboration with UNICEF, on supporting those girls who have dropped out of Myanmar Curriculum Education to first continue their studies within the MPWCs, with UNICEF support. As the MPWCs provide a female only safe space, they have garnered wide acceptance from community members, both men and women, as a space for women’s and girl’s learning and livelihoods activities. In addition, 3 out of 8 UN Women MPWCs in the camps are implementing the ‘Girl Shine’ programme, designed to enhance the protection of girls from GBV and to build their skills and knowledge of related types of GBV and available services.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

The project aims to empower women in both communities through learning, skills development, and cash grants and/or in-kind materials support to become productive and self-reliant in the long term. AAL will support women to pursue different pathways, expanding their scope to engage in volunteer activities and pursue vocational training. In 2025, the role of refugee women in delivering learning and livelihood services in MPWCs will be enhanced, increasing their role as trainers, through coaching and twinning with qualified Bangladeshi trainers. In the host communities, partners utilize government frameworks and collaborate with local officials to deliver trainings. The sub-partner SKUS is accredited by the Government to deliver vocational trainings. Contributing to understanding of the development needs, analysis of livelihoods and income generation opportunities among women in both communities, and analyses of the situation of women vendors and marketplaces will be developed.
