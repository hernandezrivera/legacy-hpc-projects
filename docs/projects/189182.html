Project Summary

South Sudan continues to face increasing need for humanitarian assistance with food security and livelihoods assistance carrying the big part of the needed assistance (80%). This is a one year livelihoods intervention program is aimed at reducing acute food insecurity and malnutrition in the targeted areas through improved crop, vegetable and fish production and livestock intervention. The project target is 40,515 households, an equivalent of 243,086 people in four counties of Longochuk, Nasir, Maiwut and Ulang. The main challenges being faced by the affected households include climatic changes that led to widespread flooding in three consecutive years and localized droughts in some areas, low cereal production due to farming small farms, destruction by climatic shocks, low engagement of youth in farming, shortage of farming inputs and pests, disease and weeds infestation, livestock disease outbreaks, destruction of pasture and unavailability of vaccines and drugs for disease prevention and treatment. The proposed activities that will contribute to improving food security include climate change adaptation training to the community members, provision of improved seeds and tools to cultivate more land and increase availability of farming inputs, sensitizing communities and encouraging engagement of youth in farming and fishing and distribution of farming and fishing inputs that include, crop and vegetable seeds, tools and fishing gear. The beneficiaries will also be trained on how to best use these inputs and improve the value of the final product for increased proper utilization and marketing.
Therefore, to contribute to addressing the above problems, AHC proposes to respond to support 40,515 most vulnerable households (approximately 243,086 individuals) in Longochuk, Nasir, Maiwut and Ulang Counties of Upper Nile State. The project will focus on the distribution of emergency livelihood kits that include crop kits, vegetable kits, fishing kits and farming tools, livestock kits to 40,515 households, an equivalent of 243,086 people as well as training farmers and fisher-folks on improved agronomic fishing practices to 11,790 households (70,742 individuals), setting up of 71 kitchen gardens, training of 120 Community Based Animal Health Workers and treatment and vaccination of 565,934 livestock. This is made possible since a majority of the households have access to land for cultivation, fishing grounds for fish harvesting and livestock.

1 - Needs

Despite 10 years of independence and 3 years of revitalized peace agreement, South Sudan food insecurity situation is getting worse. This is due to the impact of flooding on livelihoods and displacements that has been experienced in three consecutive years and localized droughts, persistent poor macroeconomic conditions, insecurity and the effects of COVID-19. With a population of 12.4M, it had been projected that between April to July 2021, 7.24M people (60% of the population) would face crisis or worse food insecurity (IPC Dec 2021). The figure has now risen to 8.3M people in need of FSL assistance (HNO, 2022). In the Greater Upper Nile region, the food security situation is worse compared to last two years due to the effects of excessive flooding compounded by insecurity, low crop production, the economic crisis, morbidity and pests and diseases for crops and livestock. The flooding this year caused too much destruction of crops and pastures for livestock, disrupting farming activities, causing a rise in incidences of water-borne diseases, displacing livestock and change of grazing routes leading to conflict as well as difficulty in accessing other vital services. It was projected that in the second projection of April to July 2021, the number of people in crisis or worse food insecurity situation in the Greater Upper Nile region would be 3.47 million people (76.4% of the population in Greater Upper Nile region) (IPC, 2021). The situation this year is worse due to the mentioned shocks. The deteriorations in food security in the region is driven by low crop production, seasonality, the economic crisis, and the impacts of COVID-19 that have been persistent (IPC Dec 2021). The county populations of Nasir is 286,628, Maiwut 128,913 and Ulang 137,691 (NBS, 2021). All the four counties were classified as IPC phase 4 acute food insecurity for current, first and second projection periods (IPC, Dec 2021). The people in need of FSL assistance in the counties are 24,548 for Longochuk, 229,160 for Nasir, 72,675 for Maiwut and 100,911 for Ulang. Country-wide, 53% of the people in need are children, 24% are women and 15% are people with disability. Generally, 80% of the need in the country is driven by food insecurity. The number of people in need of FSL humanitarian assistance is 8.3M with the gender being 24% women, 26% girls, 27% boys and 23% men. According to age, 53% are children, 39% are adults and 8% are elderly while 15% are people with disability (HNO, 2022). In addition to the cross-cutting challenges faced nationally, other challenges faced in the counties during the farming period were shortage of farming inputs such as seeds and tools and unusually high level of pests, diseases and weed infestations. Livestock ownership also faced high reduction due to looting and livestock diseases (FSNMS 26). Floods also destroyed pastures and increased outbreak of livestock diseases. It is estimated that 55.2% of livestock were affected and 5.0% died due to floods in the country in 2021. In Nasir county where this information was available, 92.9% of livestock were affected and 6.9% died due to floods in 2021 (FAO floods assessment, 2021) The situation is thought to be worse than the last two years. Cereal production in 2021 was also low. It was even lower than 2019 due to the worsened effects of floods (IPC, Dec 2021). In 2019, the net cereal production in Nasir from 10,664 Ha of land was 6,825 tonnes with a deficit of 20,191 tonnes; Maiwut from 1,987Ha of land was 1,033 tonnes with a deficit of 2,737 tonnes; Ulang from 4,165 Ha was 1,999 tonnes with a deficit of 6,973 tonnes (CFSAM, May 2021). The situation this year is estimated to be even much lower than the previous two years. It is estimated this year that 65,107 ha of land planted with cereals were damaged and 37,624 tonnes of grain were lost due to the floods (FAO, 2022).

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

African Humanitarian Corps will use beneficiaries’ feedback to improve quality of activities implemented in the area of operations. From this feedback we will be able to make necessary adjustments to accommodate the beneficiaries’ needs especially when the information provided promotes accountability to the community. Beneficiary feedback will be used throughout the program. AHC actively collects beneficiary feedback through specific data collections tools .We have further trained our staff to actively listen and solicit feedback when engaging with the beneficiaries throughout the project cycle. At all times we strive to offer immediate response by communicating to the relevant authorizes concerned. We actively negotiate with our donors and with the local authority to act on beneficiary feedback. We have also put in mechanisms that promote clear referrals pathways for feedback.These feedbacks will be used for the following:1. Improving Accountability. African Humanitarian Corps recognizes the importance of providing opportunities for those affected by aid efforts to ask questions and to voice concerns or complaints. It has been found that encouraging feedback and complaints from recipients give staff the necessary pressure to make changes in operations because they feel a sense of responsibility to those that voiced their input. 2. Improving Effectiveness. Maintaining on-going primary stakeholder feedback mechanisms throughout the cycle of a project result in greater impact and higher satisfaction compared to projects that do not have much beneficiary feedback. Oftentimes, changes made to project operations based on feedback are not difficult to incorporate, and changes to some small details lead to significant improvements in beneficiary satisfaction. The successes that have resulted from increased feedback have helped make beneficiary feedback mechanisms a greater priority in our organization.3. Responding to donor requirements and/or media. We always seek beneficiary feedback as a donor requirement to demonstrate recipient involvement. This is a value that can bring greater impact when the management and staff buy-in. 4. Increasing security for staff. There are several instances of violence against staff working in difficult environments, but this can be reduced by improving the communication and feedback processes with affected communities. It leads to positive terms with local communities, and in some cases even protection by the local community from armed groups.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

We will ensure that feedback mechanism put in place are sensitive to age gender and diversity and accessible to the most vulnerable members in the community. We will further consult with the vulnerable population representatives to gain a greater understanding of their views, needs and concerns. The beneficiaries will have the opportunity to be involved in project design, planning and decision-making concerning activities as well as opportunities to lead or support the implementation of the activities. The participation is expected to be meaningful and enable beneficiaries to help shape, be involved in, and – whenever possible – lead on some of the activities. We will make a priority during implementation to make the beneficiaries understand when and how they will participate and how decisions will be made. This will be discussed and agreed with beneficiaries. Persons with disabilities often experience heightened protection risks because of their disability, and being a woman or a child with disability further compounds these vulnerabilities. Girls and women with disabilities endure violence, abuse, neglect and exploitation twice as often, over a longer period of time, and suffer more serious injuries as a result of violence compared to women without disabilities. Women and girls with intellectual disabilities are at a particularly higher risk of violence, including sexual violence. As such, these special groups will also be identified, considered and encouraged to provide their feedback in a manner that they are comfortable in.Some of the strategies we will use for persons with disabilities include: We will ensure an active outreach to collect feedback from persons with disabilitiesDisseminate information that uses clear and simple language. Involve organizations of persons with disabilities in consultation and decision making. Provide tailored approach to meet individual needs, work with personal carers and other social support networks.For women and girls, we will:Ensure that community engagement teams are gender balanced and promote women’s leadership within these. 7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

African Humanitarian Corps (AHC) will provide beneficiaries with information about protection against sexual exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) during community engagement forums. Through these forums we will strive to raise awareness on issues relating to PSEA by engaging women, girls, boys and men as well as the local leadership. AHC staff will actively be involved in making sure that beneficiaries are aware of their rights and entitlements regarding the project being implemented in their region. For instance beneficiaries entitled to the livelihood assistance will be informed that they are not expected to provide any forms of sexual favors and that demand for favors in exchange of livelihood assistance is strictly prohibited. We will actively put mechanisms that will enable beneficiaries to know how and where to report PSEA incidences assuring them safety and confidentiality. We will go the extra mile in ensuring that information regarding PSEA issues are disseminated in community meetings, forums and trainings.The following principles will be passed to the communities through the specified channels:Sexual exploitation and sexual abuse perpetrated against beneficiaries constitute serious misconduct and are therefore grounds for disciplinary action to the staff.Sexual activity with persons under the age of 18 is prohibited regardless of the age of majority or age of consent locally. Mistaken belief in the age of a person is not considered a defence.Exchange of money, employment, goods, services or any type of assistance for sexual favours is prohibited.Sexual relationships between AHC staff and aid beneficiaries are strongly discouraged, as they are based on an inherently unequal power relationship and undermine the credibility and integrity of AHC’s work.Where a AHC staff develops concerns or suspicions regarding sexual abuse or exploitation by a fellow worker, whether in the same agency or not, he or she must report such concerns via established agency reporting mechanisms.AHC staff are obliged to create and maintain an environment which prevents sexual exploitation and abuse and promotes the implementation of their code of conduct.The organization has put in place an Integrity Hotline. Anonymous reports by beneficiaries to the AHC Integrity Hotline are allowed and any beneficiary may request to stay anonymous. The established process within the organization then takes over to do investigation.AHC does not tolerate any form of retaliation against team members, partners or beneficiaries who report sexual exploitation or abuse if the report is done in good faith. These types of allegations are very sensitive and AHC treats all information with the greatest respect for confidentiality. The safety, security and well-being of survivors is our first and foremost concern along with the safety and security of the witnesses and accused. AHC will take allegations of this kind seriously and is committed to the support of survivors. If a survivor or witness desires assistance, AHC will provide or refer for medical, psychosocial and/or legal support.

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

Gender-based violence (GBV) is a life-threatening global health and human rights issue. During emergencies such as conflicts or natural disasters, the risk of violence, exploitation and abuse is heightened, particularly for women and girls. At the same time, national systems and community and social support networks may weaken. As such, AHC recognizes this and follows The Minimum Standards for Prevention and Response to Gender-Based Violence in Emergencies.GBV is a human rights violation and cannot be condoned or justified by cultural norms. It occurs in all contexts but is under-reported and establishing and maintaining safe access to quality response services is essential to the protection and assistance delivery of our AHC interventions. We ensure that everyone directly or indirectly engaged with survivors must ensure a survivor-centred approach, and the following guiding principles: Respect by treating survivors with dignity, ensuring their participation, and respecting their decisions; Confidentiality by respecting survivors’ right to privacy and ensuring that any information about them should only be shared with their informed consent, and in line with data protection principles; Safety of the survivor being of primary concern at all times; Non-discrimination by treating everyone based on their needs alone. We use a ‘Do No Harm’ approach which ‘involves taking all measures necessary to avoid exposing people to further harm as a result of our actions. The best interests of the child are the primary consideration in all decisions affecting girls and boys who are survivors and those at-risk of GBV or affected by GBV. Information is critical to empowerment and informed decision-making, and survivors must receive the information they need to fully exercise their rights. AHC recognizes the inherent skills, resources, and resilience of communities, and is committed to working with them as partners, particularly with women and girls. AHC recognizes the specific needs of all communities we serve, particularly women and girls, and how multiple forms of oppression and power intersect and influence their experiences and exposure to GBV and safe access to services, including gender inequality, racism, ableism and class inequality and other contextually-relevant systems of power. Applying an intersectional analysis approach to AHC’s work ensures a comprehensive approach to programming through tackling systemic discrimination and exclusion. For most marginalized women and girls, there is often a need for targeted services, such as, safe, static or mobile spaces, to facilitate safe participation. We always refer to other agencies women and girls eligible receive dignity kits and/or cash and voucher assistance to reduce GBV risk, and promote safety and dignity, where appropriate. AHC promotes the non-discriminatory inclusion of community members in all public services such as health, social welfare, education, and justice, and where necessary strengthens national systems. AHC advocates for the government to assume their responsibility to prevent violence, mitigate risks, and respond to survivors’ rights and needs in accordance with international standards. To strengthen national systems to address GBV, AHC recognizes the importance of partnerships with local civil society and community groups, refugee and internally displaced people (IDP)-led and refugee and IDP women’s groups as well as local and national women-led and women-focused organizations. Gender equality and GBV programming are complementary, not interchangeable. Gender equality programming is essential to any long-term effort to address GBV and should be initiated at the outset of any humanitarian response. Gender equality and women’s empowerment are cross-cutting issues and should be integrated into every aspect of GBV programming.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

Flood related displacement is caused by climatic shocks and misuse of natural resources and environmental degradation. Therefore, we encourage communities to contribute to sustainable management of natural resources, and respond to climate change shocks in order to reduce the risk for disasters.We will incorporate these issues in our training and community outreach. These will include trainings and efforts to maintain the natural resources, prevent flooding related damages by training and encouraging building of dykes, agroforestry and irrigation.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

The households in IPC phase 3, 4 and 5 are normally prioritized in humanitarian interventions. As such, people in IPC 4 and IPC 5 will all be targeted. This will be used as one of the vulnerability criteria for selection of beneficiaries that will receive livelihood assistance. Households with malnourished children and pregnant and lactating mothers will be given priority for registration to receive livelihood inputs and training especially on vegetable and fish production and nutritious utilization. This will ultimately lead to improved food security for the targeted beneficiaries.The major drivers of food insecurity that drives the populations to worse IPC phases include low cereal production, effects of climatic shocks such as floods and droughts and shortage of farming and fishing inputs. The intervention is geared towards addressing some of these issues through training of households on improved agronomic and fisheries practices, climate change adaptation and provision of farming and fishing inputs that include seeds, tools and fishing gear. In the end, the amount of food produced will increase and the communities will be better adapted to changing climatic conditions. Therefore, more people will become food secure and reduce the populations in IPC phase 3 and above. Youth also are an important resource to the community, and are normally abused in conflicts and intercommunal clashes that include cattle rustling. They will be actively engaged in issues of farming and fisheries that will ultimately benefit their families and the community at large.
