Project Summary

Rollover Project: HPSE19-EDU;SHL;FSC;HEA;NUT;PRO;WSH-150367-1
Building on previous combined actions, this multi-sectoral Project contributes to the protection of 44,283 vulnerable Palestinians in the West Bank, including East Jerusalem, from forcible transfer (FT) through an integrated, protection-centred approach to humanitarian responses. Specifically, the Project aims at: (1) preventing and responding to protection incidents (demolitions, settler-related, military operations etc.) with emergency assistance (cash and in-kind) and protective measures (RESPONSIVE ACTIVITIES); (2) mitigating protection risks for highly vulnerable communities (150) through synergizing community-based protection, the provision of basic and social infrastructure, legal aid and stakeholder mobilization (REMEDIAL ACTIVITIES); and (3) promoting increased respect for IHL and IHRL and accountability for violations through coalition advocacy undertaken with Third States, International Organizations, Palestinian authorities and media outlets (ENVIRONMENT-BUILDING ACTIVITIES).
The Project is predicated upon empowerment and sustainability goals. Context-adapted responses and accountability to affected populations (including gender-age sensitivity) are ensured through active beneficiary engagement at all stages of the project cycle, starting with participatory protection assessments and response planning. National ownership is further promoted through community capacity-building and proactive coordination with Palestinian authorities, at both central and sub-central levels. Wherever possible, the Project also champions a ‘nexus’ approach in order to link the target communities with development cooperation measures, aiming to reduce their dependency on humanitarian assistance and to ensure they are not left behind in pursuing national development goals and sustainable development goals (SDGs).
Within the framework of the HCT, the Project supports the implementation of the Area C Policy Framework and coordinates with other actors addressing the humanitarian and protection needs of communities at risk of FT. The Project provides a mechanism to respond to OCHA triggers for demolitions and settler-related incidents. Shelter, WASH, Education, Health, FSL, legal aid and emergency responses and advocacy will be coordinated through the relevant Clusters/WGs. Consequently, the Project is envisaged to contribute to the realization of the following HRP objectives: 1. Increased respect for International Humanitarian Law (IHL) and International Human Rights Law (IHRL) and accountability for violations; 2. Protection response mechanisms are in place to prevent and mitigate the effects of the occupation and conflict related violence.
Note that implementing partners also plan to submit a number of sectoral projects, as single agencies, which will include some of the same activities (including legal aid services; shelter upgrades). Some of these projects will complement this Project’s assistance, but will respond to a logic of ‘contiguity’ rather than ‘continuity’, since they are informed by different rationales and approaches. For this reason, financial resources will be kept separated. Our planning, monitoring and reporting systems prevent any duplication.
In 2019, there has been a continued deterioration of the protection environments in the West Bank. This is demonstrated by an increase incidents of settler violence and demolitions. In 2019, as of October, 476 structures have been seized or destroyed in the West Bank, compared to 460 for the whole of 2018. Additionally, the number of incidents settler violence in 2019 has increased by 11% from 2018 and 97% from 2017. This has necessitated a more significant emergency response by the Consortium. Reflecting the continued deteriorating environment, the Consortium expects to reach 44,283 beneficiaries, up from 42,174 in 2019. As a result the Consortium’s budget has increased by $409,677 to $8,603,210.

1 - Needs

The situation in the WB remains a protracted protection crisis, driven by a lack of compliance with IHL and IHRL. Routine rights violations are a driver of both severe protection threats and chronic humanitarian vulnerabilities. In Area C, H2 and East Jerusalem, specifically, Israel, the Occupying Power, has established a ‘coercive environment’ to push Palestinians out of the path of settlements through: a discriminatory permit regime for construction, lack of access to basic services, forced evictions and planned relocations, confiscation and destruction of civilian property, obstruction of humanitarian relief, land expropriation, settlement expansion, construction of the Wall, movement and access restrictions, declarations of live fire zones and closed areas, settler violence and military operations. Those most at risk of forcible transfer include 46 Bedouin and herder communities slated for relocation; those vulnerable to evictions and destruction of property in Area C; those affected by demolitions or the revocation of their residency rights in East Jerusalem; and over 350,000 Palestinians in 67 communities affected by settler violence and harassment (HNO 2019). Forcible transfer has serious immediate and long-term physical, socio-economic and emotional impact. It deprives Palestinians of their home and results in disruption to livelihoods, a reduced standard of living and limited access to elementary services. The progression of relocation plans for Bedouin communities remains a grave concern. The Project’s response to these challenges is guided by threats/vulnerability/capacity analyses (‘protection equation’) conducted with the target communities. The Project’s community protection approach is specifically designed to support adequate beneficiary participation in the design, implementation, monitoring and evaluation of interventions and to involve groups traditionally excluded from decision-making (women, children, PWDs) through dedicated focus groups and transect interviews. Likewise, assessments conducted as part of emergency and legal responses include the entire household to ensure vulnerable and marginalized individuals have a voice. Priority is systematically given to the most vulnerable, including female-headed households, the elderly, ill or disabled. Quantitative assessments conducted as part of this approach based on a Protection Vulnerability Index (PVI) continue to show a high level of protection vulnerability overall in the 163 communities targeted by the Project in 2018. 69% fall under the high vulnerability level (>50 in PVI); 31% record medium vulnerability (between 30 and 49 PVI); and none can be classified as low vulnerability (<30 PVI). Complementary qualitative assessments based on focus groups indicate that occupation-related practices affect men, boys, women and girls differently. Young Palestinian men are particularly affected by physical violence exerted by the military or settlers. Meanwhile women and girls are particularly affected by sexual harassment from settlers and gender-based violence within their own communities and households. Military operations and demolitions also disproportionally impact women and children. Trends analyses indicate that the Projects’ integrated approach is, overall, effective at mitigating protection risks. 68% of the communities targeted to date recorded improvement in protection from forcible transfer, reflected by a decrease in PVI score, while 3% remain stable and 29% regressed. The worsening ratio (29%) was due to a significant deterioration in the protection environment of these communities, generally due to a spike in settler-related incidents. Of note, 20% of the communities are “moving ahead” (vulnerability was reduced and their vulnerability level is below 50) indicating their readiness for transition, a gradual phasing over to development actors.

2 - Activities

Result 1. Protection incidents (demolitions, settler-related, military operations etc.) are prevented – to the extent possible – and the worst effects are alleviated: 1.1: Systematic emergency response to demolitions with in-kind and/or cash assistance in order to cover damages to shelter, livelihoods and WASH assets; and 1.2: Provision of emergency assistance (cash/in-kind), livelihoods support and protective assets to households and communities affected by settler violence. Protective assets are identified and prioritized through a community-based approach, including focus groups with men, women and children. Result 2. The vulnerability of 150 communities subject to a coercive environment is reduced through: 2.1 A community protection approach to promote greater agency, autonomy and self-protection capacities. The community-based protection approach involves multiple forms of community engagement to ensure the needs, priorities and concerns of men, women and children are captured. 2.2: The provision and rehabilitation of basic and social infrastructure to improve access to services and restore more dignified living conditions; 2.3 Legal aid to individuals, communities, Local Government Units and Ministries in the areas of housing, land and property rights, residency, freedom of movement and settler violence in order to prevent violations of IHL and IHRL, mitigate risks of aid obstruction and challenge discriminatory policies; 2.4: Engagement with humanitarian and development actors to address critical governance, humanitarian and development challenges in the target communities and support transition efforts, wherever possible. Result 3. Increased respect for IHL and IHRL and accountability for violations are promoted through: 3.1: Rapid response advocacy to mobilize Third States, International Organizations and Palestinian authorities towards the prevention of demolitions, seizures and evictions; 3.2: Targeted research, policy and legal advice and humanitarian advocacy with Third States and International Organizations to leverage their obligation to condemn violations of international law and to take measures to induce compliance with IHL, with a focus on destruction of property, obstruction of relief, deprivation of services, land confiscation, impunity for settler violence and other practices that generate a coercive environment leading to forcible transfer; 3.3: Policy development support and technical assistance to Palestinian Authorities at both central and sub-central levels to support their role in protecting and extending basic services to at risk Palestinian communities, particularly in Area C; 3.4: Strategic outreach to international and local media in order to raise awareness about the humanitarian crisis and build public support for more robust measures to suppress violations among key constituencies. The Project involves a continuous process of self-assessment and analysis of vulnerabilities and needs, used as an entry point for the development of local protection strategies. These provide a joint-programming framework for needs-based responses by the Project as well as other relevant stakeholders. Beneficiaries are involved as implementers to reduce potential security risks and supplement their income. Accountability is further promoted through complaints handling and feedback mechanisms. Gender/age markers are taken into account during assessments, determination of priorities and M&E processes. Gender-responsive relief and protection activities are undertaken to meet women's specific material and safety needs. Crucially, the Project aims to reduce the dependency of beneficiaries on humanitarian aid through a phased approach to implementation over 3 to 4 years, transitioning communities from humanitarian relief once the minimum standards required to enhance protection have been met and linking them with subsequent development cooperation measures, local processes and programs.

8.6.c - How will feedback be used?

Beneficiaries are involved in all aspects of the Action through a shared decision-making process. The community-based protection approach involves a continuous process of self-assessment and analysis of their vulnerabilities and needs, used as an entry point for the elaboration of local protection strategies, and evaluation of past Actions. These strategies provide a joint-programming framework for needs-based responses through the Project and other relevant stakeholders. Beneficiaries are involved as implementers to reduce potential security risks and supplement their income. The close cooperation with involved communities ensures transparency and accountability towards the affected population. All activities will be publicly announced through channels such as the village council and meetings to ensure transparent information sharing. Accountability is further promoted through complaints handling and feedback mechanisms. Feedback, collected through all stages of the project in the form of information cards with contact information, surveys, interviews and focus groups, is used to adjust and develop the Action’s interventions. Changes sessions, held at the end of the project cycle in the Action’s target communities, aim to review of the action in order to incorporate changes that may strengthen the Action moving forward, assess changes to needs and incorporate feedback and/or complaints. In addition, the Action is committed to conducting referrals of identified needs outside the scope of the Action. Involved staff are continuously trained in identifying needs and conducting referrals to specialised service providers. During this coming project cycle, the Action’s cash-based interventions will be modified to incorporate feedback and an appeal process from beneficiaries. All Action indicators that involve beneficiaries (amount or percentage) as measuring unit are reported by the Project’s M&E framework in sex and gender disaggregated format. 9 - Protection mainstreaming
9.1 - Do No Harm

Participatory risk analyses inform all programming decisions, based on informed beneficiary consent. The Consortium model synergizes risk awareness activities, with operational risk mitigation measures and preventive legal action to prevent assistance-related protection concerns. Prior to the implementation of rehabilitation or construction activities detailed risks and technical assessments and legal analyses will be conducted with beneficiaries to determine whether the activity should proceed and on what basis in order to insure do no harm and informed consent. Beneficiaries will be involved not only in activity design but also as implementers of substitution actions in order to reduce potential security risks and enable beneficiary families and broader communities to supplement their income. The technical designs ensure privacy, safety, dignity and accessibility for men and women, with or without disability. Implementation modalities (contractor, community or beneficiary implementation) are selected in consultation with beneficiaries, based on do no harm principles, and aim at enhancing local capacity, ownership and social cohesion – including through cost-sharing. Risk mitigation/do no harm is thus paramount in deciding appropriate implementation modalities. Further, a survey in targeted communities is used to measure percentage of beneficiaries reporting an improved feeling of safety and dignity and the percentage of beneficiaries (disaggregated by sex, age and diversity) reporting that humanitarian assistance is delivered in a safe, accessible, accountable and participatory manner (by the end of the intervention). Changes session focus groups in target communities are furthermore used to receive feedback on safety and dignity of beneficiaries. Lastly all staff are trained in PSEA policies and the action continuously engages in GBV mainstreaming.

9.2 - Equal and impartial access to aid

The WBPC has taken additional measures to strengthen its compliance with Age, Gender and Diversity (AGD) international standards and ensure that AGD is mainstreamed at all stages of the project cycle. The AGD approach of the WBPC focuses on four main areas, namely the undertaking of AGD analysis, collection of disaggregated data, provision of AGD sensitive responses and the strengthening of accountability mechanisms. The Age, Gender and Diversity Working Group of the WBPC is responsible for the oversight and technical support for the mainstreaming of AGD throughout the program. A major output of the community-based participatory approach to protection is the development of a thorough protection analysis that includes information by sector and geographic areas on vulnerabilities of targeted communities, as well as specific data related to safety and dignity. The collection of AGD disaggregated data has been a priority concern for the consortium and led to the revision of assessment and monitoring tools in order to capture more systematically AGD information. Field staff have been trained on GBV identification and referral and the composition of assessment teams has been revised in a view to ensure that both male and female beneficiaries are able to share their perspectives in a safe and confidential environment. The Project’s participatory assessment is specifically designed to involve groups traditionally excluded from decision making through separate focus groups discussions as well as transect interviews. Gender/age markers are taken into account during assessments, determination of priorities and M&E processes. The identification and implementation of emergency responses and material assistance have been enhanced in a view to take into account the specific needs of vulnerable individuals, with special attention given to women, children and persons with disabilities. Measures have been taken to promote increased participation of at-risk individuals in assessments and decision-making processes and to provide an assistance that is accessible and tailored to the specific needs of those individuals. Technical designs (e.g. for shelter rehabilitation) have been upgraded to ensure privacy, safety, dignity and accessibility for men, women, boys and girls with or without disability. In 2020, the Action plans to strengthen its referral mechanism to ensure that “no one is left behind” and that protection cases that fall beyond the scope of intervention of the consortium are adequately addressed through the cluster system. Complaints response mechanisms will also be reviewed in order to ensure that they are accessible to those most in need. Furthermore, the WBPC will conduct an external review of its approach and activities in light of international standards and good practices related to AGD mainstreaming. It will use an AGD self-audit tool – developed internally during the 2019 project – to gain baseline information on its level of compliance with international AGD standards and to better identify and prioritize AGD mainstreaming activities.
