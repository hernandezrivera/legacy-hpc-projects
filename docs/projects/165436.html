4 - Needs

The war in Syria continues to negatively affect livelihoods opportunities for individuals and communities in Northwest Syria. While markets are still functioning, the lack of job opportunities, critical community social services, and the increased cost of living mean that people are more vulnerable to shocks and stresses and more likely to resort to negative coping mechanisms. Restrictions on the Turkish border as well as disruptions in previous trade routes with other cities in Syria have led to large price increases in certain goods and commodities. Per the 2020 HNO, over eighty-three per cent of the Syrian population is living below the poverty line. Women face additional barriers seeking services and employment due to cultural norms, though the IRC and other actor’s finds more and more are now more active in income generation for their households, this is often because of disruption in male household member’s income sources. In its Gender Assessments, the IRC finds that women have limited options for employment, and are usually constrained to low-skilled professions include farming crops, rearing livestock, sewing, and hairdressing, working in factories and cleaning houses. A recent Gender, Age, and Disability research study conducted by Proximity International for the Syria Resilience Consortium (of which IRC is a member) found that traditional gender roles in Syria are still strong with decision making at the community and household level in control of male abled bodied community members. However, some women’s roles had changed due to the loss of male family members within the household meaning they have become more engaged in income generation in order to meet their household needs. Despite this, especially for women with disabilities, their socio and economic wellbeing was affected by their ability to participate in work, which had different levels of success meaning this group is often extra vulnerable.

5 - Implementation of activities

1) Labor intensive cash for work (CfW) projects aimed at improving community infrastructure while providing temporary employment opportunities for unemployed or under employed men and women ages 18 to 60 including PwD. Projects include street repairs, school or health center rehabilitation, and production of winter clothing items. Labour Market Assessments held early 2020 will identify other projects women that are culturally appropriate and do not expose them to risks. The timing of CfW projects will consider community wide needs, for example IRC will avoid large scale CfW projects during the planting or harvesting season because many people find work during this time. 2) Market relevant skills training programs through two Livelihoods Center in Idleb governorate; the centers have one training space for women and girls, and one for men and boys. Activities will include business and life skills training and vocational training followed by an apprenticeship placement targeting males and females ages 17 to 35 years old. Specific sectors will depend on the findings of the labour market assessment, and projects for women will attempt to diversify vocational training subjects targeting women beyond hairdressing and other traditional subjects. The IRC provides childcare with all center based activities to encourage participation for all beneficiaries including female headed households. 3) Microenterprise cash grants and entrepreneurship training to both startups and existing businesses in the community who have developed a business plan. The project will provide specialized support women to start small home based projects, and will encourage youth (16 to 30) to gain practical work experience and start low risk projects that require little start up capital. Through an existing partnership with Humanity International, people with disabilities will receive specific support in all projects.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

During project design and planning, IRC considers do no harm principals and conflict sensitivity concerns, including a solid understanding of the number of IDPs in a targeted location, how long they have been there for, and the level of integration they have in the community and markets. IRC does not target activities solely for host or IDPs, but instead included and encourages both host and IDPs in every project. Anecdotal end-line data shows that this is very beneficial to IDP’s integration in a community. To address the perception of bias, IRC does not take lists from local councils and prefers open registrations that are widely announced in various mediums within a community. Focus group discussions and various qualitative research have identified some protection concerns specific to livelihoods programming. One risk is that delayed payments negatively affect house-hold dynamics, especially for women who are pressured into dropping out of projects if stipends are not paid on time. The IRC has identified multiple payment methods for livelihoods projects to mitigate this risk, as well as multiple money transfer agencies to ensure on-time payments. The IRC has identified the risk of beneficiary exploitation, particularly related to perceived power that would-be beneficiaries think local authorities have over registration and selection processes. Exploitation can include sexual exploitation by local authorities or staff. The IRC has conducted PSEA trainings for all staff, including field staff. Confidential and trust worthy reporting mechanisms have been shared with all staff, and both staff and beneficiaries are encouraged to use the IRC’s beneficiary feedback and complaints mechanism. When a complaint regarding protection concerns, including sexual exploitation is found, this complaint is handled and investigated sensitively per best protection practices.

7 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks to project implementation include loss of access to project areas due to conflict (inter armed groups or due to Government of Syria offensives). As mitigation, IRC conducts pre-project security assessments that includes risk of future offensives. IRC will have ‘exit plans’ for all locations and planned measures to implement to ensure staff safety and duty of care, building on lessons learned from the South Syria offensive in summer 2018. Another high risk is interference by armed groups or local councils, whomay attempt to control or influence projects by requesting or demanding IRC to take certain actions. IRC adheres to humanitarian principles and the established Joint Operating Procedures for northwest Syria. IRCs well-resourced humanitarian access team will conduct principled negotiation and conflict resolution in order to continue project plans and meet the identified needs of a community. IRC has seen success inleveraging local communities to put pressure on armed groups to drop their demands. When no negation is possible, in order to abide by humanitarian principals, IRC will withdraw from the area. Due to the conditionality of these ER Cluster projects, the IRC sees less attempted interference There is a risk of local authorities or armed groups targeting IRC beneficiaries for taxation or exploitation. IRC holds open registration for most ER Cluster activities, and when this is not possible, does household-level verification of lists that are provided to IRC. This reduces the power that local councils or others have on applicants. IRC also provides information sessions at registrations that include clear communication that interested individuals are not required to provide anything to be part of the project, and that selection will be done by IRC staff in Amman. IRC also widely advertises IRC’s beneficiary feedback and complaints mechanism at multiple stages of the project, including the initial information session.
