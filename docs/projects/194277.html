Project Summary

Harmful gender norms and stereotypes as well as other key supply and demand side factors significantly affect girls access to education as well as retention and completion in South Sudan and plays a large role in perpetuating gender inequality in education. This project aims at addressing critical barriers that impede girls access to quality education at the school and community levels in 5 counties with a significant burden of IDPs and returnees due to communal conflicts, flooding and food insecurity and economic hardships namely Tonj South Gogrial East, Gogrial West and in Warrap State, and Ulang and Nassir in Upper Nile by addressing the community and school based barriers that deny or restrict full participation of girls in education activities. In addition, COVID19 has seen a number of girls fail to return to school when schools reopened.
The project will benefit 4947 individuals (4730 children and youth (2483 girls, 2247 boys) 96 teachers (54 male, 42 female) and 121 community members and MoGEI officials (37 men, 84 women) as direct beneficiaries for this project through a mix of activities that target key barriers to access and quality education in the target counties. This will include conducting return to learning campaigns, community mobilization and sensitization efforts on the value of girls’ education, supply of teaching, learning and recreational materials, provision/ rehabilitation of WASH facilities and temporary learning spaces, implementing targeted capacity development and support for teachers on EIE methodologies, strengthening mentoring and referral systems addressing protection risks for girls, improve access to menstrual hygiene management and Sexual and reproductive health services for girls and strengthen the capacity of state ministries of education to collect and harness the use of data to inform EiE interventions.
UNESCO and partners TADO, will build on their strategic expertise, mandate, and field level presence to successfully implement this project.

1 - Needs

Girls in South Sudan face predictable barriers to access and complete their education. These include negative gender norms and stereotypes, poor or inadequate facilities at the school level, including WASH, menstrual hygiene services, lack of teaching and learning materials as well as inadequate qualified teachers, poverty, displacement among other factors (ENA 2021). Hence girls are one of the groups that are least likely to participate in education and are most likely to drop out of school at upper primary grades than boys (AEC 2018).Communities in selected counties in Warrap and Upper Nile face both man made and natural hazards that impacts on access and quality of education. By October 2021, 8 schools in Gogrial West and 12 in Ulang were affected by floods that destroyed schools or rendered them inaccessible while learners are routinely displaced due to conflict (intercommunal and conflict involving armed actors) (inter-cluster rapid assessment). Enrolment of girls in the two states is lower than that of boys and sharply declines at the secondary level indicating inequality in access and retention. According to the AEC 2018, the gender parity index for Warrap (0.71) and Upper Nile (0.71) at primary level is lower than the national average (0.75) and ranks among the lowest nationally. This is similar at secondary level where Upper Nile has the second lowest index (0.33), which is lower than the national average (0.54). Moreover, the dropout rate for girls is higher with Warrap ranked second in the number of girls drop out (AEC 2018). For a significant number of girls, access to water, latrines and handwashing facilities is low. 1 out of 3 schools have no access to a safe water source within or near the school (ENA 2021). In Warrap (19.4%) and Upper Nile State (12.9%) of schools had no access to safe water. In addition, 1 out of 5 school reported having no functional toilet/latrines in their schools with some schools having no facility at all. The challenge for learners, more specifically for girls, is compounded by lack of gender separated facilities. The ENA reports that over three quarters of schools (76.9%) did not have bathrooms or washrooms available for female while 1 out of 3 schools don’t have latrines that were separated by gender. Moreover, teenage girls’ access to facilities that support menstrual hygiene management are lacking. The ENA 2021 shows a significant number of sampled schools (50.4%) had not received dignity kits for female learners since 2018. This has serious implications for their participation in education as they miss 0ut 10-20 school days every month because of menstruation (Oxfam 2019). This is due to shame/stigma, lack of access to sanitary products, or lack of access to/limited knowledge about proper hygiene management practices (Oxfam 2019). This is compounded by lack of access to handwashing facilities near toilets/latrines with close to 50% of schools reporting lack of handwashing facilities as well as soap. The Covid-19 school closure undermined participation in education as well as exacerbating protection risks for teenage girls. The ENA 2021 reports a rise in cases of early/forced marriages, teenage pregnancies, sexual and gender-based violence during the pandemic. This has grave implications for their future. Many of the affected girls are unlikely to report back in school without efforts to mobilize them and bring them back to school. Lack of referral mechanisms was identified as an impediment to address protection risks leaving children exposed and vulnerable. For example, over 40% of assessed schools in the ENA indicated that they had no referral mechanisms to support vulnerable children.Preparedness to address the effects of emergencies on education remains a significant challenge. There is lack of accurate and timely data to prepare and launch timely interventions leading to children and youth missing on valuable learning time (UNESCO 2020).

2 - Project activities

Activity 1.1 Conduct community mobilization targeting women and men at community level on the value of girls’ education through organizing back to school campaigns at the start of each school term and conducting campaigns and public forums to raise and increase awareness. Awareness messages on Covid-19 prevention and referral as well as other key messages on the value of the girl child education will be included in these sessions. Activity1.2: Construct 6 temporary learning spaces in target areas to provide learning opportunities for more learners. Construction of TLS will be guided by Inter-INEE's Minimum Standards for Education in Emergencies and Standards in Education Cluster Strategy 2019Activity 1.3: Construct/rehabilitate 6 gender sensitive latrines in target counties. Construction or rehabilitation of latrines will follow the INEE and sphere standards. Activity 1.4: Distribute dignity kits targeting 1000 girls to address the challenge of access to pads. Activity 1. 5: Procure and distribute recreational material supplies for girl learners to ensure they have access to materials for games and other extra-curricular activities. Activity 2.1: Procure and supply target schools with relevant teaching and learning materialsActivity 2.2: Organize and conduct teacher training workshops and follow up on EiE, gender responsive pedagogy, guidance and counselling and learner-cantered methodologies. Activity 3.1: Organize and conduct training targeting community education committee members and local education officials trained in guidance and counselling including referral systems and Psychosocial Support (PSS).Activity 3.2: Procure and provide appropriate IEC prevention and mitigation of gender based violence (GBV)Activity 3.3 organize and conduct back to learning campaigns, specifically targeting girls to enroll or go back to school epidemics.Activity 3.4: Support TADO to strengthen organizational institutional capacity gaps and needs.

7.4. Community Engagement Strategy

Yes

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

The project implementation strategy will involve use of staff who are conversant with the language used in the target locations. This will ensure that they can communicate the project and interact with beneficiaries in a language they can participate regardless of levels of education. Where possible, the project will engage qualified women as part of the implementation team so that they can engage with girls on sensitive issues. The project staff will also be sensitized on aspects of disability, through work with organization that are focused on the disabled to better interact with the disabled in a meaningful way.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

During the community entry stage of the project, the project will engage community leaders in the respective school communities, men and women in the PTAs and SMCs to share details about the project. This will include information on the objectives of the project, type of activities to be implemented, the project expectations from the community and what the community can expect from the project. The community members will also be informed on how they can engage with the the project implementers. how to report any issues of concern and how the organizations will approach such reports. At the monitoring stage, members of the community will provide feedback, through focus groups or key informants, on the overall implementation of activities and this will be used to strengthen and improve on project implementation.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

At the project inception, staff from the implementing organizations involved in the project will be sensitized on PSEA and required to sign an undertaking to prevent and report on any cases. the community, including children will also be sensitized on the ways to identify, prevent and report on cases of PSEA.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

All feedback received from participants through different means will be analyzed on periodic basis and addressed in consultation with the beneficiaries, community leaders and project management team. The project implementation strategy will be reviewed if needed to address the genuine feedback.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

The lack of referral pathways for those experiencing different protection risks has been highlighted in the 2021 Education Needs Assessment report. One of the project activities is to establish referral pathway for children, especially young girls facing protection issues such forced marriages, PSEA at school level or harassments on the way to school. The project will provide awareness sessions to the community and schools, encourage and support them to establish local mechanism to address protection risks. However, being cognizant of the fact that protection risks can be perpetuated and tolerated at the community level, the project will explore linkages with the protection cluster to establish or strengthen referral mechanisms in the targeted counties. To address the risk of lack of participation, for key groups ( girls, older women and men, people living with disabilities, the project will establish/strengthen representation of the different groups in the PTAs/SMCs so that their representation is guaranteed. Moreover, sessions with the beneficiaries of the different groups may be organized where it becomes clear that the main structures do not allow full participation due to societal norms and expectations. the project will also employ different strategies to encourage full and meaningful participation.
