Project Summary

This project aims to provide sustainable and equitable access to safe water, sanitation, and hygiene to reduce the suffering of displaced persons and vulnerable host communities in Tonj East, Tonj North, and Tonj South Counties of Warrap State. The focus will mainly be on women and children who constitute the bulk of the displaced persons and at-risk host families. The project will provide lifesaving WASH assistances to households with children suffering from malnutrition and preventable communicable diseases due to poor access to safe water, hygiene, and sanitation facilities. These malicious impacts are caused by severe flooding and widespread inter-communal violence in Warrap State particularly in Tonj East, Tonj North, and Tonj South Counties of Warrap. The project will be implemented in underserved and multi-hazard affected areas of the Greater Tonj region of Warrap state. The affected population includes internally displaced persons and vulnerable host communities; priority will be given to female-headed households, households with person(s) living with disability, households with children suffering from malnutrition, and households with pregnant and lactating mothers - exact criteria will be established in collaboration with communities (see below).
Programme participants will be targeted based on humanitarian principles complemented by CAFOD protection and safeguarding framework, which is central to our work in guiding overall program management and development processes to be safe, accessible, dignified, and inclusive. A participatory and consultative approach will be used to agree on targeting criteria, ensuring that the most vulnerable individuals/households' voices are heard and influence the targeting processes. This will be implemented by building on existing CAFOD targeting methodologies.
In 2021-2022, CAFOD received funds from SSHF for FSL and Protection to address needs of the affected population in Greater Tonj area with the absence of funding to integrate this WASH component. This project will build upon CAFOD’s previous work in Greater Tonj supported by SSHF and other funding sources - bringing in a WASH component will complement other sectors and better support households to overcome challenges. CAFOD will implement a multisectoral response to address the multiple needs of the target communities to holistically address the needs. This project will provide WASH assistances through repair and rehabilitation of none-functional boreholes, house-to-house hygiene promotion through project volunteers, and training of water users committees to aid sustainability of access to safe drinking water. Training for handpump mechanics which will also be conducted by CAFOD and its partner staff, and construction of household sanitation facilities through 50% community engagement. The project will provide sanitary kits for adolescent girls in primary school to curb menstrual hygiene-related absenteeism and reduced performance. The project will train women, girls, and boys on how to make reusable menstrual pads (RUMPS), which in turn can become an income generating skill. Acknowledging children being social agents for change, the project will establish and train school hygiene clubs to pass on good hygiene practices to their peers in school and in turn to the entire community. The main messages would revolve around good hand washing practices, personal hygiene, food hygiene, environmental sanitation, safe water chain management, and safe disposal of solid and human waste. Hygiene campaigns in public places such as markets, health facilities, village centres, and schools would also be conducted to enlighten the communities on environmental and public hygiene. COVID19 and EVD Infection, Prevention, and Control messaging will be mainstreamed in every activity throughout the project cycle. This project will be complemented with other ongoing projects in the same locations for beneficiaries to receive multiple support.

1 - Needs

In 2022, the humanitarian community in South Sudan estimates that more than two-thirds of South Sudan's population, 8.9 million people, are in need humanitarian assistance, an increase of 600,000 since 2021 (HNO 2022). According to the WASH cluster PIN 2023, 6,000,000 people are in need of WASH services, with Greater Tonj (the proposed project location) falling under WASH severity classification level 3. Continued conflict and instability in the country combined with flooding have resulted in large-scale internal and cross-border displacement. The continued macroeconomic crisis, the low preparedness capacity of national and state authorities, COVID-19 pandemic and consecutive years of flooding are exacerbating existing vulnerabilities and humanitarian needs of people, while pushing more people on a downward spiral into crisis and reliance on humanitarian assistance. People’s access to safe and improved water and sanitation is low. Commonly reported issues in accessing WASH facilities include long queues and congestion at water collection points, leading to disputes and tensions. Women and girls frequently face physical and sexual assault and harassment at water collection points, bathing areas and latrines. Lack of latrines and/or overfull latrines lead to open defecation resulting in waterborne diseases. Additional access issues reported by IDPs are taxes imposed by host communities to use WASH facilities (reported in Maban and Tonj South) according to HNO 2022. Approximately 41 per cent of the total population have access to safe water while 10 per cent have access to improved sanitation. 61 per cent of the population practice open defecation (UNICEF, WASH briefing note, November to December 2021). An estimated 6 million people’s living standards and their wellbeing will be impacted in 2023 because of inadequate or lack of access to safe water and improved sanitation. The findings from the 2021 FSNMS + analysis indicate that access to WASH facilities by women and girls, boys, persons living with disabilities, and elderly remains a challenge. According to CAFOD KAP (knowledge attitude and practices) survey conducted in Tonj North and Tonj South in September 2021, 24.2% of the population do not have sufficient water for drinking and other domestic purposes. The same survey indicates that 32.21% of the population wait for about an hour to access water from the water source, and 27.85% walk for an hour or more to reach the nearest safe water source. The reported long walking distance to the water source increases exposure and vulnerabilities of women and girls to protection related risks. Poor access to clean water, hygiene and sanitation services combined with high levels of food insecurity compromises people’s health and nutrition securities across South Sudan and this has contributed to the malicious impacts on children growth and developments. Communities in Greater Tonj cited that a lack of access to water contributes to tension and conflict in the population. Access to sanitation is very low in Greater Tonj with only 51% having access to a functioning latrine. Community hygiene awareness and practices require continuous efforts for messaging to help in gradual behaviour change on hygiene practices. Reach assessment conducted between January to March 2022, indicated similar findings. Poor access to WASH services combined with high levels of food insecurity continue to have a detrimental impact on the health of children, as seen through the high prevalence of malnutrition and water-borne diseases. Counties reporting high GAM rates have also high WASH needs which include Greater Tonj areas. This project will address the identified WASH needs in close coordination and collaborations with key humanitarian and development actors.

This will be delivered through rehabilitation of none-functional boreholes, house-to-house hygiene promotion through project volunteers, training of water users committees to aid sustainability of access to safe drinking water, and refresher training for handpump mechanics. Acknowledging children being social agents for change, the project will establish and train school hygiene clubs to pass on good hygiene practices to their peers in school and in turn to the entire community. The main messages would revolve around good hand washing practices, personal hygiene, food hygiene, environmental sanitation, safe water chain management, and safe disposal of solid and human waste. Hygiene campaigns in public places such as markets, health facilities, village centres, and schools would also be conducted to enlighten the communities on environmental and public hygiene.Messaging on COVID19 and EVD Infection, Prevention, and Control practices will be mainstreamed in every activity throughout the project cycle. The project will use multi-sectorial approach, integrating FSL and Protection activities to address the drivers of vulnerabilities and underlying structural issues across the targeted counties. The project will also collaborate and complement the work of other stakeholders in the area to address WASH in Nutrition and WASH for GBV mitigation.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

Country programme undertook rigorous preparatory work including sending its cash transfer programming (CTP) focal staff for training CaLP in Kenya in 2019 and recent training in July 2022 by CAFOD CVA Surge Advisor. Additional staff and partner staff were trained on carrying out market analysis for cash transfer programming. This enhanced staff capacity on cash transfer programming. The skills in cash transfer programming were honed by carrying out an actual market analysis by CAFOD in Yirol East, Gogrial and Tonj in 2022 in preparation for its food and NFIs distribution project, which was successfully carried out involving traders in some project areas. Consultations during the market analysis were held with communities, local chambers of commerce who mobilized the traders, other local vendors in consultations with other NGOs, and government officials. This was to dig out and unpack information to streamline processes before it rolled out its cash-based response. As mentioned above, a market assessment and cash feasibility analysis were undertaken which informed CAFOD to embark on the commodity voucher programme. Based on that experience CAFOD will build on lessons learnt in shaping the CVA approach for this project. Currently CAFOD and its partners are undertaking Market Systems Development (MSD) training to enhance staff capacity on market and value chain analysis. In 2022 CAFOD embarked on market analysis for supply of various commodities such as crop and vegetable seeds; this project will benefit from this analysis through the expertise developed. To ensure beneficiary choice and flexibility especially with regards to the types of items and the mode of assistance, there will be consultations with the project participants whose lives are affected. An important secondary aim for WASH NFIs cash voucher is to inject cash into the local economy and stimulates local markets.

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

CAFOD implemented cash voucher assistance through seed fairs and direct cash transfer for communities in Tonj North, Tonj South, Gogrial West and Gogrial East in 2022. The venture into CVA was after a comprehensive market assessment and feasibility analysis which informed the decision to undertake CVA in Tonj North, Tonj South, Gogrial East and West counties. The market assessment was to ascertain the market potential to support the approach. Things considered in the assessment included functionality and access to the market by the affected population, availability and source of goods and services, and traders’ potential to supply the market. Cash transfer programming (CTP) refers to all programmes where cash (or vouchers for goods or services) is directly provided to beneficiaries. It is an approach that builds upon linkages, capacities, incentives, and relationships to encourage effective market recovery. The logistical burdens and risks that are usually experienced by agencies in transporting (usually bulky) in-kind supplies to project locations are reduced. The use of vouchers or cash reduces overall costs of the response, and more people can be reached with limited resources. The engagement of local vendors in supplying items to the beneficiaries is intended to support the local market dynamics. Through this approach, the cash or vouchers given to beneficiaries and exchanged with local vendors for supplies would advance financial linkages in the local markets as well as boosting the purchasing power and local economy. Cash and vouchers assistance (CVA) is globally felt to be a more dignified form of assistance for humanitarian aid recipients because it promotes dignity and autonomy of the project participants. Individuals accustomed to supporting themselves and their families can suddenly become dependent on aid and charity; while their sense of dignity cannot be easily and fully restored, cash and voucher transfers offer some choice and a small degree of control in a situation where many feel they have none. The affected populations preferred the flexibility and choice that cash offers them to prioritise their own needs. We have realised that use of cash and vouchers empowers project participants through ownership of their needs and the opportunity to purchase locally preferred supplies. Since CAFOD intends to provide WASH NFIs through cash vouchers, this will further boost the local economy and potential of local traders and trigger market growth.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

The project will use SADI (safe accessible, dignified and inclusive) framework which is a tool for protection risk assessment of the various categories of the vulnerability. It ensures the design of activities is accessible to the most vulnerable across Sex, Age and Disability, as well as targeting the most vulnerable groups including women, boys, girls, PLWD, and elderly. CAFOD has created women and girls’ friendly spaces where they can give their feedback and complaints in a safe and dignified way to trained staff who know how to handle such issues. This has cultivated confidence in the vulnerable people coming forward to air out their views. Focus group discussions are conducted with different categories of vulnerable persons and key informant interview with service providers who work with vulnerable groups such as people living with disabilities, elderly and women and girls. CAFOD works with local partners who are embedded in the local communities. They employ local staff who understands the culture and languages of the communities. This improves understanding of the local context and cultural structures in the areas of operation which is important for access and acceptance, and in-depth analysis of the needs of different vulnerable groups. Some project documents are produced in local language e.g., posters, IEC materials, and information on complaint and feedback mechanism.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

CAFOD has a robust, safe, and responsive feedback and complaints handling mechanism across its programmes for every group in a community, particularly the most marginalised, and works to ensure there is consultation in the project design, the feedback loop is closed, and that the effectiveness of the mechanism is monitored. This allows room for flexibility in programmes to ensure they have a system that is appropriate and adapted for their context. The community decides on the feedbacks and complaints channels which are appropriate to their context. Lessons learnt from the feedback and complaints handling are used in decision making to adjust programme implementation in real time to ensure gaps identified are urgently addressed. Requirements to have a feedback and complaints mechanism in place have also been incorporated into funding agreements signed with partners, with requirements for any sensitive complaint to be shared with CAFOD within a set time limit. Communities are trained on how to make use of the existing complaint and feedback handling mechanism. CAFOD carries out community awareness on the existing channels of airing out their complaints and feedback as well as making them understand that it is their right to complain. In addition, there is a policy on whistleblowing which protects the whistle-blower. CAFOD has an information kiosk which is a stand, booth, or type of gazebo that has one open side, and includes free informative literature about the project. The information kiosks agents’ main purpose is to help guide community members by offering information and advice about the project. CAFOD has developed an information sharing pack which is disseminated to project participants. Inception meetings are held at the start of the project with communities, local authorities, and different stakeholders to give information about what the project intends to achieve, the roles and rights of the community throughout the project cycle. The purpose of this is to ensure transparency and to create opportunity for communities to share their opinions before the start of the project. During project implementation, regular meetings are held with various project management committees such as water user committees, hygiene clubs, women’s groups etc. where decision-making and project planning are done. This includes various information gathering exercises, for example, the project carries out various post distribution monitoring on project inputs to assess the satisfaction levels of project participants.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

CAFOD has strict policy on PSEA which guide all staff in their daily interactions with beneficiaries. All staff and volunteers, consultants hired by CAFOD must adhered to CAFOD’s PSEA policy and staff sign staff code of behaviour and safeguarding policy. Staff are inducted on the policies upon recruitment, volunteers and contractors are also oriented and expected to abide by the policies. Programme participants are made aware of the policies and what to expect from CAFOD’s staff. CAFOD has safe and confidential channels for reporting SEA concerns directly to the Country Representative or focal person in UK or through ethics point website, helpdesk, hotline, information kiosk and other channels which is regularly checked, and timely responses provided to the complainant. CAFOD carries out regular community awareness on the existing channels of airing out their complaints and feedback as well as making them understand that it is their right to complain. In addition, there is a policy on whistleblowing which protects the whistle-blower.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Feedback and complaints are received, logged, and regularly analyzed by the focal person to a timely respond. Lessons learnt from the feedback and complaints handling are used to adjust programme implementation on real time to ensure gaps identified are urgently addressed. The project will carry out regular monitoring, project reviews, and community consultations, which are used to inform the project decisions. For example, the project will carry out various post distribution monitoring on project inputs to assess the satisfaction levels of project participants. An example from an earlier project; based on feedback from project participants, CAFOD had to prefinance seed procurement to ensure timely provision of seeds before the back donor agreement was signed. In another project, CAFOD responded to community request to provide maize flour instead of sorghum. In Yirol East, CAFOD increased the number of distribution points from seven to eleven to reduce the distances covered by beneficiaries which is crucial in minimising protection risks. These are some of the examples of how the beneficiary feedback is used to adapt project assistance. In Tonj, during the implementation of the SSHF SA1 in 2021/2022, CAFOD procured food as a way to motivate farmers to participate in training at demonstration plots to promote adoption of agroecological practices. This was in response to feedback from the farmers that they can engage on the training because they are food insecure and have no energy to cultivate or participate in the training. All these learnings and expertise developed in handling previous feedbacks on other projects will be used in this project.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

The project will make a deliberate effort to explore, understand and mitigate programme related risks leading to the development and implementation of strategy that minimizes and addresses all ranges of threats and risks both external and internal. In practice, this means that all intervention responses will be guided by Core Humanitarian Standards to which CAFOD is certified and Do-No-Harm and conflict-sensitivity approaches through analysis of risks to programme participants and environment. In relation to risk 8.1 on long distances to distribution centres CAFOD has proactively mitigated this risk in its projects and will do so for this project. An example; in the past there were 7 distribution sites in Yirol, but because of protection risks related to long walking distances, an additional 4 distribution centers were created to reduce distances. In Gogrial distribution points were increased after consultations with traders who were asked to open temporary shops closer to participants. As part of its responsibility of mitigating risk 8.2 and 8.3 on SEA, CAFOD has various policies on PSEA, code of behaviour and safeguarding policies which are signed by every staff member, volunteers and contractors before any official engagement to ensure protection of project participants. Project participants are also made aware of these policies through various information sharing ways including setting up of information kiosks and agents in the project locations. CAFOD and its partners have a robust complaint and feedback handling mechanism in place, and hotline to receive complaints and feedback. CAFOD carries out regular community awareness on the existing channels of airing out their complaints and feedback as well as making them understand that it is their right to complain. In addition, there is a policy on whistleblowing which protects the whistleblower. CAFOD has clearly laid out procedures on handling and responding to feedback and complaints. Complaints are categorised, and serious, sensitive complaints such as SEA are handled by senior management with clear investigation and disciplinary protocols e.g. it must be resolved within set time limits and feedback provided to affected persons. In relation to risk 8.4 on no access to or awareness of protection assistance or referral services for survivors, CAFOD has a robust protection response and prevention activities in most of its projects. CAFOD programmes have provided community based GBV protection measures such as case management, psychosocial support, creation of women and girls friendly spaces. CAFOD and its partners have mapped out referral pathways and trained service providers on their use. There is also training of various service providers (health workers, police, chiefs etc.) in Yirol East and Gogrial to strengthen their capacities on GBV case reporting, survivor centred handling and management of cases. As regards risk 8.8 where the risk of interference from external stakeholders in the impartial and needs- based selection of beneficiaries CAFOD has employed measures to restrict or limit this influence. CAFOD makes use of a targeting committee comprised mostly of women from the community. Targeting criteria are agreed with the community in inception meetings and their inputs incorporated to ensure no vulnerable families are left out.When targeting is completed a public meeting of community members is called, for public verification and acclamation of selected persons. CAFOD also uses its SADI (Safety, Access, Dignified and Inclusivity) framework to mitigate exclusion of marginalized and vulnerable groups in a project. The framework carries out a comprehensive assessment of at-risk groups and how they are affected differently by a disaster or conditions of their vulnerability using such diversity statuses such as sex, age, disability, being a refugee, or IDP, minority status and others. This leads to design of projects that meet the specific needs of groups of people.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

The proposed intervention will contribute to the collective outcomes on food insecurity by improving access to basic services in emergency, mitigate GBV and protection issues and build WASH resilience. It will improve the level of WASH services for both IDP and host community in Greater Tonj Counties. WASH is a critical complement in enabling reduction of morbidity and mortality. The close correlation between malnutrition and diarrhoea due to poor WASH services is a well-established cause of morbidity and mortality amongst children under five years old. The undernourished are more likely to suffer higher incidences and increased mortality rates from diarrhoeal diseases, while on the other hand those suffering from diarrhoea are more likely to suffer from wasting, stunting, or other complications resulting from abrupt dehydration and loss of valuable nutrients and electrolytes. Furthermore, recurrent parasitism due to faecal-oral contamination associated with poor hygiene and sanitation leads to repeat cyclical infections, diarrhoeal diseases and malnutrition. This intervention will complement CAFOD's ongoing response in the locality and will improve community’s access to safe drinking water as well as increasing adoption of good hygiene practices thus, reducing incidences of diarrhoea and other hygiene and sanitation related diseases and building resilience of communities to shocks and stresses.
