Project Summary

Building on experience of project interventions in Syria since 2013, Oxfam is planning to respond in the most vulnerable districts/sub-districts with high severity in Aleppo, Rural Damascus, Homs, Hama, Dar’a and Idleb based on the severity of WASH needs
The sub-districts that will be targeted by this project have all been heavily affected by conflict and are in need of additional WASH support, based on a rapid Oxfam assessment in some of the selected villages, consultations with Syrian and international NGOs, and reliable secondary data, including the Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO) 2019. The project is designed to meet specific life-saving needs around water sanitation and hygiene (WASH), and is aligned to HRP 2019 for Syria. The project focuses on WASH response with gender mainstreamed, and with a well-defined response strategy suitable to access situation and security challenges; this includes implementation through reliable and experienced site engineers, public health promotion volunteers and focal points, carefully screened private contractors, and coordination with other actors. The total estimated populated, as per UN-OCHA datasets in these sub-districts, is over 1,752,742 people. This estimation has to be taken as a rough guide, given that many people have been displaced, with many IDP and returnee fluctuations within Governorates and Districts across Syria.
Target locations have been proposed according to severity of humanitarian needs and number of population in need. They may be altered depending on the evolution of the context on the ground, access and people's movement.
The project aims to support life-saving WASH interventions as well as activities that focus on the maintenance and/or the light rehabilitation of existing WASH systems: a) improve the target communities' access to safe water facilities, b) appropriate capacity to maintain key health and hygiene practices; c) access to safe sanitation facilities and services and; d) WASH emergency response including the provision of WASH materials, equipment and services i.e. water taks, O&M water trucking and emergency WASH facilities
Oxfam planned interventions will be inline with: SO1 that captures sector efforts to restore or keep Water and Sanitation infrastructure at the minimum level of operation and SO2 which describes life-saving WASH interventions such as emergency provision of water, sanitation and solid waste facilities and services as well as WASH NFI distributions.
In the terms of the interventions above, Oxfam will target all age groups for water and sanitation interventions. For the hygiene interventions, these will be targeted women and a focus on children as a proportion of the activities will be conducted at school level.
The specific locations for activities in these sub-districts are still to be declared. Specific WASH interventions will be assessed by Oxfam WASH teams, including full costings, and will be discussed with LWE officials, local authorities, and WASH Sector actors to ensure complementarity of interventions versus work of other actors in targeted areas. As final interventions will require approval of both the MoWR and MoE, Oxfam will convene a project steering committee which will include both Oxfam management staff and Government representatives at Damascus level, who will discuss and agree selection of possible activities and operational challenges

Needs

Based on WASH assessments there are an estimated 15.6 million people who require WASH assistance, 6.2 million of whom are considered in acute need. the average water consumption during 2018 amounted to an estimated 71 liters per capita per day. However, communities purchasing water exclusively from commercial water trucks received on average only 57 liters per capita per day, with at least 10 per cent of them receiving less than 30 liters per capita per day. Areas well served by free water from networks use far more than the average of 71 liters per day. Families who have no option but to purchase water from commercial water trucks spend on average 10 per cent of their income on water, with 3 per cent of the population spending more than 20 per cent of their income to purchase water and in extreme cases as high as 40 per cent (Areesha). The situation is even more dire for people living in informal settlements, where families can spend more than 50 per cent of their incomes on water. Reduced incomes and purchasing power of families in Syria could lead to adoption of harmful coping strategies, with potentially significant implications on health and hygiene behavior/practices.Regarding sanitation, more than 99 per cent of the surveyed population has access to a functioning toilet. Nevertheless, this figure may give a false impression that the sanitation situation in Syria is satisfactory, while at least 70 per cent of sewage is untreated and at least half of the sewage systems are either not functional or only partially functional leading to significant community-level health risks. Recent estimates from MoWR indicate that only 9 per cent of the population is still served by functional wastewater treatment plants. Dysfunctionality of existing wastewater treatment plants and sewage networks contributing to the discharge of raw sewage, will continue to have a significant impact on water sources, soil and the overall water resource management. A combination of factors including a lack of investment in water safety, the distribution of raw untreated water and the lack of safe sewage management has contributed to increased public health risks. The high proportion of water-borne diseases in some communities, including across Idleb or last year outbreak of acute bloody diarrhea in some areas of Deir-ez-Zor, are examples of some of these risks.The WASH Household assessment indicates that at least 10 per cent of garbage is left in public areas, potentially contributing to the increase in leishmaniosis cases observed since the beginning of 2018 in some areas. The situation seems to be particularly dire for some areas with a high returns ratio, with 35 per cent of returnees reporting leaving garbage in the public areas. In addition, some places used for garbage dumping are not prepared and managed well that could contribute to increase vector breading. There is a significant need for leishmaniosis prevention activities including solid waste management, particularly in northwest and northeast Syria where the more serious form of the disease (Visceral Leishmaniosis) exists. Reports for Idleb indicate that in the first half of 2018, there was a 100 per cent increase in the number of cases in comparison with the number of reported cases last year.IDPs living in IDP sites are amongst the most vulnerable groups in Syria. In the northwest the scale of displacements to IDP sites witnessed since the beginning of 2018, has contributed to a deterioration of already overused WASH facilities and increased the need to scale-up operations and enhance the focus around more sustainable solutions (connection to existing water networks and/or establishment of simplified water and sewer networks). Access to WASH services has been identified as the number one priority by displaced people in 30 per cent of camps and collective centers in northwest Syria, the number two priority in 40 per cent and the number three priority in 30 per cent.

Implementation of activities

Combining Short Term Interventions with Long Term Vision: Oxfam’s approach combines initial water trucking in case a rapid response is needed, with infrastructure rehabilitation for a more sustained outcome. Infrastructure rehabilitation includes repair to water pipes,rehabilitation of water sources such as wells, and of network infrastructure such aspumping stations to ensure services are brought back on line. Additionally, Oxfam also furnishes contingency materials to targeted pumping stations so that the operation and maintenance can take place in a timely manner, which ensures that uninterrupted water is available to the communities always. Given this, Oxfam will undertake direct implementation of WASH activities using implementation strategies as discussed above. Currently, due to access constraints, athorough risk analysis of persons of concerns (PoC) and Do No Harm (DNH) has not been possible. However, Oxfam has ensured that its interventions are based on consultations with a spectrum of PoCs on the ground, representatives of LWEs, mukhtars and other community representatives, carried out by Oxfam Syria field based staff and by those performing field visits, including MEAL.Oxfam will prioritise project activities in communities with high number and ratio of returnees to the local population, where there is a high level of vulnerability and poor coping mechanisms, for both returnees and static residents. WASH interventions, priority for specific household level needs interventions will be based on the following criteria:1. Female-headed HHs;2. HHs with persons with disabilities, among whom those with lack of mobility related to old age;3. HHs with high number of children, both school age children and under 5 years of age;4. Spontaneous returnee households who have lost assets and property through the conflict; Oxfam will aim to target communities for interventions which have seen many households returning back, and where there are issues of social cohesion, service provision. Beneficiary criteria will be further confirmed after interactions with the affected communities to include other possible excluded and vulnerable groups. For both the public health promotion and interventions at HH level, beneficiary identification will be conducted with the communities and will be verified at field level by Oxfam teams. Beneficiary selection for Public Health Engineering activities (improving Water Supply) activities will be based on site selection of different engineering projects that could be undertaken, and the catchment populations that will be served by a particular engineering intervention. Priority will be given to interventions which serve a high caseload of people who identify with the criteria above. Beneficiary involvement at the initial design phase of this action has been important in order to get an understanding of the most urgent needs. Further consultation with women, men, children, elderly people and persons with disabilities will continue to be important to ensure their differing and specific needs and protection risks are identified and considered in aspects of Progamme delivery.Oxfam operates in Syria through MoUs with the Ministry of Water Resources, Ministry of Social Affairs and Labour, the Ministry of Local Administration and Environment, and with Ministry of Agriculture. With respect to more localised coordination, Oxfam supports the Water Establishment and Water Units, facilitated by Oxfam's site engineers. The Ministry of Water Resources also convenes coordination meetings between WASH agencies, of which Oxfam is a participant. Oxfam also closely coordinates with the WASH agencies active in Syria- ICRC, UNICEF, Medair and ACF. Oxfam will also be working to strengthen INGO coordination within Syria. These coordination mechanisms are essential to ensure that gaps are addressed and different needs are met.

Protection Risk Analysis

Oxfam's humanitarian programming in Syria is conducted in accordance with Oxfam's Minimum Standards for Gender in Emergencies. Gender is integrated throughout the project cycle and to measure that IASC Gender and Age monitoring tool is used.Oxfam in Syria has institutionalized a Humanitarian Gender Action Plan (updated every 3 months) as a planning and monitoring tool for a more coherent response to immediate needs, internal institutional change and capacity building. Some of the activities include: multi-sector and multi-level rapid gender analysis to inform our programming and advocacy, Gender and GBV/Child Protection training for all staff and volunteers, developing partnerships with local actors, strengthening coordination with Protection Sector including Child Protection and GBV Sub-sectors. Due to limited presence and capacity of local protection service providers, Oxfam has been engaging with specialized agencies (UNHCR, UNICEF and UNFPA and their local partners) to gain better insight on protection risks as well as define Oxfam’s role in supporting referrals and case management system. Reporting protection concerns, especially the ones in which the parties to conflict are concerned, is a complex matter which requires coordination with local protection service providers as well as solid conflict analysis. Oxfam is currently working on clear guidelines for staff and volunteers to support this.In parallel, the country team agreed on annual Child Protection (in March18) and Safeguarding (PSEA) Country Plans (in June18) to further promote staff and volunteers’ awareness of protection risks and vulnerabilities. Oxfam engages communities in a conflict sensitive manner, taking care not to exacerbate tensions - while supporting local capacities for peace, particularly among women-led initiatives, wherever possible. For the PHP activities, Oxfam conducts assessments of project locations (for example schools and collective shelters) through its focal points, volunteers, site engineers, to identify the needs, and accordingly designs its awareness sessions and IEC materials. Female and male staff of local health centres, as well as female and male teachers of schools are involved in design of sessions, and while awareness sessions are being conducted in schools and collective shelters. Needs and preferences of target beneficiaries inform the content of hygiene kits; for example: menstruation material support for women and adolescent girls. Activities linked risks: Inequality in distribution of NFI packages.Mitigating Measure: Promote Establishment of adequate management plan. Promote involvement communities in distribution planning and feedback on package for improvement.Emergency sanitation:Toilets represent GBV risk Access to toilet is not available to all 24/7.Mitigating:Ensure women and adolescent girls are consulted during design, implementation and monitoring phase of projects; Accountability mechanisms are put in place and received feedback from users is followed up. Lessons from previous experience are shared and learned. Support to water supply systems: Unequal distribution of services inadvertently empower or strengthen the position of armed groups or other actors.Mitigating: Establish strong links with authorities and operators to promote equity and “do no harm principle” all the time; Organise joint planning culture and agree on minimum standard; Include community and authorities in targeting; Ensure sensitive information is handled with care.Support to water supply systems: Risk of theft of WASH rehabilitation/construction materials such as generators and construction materials (during delivery/storage/ after installation) by groups and used in conflict activities. - Mitigating: MoUs to be agreed with local authorities/councils to ensure local ownership of materials and rehabilitated/new facilities ; Promote cost recovery systems for longer term maintenance and ownership to infrastructure to promote community ownership

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risk: Increased instability of security situation in project location limits the ability of Oxfam to achieve project objectives.Mitigation Measures: Engage in country security networks and other humanitarian organizations to monitor and evaluate the changing security environment, conduct regular risk assessments and adopt mitigation measures where appropriate, allocate funds to security personnel and equipment (first aid kits, security alarms, safes, etc. to address shortcomings,adopt strict safety procedures for personnel, especially when travelling outside of Damascus, collaborate closely with UNOCHA and other appropriate organizations on the ground to share operational details on Oxfam's programming. Risk: Difficulty in accessing communities for data collection in order to assess their actual needs and/or measure impact.Mitigation Measures:Oxfam has developed new relationships with the MOSAL and the MOLA to help aid in data collection and assessment at the local level directly.To reach vulnerable groups in HTRAs, coordinate and collaborate with private contractors and partners with enhances capacities to access these areas.adopting remote reporting techniques to allow for data collection and reporting. Oxfam uses local personnel as site engineers and focal points at the governorate level, who also aid in data collection and monitoring, closely guided by the MEAL Officer.Risk: Inflation and exchange rates are unstable, complicating spending and expenditures that must be made in Syrian Pounds (SYP) within Syria.Mitigation Measures: Explore options to overcome this challenge employed by other NGOs and international organizations despite the government requirement for contracts with local supplies to be denominated in SYP,make payments to vendors in tranches to ensure that suppliers remain interested and that neither Oxfam nor the supplier is affected too much by the exchange rate fluctuations) Exchange rate gains are reported to the donor and are used for additional activities based on donor’s approval.Risk: Limited equipment and supplies available in the Syrian market due to instability, sanctions and limited imports delays or prevents the implementation of project activities.Mitigation Measures: Work with other international humanitarian organizations to see what other options exist to get needed supplies and equipment in country while remaining in compliance with applicable sanctions and counter terror legislation. Risk: Pressure from authorities on Oxfam to adjust programme priorities or address non-critical WASH concerns for their own political benefit.Mitigation: To date, Oxfam's partners within the MoWR and local water establishments have generally shared Oxfam's vision of supporting humanitarian WASH needs. The collaborative approaches have proven successful in ensuring funds are utilised as intended and in a way that suits the general needs of Syria's vulnerable populations.
