Project Summary

ACF proposes an intervention through several axes, as suggested in international Guidelines such as the IASC and the mhGAP and as discussed in the MHPSS working group. Within the IASC pyramid level of MHPSS intervention, ACF works mostly within layer two and three.
.
With host communities ACF will continue to build capacity of Health Care Providers, Nurses and Paramedics as well as building capacity of local CBO members on mhGAP, Thinking Healthy + and mental health and skills training, including trainings on protection issues to teachers and parents. ACF’s period of direct implementation includes trainings for parents, teachers and other community focal points to be “duty bearers” in support of identifying children and adolescents who are in psychological distress and consider referral pathways, and attending to children and adolescent protection needs. The program will equip duty bearers with the necessary skills to identify and actively respond to psychosocial distress and protection issues. MHPSS activities aiming at identifying protection issues and providing emotional support will also be implemented with specific validated protocols, and referral to other protection partners will also be done when necessary.
ACF is currently collaborating with the DOH and MOH in Dohuk and Mosul and has been conducting a Health Systems Strengthening Diagnostic in Zahko and Semel Districts. ACF is collaborating with both DOH and WHO in preparation of training 40 health care providers in mhGAP in these two districts. ACF is currently planning MHPSS programming in Mosul and in partnership with the MoH and the DoE received authorization to begin implementing training programs for teachers, parents, and community leaders. Training materials will be consistent with WHO and UNICEF approved materials and include PM+. Locations include community spaces in West Mosul: Jadida, Risaala, Wadi Hajar, and Shifa; and in East Mosul: Sukkar, Baliyadat as well as locations in southern Mosul. ACF has also started an initial assessment of mental health services within the PHCC’s in Risaala, Bab albeed, Wady Hajar, Mammam al-Alil, Almansor, Tulol Nasir, 17 Tamuz and Alhood Tahtani.
ACF has been working in HAA camp 1 since 2017 and plans to continue to provide mental health and psychosocial supports in HAA camp 1 and 2. ACF’s programming in HAA camp 1 and 2 includes expanding its psychological support program for people in distress in camps. ACF’s exit strategy within these camps is to increase the capacity of local camp actors as well as those PHCC’s where MHPSS cases are referred.
In partnership with Health and Protection cluster agencies and partners, ACF plans to ensure that the Ministry of Health in the Iraqi federal government and the Kurdistan region of Iraq have increased resources and capacity building that will contribute to build sustainable systems and enhance the quality of mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) service provision. ACF continues to collaborate with the MoH and DoH to identify training and capacity needs, as well as providing clinical supervision to support the MoH’s mental health strategy implementation. ACF’s exit stategy includes capacitating and supporting local PHCC’s and the government of Iraq along with other MHPSS partners and agencies.
ACF addresses protection issues in all its activities by considering safety and dignity for all beneficiaries and ensures activities take place in locations and routes to them are away from potential threats. ACF’s MHCP program incorporates trainings for staff and beneficiaries on protection issues, considering child and family safety, tips for staying safe during emergencies including safety plans, as well as respecting and considering dignity for those who may have disabilities and face access issues.

Needs

Since 2013, Iraq was engaged in a war against the so-called Islamic State (ISIS). Iraq has lead several military campaigns to regain control of its territory, which resulted in a number of large scale population displacements. Although the Iraqi Government officially announced the end of the fight against ISIS in December 2017, the humanitarian crisis in Iraq remains one of the largest and complex in the world. The large return movements in liberated areas expected in 2018 did not happen, with still 2 million displaced in September 2018, and in 2019, most IDPs will likely not return until sustainable conditions are met. According to the Multi Cluster Rapid Needs Assessment (MCNA) findings disseminated in September 2018, 40% of IDPs are in need of Protection assistance, 23% of the returnees and 20% of the host communities. An assessment conducted by ACF in 2017 in Mosul showed that the returnees, IDPs and host communities is experiencing high level of distress It also indicated that women, men and children find coping strategies in family and community life. As such, there is a strong need for community based approaches and clear pathways of referral. Save the Children’s MHPSS assessment, 2016, highlighted the impact of the conflict and displacement on the mental health of displaced children and youth in Iraq. The findings show that children have experienced very different mental health issues depending on whether they fled ISIS early on, or stayed and endured ISIS occupation for several years. Key findings for children recently displaced by the Mosul offensive and who were previously living in ISIS controlled area for almost three years included witnessing unspeakable horrors leading to “toxic stress”, extreme sorrow and a traumatized community. Key findings for children who lived under ISIS for a short period and are currently in prolonged displacement include disconnection from a harsh reality and normalizing the presence of violence. An IASC support mission to Iraq highlighted the fact that despite the tremendous efforts invested to improve the MHPSS intervention and coordination since 2015, the MHPSS systems faces various gaps in the delivery of mental health and psychosocial support services on the four levels of the MHPSS pyramid. Psychosocial care and support to address mental health and psychosocial needs of the conflict affected population and host communities (including access to basic needs, family and community support, focused non specialized support and specialized services) are grossly inadequate, particularly given the intensity and the frequency of psychological distress faced by the population . Mental health services have been integrated into primary health care in less than 50% of primary health care centres in the country however turnover rates are exceedingly high in terms of human resources trained on assessment and management of MH conditions due to the difficult economic situation. WHO estimates that the prevalence of mental disorders among the population is 35.5%, while the treatment gap for management of mental disorders is estimated at 94%. Findings of assessments conducted by ACF and other organizations also highlight needs related to care practices and breastfeeding in several areas. When caregivers, especially pregnant and lactating women, are affected by psychological distress, it results in the inability to carry out routine activities and provide appropriate care practices to young children. In Hamam al-Alil camp, ACF conducted an Infant and Young Child Feeding (IYCF) survey along with nutrition mass screening to collect a representative sample of stated practices and views related to IYCF. It revealed that 37% of interviewed women believe they did not produce enough breastmilk to meet their baby’s needs; 30% became pregnant again and believed they could not breastfeed during pregnancy; and 19% reported experiencing a “psychic shock” which they perceived as causing their body to stop producing breastmilk. These findings are consistent with UNICEF SMART survey conducted in January 2017. Implementing care practices and IYCF-related activities is thus important in the current context and can enable to address some of the causes of malnutrition. There are several assessments ACF has taken into consideration besides the ones mentioned above, International Medical Corps completed a Rapid Mental health and Psychosocial Support Assessment in 2016, WHO AIMS & Report on Mental Health System in Iraq from 2006, as well as OCHA / UNHCR Situational Reports on the Mosul Emergency . Consistently in the past few months, MHPSS actors attending Health and MHPSS cluster meetings have identified the need for capacity building, the need for trainings and clinical supervision and the need to support the government with implementing the Mental Health Act and building the HR resources to ensure quality and sustenance of programming.

Protection Risk Analysis

Specific protection risks identified by ACF include: 1) Security and political environment in the targeted areas deteriorates. The security threats includes possible ISIS attacks, and possible violence due to tensions between tribal and ethnic groups. While the political issues include tensions between Kurdish Region of Iraq and Federal Iraq, the militia groups running the roadblocks and the local communities and humanitarian agencies. There is also uncertainty due to the newly install Government in Federal Iraq, as it is new and thus unproven and there are tensions between the Ministries and the Governors. 2) Reduced access to targeted population At times due to security or political issues, ACF has found itself unable to access its project sites. When there are access issues ACF coordinates with UN OCHA.There are multiple levels of lack of access:• Foreign staff cannot access the project sites, but national workers can.• Foreign and non-local national staff cannot access the project site but local staff can.• No staff has access to the project site. 3) Unexploded ordinance and mines in the targeted areas 4) Inter group tensions can occur that could have the potential for violence (IDP/refugees/host community), along with tension between ACF & communities/beneficiaries due to targeting issues. 5) Sharing personal data of refugees, IDPs or others affected individuals or households with third parties, potentially putting them at risk of violence, detainment or discrimination

Mitigation Measures

1) ACF has a Safety and Security Advisor based in Iraq to maintain abreast of all new safety and security issues as they represent themselves as well as maintaining awareness for consisting safety / security concerns. ACF’s Safety and Security Advisor is a member of the coordination team and is consulted when considering new locations / areas for projects. ACF constantly monitors the security and political environment by maintaining contacts with UN agencies and other partners (INSO, local authorities). ACF frequently revises/updates its operational security plan. If the conditions warrant, ACF may temporarily stop implementation in areas, or change areas or methodology of implementation. 2) ACF ensures access through regular and effective communication and cooperation with authorities at Governorate and community level and with other relevant actors. ACF reports access issues to UNOCHA. If needed, ACF will try to implement remote implementation through local staff, etc, in order to continue to serve the beneficiaries. 3) ACF works with UNMAS and the mine/UXO clearance agencies to train ACF and contractor staff and to ensure that work areas are cleared of mines and UXOs. When a UXO or mines is found. ACF will report it and request it be cleared. If it is in an area where a work is being implemented work will be stopped until the danger is removed. 4) ACF will integrate the recommendations developed on “conflict sensitivity approach into Project Cycle Management”. ACF conflict sensitivity lenses used in the country include meeting with community leaders and local officials to discuss and coordinate projects. ACF will have well-designed eligibility criteria and targeting based on context, community inputs, evidence and objectives of transfers; may need to modify targeting to ensure inclusion of different groups, host community, women, etc. ACF clearly explains its rationale on operational strategy to the community, local leaders and government authorities. ACF establishes and explains a Complaint and Feedback mechanism for the beneficiaries. Post distribution monitoring to include questions on social relations and “Do No Harm”. ACF ensures transparency on criteria and clear implementation guidelines. 5) ACF is entitled to a Data protection Policy, which is included in the implementation. For example, contract with service providers include provisions in line with data protection policy.

How is your project accountable to affected population

For this project, ACF will have feedbacks and complaints mechanism system for each sector and activities, part of the MEAL plan. In addition to establishing a formal feedback and complaints mechanisms, the collection of feedback and complaint will also be incorporated into regular monitoring activities during implementation. For instance, routine on-site monitoring conducted by project and MEAL team will be an opportunity to ask beneficiaries questions to solicit feedback.Direct interview, formal surveys (such as post distribution monitoring, satisfaction survey) and informal ones (fields visit) will also allow the ACF team to get feedbacks from the population we aim to help and evaluate not only their satisfaction regarding to ACF services but also relevancy and quality of our actions. Feedback obtained through these mechanisms will be included in analysis and, when possible, will be used to inform programmatic decision-making and adjust the intervention. Analyzed feedback will be reported to relevant stakeholders and managers and lessons learned will feed into decision making, readjustment of project plans when possible, and as well as, future project planning.During the implementation, ACF will also directly involve its beneficiaries in the decisions related to them: - Households and individuals will give their informed consent to benefit from the project’s activities, including ACF’s direct services and referrals to other organizations. - ACF’s beneficiaries will always have the right to refuse the offered services. - ACF’s beneficiaries will have opportunities to provide feedback on the services, in particular through MEAL survey ACF aims at building the capacity of the community. Therefore, ACF will also involve community through the identification of areas and certain types of activities. Moreover, ACF will work in a close collaboration with the different authorities concerned which will be also a mean to ensure the sustainability of the project. In this regard, liaison with relevant governmental departments for field assessments (joint where possible) and written authorizations will be ensured.When conducting formal and informal interviews with beneficiaries, ethical consideration such as confidentiality is always an important consideration. Therefore, ACF teams will constantly reassuring respondents who are consulted or who voluntarily gave feedback, that the information they are giving will remain confidential and will be kept within ACF. The consent of the beneficiaries will be asked prior to each interview; none beneficiaries will be forced to answer the questions. In case of any critical or sensitive protection issues that may be raised, ACF team will report the sensitive case to the identified senior management team. According to ACF code of conduct, the policy on anti bribery corruption and abuse of power and the child protection policy, the organization is committed to zero tolerance policy of sexual abuse, bribery and corruption, and abuse of power and in taking immediate actions using professionalism and transparency principle. The Code of Conduct and the policy provide for the protection from sexual exploitation and abuse – to our beneficiaries and staff – as well as discrimination, sexual harassment, and sexual abuse perpetrated against female and male staff. ACF is committed to sensitize beneficiaries, and other stakeholders on preventing sexual abuse exploitation, providing sensitization messages to the company and training to all beneficiaries. Training for staff on the prevention of sexual abuse and exploitation and child safeguarding, including on referral pathways to protection and psychological services. For any Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) allegations, ACF will therefore carry out an impartial investigation manage by an impartial investigation committee, though maintaining confidentiality for survivor, in order to base any management decision making on.
