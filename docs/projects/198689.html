Project Summary

The objective of the project is to contribute to reducing the impact of conflict-related food insecurity and improving the protection of the most vulnerable groups of the affected population. Food security will be supported through a mix of cash transfers and in-kind assistance to 1436 households, depending on the local context, sector-specific requirements, and preferences of the affected population and 280 households would be supported with vouchers for seeds and agricultural tools to improve access to/use of land for agricultural self-sufficiency during the lean season, accompanied by training in good agricultural practices. These interventions are complemented by screening 2500 households for malnutrition, treating cases of moderate and acute malnutrition, and training on the use of nutrient-rich foods. In addition, the program will address the protection of those affected by GBV, child abuse, and trauma by identifying and treating up to 300 cases or referring them to specialized services. The project will strengthen community-based Protection Monitoring Systems by training local Protection Monitors and community-based Protection Panels. Accompanying this, 300 victims and survivors of GBV or people at high corresponding risk will be empowered with cash transfers to build income-generating activities. Awareness-raising activities on GBV, child protection, and mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) will also be carried out, targeting the entirety of the project communities and local key actors in the project regions. In addition, the schooling of __ IDP children will be supported through the payment of school fees. Finally, legal assistance in issuing documents will be provided to __ individuals. The project will also strengthen the engagement of local actors in humanitarian coordination processes as well as in the relationship with local civil society actors and contribute to improving the environmental performance of humanitarian assistance.

1 - Needs

According to the latest Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO), food security and protection are among the top three sectoral needs of the population in Northeast Nigeria. Of the estimated 8.7 million people in need in Nigeria, 5.1 million require food security assistance and 4.1 million require protection assistance. Borno, Adamawa, and Yobe (BAY) states in northeastern Nigeria remain the epicenter of the crisis, with Borno state being the hardest hit. Acute hunger affects 4.4 million people. A number of Local Government Areas (LGAs) in Borno State were already in IPC Phase 3 as of December 2021 (e.g., Bama, Gwoza, Magumeri) and several LGAs in Borno State are expected to fall into Phase 3 and Phase 4 as of June 2022 (e.g., Askira Uba, Kaga) . Combined with projected reduced funding for the World Food Programme (WFP) and other actors providing food assistance in the Northeast, and the impact of the Ukraine war on food security, this is likely to result in significant supply gaps, particularly in hard-to-reach areas outside urban centers. Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) among children aged 6-59 months remains a concern. In 18 LGAs in BAY states, over one million children suffer from Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) and Moderate Acute Malnutrition (MAM). One in four affected children is under the age of 5. The latest report from the Food Insecurity System for Inaccessible and Hard-to-Reach Areas found a global acute malnutrition (GAM) rate of 28%, based on assessments of new arrivals from these areas. This is already very close to the 30% GAM rate, which is an indicator of famine-like conditions. Humanitarian assistance to secure agricultural subsistence also remains a problem in Nigeria, with few LGAs receiving assistance outside of Maiduguri and Jere. Post-distribution monitoring reports from our previous AA-funded project, as well as a project evaluation, indicate that where cash transfers for food are provided and agricultural land is accessible, beneficiaries use up to 15% of their cash transfers for subsistence investments. In Nigeria, 4.1 million people are expected to require professional protection services in the coming year. Movement of the population remains restricted, and people of all genders and demographics are at risk from both non-state armed groups (NSAGs) and government forces. Even before the crisis, women and young people were marginalized by cultural norms, and this has worsened over the past decade. Women and girls are disproportionately affected by violent incidents in northeastern Nigeria - accounting for 60% of all recorded victims. Women and children are not only affected by food insecurity, but also frequently become victims of abuse and must resort to negative coping mechanisms such as survival sex, child marriage, or joining armed groups. Although cases of gender-based violence remain largely unreported for fear of stigmatization and retaliation against survivors, gender-based violence is pervasive in project areas. Of the 311 human rights violations recorded by Caritas Nigeria and UNHCR from January to June 2020, 54 cases were due to gender-based violence. While UN OCHA has noted the importance of providing psychosocial support to affected populations to mitigate harm and promote recovery, the Nigerian Protection Sector has noted that such services are almost entirely absent in the Northeast.

2 - Activities

Outcome 1: Food securityWe are taking an integrated approach to food security with a focus on cash transfers and food distribution (activities 1.1. and 1.2.) to improve food security for different status groups in areas classified as IPC 3 or worse during the shortage period (usually a period between May and September). Cash and in-kind contributions are selected based on sector recommendations and the preferences of the local population. The scope and frequency of the selected modality are based on local minimum expenditure baskets and calculations of the income and food security status of the target population. Both the size and frequency of transfers are determined based on the annual needs assessments to be conducted. Cash and in-kind assistance for acute food security will be complemented by cash transfers to enable IDPs, returnees, and vulnerable host community members to access land for subsistence farming (Activity 1.3.). This activity supports food security beyond the scarcity season. The cash and in-kind distribution and subsistence assistance target two distinct groups. The cash subsistence component is supported by training on good agricultural practices that ensure proper use of the land and is linked to the goal of improving the environmental performance of the intervention (Outcome 4). Finally, an integrated nutrition component (Activity 1.4.) supports the achievement of food security for some of the most vulnerable populations (young children and pregnant women). The project will screen individuals, categorize them into malnutrition categories, and treat them according to severity, either through supplemental feeding or referral to outpatient treatment centers. In parallel, preventive education activities are conducted to build capacity for healthy eating with local foods. The target group for this intervention overlaps with the target groups of activities 1.1. to 1.3. Outcome 2: ProtectionWe aim for a community-centered and comprehensive protection approach that uses community structures for protection monitoring, awareness raising, capacity development, and professional case management. Protection activities will target different action areas (GBV, child protection, trauma) and populations (women, children, people affected by and at risk of GBV). The protection component will take a phased approach, using multiple levels of staff to ensure that barriers to entry to report cases are minimal. Local Protection Monitors and Officers will be trained (Activity 2.1.) to conduct protection monitoring and awareness-raising activities in communities. These activities will be conducted throughout the intervention period. The establishment of community-based protection structures (Activity 2.2.) will support community ownership and ensure the sustainability of protection monitoring and referral functions beyond the end of the project. Together with the Protection Monitors and Officers, these community structures will also serve as focal points for all other protection activities. Local advocacy activities with traditional and government leaders, as well as other key stakeholders such as vigilante groups or government forces (Activity 2.7.) will further strengthen community ownership of the protection component. The protection team will ensure professional case management for GBV, child protection, and trauma cases (Activity 2.4), with both material and psychosocial support available. A number of these identified cases, as well as others at risk of GBV, will receive cash grants to support income-generating activities (Activity 2.5.). These cash grants are provided in consultation with communities. In addition, school-age children will be supported through direct payment of school fees or other financial support to local schools (Activity 2.4.) to ensure their participation in educational institutions. Finally, legal assistance is provided to undocumented individuals of various status groups (Activity 2.7.).

8.6 - Please describe briefly the modalities your project will put in place to ensure protection mainstreaming (particularly referring to the above questions).

Beneficiaries are actively involved in all phases of planning and implementation of project activities. This includes training the affected population on the project's objectives and approaches, and establishing forums for regular and equal consultation with all community members. In our experience, appropriate targeting requires full and participatory mapping of communities to identify relevant sub-groups and assess their relative vulnerability. This is to avoid exclusion bias based on community members not belonging to a particular tribe, religion, or other social group. Community Project Implementation Committees (CPICs) will be established as part of this process to ensure that activities are participatory, inclusive, community-driven, effective, sustainable, and do not harm target households or the community as a whole. CPICs will be formed in each site with representatives of beneficiaries and chiefs (governmental or traditional), taking into account gender balance and vulnerability criteria. They will consist of 50% men and 50% women, and will also include older people and people with disabilities. Traditional community leaders play an important role throughout the Lake Chad region and many beneficiaries prefer to provide feedback through traditional channels. In addition, an independent feedback and grievance mechanism (FCM) will be established to allow anonymous feedback on interventions through various channels (suggestion boxes, toll-free lines, email, text messages, face-to-face). Community context, network coverage, and literacy levels are taken into account when designing the FCM. All community-based groups (such as community committees established under Outcome 2) will ensure gender and age diversity and include people with disabilities and representatives of particularly vulnerable groups. Where available, existing community-based organizations will be directly involved in these activities. In particular, protection activities will be closely coordinated with community-based monitoring and referral structures to ensure that the protection team gains acceptance and has consistent access to vulnerable people (see also response to previous questions). In cases where the implementation team faces decisions, such as beneficiary selection or programmatic changes, the community is involved in the decision-making process through the CPIC.
