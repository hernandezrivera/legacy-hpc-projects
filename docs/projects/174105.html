Project Summary

The decade long violent conflict in northeastern Nigeria committed by Boko Haram terrorists has led to a drastic surge in conflict-related sexual violence (CRSV) and other related sexual offences against women and girls. The Boko Haram armed group, as well as state actors in northeastern states including Adamawa, Borno and Yobe (BAY) have targeted women and girls as the main victims of rape, abduction, forced marriage and sexual abuse during their tactics to cause devastation and fear in communities. In their counterterrorism efforts, state and non-state security agents have also been accused for their involvement in sexual violence crimes against the people they are supposed to protect.
The goal of this project is to strengthen the existing network of survivors of CRSV in the Northeast through enhanced leadership and organisational capacities to effectively advocate for victims' rights within transitional justice processes, including reparative justice and to enable them to act as valued key partners for consultation, policy and programme development relating to all transitional justice matters.
The project will be owned by the survivor networks, who are extremely motivated to break the cycle of silence. GRA will coordinate survivor-led non-judicial accountability actions to recover truth about CRSV against women, children and young girls in North-eastern Nigeria, as well as facilitate access to pro-bono judicial and non-judicial support services.
The overall goal of the project is to end the culture of silence, impunity and the continuation of abuses by strengthening survivors’ capacity to advocate their rights and mobilise support in accountability and empowerment services.
In July 2019, The Dr. Denis Mukwege Foundation partnered with Grassroots Researchers Association (GRA) in building a network of CRSV survivors in Adamawa, Borno and Yobe under the ‘Building Survivors Network in Nigeria (BSN) project.GRA will leverage on its partnership with the Dr. Denis Mukwege Foundation for support on capacity building and strengthening of the local networks. Our partnership relationship with Human Right Watch (HRW) in Nigeria will also benefit the project through advocacy and partnership building skills.
This project is unique given the fact that there is lack of programme mainstreaming for survivors of conflict-related sexual violence (CRSV) in Nigeria. More importantly, until now, survivors are excluded from programming decisions and on discussions around policies that affect them. This project is different from others in the truly survivor-led approach it seeks to take, by providing survivors with the necessary tools to advocate for their rights.

1 - Needs

Since 2009, Nigeria has been facing coordinated attacks by Boko Haram terrorists operating majorly in the Northeastern states of Adamawa, Borno and Yobe. The conflict led to a rise in human rights violations committed majorly by Boko Haram and armed security agents in their effort to contain the conflict. While in captivity, survivors report being subjected to physical and psychological abuse, forced labour, forced marriage, forced participation in military operations, and sexual abuse, including rape and sexual slavery. Boko Haram also used women and girls to lure security forces into ambushes, force payment of ransoms, and leverage prisoner exchanges amongst other types of sexual crimes, which occurred in the context of possible sexual slavery and sexual exploitation. In its response to Boko Haram attacks, armed state and non-state security agents also commit various sexual crimes including rape and sexual abuses. Despite the severity and magnitude of these crimes, there has been lack of mechanisms to demand accountability, recover truth and provide justice for survivors. CRSV is covered up mostly by the state actors while Boko Haram terrorists continue to perpetrate mass atrocities. Survivors are left with the reality of their violations and abuses for fear of double victimisation amidst lacking transitional justice systems while the perpetrators move on unabatedly to repeat the heinous act elsewhere. According to the Gender-Based Violence Information Management System (GBVIMS) mid-year report in 2019, 100% of conflict-related sexual violence (CRSV) were reported by women and girls. This is in line with global GBV trends, highlighting that women and girls are disproportionately affected by GBV. Of all the incidents reported, 21% were committed against children under 18 years, with adults constituting 79% of the reported incidents. The Centre for Democracy and Development (CDD) reported in 2018 that 66% of conflict-related sexual violence (CRSV) against women in northeast Nigeria involved rape, 58% were sexual slavery, while 58% resulted in unwanted pregnancy. In August 2019, GRA reported that many survivors and their children - some of whom were born out of rape committed during their captivity - live in host communities and IDP camps where they are often ostracized, stigmatized and secluded, and sometimes rejected by their family members. While large-scale human right abuses continue to escalate unabated, the judicial and non-judicial mechanisms to address situations in which these abuses occurred are inadequate. The major attempt at transitional justice in Nigeria was the Human Rights Violation Investigation Commission otherwise known as the Oputa Panel, and the Niger Delta Amnesty Programme. There has been Committees of inquiry and reconciliations with reports that were scarcely implemented mainly due to lack of political will. One example was the Judicial Commission that investigated the December 2015 clash between the Military and the Islamic Movement in Nigeria. In the area of reparative justice, in September 2019, Nigeria passed the Violence against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act which addresses sexual violence, physical violence, psychological violence, harmful traditional practices, and socioeconomic violence. Under the VAPP, victims and survivors of violence are entitled to comprehensive medical, psychological, social, and legal assistance by accredited service providers and government agencies, with their identities protected during court cases. However, until it is adopted by the 36 states in Nigeria, the provisions of the VAPP Act are only applicable to the FCT at the moment.

2 - Activities

This project will make an impact by taking a survivor-led approach to advocacy and achieving the aims of transitional justice needs that have been outlined by survivors. In the work that has already been carried out in 2019, the existing survivor networks in the BAY states have identified reparations and awareness raising through truth finding as key to their advocacy goals. By building the skills and capacities of those who suffered most we believe the impact on future programmes and policies around transitional justice will be more survivor-centered. So far, there is lack of comprehensive Transitional Justice framework in Nigeria. As a result, victims and survivors have little or no recourse to justice. The 2019 GBVIMS report highlighted that referrals to legal and security services were often declined by survivors in 37% of cases. Societal pressure and the stigma associated with rape also reduce the percentage of rapes reported, leading to a cycle of silence and continuation of abuses. Rape cases are often under reported because of the fear of stigma, blame or to protect family reputation as it is considered a shameful act. Late reporting can be as a result of lack of services when the incident occurred e.g. when the survivor was in captivity or in transit. In many cases, rape incidents have been reported when the survivor is already pregnant. By bringing the survivor networks together through the project, and helping them with building their capacities: 1. They will become stronger and agents of change – and can break the silence and stigma they suffered;2. They can influence policies that affect them, namely those around accountability, truth recovery, reparations, institutional reforms and reconciliation.The project activities will be tailored around building survivors capacity and skills in the following areas:1. Advocacy and partnership;2. Reparations; 3. Public speaking; 4. Communications; 5. Leadership; as well as 6. Other trainings to strengthen the network. The project is unique because it is truly survivor-led advocacy, and this helps to improve TJ processes because they will certainly meet the needs of victims. The project activities will include: 1. Scoping workshop: Conduct a scoping visit together with the Mukwege Foundation to discuss and explore key challenges and needs.2. Advocacy Mapping: Conduct a national mapping of the advocacy landscape to best understand how to implement advocacy goals and to assess the scope for survivor-led activism. Potential advocacy stakeholders, relevant local and national policies relating to reparative justice will also be identified. 3. Capacity building training: Conduct capacity building trainings on leadership, TJ and reparations in advocacy for Nigerian survivor activists. 4. Survivor-led Advocacy: Build survivors capacities in strategic advocacy, partnership building, reparations, reconciliation and public speaking etc., to enable them to act as valued key partners for consultation, policy and programme development relating to all TJ matters.5. Survivors Network Cluster Clubs (SNCC): Form cluster clubs within the existing survivor networks in BAY states that will meet monthly to network and discuss around CRSV. This activity is aimed to serve as a form of psychosocial support to the survivors.6. Truth Recovery Activists (TRA): Form and coordinate TRA groups made up of community-based volunteers who would support in non-judicial truth recovery about CRSV cases in their communities.
