Project Summary

In the current civil war in the North-West and South-West Regions of Cameroon, Gender-Based Violence is rising, but access to specialized services has reduced critically with the shutdown and burning of health facilities, and the closure of some social services, such as delegations of the Ministry of Women Empowerment and local NGOs. The humanitarian response has severe access limitations due to intense scrutiny by both parties of the conflict, and the rural areas have very few identified actors that can support survivors of violence.
There are, however, non-specialist, non-conventional actors that could be empowered. Health practitioners who are displaced and operate in the forest communities, faith leaders and traditional leaders who still operate in the hard-to-reach areas, small health facilities that defy the odds and operate in the middle of the conflict, Community Health Workers and women leaders who provide the only counsel to survivors.
This project will strengthen non-specialist grassroots actors of hard-to-reach areas on GBV response, to extend effective service coverage in the two Anglophone areas of Cameroon.
This will be done through 1) extensive stakeholder mapping in 35 rural sub-divisions of the two Anglophone regions 2) Training on GBV SOPs for non-specialized GBV responders (100 non-medical and 100 medical) 3) Direct case-management and health services provided by rural GBV stakeholders with supportive supervision from the project team. 4) community mobilization to create awareness and tackle gender inequalities in communities.
The project comes from the realization that specialized services are lacking in rural conflict-affected areas, and it targets 35 of the 49 remote Sub-Divisions of the South-West (22) and North-West Region (13).
Activities:
1) 2 months of mapping through 35 Sub-Divisions through community analysis and key informant interviews.
2) Drawing of the local referral pathway for all target communities.
3) Selection of Community Leaders and 5 days training on GBV guidelines, local referral pathway, Psychosocial first aid, and community facilitation.
4) Selection of Health Practitioners and 5 days training on Clinical Management of Rape, GBV guidelines
5) 3 months’ massive awareness campaign in remote areas targeting 60,000 persons, including men, women, boys, and girls.
6) At least 100 community facilitation sessions held.
7) At least 6,000 women and girls individually will be counselled by grassroots volunteers, at least 20% of them will be under 25 years of age.
8) Provision of referrals and basic care to at least 1,000 survivors of violence.
9) Monitoring and Evaluation
10) Reporting and Coordination with GBV sub-cluster.
Target groups: Adult women, widows, young women, adolescents and children, LGBT and male survivors, grassroots service providers.
Young women and adolescents have seen the discontinuation of their education, and are exposed to sexual relationships, early marriages, and early pregnancies. The specific needs of these young girls that saw their childhood suspended will need to be taken into account when picking grassroots stakeholders. Actors that are orientated towards young women and each target group will be selected.
Male pastors, medical personnel and traditional leaders, have had a critical role in this process in the past. However, they have interiorized inequalities and often carry out pernicious activities with good intentions. These include public reconciliation and confrontation, the re-channeling of legal grievances through traditional practices, and stigmatization.
Satisfaction surveys will be done with beneficiaries and suggestion boxes placed in key community spaces. Plenaries with the communities at the start and throughout the project to receive feedback about what works and what not will be held. Major age and gender groups are received and represented in these sessions.

Besoins / Needs

The Anglophone crisis started in 2016 and became armed conflict in early 2018. According to the Center for Human Rights and Democracy in Africa, 206 villages (99 in the South-West and 107 in the North-West) have been partially or completely burnt down. According to The International Office for Migration, more than 480,000 persons are currently internally displaced due to the Anglophone crisis. Cameroon also has structural gender inequalities. The country ranks 151/181 in the United Nations Development Programme Gender Inequality Index and in 2011 theDemographic Health Survey revealed that 63% of women aged 15 to 49 had been subject to physical or sexual violence.The current crisis has made these inequalities more acute. The International Rescue Committee Rapid GBV assessment in the South-West, states that 85% of female participants reported having experienced GBV and 80% of respondents confirmed unavailability of appropriate services for survivors. In April and May’s 2019 UN OCHA’s situation Reports on the South-West and North-West crisis, 715 and 946protection incidents were documented each month, 19 of them cases of rape. In July alone, OCHA reported 74 cases of rape of which only 13 were able to obtain basic health care services, due to the absence of health services in their localities. The GBV Sub-Cluster’s published referral pathway, updated in June (18/06/2019) shows that only Buea, out of 28 Sub-Divisions in the South-West, hasspecialized services in 5 key sectors (psycho-social support, health care, safety, legal assistance, and economic empowerment), 68% of Sub-Divisions have no identified GBV specialized service at all. The context exposed above puts women and girls in conflict-affected areas in a deteriorating situation. While Gender-Based Violence is on the rise, access to Gender-Based Violence and Sexual Reproductive Health services is more limited than ever. In the search for safety, or forced by the conflict, women have found themselves in farms and forests connected by non-motorable roads, where they have settled in communities without any basic service, increasing their isolation and vulnerability. Living with constant fear of military raids, and under the control of Non-State Armed Groups, women and girls find themselves vulnerable to numerous forms of gender-based violence and most especially physical and sexual violence. Perpetrators are harder to prosecute, and the impact of the act harder to mitigate. Today, a survivor of sexual violence in the North-West and South-West is significantly more likely to not seek or get access to clinical management of rape within 72 hours, than to obtain it, talk less of legal retribution or empowerment services.In the above-exposed environment, with limited access, only local stakeholders can provide comfort and response to the needs of survivors. As of now, most organizations cannot access the remote areas, and even those organizations that are accessing them do not have full safe access, but sporadic and under certain conditions. They cannot offer comprehensive proximity services, but only offer these at a safe distance, a distance that is safe for the organization but unsafe for the survivor. It is only local community based organizations, health practitioners, and leaders that can bring meaningful access to health services, counseling and referrals for survivors of violence.Non-specialized service providers in hard-to-reach areas, however dedicated, also lack critical skills and could be harming survivors of violence, or providing services oflimited effectiveness. To provide meaningful access and a survivor-centered approach in the target areas, the technical gaps of front-line service providers and activists need to be covered. This project is a direct realization from interacting with GBV survivors in the field. In rural Ndian, Reach Out's volunteers have received disclosure from more than 45 survivors, none with access to specialized GBV services.
