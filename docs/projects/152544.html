Project Summary

This project will be implemented in Bor, Twic East, Duk, Pibor and Uror counties in Jonglei state. The 2019 project includes different interventions including support to farming, livestock and fisheries producer groups with a range of livelihoods interventions including but not limited to training, inputs like vegetable kits, staple crops’ kits and tools. The project will provide 9,421 MT of target unconditional food and conditional food assistance. The project will also establish and strengthen community structures like the disaster risk reduction committees to be able to plan and respond to disasters. They will then implement various small projects of their choice through food for assets.

Needs

In 2018, the rainfall in Jonglei state started normally in early May and planting was on time. In Bor however, the rains were above average which resulted in floods from mid-May up to mid-June (South Sudan Crop Watch, July 2018). It was followed by a prolonged dry spell for about six weeks affecting most of the crops including sorghum. Because of those two factors, most farmers had to re-plant their fields once or twice this season. Just like in 2017, the Fall Armyworm (FAW) again attacked crops especially maize and sorghum with varying levels of damage ranging from mild to serious. Other non-migratory pests like cut worms, stalk borers, birds which attack cereals and blight on vegetables were observed. These factors will reduce crop yields. The IPC findings for the 2018 peak and lean period for Jonglei state put the number of people facing severe food insecurity (crisis, emergency and catastrophe) at 1,090,000 which is 62.5% of total population (IPC, September 2019). Of the total Jonglei population in need, only 20% had been reached as of end of August 2018 (IPC Situational Overview, August 2018). Because of the on-going harvest that will continue until December, the food security situation in Jonglei state is expected to improve and the population under severe food insecurity will drop to 54.1% but because of limited food stocks, it will again increase to 65.2% between January and March 2019 (IPC, September 2019). Jonglei will be the most food insecure state after Unity (69.1%). Because of the humanitarian response over the last seven years (2012 – 2018), the proportion of the population under phases 2 (stressed) and 4 (emergency) has stabilized. However, the same period has seen an alarming increase of people in phase 3 (crisis). It is important to note that within the last two years, the proportion of the population under phase 4 (emergency) has doubled and it is important that more efforts are made to prevent more people in phase 2 and 3 from becoming more food insecure. The key drivers of food insecurity during the peak and lean period have been: low/ depleted food stocks, disrupted markets, high food prices, lack of basic services and animal diseases. From October to December, the main factors leading to food insecurity will continue to be insecurity (political and inter-communal), economic crisis, high food prices and lack of services. Moving into 2019, the same factors as those in the October – December period will continue to be experienced. However, in addition, harvested food stocks will begin to get depleted, cattle will migrate away from homesteads to the cattle camps where they can get pastures and water, and availability of wild foods will decrease. In respect of all the above factors, it will be important to protect people’s livelihoods by promoting interventions that enhance availability and access to food in addition to increasing the resilience towards natural and man-made disasters, many of them likely to continue into 2019. CRS will therefore target 25% (273,186 people) of the population facing severe food insecurity. They will benefit from a range of interventions including targeted unconditional food assistance, food for assets (FFA), and livelihood interventions like provision of inputs to farming and fishing communities.

Activities

• Provide targeted and conditional food assistance; and support bomas to implement their community development plans through FFA projects. 146,183 beneficiaries (42,759 men, 52,261 women, 23,023 boys and 28,140 girls) will receive 8,479 MT of food through the FFA arrangement• Support farmer producer groups with basic inputs (through input fairs and direct distribution), training and extension services• Support livestock producer groups with extension and veterinary services• Train and equip fishery-based producer groups on improved technologies and sustainable fisheries.• Support progressive producers to engage in high value commercial farming• Build household (including women and youth) productive assets through credit-based livestock restocking• Engage youth groups and women in commercial honey production, processing and marketing• Support household savings, asset rebuilding and access to markets.• Identify and engage youth in market-based income generating opportunities • Strengthen the capacity of DRR committees to oversee community development• Facilitate the creation of boma level community development plans• Train communities on improved natural resources management practices• Identify and support DRR projects which target youth

How does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

The program uses the participatory disaster risk assessment (PDRA) mechanism and undertakes planning and implementation and monitoring of assistance through community managed disaster risk reduction (CMDRR) committees formed by the program. The program staff address and explain to beneficiaries their food assistance entitlement for FFA activities.

Feedback /complaint mechanisms?

Yes

Briefly describe the mechanisms

The project conducts post-distribution and end-use monitoring interviews and implements accountability and feedback mechanisms in food distributions and nutrition activities to assess beneficiary satisfaction. These activities help the program assess its efficiency and improve programming. The project uses pre-distribution addresses and project implementation teams to provide feedback on issues raised by the communities. The program uses a complaints log sheet to track those issues. The MEAL Team analyzes issues and generates a report of the analysis monthly. The MEAL Team then monitors the status of the issues through these reports ensuring that complaints are responded to within the same month. The project conducts beneficiary-based biannual surveys with program beneficiaries across different interventions in the implementation area. These surveys assess beneficiary access to program services, track progress towards achieving outcome and impact targets, highlight areas for improvement, and track progress towards achieving resilience or recovery by program participants.
