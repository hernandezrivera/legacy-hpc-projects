Project Summary

Over eight years of crisis have had a devastating impact on Syria’s economy and social fabric. This has resulted in an economic environment characterised by high basic commodity prices, shortage in life necessities, low wages and high rates of unemployment. Syria's human development indicators have regressed by almost four decades. According to the UN estimates, around 11.7 million people are in need of humanitarian assistance in Syria, including 4.9 million children. It is estimated that over 80 per cent of the population live below the poverty line. As highlighted in the World Bank (2017), the effects of conflict on human lives and demographic displacement have been most catastrophic with a more persistent effect on the long-term recovery of the country. According to the International Monetary Fund working paper of June 2016, rebuilding Syria’s human and social capital will be a far greater and lasting challenge than reconstructing the physical infrastructure.
Since the onset of the crisis, the significant downscaling in the government social welfare programmes – as a result of fiscal pressures – has further led to a lack of support for many vulnerable children and families. People with disabilities are among the most vulnerable sections of the population, both socially and economically. Global evidence shows that they are more likely to be poor than others, and have more difficulties in accessing services – especially when services are already depleted. In a context where poverty is widespread, families with children with severe disabilities have even less opportunities to provide for their livelihoods – mainly as a result of the full-time care requirements and disruption of social care services.
Therefore, this programme focuses on children with severe disabilities.

Needs

Over eight years of crisis have had a devastating impact on Syria’s economy and social fabric. As highlighted in the World Bank report (2017), the effects of conflict on human lives and demographic displacement have been most catastrophic with a more persistent effect on the long-term recovery of the country. According to the International Monetary Fund working paper of June 2016, rebuilding Syria’s human and social capital will be a far greater and lasting challenge than reconstructing the physical infrastructure.The decline in the living standards of Syrian people is evident in the drastic rise in poverty levels. Overall poverty has risen from 34 per cent in 2007 to 85 per cent in 2015. The World Bank (2017) estimates that today six in 10 Syrians live in extreme poverty. Regional disparities that had been significant before the crisis, are believed to have grown even further. Poverty has historically been higher in rural areas, with the rural north-east of the country having registered the poverty head count ratio of 15 per cent in 2007, and accounted for over half of the poor population of the country. Today, poverty rate continues to be highest in governorates that have been most affected by the conflict and that were historically the poorest in the country. According to the UN, in 2017 an estimated 90 per cent of households spent more than half of their income on food, with around 50 per cent reducing the number of meals per day, and 30 per cent cutting back on food consumption of adults in order to feed their children. The unemployment rate has also increased from 8.6 per cent in 2010 to a record high level of 53 per cent in 2015, with youth unemployment reaching catastrophic rate of 78 per cent. Crisis has taken a toll on children; Syria had made significant strides in its key child wellbeing indicators since 1990s. However, the crisis led to a reversal, eroding the country’s human development achievements of several decades. Under economic duress, households adopt different coping responses that are magnified by the multiplicity of shocks, and are often found to be disproportionately affecting women and children. Selling household assets, borrowing, reducing food consumption and relying on child labour are among the most common negative coping strategies to compensate for the lack of basic necessities among Syrian families.People with disabilities are among the most vulnerable sections of the population, both socially and economically. Global evidence shows that they are more likely to be poor than others, and have more difficulties in accessing services – especially when services are already depleted. In a context where poverty is widespread, families with children with severe disabilities have even less opportunities to provide for their livelihoods – mainly as a result of the full-time care requirements and disruption of social care services.

Implementation of activities

Social protection interventions can be key to enhance the resilience of the most vulnerable families in the face of adversities of crisis and displacement in Syria, to strengthen the continuum of humanitarian and early recovery interventions, and to preserve the social protection space in the country. Building on the pre-crisis national cash transfer programme for people with disabilities, in 2016, UNICEF launched an innovative integrated social protection scheme that combines cash transfer to families of children with severe disabilities with professional case management services. Therefore, the programme seeks to address both economic and social vulnerabilities of children with disabilities and their families. While the cash transfer responds to the “demand side” barriers, in terms of financial barriers, to access to services, the key role of the case managers is to ensure that certain “supply side” barriers, such as unfavourable attitudes or reluctance of schools to enrol children with severe disabilities, are being addressed. While, addressing all the shortcomings with regard to service availability and service quality is beyond the scope of this programme, case managers play a critical role in ensuring that children with severe disabilities are integrated in the available services. In addition to public sector services, the case managers also have a role in maximising the enjoyment of children, boys and girls, with disabilities and their families of other types of assistance available through other national and international relief organisations. This includes devising individual action plans for girls and boys in the programme to address their specific needs and circumstances. The services of the case managers also includes facilitating the referrals of care givers to livelihood support initiatives, such as vocational and entrepreneurship training or microfinance services, available at the local level. Therefore, case management is an integral component of this programme that seeks to empower the family to better respond to the needs of their children with disabilities beyond the life of this cash transfer intervention. All case managers in the programme receive professional training based on a comprehensive package that has been introduced by UNICEF and endorsed by the national government. UNICEF uses services of a third party financial institution for the distribution of the cash. The financial institution also verifies the identity of the care givers based on the verified list shared by UNICEF in advance of every round of payment before disbursing the cash. UNICEF has set up a robust monitoring mechanism, which includes regular and on-site monitoring of the programme through the case managers and the implementing partner, as well as regular post-distribution monitoring surveys. Post distribution monitoring (PDM) is an integral component of UNICEF’s monitoring framework. UNICEF uses PDMs systematically to assess the challenges the beneficiaries face during the implementation and the overall effectiveness of the cash transfer, as well as change in beneficiaries’ coping mechanisms as a feedback loop to improve the design and delivery of the programme.

Protection Risk Analysis

The main risk the population involved could be exposed to is social tensions generated either by lack of clarity in eligibility criteria, or harassment towards those receiving the cash transfer. Mitigation measures include: (i) clear communication strategy on eligibility criteria and selection modalities; (ii) strict verification mechanisms (ii) continuous on site monitoring; (iii) complaint mechanisms, and post distribution monitoring schemes.UNICEF’s approach to social protection emphasizes the need to prevent and reduce economic and socialvulnerabilities. The linkages between social protection and child protection are embedded in the design of the cash transfer scheme through the case management component.
