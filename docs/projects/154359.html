Project Summary

The 9 CSO's under LCN’s supervision will provide legal assistance, SGBV services and referrals as well as protection monitoring to Center-South areas of Iraq. The persons that will be affected by these projects include PoCs that have been negatively influenced by ISIL occupation and military operations.
Legal Needs
During the ISIL and terrorist attacks, many people have lost or had their documentation destroyed, life events occurred without being documented, and some PoCs were incarcerated for multitudes of reasons under false accusations and lack of probable cause. These persons need assistance in re-obtaining lost or destroyed documentation, may need representation in court for family of life events, official directorate, such as Jensiya Directorate, as well as may need to be represented for their detention concerns.
The number of returnees is ever increasing within Iraq, and has become a huge problem with the returnees' area of origin is not suitable for inhabitance. LCN plans to assist these returnees with their HLP compensations claims in order to more efficiently facilitate their reclaiming of compensation when the Compensation Committee is capable of reimbursing those whose homes were destroyed.
SGBV and CP Services
Many women and children suffer, and continue to suffer from the particular hardships during the militant and ISIL reign. Some of these women need psycho-social services, or access to other basic needs. LCN shall provide those women with safe spaces, to both the SGBV staff and the survivor, in order to assess what the survivor needs. Once the needs have been assessed, she will be given the services LCN can offer and/or referred to other service providers that can better assist her current situation.
LCN also wants to empower women to reach economic and social independence. LCN will partner with other NGOs, including Prosperity Catalyst, in order to empower women to educate themselves and learn their self-worth.
Protection
LCN also provides members that will go out into the field to find persons that need the services available, as well as check on the accountability of the camp services. These protection team members will be in contact with focal points within the camp that will gather the PoCs that have needs, and the LCN protection team members will ask the PoCs a series of questions in order to assess what kind of needs the PoC has. After the assessment has completed, the protection team members will refer the issues to the appropriate parties.

Needs

Legal Protection: During the military and terrorist attacks, many PoC’s documentation was lost or destroyed, and life events occurred without being documented. The current guess as to how many IDPs remain in Iraq have been calculated to include over 2,890,000 and returnees are now well over 4,075,000 (UNHCR Factsheet, October 2018). Areas which include SAD (1,518,354 IDPs), Ninewa, (584,520 IDPs), Diyala (61,008 IDPs) and Baghdad (73,836 IDPs) need protection assistance. (DTM Baseline – Oct 2018). MCNA assessments state that over 10% of IDP-in-camp HH do not possess their legal documentation, however according to the LCN’s Baseline Assessment, this is a distortion of reality (MCNA Assessments, September 2018). LCN conducted a baseline assessment of IDPs in Salah Al-Din (400 PoCs), Diyala (300 PoCs), Anbar (700 PoCs), Ninewa (100 PoCs), and Baghdad (100 PoCs) to interview IDPs about their successes and challenges when seeking documentation such as civil IDs, National IDs, passports or unified cards, birth certificates, marriage certificates, as well as HLP issues. This assessment found in SAD and Diyala, 100% of the PoCs interviewed had either they themselves or a family member had lost important documentation, and only 23% are now in possession of their needed documentation, which leaves a staggering 67% lacking documentation. In Ninewa, only around 57% of the PoC population possesses the needed documentation in order to secure their own safety from security challenges or to attempt to move back to the AoO (LCN Baseline Assessment – November 2018). Lacking documentation presents security challenges, increased risk of harassment, lowered ability to receive services, as well as enrolling in educational opportunities. Eviction Tracking: Evictions and relocations have been ramped throughout Iraq, particularly in Anbar and Baghdad. Throughout 2018, LCN alone was able to track over 2,248 persons who were forced to return to their AoO, 515 persons were relocated to another camp and over 990 persons willingly returned to their AoO (Eviction Tracking Matrix, UNHCR). The number of returnees is increasing and each return requires inter-agency partners to ensure that there is accountability during screening procedures, attempt to minimize harassment that may take place, insist that buses and trucks are provided for returning and work together to keep each PoC accounted for in case of their return is denied within their AoO. “[I]t is clear that many of the returns taking place are premature and do not meet international standards of safety, dignity, and voluntariness.” (The Long Road Home, Feb 2018). The PoCs returning to their areas of origin is taking longer than expected and will be a focus for 2019 which require emergency case management and information dissemination to enable IDPs to make informed decisions on how to best return to their AoO. HLP: Many of the PoCs desire to move back to their areas of origin, however, due to financial difficulties, tribal issues, or their homes being inhabitable, the PoCs are not able to move home. (LCN Baseline Assessment, November 2018, 1672 respondents). Over 33% of In-Camp-IDPs are not intending to return to their home due to HLP-related factors such as damaged or destroyed houses, lack of documentation, lack of assistance with documentation, or there are other PoCs occupying the home without their consent (MNCA, September 2018). The IDPs are going to need legal assistance to complete the procedures and gather the evidence necessary to file for an HLP claim, awareness sessions to better understand HLP. Unjustified Detention has also become an overwhelming issue throughout Iraq; not only to men but also to women and children. A large and growing concern have been those that were detained underneath Article 4, which allows for the arrest of persons that have name similarity with anyone affiliated with extremist groups. Iraq’s National Security Service (NSS), has acknowledged for the first time that it is detaining individuals for prolonged periods of time, despite not having a clear mandate to do so, some of whom had been held for more than seven months.” (Iraq: Intelligence Agency Admits to Holding Hundreds Despite Previous Denials, July 22, 2018). The nature of detention has made it difficult to capture the number of persons falsely accused of IS’ affiliation. However, there is still a need for legal representation for those who can be found to have been accused of name similarity, issue criminal record letters to released detainees in order to prevent rearrests monitoring official detention centers when allowed access and to assist with family tracing and reunification. Family unification has proven to be a challenging pursuit for juvenile IDPs currently in Baghdad that are displaced from Mosul.

Activities

There will be 4 CSOs under LCN supervision that will be focusing on general protection, and providing legal service provisions. The Justice Center has 32 lawyers that are able to cover almost all of SAD, especially areas such as Balad (11868 IDPs – IOM DTM), Shirqat (4464 IDPs, IOM DTM), and Baiji (5610 IDPs – IOM DTM) and Al-Hawaiga (138,474 Returnees – IOM DTM). Justice Center will provide 32 lawyers, all of whom are able to provide general legal provisions, HLP assistance and detention assistance or those unjustly detained for IS’ affiliation. Justice Center will also be able to cover areas in Ninewa including Al Hamdaniya (56,383 ADP & 147,294 returnees – IOM -DTM), Al Shikhan (55,734 IDPs – IOM -DTM) and Mosul (380,328 IDP & 944,718 returnees). Al-Awag & Al-Erada are other CSOs stationed Diyala and will be able to cover areas such as Ba’quda (25,272 IDPs – IOM-DTM) and Khanaqin (18,804 IDPs – IOM DTM). These two CSOs in Diyala may also coordinate with Justice Center in order to disperse legal challenges that may arise or emergency situations such as forced evictions, etc. LCN and Tammuz are located within Baghdad and are able to provide services to areas such as Abu Ghraib (11,616 IDPs – IOM-DTM) and Karkh (30,312 IDPs - IOM DTM). Each of the NGOs above will hire 1-2 attorneys per NGO and train protection team officers specifically for SGBV issues in order to know how to handle the delicacy of these types of cases. These types of protection officers and lawyers will enable their referral and legal background to be intermixed with SGBV needs. In 2018, LCN has trained over 50 persons to monitor challenges such as SGBV issues, child protection issues, legal cases, and medical cases and will maintain these employees for 2019. The COVs live directly in the camps and function as an inside informer of urgent cases as well as general informer of the overall camp status. The COVs also act as referral mechanisms for the PoCs to service providers and put on awareness workshops for their SGBV and legal rights. COV services are offered in Baghdad (Al-Tank Quarter, Al-Ahil Camp in Abu Ghraib), SAD (Yatherb, Al-Shahama, Al-Karama Camp), Diyala (Khanaqin camps), and Anbar (HTC Camps, and Al-Khalidiah camp (before closing)). These 50 volunteers report back to the Project Manager for general concerns and issues and reports to the SGBV specialist when SGBV concerns arise (using confidentiality protocol as mentioned above.)Protection teams will be in coordination with camp management to: 1. facilitate meetings with the PoCs to assess their needs, 2. communicate with COVs or other reporters that make LCN aware of any security issues within the camp, 3. Be made known of any forced evictions that are taking place, 4. Personally count and track and rejected returnees, 5. Update information into ASSIST in order to best track referral mechanisms. These protection teams make bi-weekly visits to Anbar (Ameriah Al-Falluja camps, Bzebez camps, HTC camps, Kilo 18 camps), Babil (Mowelha 2 compound in Alexandria, Mowelha 3 compound in Alexandria district, Ghariba - Alsawb Alsaghir compound in Alexandria district, Ghariba - Alsawb Alkabeer compound in Alexandria district, Al-Fathel Street 1 compound in Alexandria district, Al-Fathel Street 2 compound in Alexandria district), Baghdad (Al-Shams camp, Al Amal Al manshood Camp, Al Wehdah camp, local Council of Abu Ghraib, Nabi Younis Camp (Al-Nahrawan), Diyala (Ba'quba/Al-Takia Al-Oula-Al-Fa'iziya, Al-Shurta quarter/ Jalawla'/ Khanaqeen, Ba'quba/Al-Siwamra Al-Oula, Al-Turath village/ Jalawla'/ Khanaqeen, Ba'quba/ Buhriz/ Al-Ta'akhi), Karbala (Caravans complex, Al-Safiya 1,2 (Hay Tal Afar ), Al-Haydaria complex, SAD (Al-Shahama camp, Al-Karama camp, Tikrit Garage, Internal Departments Complex, Dream city complex, Al-Qadisiyah complex, Al-Qubaa school), and Wasit (Al-Hawraa compound in Al-Kut district, Zain Al-Qaws compound in Al-Kut district, Hay Al-Jamea’a compound in Al-Kut district, Hay Al-Jehad compound in Al-Kut district, Al-Fallahiya compound in Al-Kut district, Al-Eza Al-Qadema compound in Al-Kut district, Hay Al-Hakeem 3 compound in Al-Kut district).

Mitigation Measures

Denial of access into the camp has occurred on multiple occasions and is out of LCN’s control. However, the LCN staff members inquire as to the reasons the security personal will not allow for NGOs to enter and also make the UNHCR aware of the situation. The likelihood of gaining camp access increases when using a multi-actor approach which creates pressure for the security forces. This has especially proven true if the personnel of security forces are simply seeking bribery or other fraudulent behavior, of which LCN is not a participant. As a grassroots NGO, the majority of the employees of LCN have been raised in the Iraq security environment and are familiar with security check points and intuitive risk mitigation strategies. Intuitive risk assessments allow for them to quickly recognize threats and/or risks that threaten the PoCs safety or self-reliance and how to devise a strategy to offer service and support to that PoC without subjection to these threats. These situations must be handled on a case-by-case analysis, but there are multiple strategies in LCN’s Risk Mitigation SOP. depending on the situation. Broadly, this SOP specifies the importance of recognizing and minimizing as many risks for the PoC so as to remove the PoC from harm. Similar strategies will be implemented as stated above. LCN staff members have strong relationships with the camp managers as well as the COVs within camps in order to know immediately when such situations arise. Immediately upon knowledge of the situation, LCN shall inform UNHCR. LCN shall not attempt to intervene, but will wait for a multi-actor approach of whom can be contacted, who and how can the actors gather information about the situation as well as the well-being of the PoCs. The LCN requires that all partners must submit either weekly or monthly reports about their progress, coupled with the physical copies of the any cases. LCN staff members at the headquarters within Baghdad insert data about each and every case in order to track the information within the M&E system created by LCN as well as attendance sheet to awareness sessions. LCN also has staff members The CSO partner is also kept in close communication with the grant department to ensure they are on track with their allocated budget. If the grant or M&E department find any flaws during a CSO’s monthly report or structure, communication will be made and steps will be taken, depending on the situation and circumstance. UNHCR also conducts quarterly visits and project performance audits on LCN as a whole to look over the successes and challenges of LCN. All of the LCN staff members must read and sign yearly the Code of Conduct which explains the behaviors that are expected and prohibited while employed by LCN. CSO partners are allowed to create their own Code of Conduct, or adopt LCN’s but they must have at least the minimum standards LCN upholds. There are also mechanisms if an employee’s behavior becomes an issue. There are internal complaint mechanisms in place that any LCN or CSO employee can make anonymously towards another employee. All staff members are also made aware of the IGO within UNHCR that allowed for someone to make a complaint if SEA behavior takes place. These complaint mechanisms are also made available to the PoCs as well through awareness sessions on the PSEA hotline that is available, the LCN’s hotline number, the PSEA’s email address, the IGO’s number, the IGO’s website, and the LCN internal complaint form.

How is your project accountable to affected population

PoC feedback is crucial for the success, growth, and accountability of Legal Clinics Network. This feedback can keep the LCN staff accountable for good work product, timely assistance, appropriate PoC-to-Professional relationships, as well as gaining new insights from the people who are living within the camp day after day. There are multiple methods in which the PoC can inform LCN about the successes and failures. Random satisfactions surveys (both for legal services and for the SGBV services), the hotline number, legal and SGBV closure forms, anonymous complaints, and direct communication with the staff members. Legal Case Closure: Before each case is closed, LCN wants to ensure that each client has received the services that were originally sought. Therefore, an LCN staff member will ask each of the beneficiaries if they have received the services they were seeking or not. Documentation may vary depending on which services are being rendered. SGBV and CP: The CP and SGBV Specialists have been trained to ask the survivor or PoC that is receiving services whether they feel they need any other services and are satisfied with these services. There is a voluntary survey that is given to the survivors that are willing to take the survey. Random Satisfaction Surveys: During site visits, the technical team picks a random sample of beneficiaries in order to call and check in on the services rendered. The technical team choses roughly 10% of the beneficiaries that have received services during the month and will call to check the status of their satisfaction. If the PoC is unsatisfied with their services, then the technical team will record this dissatisfaction on the survey. These dissatisfactions will then be recorded either on the site visit report, which is sent once a week, or on the monthly report that is sent to the UNHCR. Hotline Services: When the PoCs are first seeking services from LCN, the LCN staff members make the PoCs aware of the hotline number that is available during work hours. The LCN staff member will explain what the hotline is for and how the PoC can use the hotline. The hotline can be used to seek service, but it can also be used to make suggestions or file complaints. If the PoC is then finding that the LCN staff member is not providing services or the timeliness that was once promised, the PoC can call the hotline and discuss the matter with the Hotline Assistant. The Hotline Assistant will ask the PoC a series of questions in order to gain deeper understanding of the PoCs issue. No matter what the complaint, the complaint will be recorded onto the complaint form. These complaints are then transferred to the appropriate parties and the LCN’s internal investigation procedures are followed. The Hotline Assistant will then collect this information into an excel sheet and this complaint is kept with the LCN records for each month. The Hotline Assistance can then refer the issue the supervisor or the specialist that is either directly managing the case themselves, or is overseeing the LCN staff member that was providing the service to the PoC. Contacting the LCN staff member directly: LCN encourages strong communication between the specialist that is providing aid and the PoC receiving the aid. This can remove a lot of confusion and miscommunications that may arise when a third-party enters into the relationship. When originally meeting with the PoC, the PoC is made aware that they can contact the LCN staff member with any questions or concerns that they have while their case is being processed. This is generally the first step that should be attempted with the PoC with any complaints or concerns of their personal case. Sometimes these instances can be difficult to determine if they ever happened because of the low amount of documentation taken, as well as tracking that may occur. However, this option is always available to the PoC and is not considered an “out-of-line” course of action.
