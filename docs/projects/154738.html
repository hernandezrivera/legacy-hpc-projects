Project Summary

The number of returnees in Sinjar District (56,418 returnees, IOM DTM, 31 October 2018) started increasing in synergy with the rest of the territory and the Ninewa governorate (1,866,594, IOM DTM, 31 October 2018), motivated by a perceived improvement concerning safety and a desire to return to land or property, notwithstanding the acknowledged lack of services and safety concerns due to social tensions (especially concerning members from the Muslim community, due to fear of retaliation from community members or local security actors). However, as of September 2018, Sinjar District ranks the highest in severity of living conditions of return in terms of Livelihoods and basic services as well as social cohesion. (Return Index Findings Round 1, Iraq, IOM, September 2018). The lack of sufficient income opportunities led part of the population to rely on harmful coping mechanisms.
Since 2015, the FRC/IRCS has developed numerous emergency livelihoods projects. The FRC has implemented activities to support IDPs, returnees and host communities to generate temporary income in Zumar and Rabia, with the restoration of community infrastructures, with an increased social cohesion as a secondary goal. Since 2017, the FRC/IRCS implemented a WASH oriented CFW program in Hasansham camp for newly IDPs from Mosul. In Sinjar area, the FRC has been implementing a WaSH/Livelihoods project since June 2016, in order to upgrade the living conditions and reduce the sanitary risk of the population and respond as well to an important need of cash. The deepened knowledge of Sinjar District, experience with emergency livelihoods activities, trained IRCS volunteer network, the rich collaboration with the local authorities, and the acceptance by the communities, are the strength which enable the FRC/IRCS to carry out the proposed action with quality and efficiency.
The proposed action aims to provide immediate access to income to support highly vulnerable returnees in Sinjar District. Through Asset replacement activities, the project will support 500 vulnerable returnees in recovering their lost asset/source of income during the conflict and restart their economic activities therefore improve their self-resilience and sustainable return. The beneficiaries will receive business skill training and individual follow up, which will increase their likelihood of achieving a successful business in the long term, and consequently induce a positive longer term impact on the economy of the whole district

Needs

Following the Iraqi Civil War (2014-2017), vulnerable populations did not escape unscathed, as they have been –amongst others – facing, due to massive displacement, an important lack of income, thus inducing a strong lack of livelihood means, a lack of resilience, a downfall in local economy and access to services and basic commodities. In the current context, as from the end of 2017 corresponding to the end of the war, part of the people who had been originally displaced since the beginning of the war started to return to their area of origin. As a matter of fact, by the end of October 2017, the number of returnees accrued to reach the step of over four million people (4,113,660 returnees, IOM DTM, 31 October 2018), outweighing by far the number of Internally Displaced Persons (1,866,594 IDPs, IOM DTM, 31 October 2018), and for the first time reversing the balance with the evolution of IDPs in the country (since the end of 2017, the number of IDPs has decreased significantly, whilst the number of returnees has kept on increasing rather steeply). More specifically concerning the Sinjar district, as per the Iraqi Security Forces taking over the area, the number of returnees (56,418 returnees, IOM DTM, 31 October 2018), started increasing in synergy with the rest of the territory and the Ninewa governorate (1,866,594, IOM DTM, 31 October 2018), motivated by a perceived improvement concerning safety and a desire to return to land or property, notwithstanding the acknowledged lack of services and safety concerns due to social tensions (especially concerning members from the Muslim community, due to fear of reprisals from community members or local security actors). As a matter of fact, as of September 2018, Sinjar District ranks the highest in severity of living conditions of return in terms of Livelihoods and basic services as well as social cohesion. (Return Index Findings Round 1, Iraq, IOM, September 2018). In Sinjar, the lack of sufficient income opportunities led part of the population to rely on harmful coping mechanisms - such as relying on their children as workforce or be recruited by armed groups, selling the last of their belongings, marrying off their children to get the dowry, etc. – in order to meet their basic needs, however resulting in a negative way on their resilience, and enhancing a greater vulnerability towards hazards. As the situation is therefore still unstable and humanitarian standards still unsatisfactory and needing to be improved, there is urgent call to help the returnees regain a normal course of life, as they are still struggling on a daily basis to meet their basic needs. During most of the returnees’ leave period from their area of origin, homes have been heavily damaged, and general infrastructures and basic services are still not functional (water, sanitation and solid waste management, health, education, etc.). Besides, one of the major consequences is the general impoverishment of the population and the use of decapitalizing survival mechanisms by the population who returns (although they do not have lots of assets left after the occupation of the area by opposition groups). In a district where agriculture (including growing crops and sheep farming), public sector employment and small business ownership have been considered as the most fruitful sectors to the local economy, the activities have now been reduced to nearly nothing, along with 2018 being a poor year for rainfall, the theft of pumps, generators and tractors, and a lack of money to purchase materials necessary in order to restart agricultural livelihoods. Last, but not least, remnants of war, such as explosives, remain a threat in the villages and fields, impeding on the revival of the local. For all these reasons, intervention is strongly required in order for returnees to give sufficient purchasing power to local households to enable the jump-start of livelihoods activities, for example by replacing damaged or stolen agricultural asset. In this context, assistance and protection remain absolutely necessary for the people without any income-generating activities.

Activities

Output 1 The access to sustainable income generating activities through asset recovery support is increased for highly vulnerable returnees and host community in Sinjar DistrictActivity 1.1 Rapid Market AssessmentA rapid market assessment in targeted areas will be conducted in order to identify the economic sectors to be supported. Beneficiary selection criteria will also be elaborated in comparison with existing practices in the area. The criteria will be based on vulnerability level, professional skills and previous business experiences.Activity 1.2 Identification and selection of beneficiariesThe communities will be informed of the project through community meetings. Interested applicants will submit their asset replacement plan together with the application form. After the confirmation of the beneficiary selection, a skills and needs analysis will be carried out to determine the value of asset to be replaced (estimated for now to 1300 USD in average). Individual asset replacement proposals will be reviewed with the beneficiaries. The beneficiary profiling exercise will be conducted with each selected beneficiary to set up the baseline for monitoring the impact of the project.Activity 1.3 Provision of asset replacement grant to returnee households An asset replacement contract with agreed amount and terms will be signed between the grantee and the FRC/IRCS. A supportive monitoring system will be developed, aiming at providing constructive feedbacks and positive reinforcement to the grantees during the asset recovery process. Individual follow-up will also be provided. The payment will be done through Hawala system.Activity 1.4 Provision of business trainings to asset granteesSessions of business skills training will be provided to the asset grantees Furthermore; an individual coaching will be provided to the beneficiaries during the routine visits in order to support them in relaunching their business with recovered assets.Activity 1.5 Impact assessmentAn endline study will be conducted with each asset replacement beneficiary to measure the impact of the proposed action at the individual level. Protection Risk Analysis

The action planning based on the above needs assessment is taking the following risks into consideration: 1- Beneficiaries not able to participate in the activities due to unrest, curfews or other restrictions on their movement inside the neighbourhoods. 2- Access to the most vulnerable communities (IDPs, Returnees, Host Communities) restricted due to prevailing security and/or military circumstances.3- Authorities interfering in ways that put the activities on hold.4- The project is subjected to corruption on a significant scale. 5- Staff insecurity has increased due to political tensions and military operations in target areas.6- The French Red Cross needs to withdraw from Iraq, as a result of increased political tensions and insecure situation.

Mitigation Measures

1- No mitigation measures possible, because this is an exogenous issue that the organization cannot have control upon.2- No mitigation measures possible, because this is an exogenous issue directly linked to the political climate that the organization cannot have control upon.3- Mitigation measures possible: maintain permanent contact with local authorities and provide explanations to all stakeholders at all stages of the proposed and planned action. The role of the IRCS here will be key. 4- Mitigation measures possible: maintain strong internal control procedures and involve all stakeholders and local authorities at each stage of the proposed action. 5- Mitigation measures possible: keep close and continuous monitoring of the context. 6- Mitigation measures possible: make sure that FRC national staff and IRCS volunteers are involved in the proposed action in order to ensure the continuity of actions, in case any unlikely event would happen, forcing withdrawal of expatriates from Iraq.

How is your project accountable to affected population

The FRC/IRCS works for improving transparent communication, feedback and complaints mechanisms which invite people to share concerns regarding the assistance provided.Information provision: To be accountable to the targeted communities and donors, project activity information will be shared with beneficiary communities. Participation: Discussions with beneficiaries will be done during project monitoring and evaluation to make sure that their voices and feedback are heard. Regular meetings with the community leaders and authorities will be organized, and especially with the Mokthars representative of their community.Feedback/Complaint: After the completion of the activities, a post-distribution monitoring will be conducted to collect further feedback from beneficiaries. Once complaints are received, the FRC/IRCS will look for the most appropriate way to respond to the complaints. The principle of accountability will be widely based on the sanitation and hygiene committee. It is a strategic choice to hire the technicians and the hygiene promoters among the targeted population. It should strengthen the acceptance and the confidence from the community. To mitigate the associated risk of arrangement and connections, they will be deeply trained to the humanitarian principles and communication with beneficiaries and the supervisors will come from outside the area. The accountability will be translated by a methodology of proximity and confidence with the targeted population as well as a strong communication of the mandate of Red Cross Red Crescent and the humanitarian principles
