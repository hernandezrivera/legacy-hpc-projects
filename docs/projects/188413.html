Project Summary

Over 10 years into the conflict, Syria remains one of the largest and most complex humanitarian emergencies. Long-term consequences including widespread destruction and the largest number of internally displaced people in the world. In addition to protection threats, the accelerating economic deterioration and impacts of climate change have become additional key drivers of needs, compounding vulnerabilities even further. In 2022, 14.6 million people need humanitarian assistance, an increase of 1.2 million from 2021. In 2021, climate and human-caused shocks affecting natural resources, particularly water, intensified. Erratic rainfall in combination with historically low water levels in the Euphrates River reduced access to water for drinking and domestic use for over five million people and triggered substantial harvest and income losses, decreased hydroelectricity generation, and an increase in water-borne diseases. In the mid to long-term, these developments are expected to have serious and cumulative impacts on health, food insecurity, malnutrition rates, as well as the protection environment.
Long-standing needs of an estimated 6.9 million IDPs remain, particularly for over two million people in 1,760 informal settlements and planned camps, often hosted in inadequate shelters and with limited access to basic services. Overstretched host communities and those who have returned to their – often destroyed – places of origin frequently face inadequate living standards. As the economic situation deteriorates further, the vulnerabilities of people that have not been directly affected by hostilities are also compounded. By population group, it is the vulnerable residents - those that have not been displaced, that show the greatest increase in severity of need - resulting in an increase from 6.4 million to 9.2 million – suggesting that economic deterioration is now a major driver of needs in areas historically less directly affected by hostilities and displacement
The IRC will support 2,130 households in NES experiencing financial shocks and depend mainly on negative coping strategies to meet their basic needs. This will be in line with the Cash Working Group’s strategy to cover IDPs in host communities according to the overall gap in NES. Through a multipurpose cash modality, affected households will be able to meet their basic needs at the end of a one-month transfer period as their situation stabilizes. To address cases where households are unable to meet their basic needs, the number of transfers will range between one, three, or six rounds. This will provide access to households facing extreme levels of vulnerability with a predictable source of multipurpose cash assistance (MPCA), enabling them to develop positive coping mechanisms that support their resilience to future shocks they may encounter.
Beneficiaries will also receive financial literacy training, which includes a variety of taught skills including identifying differences between needs and wants, living costs, and key start-up business skills. Following three rounds of MPCA, the IRC will integrate project participants into its livelihood-based programs. The IRC implements various projects designed to put participants on the path to early recovery, including vocational training, apprenticeship, small-to-medium enterprise support, and financial management training. In addition, beneficiaries are also trained on how to set up village savings and loans (VSLA) to enable communities to set up projects that serve their communities, build social cohesion and promote joint resource management.
In NWS, IRC will provide support for livelihoods through market-driven skills development and self-employment training to beneficiaries, targeting economically vulnerable households and prioritizing women and youth. Services will be provided via cash for work, MSME, VSLA, vocational training, support to farmers, MPCA, and cash for winter.

4 - Needs

Across Syria, households are reverting to negative coping mechanisms more frequently than before. This includes child labor and child marriage and the sale of productive assets – all of which increase protection needs and/or reduce households’ capacity for self-sustenance in the future. Across the country food insecurity has grown, with an estimated 12.8 million severely food insecure in 2021. The depreciation of the Syrian pound (SYP) and related economic downturn have resulted in many more individuals and families requiring essential assistance. People’s access to basic services across Syria continues to decline, hampered by damaged infrastructure, lack of critical supplies, and decreasing purchasing power. One of the most pressing concerns is the lack of technical staff required to deliver and maintain basic health services or to operate potable water supply systems, because of displacement, death or impairment, and lack of technical training. In NWS, 95% of households reported insufficient income was a barrier to meeting basic needs, and an average worker would have to work 73 days to earn enough to cover the SMEB. Over 60% are skipping meals or borrowing money due to lack of food. However, most households have the ability and desire to generate income to cover their own food needs, with 52% of NWS host community members and 49% of IDPs indicating that securing a livelihood is among their top three needs.

5 - Implementation of activities

In NES & NWS, IRC plans to provide a transfer value of 100% of the SMEB based on CWG technical guidance. The average transfer value is set at 100 USD but will vary based on local prices and monthly SMEB calculations. When markets are functioning and cash is an appropriate modality, evidence points towards strong positive impact of cash programming on people’s ability to meet their basic needs as evidenced through increased total household expenditure, household consumption, maintaining productive assets while simultaneously decreasing reliance on negative coping strategies. Cash transfers will be in SYP or USD depending on CWG recommendation, liquidity, and SYP value against USD. In NES, IRC will vet and contract Hawalas to deliver the MPCA to target locations, as hawalas remain the most appropriate mechanism to transfer cash assistance into Syria in the absence of a functioning banking system or formal payment mechanism. Hawalas are the most established and cost-efficient mechanism for transferring funds inside Syria. In NWS, IRC will deliver market-driven skills training and financial literacy, followed up with MPCA and SMEs. IRC will also support cash for work projects to improve community infrastructure while providing employment opportunities for people ages 18-60, including PwD. Support to startups and existing businesses in the community through business training and cash grants will be provided, with a focus on engaging women starting home-based businesses & youth.

10 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

Project staff and volunteers including the cash and livelihoods team, with support from the protection mainstreaming officer, will sensitize on the "Do no harm" principles and PSEA. By working with community leaders, community representatives, and elders, IRC undertakes through sensitization and awareness-raising of stakeholders on the rights and responsibilities of all project parties. Beneficiaries are made aware of their entitlements, freedom of choice, and how support is provided. Stakeholders and project staff are sensitized to exercise and abide by the core humanitarian principles of impartiality, neutrality, humanity, and independence while providing and accessing support. Beneficiary exploitation by local authorities or staff. IRC has conducted PSEA trainings for all staff, including field staff. Confidential and trustworthy reporting mechanisms have been shared with all staff, and both staff and beneficiaries are encouraged to use the IRC’s beneficiary feedback and complaints mechanism. IRC will exercise program integration with the protection sector to provide protection assistance to survivors and ensure referral pathways including during beneficiary selection through referrals, data triangulation and sharing, reporting and follow-up. The project will target the most vulnerable groups.


11 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

IRC will ensure representation of marginalized groups in reviewing selection criteria, and conduct FGDs to increase awareness of IRC Complaint and Feedback Mechanism, with embedded gender-sensitive feedback. During sessions and throughout implementation, beneficiaries will have the platform to provide feedback. IRC will engage authorities and key local stakeholders to explain services and expectations of community leaders and beneficiaries. Beneficiary selection criteria will be shared and agreed upon registration, mitigating the risk of impartiality or inequality during selection.A risk includes loss of access to project areas due to conflict. As mitigation, IRC conducts pre-project security assessments that includes risk of future offensives. IRC will have ‘exit plans’ for all locations and planned measures to implement to ensure safety and duty of care. Another risk is interference by armed groups or local councils, who may attempt to control or influence projects through aid diversion. IRC adheres to humanitarian principles and established Joint Operating Procedures for NWS. IRC’s humanitarian access team will conduct principled negotiation and conflict resolution to continue project plans and meet the identified community needs. Risk of local authorities or armed groups targeting beneficiaries for taxation or exploitation. IRC holds open registration for most activities and does household-level verification of lists, reducing the power others have on applicants.
