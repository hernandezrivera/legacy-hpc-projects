Project Summary

The project aims at supporting the most vulnerable areas in Area C and the H2 area of Hebron city by providing emergency support to schools including of psychosocial activities to support students and schools’ communities. Action Against Hunger (AAH)’s proposed intervention, together with the partner Volontariato Internazionale per lo Sviluppo (VIS), will focus on safe and equitable access to quality education for children in Area C and H2 area of Hebron city in the West Bank. Specifically, the proposed project aims to provide emergency response to the most vulnerable schools in Area C through integrated and targeted activities, aiming to increase the protection of 1,303 students (girls: 652, boys: 544, teachers: 68 females, 39 males). The intervention will also be responsive to; the negative impacts of pandemics on education, particularly of what COVID-19 has had and is continuing to have on education, and on enhancing awareness of students and the school community on the negative impacts of climate change and ways of mitigation through creating knowledge, understanding and values, attitude, skills and abilities among the school community towards a better-quality environment. The response will be implemented by upgrading the educational environment in schools through the rehabilitation of classrooms, schools’ facilities including of infrastructure and play ground areas, and providing key support to enhance healthy educational environments. It will also supply and construct new classrooms in overcrowded schools to facilitate separate spaces for students from different grades. Activities will also target the schools’ staff and parents to improve their capacities in delivering knowledge as well as dealing with risk reduction; risk mitigation (settlers attacks, clashes); and to give the students the needed support in the face of these risks and hazards (evacuation procedures, first aid, etc.). Simultaneously, the project will respond to psychosocial issues faced by students and teachers alike because of the coercive environment they are subjected to. The proposed psychosocial response will be innovative; various educational and recreational tools targeting students, teacher and parents will be adopted to ensure the maximum effect on the targeted students. The project will also contribute to increasing awareness on climate change negative consequences for the school family and enhance mitigation plans and behaviors. The proposed interventions are in line with Education Cluster 2023 Strategic Objective 1 for the occupied Palestinian territory (oPt), Strategic Objective 2. and Strategic Objective 3 and aligns with the 3 Strategic Objectives of the oPt Humanitarian Response Plan.
The project uses a community-based approach, with students (girls and boys) and teachers (women and men) participating in all stages of the project including in the assessment, design and implementation stages. Hence, the students, teachers as well as parents’ needs and ideas will be discussed and analyzed to inform effective technical and psychosocial activities considering gender and age disaggregated data, resulting in adapted assistance. As outlined, the proposed intervention ensures protection mainstreaming as it is guided by protection principles and considers safety and dignity; meaningful access; accountability to affected population as well as participation and empowerment.
The proposed project shall be carried out in close coordination with MoE. AAH has signed a general MoU with MoE, and regularly coordinates with the related departments during all assessments and construction activities. AAH also shares progress reports including achievements and challenges on quarterly basis to ensure all interventions are inline with the ministry strategic plans and prioritized needs.

1 - Needs

The ongoing protracted conflict in the oPt has had a significant impact on all aspects of people’s lives, particularly on the right to access to basic needs. Access to education in the oPt continues to be compromised due to the occupation and recurrent conflicts. Education related violations include attacks on schools, military use of schools, threats of attacks against school staff and students and interferences with access to education. According to the 2023 HNO, 608,556 people need Education in Emergencies humanitarian assistance across the oPt. These include 584,000 (almost 96%) children of school aged children of whom 25,385 are children living with a disability (CWD), who face challenges in accessing quality education in a safe, child-friendly environment. In the West Bank including Jerusalem, checkpoints and the Barrier impede access to education for children living in the “Seam Zone” and in East Jerusalem communities isolated by the Barrier. Based on the Education Cluster, recent years have witnessed an increased number of Israeli attacks on schools located in Area C and Hebron H2. The REACH multi-sectoral needs assessment in 2022 has identified that 19.5% of households reported safety concerns to travel to and to study in the education facilities, the most frequently reported safety issues were traffic/crossing roads (63% in WB), military entry to schools (4.5% in WB), firing tear gas on schools and students (16% in WB) and violence/harassment by settlers travelling to school (13% in WB). The negative consequences of the political context affects all ages and gender groups (elementary & secondary students) either boys or girls and impacts their level of attendance particularly of the young students (aged 5 for boys & girls) and older boys students (16-17 yrs) in line with the MSNA 2022 findings.In a latest assessment conducted by AAH in 2022 in cooperation with the MoSD to assess the socio-economic conditions of the families registered under the MoSD’s system to receive social assistance, it was noticed that the conditions in the H2 area is deteriorating further; 100% live below the poverty line and 98% below the extreme poverty line which has intensified the reliance on negative coping mechanisms including of selling productive assets and children dropping out of schools, 29% of families reported drop outs of school.Schools remain at the front line of facing demolishing orders, 3 incidents of demolition orders for schools were served in 2022 in Hebron (final court order to demolish two classrooms in Shi’b al Butum school and parts of Um Qussa school) and Ramallah (a demolition order for Ein Samiya school). The Palestinian Authority has been unable to significantly intervene in these communities as they are in Area C, i.e. under Israeli occupation. Additionally, all the existing school structures have been implemented under emergency conditions following minimum standards aiming to ensure an access to education and keeping space for further upgrading works in future. Consequently, all the schools have become uncomfortable spaces for students who are already struggling to reach them due to access restraints, particularly during winter. Poor school conditions and access have driven many families to adopt negative coping mechanisms such as withdrawing their children from school or displacing part of the family. AAH regular multi sectorial needs assessment and protection analysis involving communities in Area-C indicated that communities in the E1 zone, Firing Zone 918, Jordan Valley and Road 317clusters are the most affected by Israeli restriction policies, affecting the educational sector in the targeted communities. Girls’ school dropout, access to schools and schools ’unsafe environment were the main concerns for the children in these communities. The intervention is coordinated with different organizations (including Medecins du Monde, UNICEF and NRC) acting in the area to support these students to access and enjoy school spaces. Hygiene services and facilities and the lack of adequate facilities affect young girls especially and creates a sense of discomfort. In addition, the psychosocial and mental challenges caused by the occupation faced by students and staff particularly in Area C leave high pressure and stress on students and schools families which is addressed by the IASC to be one of the main humanitarian response priorities in line with “key interventions and supports for protecting and improving mental health and psychosocial well-being through: 1- Emergency preparedness steps to be taken before emergencies occur; 2- Minimum responses to be implemented during the acute phase of the emergency; and 3- Comprehensive responses to be implemented once the minimum responses have been implemented.”

2 - Activities

Based on its experience in implementing over 60 educational interventions in Area C, AAH has proposed the needed technical solutions for each school in order to create a more healthy, safe, and friendly educational spaces for students and considering ICA construction restrictions in Area C. It will do so by rehabilitating the existing infrastructures to ensure the basic and humanitarian needs that will thereby support students in continuing their education. To complement, VIS, who has extensive experience in conducting psychosocial activities at both community and school level, has conducted different focus group sessions with students, teachers and parents from different Area C communities. Throughout these sessions, VIS psycho-social experts identified the psychosocial problems that targeted groups suffer due to school access and environment; out of which they were able to propose technical solutions and share them with relevant stakeholders (Ministry of Education (MoE) and school headmasters, etc. AAH and VIS have experience in implementing educational activities in coordination with (MoE). For this project, the coordination with MoE will be on two levels- Ministry level: to ensure that all the project activities will be implemented according to the Ministry’s strategies and priorities, and on Directorate’s level: to ensure that the education Directorates in each area have been consulted regarding the assessment, design and even during the implementation of the intervention. Furthermore, the activities will be handed over to the MoE as the service provider of the educational sector. The following activities will ensure meaningful access to education and psychosocial support, prioritizing safety and dignity: 1) Provision of emergency education supplies: targeting schools that suffer overcrowding or merging students from different grades into one classroom and schools that face a deficit in power supplies, affecting the safety, health and educational attainment for students. Under this activity classrooms will be added, and power supply solutions will be developed (including solar systems for isolated communities). 2) Basic emergency rehabilitation to schools: to enhance the students’ learning environment, classrooms and other facilities will be rehabilitated/upgraded, creating a friendlier, more enjoyable, and safer space for children. The upgrading of sub-standard structures in the most vulnerable communities will ensure a more protective educational space and the improvement of facilities in schools. This may include the upgrading of WASH facilities when needed to provide a more comfortable environment for students, especially girls. 3) Provision of school-based psychosocial support to vulnerable school children and staff: the activity will target the most vulnerable schools that suffer from a variety of threats including settler violence, natural hazards, and those with a generally unsafe and unhealthy environment. Detailed psychosocial assessments will be conducted in each school and consequently VIS and psychosocial support teams will identify the appropriate psychosocial intervention in each school. They will use various educational, recreational (events for kids, theater shows, clown shows) and sport events (competition games, football friendly matches…etc) and sessions with students, teachers and parents, including activities focusing on stress management, trauma and class communication. 4) Provision of hygiene kits and conducting hygiene awareness raising sessions and campaigns, to improve student’s hygiene practices and help them facing pandemics possible waves and impacts. This activity will also involve awareness sessions on climate change negative consequences and options on mitigation towards encouraging this culture of knowledge, values, and skills for students, as they are the future human asset, towards more students driven and societal approaches to combat climate change for a better environment at school, home, and in community. This activity will be specifically targeted towards girls who lack access to proper hygiene services in their schools, which forces them at times to miss school when needed or feeling uncomfortable in the classroom.

3 - Indicators

Technical and psychosocial baseline surveys will be conducted by VIS and AAH to identify the target groups’ needs; the survey will facilitate the supervision, monitoring and evaluation of the activities. Teachers and students (girls, boys and persons with disabilities) will be involved in the design, monitoring and evaluation of the activities to ensure the full participation of all groups. Baseline & endline surveys will be conducted to assess and evaluate the satisfaction and impacts of the activities on all groups, including an evaluation of improved access to schools and the students’ psychosocial status. In particular, VIS will measure the level of learning and cognitive development of children using the RSCA (Resilience Scales for Children and Adolescents).. Project activities will be monitored by 1) Working field staff; 2) Community representatives; 3) MoE visits and participation in the activities; 4) Baseline and post-implementation reports; 5) Donors and cluster monitoring visits; 6) Project progress and impact reports. The communities’ representatives and village councils will support AAH and VIS to provide ongoing field monitoring of the rehabilitated schools and advise AAH on the security status to determine optimal timing of field operations. In order to ensure accountability, AAH has a Feedback and Complaint Mechanism (FCM), which allows beneficiaries to provide their comments and complaints on the activities and is designed to increase their protection, in compliance with sensitive data management and adhering to the “Do No Harm” principle. The knowledge of this system has been promoted through several field assessments and monitoring visits. In addition, a service phone number will be distributed to the population in the proposed target communities enabling them to report feedback or complaints relating to project implementation by AAH and its partners. AAH will process and treat all information collected through an ad-hoc committee composed of project staff to understand the situation, identify appropriate solutions and include technical adjustment or modifications if needed. 9.1c - How will feedback be used?

Beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries alike will be able to reach out to the project with complaints, suggestions, and any other feedback through the mechanism managed by AAH MEAL Unit. AAH actively promote the existence of its feedback mechanisms through information sessions and information material (e.g., flyer) handed over during projects’ activities (e.g. assessments, distribution, training session etc.). The information collected through complaints and feedback mechanism or directly in the field will be regularly fed into our programming to ensure no harm is done and to guarantee continuous improvements of assistance delivered respecting internal humanitarian standards, including informing the budget. Beneficiary feedback will be analysed and presented in a systematic way, including to project partners that we work with. Learnings following this feedback can often be used across projects and we make sure this is shared among relevant stakeholders so that complaints are taken seriously and can be anonymous where necessary, to make beneficiaries feel safe in expressing their opinions. The AAH MEAL unit will be in continuous contact with the Program and Coordination teams to analyse the data, address concerns, respond to complaints and work with the project team to make adjustments to the project. For all different groups particularly for gender, age, disability, and those who prefer to stay anonymous, AAH will accommodate their preferences and serve this purpose through different mechanisms (home visits or private spaces can be provided), to investigate and discuss a complaint received in line with the set internal operating procedures. In line with AAH Standard Operating Procedures, AAH considers all the data received through its FCM as part of the project core data and of utmost importance in terms of inclusion of beneficiaries. This includes complaints and feedback related to the Sexual Exploitation and Abuse which will be addressed separately.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

Through the whole project cycle AAH ensures protection mainstreaming and Do No Harm. AAH considers safety and dignity, meaningful access, accountability as well as participation and empowerment of paramount importance. AAH has mechanisms in place to prevent and respond to sexual exploitation and abuse by all staff. AAH has in place focal points at base and mission levels, as well as safe and confidential ways to report abuse. All AAH staff have the obligation to adhere to the organisation code of conduct and our held accountable if they do not respect it. AAH has made use of cluster specific Protection and Gender Mainstreaming Tip Sheets, along with learnings from various interventions and external evaluations. Overall AAH’s aim is to take decisive humanitarian action with attention to risk mitigation thereby avoiding doing harm to the greatest extent possible. Risks assessments and analysis of the protection context are let for all the projects, and even more so when implemented with at risk groups (e.g. minors) or in areas where risk of doing harm is exacerbated (e.g. Area C in the West Bank) In order to inform its assessments and implementation AAH also continuously works on understanding gender roles and how its interventions might impact them without aggravating existing power relations between genders. By understanding gender roles and household dynamics, AAH believes that the organization mitigates the risks of doing harm. In 2019, AAH conducted a masculinity study in both Gaza and the West Bank, which focused on identities/roles of men in order to fully understand men, women, girls and boys and gender dynamics. As part of AAH accountability to affected populations, a feedback mechanism emphasizing fluid and transparent communication will be in place.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

AAH upholds the humanitarian principles of Humanity, Impartiality, Neutrality, and Independence. During the course of the project, AAH will ensure all projects are easily accessible to those most at need, avoiding risks to the beneficiaries. AAH is committed to operate in an ethical manner and on a programmatic risk mitigation strategy for risk of fraud, waste, and abuse within the proposed activities. At a global level, AAH relies on the organizational Policy on Anti-Bribery, Corruption and Abuse of Power and Code of Conduct. Organizational whistleblowing mechanisms and procedure are in place, `ZERO TOLERANCE ́ towards fraud, bribery or corruption is applied. Reporting mechanisms have also been implemented to report these behaviours to headquarters. Ethical Risks related to any kind of abuse(sexual, power, etc...) are mitigated by providing compulsory briefing to each new staff member on ethical policies. AAH Code of Conduct is aligned with IASC principles. AAH has a Policy for Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) and in 2019, AAH conducted in its Middle East country offices a Protection Mainstreaming Internal Evaluation for which the consultant spent March in oPt. Particular attention was paid to the way AAH includes vulnerable groups in its work, for instance through inclusive participatory assessments. As one of AAH’s core values, AAH thrives to prevent discrimination and to ensure equal and impartial access though transparency and accountability to affected populations. AAH aims for all community members to be involved and consulted as much as possible in project design and implementation, as well as evaluation, including through household and community consultations and focus group discussions, accessible to all. In order for their participation to be meaningful, AAH focuses on ‘qualitative inclusion’ meaning safe spaces for beneficiaries to participate. AAH is reporting under the HRP disaggregated data by sex and age; though pending the reporting format, AAH usually includes further disaggregation on vulnerability such as data on people with disabilities. Random checks will be carried out by the MEAL team members to ensure transparency, neutrality and objectivity and spot potential bias or incongruency.
