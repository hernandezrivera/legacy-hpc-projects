Project Summary

The humanitarian crisis in Iraq is entering a new phase. Combat operations against the Islamic State of Iraq and Syria (ISIS) have ended and hundreds of thousands of displaced people are returning to their homes and communities. In 2019, Save the Children (SC) will continue to provide Child Protection (CP) assistance to the most vulnerable communities, targeting conflict-affected boys and girls and their families across Ninewa, Dohuk, Salah Al-Din, Diyala, and Kirkuk.
To respond to the needs of youth and boys and girls, structured psycho-social support (PSS), such as child resilience and youth resilience programs, will be provided through existing child protection spaces, community centres and schools. Case management will be provided to children at risk, with a specific focus on unaccompanied and separated children, child victims of neglect and abuse, child-headed households, children with disabilities and out of school children. Child Protection Committees (CPC) and other community-based structures will continue to be supported and equipped with necessary skills including basic child protection, identification and safe referrals, gender equality and inclusion, community mobilization and awareness raising, to increase communities’ resilience and capacity to respond to child protection needs. SC will continue to work and coordinate closely with the Ministry of Displacement and Migration (MoDM) Ministry of Youth and Sports (MoYS), Ministry of Labor and Social Affairs (MoLSA), Ministry of Education (MoE), and other relevant government stakeholders.

Needs

The conflict in Iraq has been characterized by large-scale child rights abuses and grave violations against children, including the recruitment of children into armed groups, sexual violence, and abductions. For the hundreds of thousands of children who have lived under ISIS control, the last four years have been marked by daily exposure to extreme violence, deprivation, and loss of loved ones. Continuing insecurity, lack of basic services, explosive hazards, contamination, and damage to homes, businesses and public infrastructures, such as schools and hospitals, continue to pose significant challenges. According to the 2019 HRP, itis estimated that a total of 6.5 million people will be in need of humanitarian assistance. From this estimate, a total of 1,750,000 people living in vulnerable and high severity locations will be targeted, namely in Anbar, Baghdad, Diyala, Erbil, Kirkuk, Ninewa and Salah Al-Din governorates. Throughout 2018, multi-sectoral needs assessments were conducted in Diyala, Kirkuk and Salah Al-Din, and Ninewa (February 2018 and August 2018) and a rapid needs assessment was conducted in Ninewa (August 2018) to identify the key needs of affected populations in target communities, as well as to help identify methods of response appropriate to the needs and context of the targeted communities. Acommunities. A number of child protection issues, including sexual violence and recruitment into armed groups, were apparent across the targeted locations. The assessments established that families’ loss of income meant that boys are at an increased risk and pressure to provide an income by working or joining armed groups. Girls are at risk of exclusion, violence, and harassment, which can lead to increasing incidents of girls being pulled out of school and forced into early marriage in an effort to preserve familial honor. In particular, the child protection situation in Sinjar/Sinoni appears to be exceptionally fragile along all the relevant, evaluated axes. The assessments found that community members have the will to address protection issues, but they do not have the tools and resources to do so. Developing support systems and structures – particularly those with roots in the community, established through training, volunteering, etc. – for dealing with some of the most pressing child protection issues evident in each of the targeted locations is likely to be important in the improvement of the child protection situation in the targeted locations. Relevant psycho-social indicators collected from child respondents suggest that there may also be issues with regard to mental health and psycho-social well-being, particularly in terms of uncertainty or even pessimism about the future. According to the multi-sectoral needs assessments conducted in August, in Kirkuk an average of 30 per cent of IDP children and 35 per cent of returnee children are in need of PSS; in Ninewa, an average of 26 per cent of IDP children and 19 per cent of returnee children are in need of PSS; and in Salah Al-Din, an average of 18 per cent of children and 14% of returnee children are in need of PSS (MSNA, August 2018). Safety has not yet been fully restored in many areas of Iraq, including those that children and their families are returning to. Ongoing violence, booby traps, unexploded ordnance and other explosive remnants of war are pervasive and continue to pose dangers to children. In SC’s Picking Up the Pieces report published in July 2018, boys and girls in Mosul described that they experience intense sorrow and extreme sadness with nearly 43 per cent reporting feeling grief always or a lot of the time. Caregivers reported their children as having very high emotional and behavioral diﬃculties, with a score falling in the ‘abnormal’ range, which is indicative of likely mental health disorders. In Hawiga, 75% of the caregivers stated that their child presents negative behaviour changeresulting from the crisis. In particular, major signs of distress mentioned are unusualcrying and violent reactions (MNSA, August 2018). They also reflected that 66 per cent of their children displayed grief most of the time. The mental health and psycho-social needs of children in Mosul and across Iraq will require targeted and contextualized, short- and long-term approaches to support children and young people, parents, peer networks and communities.

Activities

Save the Children will implement the following CP activities across all targeted governorates: - Support children at risks and survivors through case management (including for unaccompanied and separated children) - Identify children at risk and survivors, and refer to specialized services (for non-case management actors) - Emergency alternative care (foster families and transit shelter) - Provide recreational activities (sports, sewing classes, reading, etc.) at protection spaces to create safe environments for children - Provide sustained, structured psycho-social support (PSS) or resilience activities to children (Child and Youth Resilience, Adolescent Girls Toolkit, Engaging Boys in Life Skills Toolkit) - Strengthen community-based mechanisms (Child Protection Committees, Child and youth clubs) to support prevention of and responses to child protection issues - Conduct awareness raising activities on child protection issues, focusing on prevention and behavior changes - Capacity building for CP and non-CP stakeholders (communities, INGOs, LNGOs, government stakeholders) on psychological first aid, GBV, and other CP topics - Provide skills and knowledge for parents and caregivers to better care and protect their children - Mainstream CP in other humanitarian actions

Protection Risk Analysis

Access: Many are unable to access urgent humanitarian assistance, including child protection and mental health services and quality education, and have limited options for the full reinstatement of their rights and achievement of a durable solution to their displacement. In addition, children across Iraq continue to be denied their right to education as a result of the targeting, use, and destruction of schools during conflict. Protection issues will continue to be significant should social tensions arise, particularly in communities where local populations are perceived as having supported ISIS. There are specific concerns for the long-term futures of children recruited or used by parties to conflict, those in detention, and those born to ISIS members who are at risk of being abandoned. Hundreds of thousands of people, including tens of thousands of children, have been exposed to extreme violence, danger, uncertainty, and denial of human rights, resulting in stress that will require immediate and long-term support and care. Inclusion: Adolescent girls in particular are at risk of exclusion, gender-based violence (GBV), in particular sexual violence, and harassment, which can lead to increasing incidents of girls being pulled out of school and forced into early marriage in an effort to preserve familial honor. For girls and boys with disabilities, traditional ideas and a lack of resources about disabilities result in families hiding these children from public view and services, which could provide them with the opportunity to learn skills and have a meaningful life. MHPSS: Children and caregivers alike reported high levels of stress and emotional problems, with scoring that would indicate borderline mental health issues. Other relevant issues include family separation, sexual abuse, physical abuse, child labor, early and forced marriage, wounded children, and recruitment into armed groups across the target locations.

Mitigation Measures

Access: SC will advocate for durable solutions, freedom of movement, and access to services for all boys and girls their families in Iraq. Advocacy efforts will be a key part of any SC activities on the ground, both at national and governorate level, to ensure safe and dignified access to education, protection and mental health services, and fulfillment of basic needs. SC will provide safe and protective school environments, build the capacities of teachers and community members, and raise awareness among communities toward protection services to ensure children have improved well-being and opportunities for the future. Inclusion: SC believes it is critical to directly address gender discrimination and promote gender equality to ensure no harm comes to children, to support their development to reach their full potential, and to advance our vision of a world where every child attains their equal right to survival, protection, development and participation. Through a gender mainstreaming approach, SC will make a concerted effort to ensure that gender equality is promoted in all stage of the proposed activities. SC has been engaging directly with girls, boys, women and men on identifying gender specific needs and considerations across all programming. This intervention aims at equally targeting boys and girls in all activities and where possible ensuring a 50/50 participation as relevant. Inclusion of children with disabilities into everyday activities involves practices and guidance designed to identify and remove barriers such as physical, communication and attitudinal that hamper children’s ability to have full participation in society, the same as people without disabilities. Through all activities, SC plans to identify children with disabilities, support them and their families with PSS when necessary, provide of assistive technologies (ATs) that can be used to help a person with a disability fully engage in life activities. ATs can help enhance functional independence and make daily living tasks easier through the use of aids that help a person travel, communicate with others, learn, work, and participate in social and activities. MHPSS: To address the severe mental health of children, SC will advocate for child-focused and gender-sensitive mental health and psycho-social support services based on child participation and community engagement, and implement gender-sensitive mental health and psycho-social support guidance and interventions in all SC Child Protection and Education projects in country, for children and their caregivers.Awareness and capacity building through community-based structures (such as Child Protection Committees, and Child and Youth Clubs) will be conducted to address issues such as child labor, child marriage, harassment, abuse and violence, while providing individual and family support through case management and MHPSS interventions and parenting sessions, and referrals to livelihoods and cash interventions as appropriate.

How is your project accountable to affected population

The SC’s accountability system provides opportunity for the communities to receive information about the project and to give direct feedback or make complaints about the delivery of program interventions. SC regularly provide accessible information to affected populations on program activities based on preferred communication methods and information needs. We share this information in languages, formats and media that can be easily understood by children and communities we work with. SC’s information sharing strategy covers the following: • Information sharing about our organization, our core values, the staff behaviors and the way we work • Information about our approach, project activities/interventions and services that will be provided. • Information about selection criteria • Information about the feedback mechanism itself explaining the clear process on how it works, for what purpose; and how the community/children can provide their feedback/complaints using what tools etc Through the life of each project, additional sessions will be organized to update the communities on project progress, challenges and changes in activities. A multi-pronged feedback and complaints mechanism is introduced in all project locations and shared widely using posters, business cards and community gathering meetings. The following mechanisms are in place for all sectors: • Telephone toll-free hotline: A telephone hotline is established and the number has been widely disseminated to beneficiaries through different communication means in the targeted areas. WhatsApp messaging and Viber call have also been set up as alternative channel for receiving direct feedback • Suggestion boxes: Suggestion/complaint are established in the relevant locations accessible to both male and female members of the community where possible, to allow people/children to provide their complaint/feedback directly. • Direct visit and interaction: During the regular monitoring visits, SC mixed gender MEAL team members organize FGDs, community meetings and one to one interaction with the beneficiaries, boys and girls and communities in gender safe spaces and at times appropriate to women and girls. Complaints received through SC systems and are logged, investigated, addressed, and outcomes shared, aiming at immediate resolution and improvements. Complaints of serious nature (if any) are investigated and responded following a standard organizational protocol. Communities are regularly provided with feedback as to how their input was used. In order to make sure that the complaint and feedback mechanisms are accessible to all people, group consultations (including girls and boys, women and men, elderly and children and adults with disabilities) are conducted through the project life, to understand if the established community feedback mechanism can be easily accessed, useful and which information sharing and feedback channels the local community prefer and trust most. The ensure the safety of SC feedback mechanism, the places where community will be conducted and suggestion boxes will be placed, will be carefully assessed by SC with regards to its safety and accessibility. The consultation will be conducted during the day and close to the place where people live. SC will ensure that all complaints and feedback will be treated confidentially and the focal person will be trained in information protection procedures.
