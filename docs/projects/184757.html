Project Summary

Food and Nutrition context in Somalia
Decades of conflict and natural disasters including repeated droughts and seasonal flooding followed by limited government and institutional support have had a profound negative effect on the food security situation and social service sector in Somalia. Nearly seven in ten Somalis live on less than USD 1.90 per day, the sixth-highest poverty rate in sub-Saharan Africa. Poverty is both widespread and deep, particularly for households in rural areas and internally displaced person (IDP) settlements. The number of food-insecure people experiencing long-term stress is trending upwards, indicating that chronic food insecurity persists, requiring sustainable solutions. Recurrent climate-related shocks and political instability are key drivers of food insecurity and pose continuing risks to crop production and food access. Malnutrition in Somalia is driven by a combination of factors such as recurrent food insecurity, child illnesses, high morbidity, micronutrient deficiencies, low immunization levels, poor access to clean water and sanitation, poor hygiene practice, gender inequality and discriminatory sociocultural attitudes leading to poor care practices. Based on the latest Post Gu FSNAU assessment (September 2021), it is estimated that a total of 3.5 million out of the 15.8m people face acute food insecurity (IPC 3 and above – crisis or worse) until December 2021. At the national level the median prevalence of Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) has remained at a serious level of 11.1%. Approximately 1.2 million children under the age of five are likely to be acutely malnourished, including 295,515 who are likely to be severely malnourished. In addition to this, around 245,909 Pregnant & Lactating Women (PLWs) will be at risk of acute malnutrition.
Acute malnutrition is a major public health problem in Somalia. It affects disproportionately children under the age of five, as well as Pregnant and Lactating Women (PLWs), especially among IDPs, vulnerable (agro) pastoralist communities and those living in areas affected by flooding. Morbidity among children is high (≥ 20%). Out of a total of 40 surveyed population groups 18 recorded high morbidity presence of above 20%.
Vitamin A supplementation and Measles vaccination remains low <40% in several population groups.
TB prevalence remains one of the highest globally with Somalia in the top 10 countries with high TB burden (262 per 100,000 population) while the HIV prevalence remains <1% however with high stigma and discrimination levels at 94%.
Project activities
The World Food Program is the leading UN agency in Somalia in fighting hunger. In Somalia, WFP together with the Ministry of Health (MoH), nutrition cluster and the partners, supports nutrition service delivery and prevention. WFP collaborates with the MoH and UNICEF to offer an integrated package of nutrition and health, following the revised MoH IMAM guidelines for treating acute malnutrition in Somalia.
WFP will cover both treatment and preventive nutrition services including:
• Targeted Supplementary Feeding Program for 665,846 moderately acute malnourished children 6-59 months; 173,746 malnourished Pregnant and Lactating Women (PLW), 10,794 malnourished People Living with HIV on ART treatment and malnourished TB patients.
• Preventive Nutrition services for 386,102 children 6-23 months and 173,746 PLW enrolled in Maternal Child Health and Nutrition services and blanket Supplementary Feeding Program for children 6-23 months and PLW in districts where GAM (Global Acute Malnutrition) rate is above 15%.
• Monthly cash vouchers of USD 15 for 24,104 PLWs in selected target districts.
• 24,206 caregivers with SAM children admitted to the Stabilisation will receive food while staying at the Stabilisation Center
• A total of 96,150 pregnant women will receive food rations for their families when delivering their baby at a health facility

1. - Projects activities

WFP is mandated as the leading UN agency for the prevention and treatment of Moderate Acute Malnutrition (MAM) in Somalia. Together with MoH and UNICEF, WFP pursues an integrated approach designed to treat MAM and prevent the direct causes of malnutrition, like inadequate diets with complementary interventions that address the underlying factors, such as poor knowledge of feeding and hygiene practices. 1) Targeted Supplementary Feeding Programme (TSFP): WFP will treat 665,846 MAM children aged 6-59 months, as well as 184,432 MAM pregnant and lactating women (PLW). It seeks to prevent them from sliding further into severe wasting and provides continuous monitoring and care to individuals discharged from the programme through preventive nutrition services. Malnourished children and PLW will be enrolled based on the IMAM guidelines and registered through SCOPE. Follow-up screening, monitoring and provision of specialized food will be done on a biweekly or monthly basis. Follow up visits will also be used for social behavior changes messaging on nutrition. The health staff will be supported to follow up on defaulters and non-responders. 2) Mother and Child Health and Nutrition (MCHN): Eliminating child wasting starts with prevention to ensure that women and children benefit from nutritious and safe diets, essential health-nutrition services and positive nutrition practices. The MHCN programme focuses on the first 1,000 days of life, from conception through pregnancy to a child’s second birthday: a critical developmental period, during which good nutrition and healthy growth will prevent irreversible damages to a child’s growth and mental development. MCHN aims to promote growth, prevent chronic and acute malnutrition, and break the intergenerational cycle of malnutrition. WFP will target 173,746 pregnant and lactating women (PLW) and 386,102 children from 6-23 months in districts with recurrent high GAM rates. A total of 96,150 pregnant mothers enrolled in MCHN will receive food rations if they deliver their babies at health facilities, ensuring safer deliveries under supervision. All children and PLW visiting MCHN will be screened and those that are malnourished will be referred to the appropriate nutrition services. Registration of non-malnourished children 6-23 months and PLW to the MCHN support will be done through SCOPE. In addition, 24,206 caregivers with SAM children admitted to the Stabilisation Center (SC) will receive food while staying at the SC. 3) Blanket Supplementary Feeding Programme (BSFP): Through BFSP WFP aims to aims to cushion vulnerable groups from a recurrent cycle of acute malnutrition. WFP provides seasonal nutritional assistance to PLW women and children (6-23 months) during lean periods or following shocks in areas where GAM rates >15% and where no MCHN services are provided. BSFP is provided for a maximum of six months in two batches (Dec to February; July to September). Beneficiaries are registered through SCOPE and screened before receiving the first food ration and then monthly. All malnourished children and PLWs will be referred to the appropriate nutrition service. 4) Food for malnourished TB/HIV clients. To promote treatment adherence and uptake, 10,794 malnourished TB/HIV clients who are on ART or DOTS treatment will receive a nutritious ration for rehabilitation. WFP aims to strengthen linkages to the social safety net programme. 5) Addressing underlying causes of malnutrition through integration into the MCHN: Mothers and caregivers will also learn through SBCC on healthy and balanced diets, and Infant and Young Child Feeding practices (IYCF). In selected areas, WFP aims to reach 24,104 PLW with monthly cash-based transfers of $15 that enables PLW to buy vegetables in support of nutritious diets at the households. WFP will prepare monthly reports on the admission and uptake of nutrition services and share these to MOH and the Nutrition Cluster Coordination.

2.1.1 - What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

In addition to treatment of acute malnutrition, WFP aims to address some of the underlying drivers of malnutrition, including diet diversity. A study conducted in Somalia (Doocy S et al, 2020); noted that mean MUAC increased significantly for PLW who received cash-based vouchers. In support of local markets, the use of cash-based vouchers is the preferred modality to improve access to fresh food for greater diet diversity at the household level

2.1.2 - Explain how the project design considered market functionality?

WFP conducts continuous market analysis to determine available foods and their prevailing market price. The cash voucher will be a conditional transfer whose value is informed by the market analysis findings. The vouchers are redeemable through local banks and retail shops.

4.6 - Mitigation Measures

Risk 4.1: MInority clans are not included in consultations and/or excluded from humanitarian assistance. WFP is conducting a conflict sensitivity analysis to inform how best to ensure that minority clans are not excluded from nutrition services. In addition, at the beginning of each project, WFP carries out consultations with all stakeholders at the community level, discussing all aspects of the project design and implementation. This includes various community members, religious leaders, clan leaders, youth leaders and political leaders. Government stakeholders and other nutrition partners are also consulted through the nutrition cluster. This engagement serves to secure community buy-in and ownership of the project and avoid any backlash that could lead to violence against recipients of assistance. To mitigate against exclusion and marginalisation, WFP employs community-based targeting principles and processes through which the most vulnerable and under-represented members of the communities, including IDPs, are able to participate. Communities are consulted on the validation of vulnerability criteria, and formation an all-inclusive selection committee for the identification of most vulnerable household at the village level. When carrying out community consultations and targeting, specific attention is paid to age, gender, minorities, disability and clan representation from across the community. WFP supports the participation of both men and women as well as other vulnerable groups by providing safe spaces for these groups to provide feedback (e.g. women-only focus discussion groups). Risk 4.2: Protection of women, girls and boys from sexual violence, including sexual exploitation and abuse ( SEA) when accessing humanitarian assistance WFP has a zero-tolerance policy for sexual exploitation and abuse which focuses on acts committed by WFP employees or others associated with its work against the people it serves. WFP’s CPs are required to abide by WFP’s regulations and to adhere to its commitments against harassment and abuse. WFP has a Somalia-specific GBV referral protocol and is continuously working with protection partners such as UNICEF and the GBV sub-cluster to update referral pathways to ensure coverage in locations with gaps in service provision. WFP has provided training on PSEA and GBV to its local partners and organises regular refreshers sessions for the CPs. WFP has a large presence in Somalia and works with local partners in providing assistance in all regions of the country that are accessible to humanitarian partners. WFP supports a network of 264 MCH centers providing preventive care and 914 sites providing nutrition treatment support and additional 65 TB and HIV sites providing treatment to malnourished TB and HIV patients. Up to a third of the TSFP sites are mobile, enabling WFP to reach populations in remote locations. This means that targeted women and children do not have to walk great distances to receive WFP nutrition assistance and reduces the risks associated with such movement. The use of cash-based transfers and SCOPE cards also allows beneficiaries to access their entitlement at locations most convenient to them. To complement field-monitoring visits, WFP has a robust complaint and feedback mechanism (CFM) that handles complaints and feedback from all beneficiaries and all stakeholders involved in the assistance process. The CFM not only aims at strengthening accountability to communities but also to identify a range of protection, fraud and diversion issues as well as other concerns related to delivery of WFP assistance. Complaints can be made through a well-established Somalia-based toll-free hotline, directly (face to face) to WFP monitoring staff and cooperating partner staff at programme implementation sites, through telephone calls and email or short messaging to WFP offices, and through local authorities and local leaders.
