Project Summary

Protracted crises in South Sudan, including climactic shocks and continued conflict, have resulted in 8.9 million people projected to be in need of humanitarian assistance in 2022. As a result of the shocks, such as flooding, intercommunal violence (ICV) and ongoing armed clashes, the majority of the population are facing increased food insecurity, lack of access to basic services, and protection risks. As per the Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO) 2022, 8.3 million people are expected to experience severe food insecurity in 2022.
Solidarites International has been operating in South Sudan since 2006, and currently operates from 3 field bases in Raja town (Western Bahr El Ghazal), Malakal (Upper Nile, with roving teams to target other counties), and Nyirol (Jonglei), and a sub-base in Aburoc IDP camp (Fashoda County, Upper Nile). In addition, SI has a fully roving Emergency Response and Preparedness (EPR) team based in Juba that implements frontline responses to new emergencies across the country. SI implements integrated programs in Food Security and Livelihoods (FSL) and Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH).
In FSL, SI aims at supporting the most vulnerable households, who are suffering from severe food insecurity and/or struggling to restore their livelihoods. In previous years, SI’s FSL activities have included large-scale seed and tool distributions, fishing kit distributions, cash transfers for households affected by malnutrition, and training on agronomic and fishing good practices.
In 2022, SI will focus on providing rapid livelihoods support in emergency affected areas, while developing resilience and reduced dependency in lower severity locations.
In HNO FSL severity 5, 4, and 3 locations in Upper Nile and Jonglei, SI will support for the most vulnerable emergency affected households (with a focus on households suffering from or at risk of malnutrition) with seeds, tools, and assets for fishing and vegetable production and follow up training. Flood adaptations to regular practices will be piloted to reduce the impact of expected future flooding in 2022. Through its roving Emergency Preparedness and Response (EPR) team, SI will deploy to emergency-affected locations identified through the Clusters and Needs Analysis Working Group to provide first phase and second phase responses in IPC 4+ locations. SI will expand its EPR programs to include rapid response kits distribution (fishing kits/fast growing seeds) to affected households in new rapid onset emergencies. Activities ensuring access to basic WASH services will be integrated with FSL activities in priority locations to contribute to reductions in malnutrition and food insecurity. In Raja, SI will focus on developing livelihood resilience among communities and reducing dependency on humanitarian aid through programs on seed replication, and value chain addition, while ensuring basic needs for livelihoods inputs (including for returnees) are met through main season seed, tool, and fishing kit distributions. All programs will be integrated with nutrition services through beneficiary targeting and support to nutrition centres to develop and maintain kitchen gardens.

1 - Needs

Extreme flooding across the country in 2019-2021, combined with intercommunal violence (ICV) and ongoing armed clashes have led to displacement, extreme lack of basic needs, extreme food insecurity and malnutrition rates, and loss of livelihoods. South Sudan is projected to reach 8.3 million people in severe food insecurity by the peak of the lean season of May – July 2022. All SI’s areas of intervention have an overall HNO intersectoral severity rating of 4. Fangak, Canal-Pigi, Nyirol, and Panyikang have been identified as counties of extreme or severe concern in FSL severity ratings, while Baliet, Manyo, Fashoda, and Malakal are of very high concern. Raja is of high concern, but also offers opportunities for supporting diversified livelihoods and increased resilience. Fangak (projected catastrophic food insecurity) has been severely affected by flooding and is one of the highest priority areas of concern in South Sudan. An IRNA assessment conducted in August 2021 found that the majority of households were not able to meet their food needs, and were not sure when and where they would get their next meal. They were reliant on fish and wild foods. Canal-Pigi County is projected to be an area of catastrophic food insecurity severity in 2022 lean season. It has seen continued flooding since August 2020. A rapid needs assessment by SI in Canal-Pigi in January 2022 found that all households in the areas assessed are now in a dire economic situation due to loss of livelihoods and sources of incomes due to floods. Fishing is the main livelihood during the rainy season, but only feasible for households with canoes and fishing nets. The population of Nyirol county (projected extreme food insecurity) is heavily reliant on General Food Distribution, with only 20% of households reporting to SI that own production as main source of cereal. Insecurity related to ICV and localized clan conflicts mean people are unwilling to move to farming lands. Flooding between 2019-2021 has also impacted livelihoods. 43% of households stated that they would not cultivate in 2022. In an SI assessment in December 2021 in Panyikang County (projected extreme food insecurity in 2022 lean season), all participants stated that most households are already applying stress strategies to cope with food needs. Participants reported that their livelihoods had been severely affected by flooding, crop diseases, and lack of seeds and tools. In Malakal, Baliet, Fashoda, and Manyo, conditions are expected to be less extreme in 2022 than other locations, but still with predicted severe food insecurity. The high prevalence of fish in the diet prevents a worse food security situation but households lack dietary diversity. Flooding has affected all areas, destroying crops and fishing equipment (e.g. as reported by an assessment in Manyo in December 2021). There are high numbers of very vulnerable people in need and support is needed to prevent worsening food insecurity and malnutrition. In Raja, needs are less severe, with fewer climate related impacts and better functioning markets. However, the population is still vulnerable and livelihoods are susceptible to shocks. An SI assessment found that 88% of interviewed households are currently practicing agriculture as main livelihood. However, household food expenditures had increased in the last 6 months and households were using coping strategies such as consuming seed stocks. Therefore, support is needed to increase diversity, productivity, and resilience of livelihoods and reduce reliance on assistance. In all locations, food insecurity affects men, women, girls, boys, and groups such as elderly and PWD, differently. Children under 5 and Pregnant and Lactating Women (PLW) are most at risk of malnutrition. Women, elderly, and PWD face additional barriers to livelihoods and food, and are more likely to be food insecure. Men and boys face greater risks from ICV. Young men and women lack opportunities for livelihoods.

2 - Project activities

SI’s 2022 strategy for FSL will focus on two Axes:1. Respond to reduce levels of critical food insecurity for the most vulnerable households in priority emergency locations through static and EPR roving emergency responses (corresponding to FSL Cluster objectives CO2 and CO3) 2. Support vulnerable people in more stable locations to continue to recover their livelihoods and to develop increased resilience and independence (corresponding to FSL Cluster objectives CO3 and CO2, primarily targeted at Raja County.) In Axis 1, SI will implement the following activities:- Distribution of rapid response kits (fishing kits, fast growing seed kits) to vulnerable households affected by emergencies in 2022 through SI EPR roving team- Distribution of fishing kits and/or vegetable kits to the most vulnerable households in static locations, in particular those referred through nutrition facilities- Conduct resilience analysis- Training for households and set up of demonstration gardens- Support for nutrition facilities to establish and maintain kitchen gardens through provision of seeds, tools, and training- Awareness raising on good food safety practices, nutritious cooking techniques, dietary diversity, etc. using harmonized messaging with nutrition and WASH- Distribution of additional tools and assets, such as canoes, to livelihood groups to improve production and flood resilience of livelihoods.- Piloting of adapted growing techniques to reduce the impact of flooding and displacement on food production (e.g. flood adapted vegetable sack gardening).- Provide additional support for households with specific vulnerabilities (e.g. people with disabilities (PWD), etc.) to adapt livelihoods to their needs and capacities, such as additional items, adapted tools, etc. to help PWD do vegetable gardening. Type of support will be provided following consultation with households.- Engage with protection actors implementing conflict reconciliation activities to facilitate community discussions on conflict and access to resources and livelihoods, and identify opportunities for improved livelihoods, business, vocational training, and market trade.- Support for nutrition facilities to establish and maintain kitchen gardens through provision of seeds, tools, and training- Awareness raising on good food safety practices, nutritious cooking techniques, dietary diversity, etc. using harmonized messaging with nutrition and WASH In Axis 2, SI will implement the following activities:- All activities in Axis 1, and also the following: - Seed fairs/voucher systems in the areas where traders are available and access feasible- Agricultural training and capacity-building through support for existing farmers groups, development of demonstration plots, and extensionist services.- Support selected farmers groups and communities to implement seed replication and safe crop storage- Strengthening of local market linkages through supported discussions between producers and buyers- Support for diversified income generating activities through provision of tools, training, and value addition equipment, particularly for mango drying and beekeeping.-Piloting for vocational activities, with topics relevant to different groups, especially young men, young women, and PWD. Safety audits and Accountability to Affected Populations consultations will be conducted before all interventions. While specific high priority counties have been identified as key locations for intervention, SI will have the flexibility to adapt and respond to emergencies in other locations through the Emergency Preparedness & Response (EPR) team.

3 - Project indicators

SI implements an extensive framework of tools for the monitoring and evaluation of the activities conducted. All the indicators are designed in order to closely monitor the meeting of the project objectives. Key indicators for this project will include:• Number of beneficiaries receiving agricultural inputs (vegetable, crop & fishing kits)• Number of people benefiting from capacity building & training, asset building and market support• Number of kitchen gardens setup at Nutrition facilities• Average Coping Strategies Index (CSI) score for the target population• % of the target population with acceptable Food Consumption Score (FCS)• Percentage of targeted household reporting an increase in their daily fishing catch• At least 20% of seeds distributed to farmers in 2023 is provided by the established local network (Raja). Safety audits and protection analysis will be conducted at the start of each project. FGDs on AAP are conducted before, during, and after implementation, and AAP committees are established in project locations to ensure ongoing feedback from communities. These activities allow SI to adapt activities in between and assess the level of AAP as per the beneficiaries’ perception following AAP framework policies. AAP checklists are also completed throughout the projects. KIIs and FGDs will be conducted in the targeted areas at different stages of the project in order to assess the distribution modalities and adapt them to the targeted population needs. SI will carry out PDM surveys covering all distributions, to gather information on satisfaction of beneficiaries in terms of timeliness and relevance of input distribution, quality and quantity distributed, overall organization and modalities of distribution including security and protection issues. A baseline survey is done at the beginning of all projects, to assess the needs, perceptions, and existing capacities of the communities in each area and adapt the activities accordingly. An endline survey is conducted at the end of all projects to support the final reporting and assess level of indicators achievement, satisfaction of beneficiaries and lessons learnt of the project to be shared with donors and Clusters for further projects. SI will apply the following actions and tools to ensure that the objectives are reached and needs covered: Activity Progress Update (APU) tools and MEAL plan; Follow-up of the different assessments and interventions implemented by the project teams; Weekly update to state level Clusters on achievements and challenges; Monthly reporting to the Clusters through 5Ws reports; and Routine field visits by the Coordination teams. At SI coordination level, a program coordinator and a MEAL assistant will monitor the implementation, achievement of indicators, and quality of the program using the APU, and with support from SI HQ.

5.1. Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA)?

Yes

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

Cash and Voucher systems will only be used in locations such as Raja, where markets can absorb the demand and provide effective means of delivering aid. Cash and Voucher assistance in the FSL sector can be very useful for the following reasons: - To develop or reinforce markets, encouraging traders to buy and sell more. For locally produced items, it can provide an additional support to reinforce the livelihoods of local producers. It creates effects at medium/long term. - Using vouchers for supply of items (especially local produced items) increases the independence of and accountability to targeted households as they have more control of the quality of materials they receive.- It will increase populations’ autonomy and independence from humanitarian support, as well as increasing their dignity. Receiving cash or voucher, especially if they are not too restrictive, will allow people to choose what they will purchase, according to their preferences and needs. If they can choose the type of FSL support with voucher or cash, they can also adapt their livelihoods to the seasonal calendar, depending on when they receive the support, they will choose farming or- Reduced transportation costs as transportation by traders is more likely to be in bulk and therefore more cost-effective

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

SI will do a market assessment before any project, tailored to the context and the area. SI can carry out rapid market assessments to complement longer term market assessments to determine if the market is functional enough to absorb cash and voucher mechanisms and avoid creating shortages in the market or inflation in the areas. Assessments will also try to identify vendors willing to accept the vouchers and the items that can be found on each market. SI is also contributing to the Joint Market Monitoring Initiative led by REACH every month in Raja Town. SI will link with market information management systems to monitor the price of items. SI will also develop procurement plans at the start of each project to anticipate supply mechanisms. This will allow for detailed cost comparison between local and non-local markets (taking into account transportation costs) and for consultation with local suppliers on their prices, supply capacity, and delivery lead times to supply the desired items. While implementing the project, SI will promote linkages between buyers and sellers, as well as the whole value chain, boosting local economy and business development. SI has already held positive discussions with traders and producers in Raja County on the feasibility of seed fairs and voucher systems for supply of inputs to farmers in Raja, and facilitated discussions between local seed producers and traders.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

At the start of each project and in each location, SI will hold AAP FGDs with different groups (including separate groups with women, men, youth, people with disability, etc.), community meetings, and meetings with community leaders and local authorities such as the RRC. During these meetings SI will explain the planning, activities and objectives of the project and take feedback from the community. The beneficiaries’ opinions will be crosschecked with findings of the assessments conducted at the beginning of the project. This feedback will be used to adapt project planning and activities. to contribute to the adaptation of the project. During these meetings SI will also ask the communities about key topics such as their perceived levels of vulnerability to define selection criteria, and preferred mechanisms for feedback and complaints. Safety audit assessments and protection analysis will also be carried out at the start of each project and before selection of distribution and training sites, to ensure beneficiaries have the opportunity to raise concerns about protection risks and to enable SI to adapt the activities. Throughout the project, SI will conduct regular FGDs with communities to take their feedback on the progress and quality of activities. In addition, all assessments implemented by SI collect SADD data and ask questions on protection risks, accountability, satisfaction, and awareness of respondents on complaints mechanisms. Moreover, SI Program Managers report quality checks based on beneficiary feedback to the Coordination level on a monthly basis. These quality checks are based on the 9 commitments and quality criteria of the Core Humanitarian Standard on Quality and Accountability (CHS) developed by CHSAlliance. Each project is analyzed through this quality check and followed up in the internal monitoring tool (Activity Progress Update). Based on the findings, Coordination and field bases determine to what extent it is necessary to adapt the project to the needs. Should any issue raised exceed the project’s scope, SI will advocate this situation to the relevant Clusters (at National, State, and County level), local authorities, and other humanitarian partners to tackle the issues and ensure needs can be covered. In addition, SI will use the FSL Cluster AAP checklist and guidance for ensuring disability inclusion in all projects to ensure that key AAP actions are being taken and meet the required quality standards.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

AAP FGDs with communities at the start, during and end of projects will be segregated into different groups, such as women, people with disabilities, youth, and elderly, to help ensure that they are able to contribute to discussions and give their feedback. These FGDs will include questions asking each group their preferred methods of giving feedback and complaints and what actions can be done to ensure that they feel comfortable giving their feedback, including sensitive complaints. Each CRM committee is selected with the communities directly and ensuring all groups of the society and particularly women with specific needs, children, People living with Disabilities and individuals who are not literate have a representative. Each CRM committee will be trained in the standard procedure for taking complaints and feedbacks. If the beneficiaries cannot write their feedback, they will be able to reach program staff and particularly community volunteers who will also receive CRM training and will be available to receive complaints and feedback during collective activities and household visits. At distribution and activity sites, complaint boxes and desks will be set up. On a regular basis, the CRM committee will gather the complaints received by this mechanism. In addition, all assessments implemented by SI collect SADD data and ask questions on protection risks, accountability, satisfaction, and awareness of respondents on complaints mechanisms. For internal monitoring and to ensure the quality of programs, SI will integrate safety audits, FSL Cluster AAP checklist, and guidance on disability inclusion into the program monitoring tools as essential sources of verification to ensure that the needs, views, and feedback of the mostly vulnerable members are registered and acted upon.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

During FGDs and community meetings on AAP at the start of projects, SI will start by presenting the principles and mechanisms for which SI are accountable. For complaints made in terms of the PSEA and CP policy, when CRM committees can be created, one man and one woman will be selected by the community as PSEA focal points. They will be considered focal points for the community to confidentially report complaints to. At each activity, awareness will be raised with regards to their availability to receive particularly sensitive feedback, especially regarding the behavior of project staff. They will receive specific sensitivity training and direct communication line with the SI MEAL and Coordination team. This will ensure all PSEA and CP policy complaints reach the coordination offices without program staff on the ground being aware. SI will also work with any existing external complaints structures (e.g. CCCM committees, etc.) to ensure communities and other partners are aware of complaints mechanisms and to receive feedback.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

During AAP FGDs and community meetings at the start of projects in each location, SI will inform the community about the planning, activities and objectives of the project and take feedback from the community about their preferred mechanisms for feedback and complaints. Based on these discussions, SI will adapt the feedback and complaint mechanisms. Further meetings will be held to inform the community of the selected mechanisms for providing feedback and complaints. Communities will be reminded of these mechanisms regularly during project activities, for example at distributions, during training sessions, and in further FGDs and AAP meetings. In all project locations, CRM committees will be selected with the communities directly and ensuring all groups of the society, particularly women and PWD, have representatives. Each CRM committee will be trained in the standard procedure for taking complaints and feedbacks. Program staff and particularly community volunteers will also receive CRM training and will be available to receive complaints and feedback during collective activities and household visits. Additionally, offices and distribution sites complaint boxes and desks will be set up. On a regular basis, the CRM committee will gather the complaints received by this mechanism and by the community volunteers, and forward to the SI Coordination at field and Juba levels. Each CRM committee will share on a regular basis directly with the Field Coordinator and SI coordination office the complaints and feedback received as well as the measures taken at field level to be remediated. The community leaders and RRC will also be involved and sensitized to this topic, especially for very sensitive complaints that could concern staff members and sharing information to be handled with respect to confidentiality. SI provides accessible and timely information to affected populations on organizational procedures, structures and processes that affect them to ensure that they can make informed decisions and choices, and facilitate a dialogue between the organization and affected populations over information provision. SI will always include the most vulnerable in its messages (i.e. women, children, elderly and disabled) and make sure the printed and Information Education and Communication materials are properly transmitted in the local dialect and include images that help understanding for non-literate people. The same materials will also include information on the Complaints and Response Mechanism.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

The mitigation measures that will be taken are:Risk 1. and 2. Through integrated program activities and protection partners, SI will conduct protection and conflict sensitivity analyses to identify potential risks and mitigation measures. At the start of projects and activities, SI will hold consultations with the beneficiaries on activity locations, such as distributions, trainings, and demonstration plots, to tailor the implementation and ensure safe access for communities. Distributions planning will ensure smaller distributions are held in more areas and close to beneficiaries’ houses, and take into consideration the time of the day for implementation of activities. Trainings will also be adapted to ensure they take place in suitable locations, group sizes, and at suitable times of day and in relation to the seasonal calendar. During the consultation, FGD are organized with separate specific groups (FGD with women representatives, FGD with youth representatives, FGD with boys, FGD with girls, FGD with groups with disabilities). Risk 3: SI has strong needs assessments, community consultation, selection criteria, and selection matrices to ensure that beneficiary selection is independent and impartial. SI also follows guidelines provided by the Cluster on beneficiary selection processes. First, community perceptions of selection criteria are discussed with the communities in separated FGDs. Based on this as well as needs assessment and situational information (e.g. type of emergency), SI prepares a criteria and weighting for selection. These criteria are then discussed again with communities and local authorities and finalized with their feedback and agreement. Then, depending on the situation and agreed mechanisms, selection maybe done either by direct household assessment and registration by SI enumerators and staff, through community committees with representatives from different groups, or through community leaders that provide a list of identified potential beneficiaries. In the case that selection is not done directly by SI staff, the list provided is checked by SI team to make sure the identified beneficiaries meet the decided selection criteria. Household registrations are also entered into a beneficiary database that includes scoring against the vulnerability criteria and weighting to ensure selected households meet the criteria. SI has regular discussions with different stakeholders, informing them of all the steps and achievement of the project, joint monitoring and reviews of the project with beneficiaries and community leaders. Risk 4: SI keeps close relationship with authorities to be quickly informed in case of security incident in any of SI areas of interventions. Before the project implementation, at design phase, SI takes into consideration the relationship between different groups, such as cattle keepers and farmers, host communities and returnees, etc. for example when determining the locations of distributions and demonstration plots. SI makes sure authorities or land owners provide land use authorization and MoUs if necessary before starting an activity.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

This project will contribute to reducing flood related displacement resulting from extreme food insecurity by supporting households and communities to maintain sources of food and income even when affected by flooding. While implementing distributions and training for vegetable gardening, SI will also pilot adapted techniques with communities that can create increased flood resilience, such as raised vegetable beds, and small sack gardening. This can prevent all household food and vegetable production being destroyed in flooding, and reduce the necessity for households to move due to lack of access to food.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

The project will directly contribute to reductions of people in IPC 4 and 5, by supporting the most vulnerable households to have rapid access to food and livelihoods through quick impact livelihoods support. The EPR team will be able to directly target new areas of extreme food insecurity following emergencies, upon request for the NAWG. Meanwhile the static teams in Upper Nile and Jonglei will provide highly vulnerable households with similar inputs to ensure rapid access to food, while also providing longer term training and follow up to ensure improved food security outcomes. Distribution of livelihood group assets such as canoes will support community livelihoods as well as access to markets and basic services, enabling remote and vulnerable communities to access additional sources of food and income. Support for nutrition centres in the form of kitchen gardens, and referral of potential households by nutrition centres will help reduce levels of malnutrition. By supporting households in fishing and vegetable production, the project support will also help improved dietary diversity.
