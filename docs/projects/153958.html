Project Summary

Displaced people living in camps will require continued service provision this year. Many will remain unable to return to their AoO due to safety concerns, lack of access to basic services, destroyed or contested property, contamination, lack of livelihood opportunities and potential retribution in AoO. Perceived affiliation and retribution through violence, destruction of housing, inability to return, detention and disappearance are significant emerging threats, as those under suspicion face increased risk of rights violations, exploitation and violence, which is tantamount to collective punishment and in contravention of IHL. These risks will likely be compounded in 2019, as funding reduces for services in camps in favor of supporting return movements, and challenges persist in maintaining the humanitarian and civilian character of the camps. In parallel, while the GoI and the JCMC have begun a process of joint planning for consolidation with the humanitarian community through GRCs, threats of closure continue at a field and camp level, demonstrating little consideration for process, guidelines and coordination and resulting in secondary displacement. In areas of return, there is a severe need for support and coordination of services for formerly displaced families.

Needs

Anbar: As of Nov 2018, HTC camps (9 camps) have a population of 1,455 HH (7,372 individuals). Due to HTC’s relatively isolated location, residents are entirely dependent on camp services. Most of the residents come from W. Anbar and wish to eventually return to their AoO, however, due to security concerns, destroyed infrastructure, lack of services and livelihood opportunities in their AoO, departure from the camps seems premature and could put the IDPs even in a more vulnerable position. In the REACH Intentions Survey (Dec 2017-Jan 2018) in Anbar camps, only 43% of IDP respondents indicated that they were planning to return to their AoO. Should those who intend to return do so from HTC camps, there will still be a population of over 6,000 individuals requiring basic services in camps. Anbar camps are at risk of eviction, due to plans for camp closure and consolidation as part of the GoI’s efforts to promote return. In the past few months, IDPs have been returning to the AoO, and the number of camp residents has been steadily decreasing. To reduce infrastructure maintenance costs and the likelihood of the spread of informal sites, 3 smaller sub-camps (Fallujah 2, 4 and 6) will be closed down and incorporated into remaining UNHCR camps. Al-Khalidiya camp closed down at the end of September 2018, and the residents who could not return to their AoO were moved to HTC. Kilo 18 is expected to be closed. SAD: 45% of REACH Intentions Survey respondents reported that they plan to return to their AoO, but only 11% foresaw doing so within the next 3 months, and the majority did not know, or expected to do so after six months’ time: 67% of the respondents reported that their AoO remains unsafe, and 92% reported ongoing sporadic clashes. Al Alam Camp (3 camps) hosts 2,312 individuals across the 3 sectors of the camp, mostly from Hawiga district (DRC Site Resident Database). It is expected that Sector 3 of the camp will be closed before the end of 2018, and the population will be moved to Sector 1. It is not possible to consolidate Sectors 2 and 3 at the same time, as this would leave no room for new arrivals into the camp. The continued delivery of support to Al Alam Camp is important in light of the risk of forced evictions from complexes and the need for a secondary displacement location for those families unable to return to their AoO. Although Qadissya Complex (1,040 HH) is a collective center, it meets the CCCM Cluster Strategy definition for a formal site. The complex was officially recognized by the Government, and a camp manager was appointed. Although the government has stated that there is no immediate intention to close Qadissya, recent eviction threats have arisen both there and at Dream City Complex. As there is currently no clear plan for closure, continued support will be needed for at least the first 3-6 months of 2019. It is expected that Dream City will close before Qadissya, and many of the current population of Dream City, particularly HH whose AoO is Seniya, will move into Qadissya Complex. Qayyarah (36,486 individuals): 23 months after the opening of the camp, the Qayyarah Airstrip is still receiving new arrivals (300 HH/month for the past three months). 48% of IDPs interviewed are planning to go back to their AoO, but only 35% of respondents stated that they might be able to after 6 months (REACH). Those who choose to return are often at high risk of secondary displacement due to lack of livelihood opportunities and services, partially or completely destroyed shelter, eviction, and security and protection concerns. According to the GoI, Qayyarah is expected to remain open for 2019, requiring effective camp management, service provision and ongoing care and maintenance services, including drainage, roads, generator, fencing, lighting and waste management to mitigate the risk of flooding (Qayyarah has flooded a minimum of 6x per year during the rainy season due to improper drainage and poor quality roads) and reduce risks to health and safety. The tents in the Airstrip have been used for far longer than their 6-month lifespan. Many roads in poor condition, reducing accessibility. Community Resource Center: While the GoI and the JCMC have begun a process of joint planning for consolidation with the humanitarian community through GRCs, threats of closure continue at a field and camp level, demonstrating little consideration for process, guidelines and coordination and resulting in secondary displacement. The threat of forced eviction and return are anticipated to increase as the government resumes its agenda of return across the country. In areas of return, there is a severe need for support and coordination for formerly displaced families, particularly in the heavily destroyed cities of Baiji and Fallujah. In the past 4 months, DRC has opened two CRCs (one in Falluja and one in Bajii) to address the needs of the returnees and the most vulnerable households.

Activities

1. Direct camp management or camp management support activities- Direct camp management in Qayyarah Airstrip Camp and support to local authorities for camp management activities in HTC (9 camps), Al Alam camp (3 camps), and Qadissya Complex - Monitoring of camps and informal site services, infrastructure, and population dynamics to identify and refer needs and advocate on gaps identified. - Community participation through the engagement and training of camp committees, such as women, men and youth committees, and through the election of their camp residence representatives. - Coordination through weekly camp coordination meetings; active participation in the South Central CCCM cluster; and contribution of data and field level information to the Returns Working Group, Anbar and SAD Coordination Working Group and Governorate Returns Committees. In Qayyarah, as the designated camp management agency of the Qayyarah Airstrip Camp, DRC will continue to coordinate service provision with other actors in the camp as well as the neighboring Jeddah camps. - Operating multi-channel complaints/feedback mechanisms. 2. Capacity building for local authorities: DRC will continue to support national authorities and local partners to establish and/or maintain coordination mechanisms (e.g., regular camp coordination meeting, 3W, camp contact list, camp resident database) in formal camps and sites. In HTC and Al Alam camps, where the capacity of the government to support camp management to improve conditions in camps and informal sites is limited, DRC CCCM will provide materials and labour to respond to identified site maintenance, and risk reduction needs to ensure safe living environments. DRC CCCM will build on the enhanced capacity building initiative started in late 2017 to support HTC camp management in preparation for the eventual handover, camp consolidation, and closure; developing reference documents to guide camp management functions, procedures and responsibilities; and advocacy to encourage government support for camp management following the phase-out of the CCCM agency following the phase-out of the CCCM agency. For example, DRC is currently working on a camp consolidation plan in Al Alam and HTC camps with both camp managers and local authorities. DRC has trained the camp management team on camp closure and consolidation based on the global CCCM guidance. 3. Care and maintenance for camp and formal sites:- Site risk reduction activities to assess and respond to basic site maintenance and electrical connection issues in HTC, al Alam and Qayyarah camps. Specifically, due to relocations across camps, tents often need to be re-wired, and as a result of overuse of electricity sources, breakers often fault and need to be replaced. - Ensuring the safety of household electricity works, through the provision of the necessary materials and labour, overseen by DRC’s CCCM technical staff.- Care and maintenance activities to maintain and rehabilitate shelter and infrastructure that have deteriorated due to usage and weather conditions - Regular assessment and monitoring of the camp infrastructure, such as the Formal Site Monitoring Assessment- Site risk reduction activities to improve the safety and functionality of electricity and other household safety risks for IDPs and returnees living in urban areas 4. Established CCCM mobile teams for site management, monitoring and community engagement: DRC will continue to track, profile and respond to the basic needs of newly and secondarily displaced populations in Anbar and SAD through the Mobile Response Mechanism. The mobile response will continue on a regular basis after the initial population movement to monitor population dynamics, identify sites at risk of eviction/forced population movement, identify gaps in services and support referrals. This methodology also increases community acceptance, ensuring that DRC is well-placed to respond quickly. 5. Supporting the establishment or expansion of the CRC mechanism in high priority affected areas through operation of CRCs in Fallujah and Baiji as well as outreach and Rapid Village Assessment (RVA) mechanisms to inform and support area-level and governorate-level coordination and service delivery. RVA is an internal DRC tool used to inform multi-sector DRC response. In SAD, DRC has been operating an "area-based approach" facilitating integration across all DRC sectors to respond to identified needs. DRC will also use the tools developed by the CRC Steering Committee to report needs and gaps. CCCM will manage the CRC including operations and maintenance, identification of area service gaps and issues through managing a feedback and referral mechanism, information provision on services to new returnees and awareness sessions and training on current issues identified through community consultations.

Protection Risk Analysis

1. DRC Staff and volunteers are at risk of pressure from local authorities given their role in the camp/complex and the power they have over the database, distributions, complaints mechanisms etc. Particularly while the GoI and the JCMC have begun a process of joint planning for consolidation with the humanitarian community through governorate-level Returns Committees, threats of closure continue at a field and camp level, demonstrating little consideration for process, guidelines and coordination and resulting in secondary displacement. GoI pressure to close camps has led to forced evictions resulting in sudden population movements and secondary displacement.2. Beneficiaries living in formal sites are at risk of eviction and forced return, including the use of coercion, ID confiscation and violence, and usage of high-risk negative coping strategies. Lately, the GoI is pushing to close specific camps and site in Anbar and SAD causing forced evictions. DRC has been pushing back on this request considering that the most vulnerable IDPs are still remaining in camps without any possibility to go back to their place of origins. Perceived affiliation and retribution through violence, destruction of housing, inability to return, detention and disappearance are significant emerging threats, particularly for widowed female heads of household who are perceived to have had IS affiliation, and children perceived to be born of IS fighters, as those under suspicion face increased risk of rights violations, exploitation and violence, which is tantamount to collective punishment and in contravention of IHL. These risks will likely be compounded in 2019, as funding reduces for services in camps in favor of supporting return movements, and challenges persist in maintaining the humanitarian and civilian character of the camps. It is difficult to estimate the number of families affected, as often people only learn of their perceived status when they are denied return, and many live in mixed-population camps where collecting such information poses protection concerns. Others in out-of-camp settings remain unidentified due to access and resource constraints among humanitarian actors, or have refused humanitarian assistance due to fears of stigmatization.3. DRC interventions might be restricted due to ISF security procedures, in particular restrictions on movements (restricted entries into/exits from camps), detention of IDPs without warrant letters and harassment by armed actors.

Mitigation Measures

1. DRC continues to regularly meet and communicate with the local stakeholders in the camp and complex, including the Government Camp Management, Mayor, Police, etc. to ensure a clear understanding about the responsibilities of DRC staff, the procedures DRC must adhere to, and the decision making authority of those colleagues in the camp. Increased efforts have also been taken to clarify which members of the governorate local authority are authorized to make decisions regarding the camp and complex status. In response to persistent barriers to return, DRC will continue supporting protractedly displaced populations through CCCM response in Anbar (HTC) Salah al-Din (Al Alam and Qadissya) and Ninewa (Qayyarah Airstrip), combining direct service provision and capacity building, and complemented by specialized protection responses. Through direct camp management or camp management support, DRC will continue supporting the provision of camp services though activities such as the information center for IDPs in HTC, monthly coordination meetings with the Emergency Cell/authorities, daily coordination of service providers and camp managers, and monitoring of camp services, infrastructure, and population dynamics. In HTC and Al Alam camps, where the capacity of the government tosupport camp management to improve conditions in camps and informal sites is limited, DRC CCCM will provide materials and labor to respond to identified site maintenance and risk reduction needs to ensure safe living environments.2. DRC CCCM and Protection Teams have been working closely together to advocate for returns and complex closures to be carried out in line with the Principled Returns Framework, ensuring the rights and dignity of IDPs are respected at all times. DRC has led the advocacy efforts to OCHA and other stakeholders through the GRC and other forums when forced returns and evictions threats have been identified. DRC has also been one of a number of partners carrying out Protection Monitoring on site in the event of these types of situations occurring. 3. DRC complies with security rules of ISF, however, the issue arises if ad hoc ISF commands collide with the humanitarian principles. DRC continues to actively advocate for the respect of humanitarian principles and international humanitarian law. By the same token, DRC remains careful in choosing its channels of advocacy in order not to jeopardize its good relations to and support granted by ISF.

How is your project accountable to affected population

DRC is committed to ensuring that the highest level of accountability to affected populations is upheld and that different segments of the population can actively participate in the planning and implementation of activities. DRC will establish/maintain representational and sector-focused committees (women's, youth, maintenance, etc.), provide material support to implement activities, and support dissemination of information among the site residents. DRC puts a special emphasis on the inclusion of women and girls in committee structures and site representation/self-governance structures. Relevant training will be provided to site representatives and committees for them to effectively participate in management, decision-making and community mobilization.DRC is in full agreement with the UN zero tolerance policy relating to sexual exploitation and abuse. All DRC staff and volunteers sign the Code of Conduct (CoC). This year, DRC produced new awareness raising materials contain specific details of how to report abuse in each different context, complemented by awareness raising sessions led by DRC protection to inform beneficiaries about different types of exploitation and abuse and how to report them. DRC PDM contains specific questions giving respondents an opportunity to disclose potential SEA occurrences or enabling conditions. All DRC contractors must sign the Code of Ethics, and DRC conducted CoC training with suppliers operating in the Qayyarah Airstrip this year. DRC operates complaint and feedback hotlines covering all governorates of operation, with focal points appropriately trained on the CoC reporting mechanism and confidentiality.DRC has functioning complaints and feedback mechanisms in Al Alam Camp (complaint caravan), the Beiji CRC (feedback desk), Qadissiya complex, HTC (one static and one mobile) and the Qayyarah Airstrip (feedback desk) to receive and respond to issues and concerns. In all DRC sites, a full referral mechanism is in place for referral to the most appropriate actor, whether DRC or external. DRC CCCM teams register all inquiries and processes them accordingly, including internal and external referrals. DRC regularly advertises the IDP call center as an alternative mechanism for IDPs to raise concerns. DRC has active community participation mechanisms in all sites. In Al Alam camp and Qadissya complex, DRC has a network of focal points who DRC meet on a weekly basis to receive concerns and comments from the community, and for DRC to feedback on previously raised issues. These focal points have a good level of acceptance amongst the community, and regularly raise concerns to DRC. DRC also uses these meetings to invite focal points to bring suggestions from community members for DRC activities and programming, including for trainings and capacity building opportunities.DRC has a network of community mobilizers and other volunteers who actively engage in all activities in the camp and complex, and provide extensive inputs from the community perspective. In HTC, DRC has a good working relationship with the Emergency Cell (central management of HTC), camp managers and the community. Community mobilisers engage with the community on a regular basis to bring specific issues and concerns to the attention of DRC. DRC also organizes regular meetings with the camp managers and monthly coordination meetings with the camp managers and other service providers.In Qayyarah, DDRC also manages a complaints desk for sensitive issues once a week linked with our hotline. Meetings with sector representatives (women, men, and youth) are convened on a bi-weekly basis, which has been very effective in building trust between the CCCM team and the committees and promoting accountability by creating a regular forum for raising residents' concerns. Camp committee leaders are regularly invited to participate in meetings held with partners to engage closely with them and raise their issues directly.
