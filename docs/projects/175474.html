Project Summary

Burundi is facing a crisis that combines the devastating effects of natural disasters and burden of communicable diseases in the country. It is located in an earthquake-prone geographic zone of the Great Lakes, thereby increasing the risk of the country to be affected by a catastrophic event. Natural disasters due to climate change such as floods and storms have negatively impacted the country through repeated displacement. Occasionally, dangerous storms involving violent winds and torrential rains cause severe damage to property and populations, particularly in rural areas.
According to the latest DTM data, at the end of August 2020, the number of IDPs identified in Burundi’s 18 provinces stood at 131,336 (28,333 households), of which 83 per cent were displaced due to natural disasters , including a majority of women (55%). Using Emergency Event Tracking figures, IOM estimates that between August 2018 and August 2020, 8,969 houses were totally destroyed and 17,075 houses were partially destroyed due to climatic events (142 torrential rains, 12 severe landslides, 65 strong winds, 7 main destructive floods and hail). The protection monitoring reports of UNHCR show that a majority of returnees did not have access to their houses and did not have viable income-generating activities – leaving families with the critical need to find emergency shelter with negative impact on the health status.
At the end of June 2020, the number of IDPs identified in Burundi’s 18 provinces stood at 136,610 (29,384 households).
Climate change also presents significant direct and indirect risks to public health. Many aspects of health are also influenced by climatic and weather conditions and should be considered in this response. A direct link to health includes illnesses from increased frequency and severity of extreme weather events such as precipitation, flooding, droughts, and storms. The ongoing cholera outbreak that was declared in December 2018 started from Rumonge. The outbreak is linked to heavy rains that caused flooding and consequently contamination of water, which allowed it to spread. Malaria is also a major public health issue in the country and was the second leading cause of mortality in Burundi in 2017.
In 2018, there were approximately 3.1 million reported cases of malaria or approximately 8,500 cases per day Cases increased especially during the rainy season, which is a factor that is often linked to flooding resulting in standing water that creates breeding sites for mosquitoes. Given the struggling public health system in Burundi, capacity strengthening of the local health systems is essential in order to increase resilience and adapt to the changing climate’s effect on health. Finally, On January 23, 2019, 162 cases of malaria, including 2 deaths, were reported in Rumonge and Bujumbura Mairie and more than 90% of the cases were identified in Rumonge.
The ongoing worldwide COVID-19 pandemic, which started in China in Hubei province, has not spared the Republic of Burundi. The first cases of COVID-19 were declared on the 31st of March 2020. As of 10th, December, a total number of confirmed cases is 732 with 2 associated official deaths.
WHO has recommended several measures including but not limit to, social distancing. Displaced people, such as those located in Gatumba internal displaced sites, are more vulnerable to COVID-19 because they are living in high densely populated where social distancing, addition to the lack of sanitation can be challenging.
Moreover, internal displaced people are exposed to violence, belongings and family member’s loss which can have grave impact on mental health. When the internal displaces status is combined with the fear of contracting communicable disease including coronavirus, anxiety, depression, sleep disorders, psychological distress and post-traumatic stress disorder will increase and impact well-being and daily activities.
