Project Summary

The WHO’s HRP project aims to contribute to the reduction of excess morbidity and mortality resulting from health emergencies and common communicable and non-communicable diseases in South Sudan. The common emergencies that are likely to impact the vulnerable populations in 2022, and 2023 include epidemic disease outbreaks, flooding, conflicts, and acute food insecurity. The project objectives will be achieved through the provision of time-critical, life-saving frontline quality health services, ensuring the availability of uninterrupted quality health services, quality essential medicines and supplies, and well-trained healthcare workers in the health facilities and clinics.
In addition to the above, WHO will capacitate the implementing partners and counties to provide quality health services through training of frontline health workers and provision of guidelines and SoPs on case management of common communicable and non-communicable diseases, and provision of safe maternal and child services, mental and psychosocial care. Furthermore, WHO will deploy a multidisciplinary team; the emergency Mobile Medical Teams (eMMTs), to provide integrated health services in deep field locations through mobile outreaches to bridge service delivery gaps in areas with critical unmet health needs.
The country’s capacities to prevent, prepare for, detect, and respond to emergencies will be improved at all levels. The public health surveillance, IDSR/EWARN, will be strengthened through continued investment in the system and expansion of reporting sites to enable timely detection, investigation, and response to public health events. The routine surveillance system will be enhanced through the deployment of mobile phone based EWARS reporting by the static and mobile clinics, and provision of technical support to the implementing partners and counties. This will enhance weekly reporting by facilities to ensure early detection of and response to disease outbreaks. Besides, the national and state Rapid Response Teams (RRTs), as well as WHO Mobile Medical Teams (eMMT), will be deployed to conduct risk assessments, alert, and outbreak investigation, and to provide initial public health response during emergencies to reduce excess morbidity and mortality. Moreover, WHO will support the deployment of the eMMTs and RRTs to conduct rapid needs assessments to identify the unmet health needs in project locations to inform appropriate responses by health actors and other relevant sectors. As part of outbreak prevention and response, WHO will lead the implementation of preventive and reactive vaccination campaigns for vaccine-preventable diseases such as measles and cholera.
Further, WHO will be conducting regular water quality testing and surveillance, IPC/WASH assessments in health facilities and implementing IPC measures as a component of risk identification and mitigation efforts to healthcare-related infections. Lastly, the project will contribute to enhancing the capacities for diagnosis and management of severe acute malnutrition (SAM) and associated medical complications with a view of reducing avoidable mortality in collaboration with the Nutrition Cluster.
Finally, WHO will address SGBV and MHPSS needs in the country by mainstreaming the needs of affected people into ongoing programming, and work with organizations of persons living with disability to assess and address their needs.
Overall the above strategy and interventions will contribute to the three health cluster objectives of (1) Improving equitable access to life-saving essential quality health care services including maternal, child, and adolescent health, sexual and reproductive health, treatment for common illnesses, disability, MHPSS, and GBV health services to crisis-affected populations 2) Reducing excess morbidity and mortality by timely detection and coordinated response to epidemic-prone diseases, and 3) Enhancing resilience and promoting humanitarian-development linkages.

1 - Needs

Cycles of political conflict that started in 2013, and the subsequent sub-national violence that followed, coupled with acute food insecurity, flooding, and population displacement is responsible for the ongoing complex humanitarian situation in South Sudan that has left 8.9 million people in need of humanitarian assistance (HRP 2022). South Sudan reported eight emergencies and disease outbreaks in 2021 including the Hepatitis E virus, CVDP2, Yellow Fever, and the COVID-19 pandemic. In 2022, extensive flooding affected over 1 million people across 37 counties; this is in addition to high levels of food insecurity that affected an estimated 7.74 million people, and localized violence In Upper Nile, Warrap, Unity, Jonglei, and Central Equatoria that caused population displacement. Moreover, the country suffered from several disease outbreaks including the ongoing explosive measles outbreaks that have affected 15 counties where 1621 cases have been reported with 21 deaths; the cholera outbreak in Rubkona where 424 cases were reported and 1 death; the persistent Hepatitis E virus outbreak in Rubkona where 3,589 cases were reported with 26 deaths; cutaneous anthrax outbreak in Gogrial West that registered 109 cases with 4 deaths. These innumerable challenges eroded the country’s already weakened coping capacity further increasing health insecurities. In 2023, the number of people in acute food insecurity (IPC Phase 3 or worse) is expected to increase to 7.78 million during the lean season (April-July), with some 1.4 million children U5 projected to face acute malnutrition. GAM rates were high (12% to 21%) in 2022 in most of the affected states, matched by proportionate high rates of SAM admissions in stabilization centers across the locations (IPC 2022). Moreover, poor WASH conditions are a major contributor to high malnutrition, yet South Sudan has very poor access to WASH services. In 2021, only 39% of the population had access to enough safe water to meet their household needs, 49% relied on surface unimproved water, and 80% of households lacked latrines (FSNMS, 2021). There is a high prevalence of waterborne diseases; 16% in children U 5 (FSNMS, 2021). Moreover, conflict and disasters exacerbate mental health and disabilities in the affected population. According to FSNMS+ 2021 qualitative study, there is a 15% prevalence of disability in South Sudan, the most affected are women representing 60% of the population. In 2019, WHO reported approximately 2.5 million mental disorders in conflict settings in South Sudan. Mental health execrates the health burden on the county’s population.Moreover, South Sudan remains among the poorest countries in the world (over 80%) limiting the ability of the government to provide basic social services due to years of under-investment, conflict, and disasters. There is a high dependency on humanitarian aid, only 7.9% of the country’s national budget goes to the health sector contributing to low financial risk protection. As a result, there is a general deficiency in service level availability across most sectors with significantly poor performance on key development indicators, for instance, there is low health worker density reported at 6.3 per 10,000 population compared to 44.5 per 10,000 population recommended by WHO, one physician for every 65,574 persons and most health facilities are poorly equipped and staffed, as such, only 44% of the population stay within 5 km radius of a health facility and low immunization coverage (MCV coverage ranged from 39% to 89%, and penta3 coverage ranged from 46% to 119% across states in August 2022) that predisposes children vaccine-preventable diseases (DHIS2 2022). As a result, the country has very high under-five mortality rates (90.7 deaths per 1,000 live births), maternal mortality rates (789 deaths per 100,000 live births), neonatal mortality rate (39 per 1000), and infant mortality rate (60 per 1,000 live births in the world (WHO CCS, 2018).

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

WHO will utilize community networks and structures to ensure feedback is taken from vulnerable members of the community. In this case, disability networks/organizations, women, and youth groups will be used to collect feedback from respective segments of the vulnerable communities. WHO will also use community leaders/representatives and caretakers in the case of children and severely impaired who have the knowledge of the needs and challenges vulnerable people face to elicit feedback against which interventions will be designed. Feedback from the most vulnerable members of the community will be used to support mainstreaming of vulnerable and at-risk groups’ needs into regular health programming and advocate for inclusive programming that takes care of the needs. It will also be used to increase representation and participation of at-risk and vulnerable groups through community meetings, the direct participation of at-risk groups in the day-to-day implementation, and monitoring of project activities Where protection risk has been identified in the feedback from affected people, WHO will use the information to engage the protection stakeholders to increase protection assistance and safeguards for beneficiaries of health services.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

WHO will work with the Ministry of Health, health partners and communities to roll out several levels of context-specific feedback and complaint mechanisms to sure affected people are consulted, informed, participating, and are given timely feedback. Gender and age-structured Focused Group Discussions (FGD) will be used to ensure that children, and adults as well as male and female gender are able to inform the choices the project will take.Project inception meetings will be used to provide important information such as the project purpose, target group and criteria, project benefits, donors, and how different stakeholders will participate in implementation and monitoring. Telephone hotlines will be used where communities can call to provide feedback where mobile networks are available, alternative feedback mechanisms will include a community complaints desk, Gender, and age, Focused Group Discussions (FGD), suggestion boxes at facility levels, and Key Informant Interviews (KI), will be used to gather feedback from the affected people. WHO eMEMTs will conduct pre-consultation addresses and use banners to inform communities about services they are provided and how different types of people including vulnerable groups will be helped, this approach provides a two-way channel where beneficiaries speak directly to the WHO and their grievances are addressed, thus they are able to participate in choosing locations for the mobile clinics among others. WHO also does post-distribution monitoring of the medical supplies where clients’ satisfaction is assessed, this will also provide another avenue for a feedback mechanism. The States and County authorities will participate in identifying project sites, identification of healthcare workers to be trained, and alongside community leaders and health facility management committees monitoring the implementation of health interventions. This is in addition to the State and the National Coordination mechanisms where partners and community representatives will share their feedback and complaints about the project. The proposed feedback mechanism will be used in complementarity with each other based on the context and feasibility of each mechanism. Information gathered will be used to provide feedback and improve service delivery to the affected people.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

WHO has a zero-tolerance policy on PSEA and has established structures and systems for disseminating and handling all PSEA issues. There is a substantive PSEAH focal at the Country office and works with states to ensure safeguarding standards are applicable across the country program. At the national level, WHO has mainstreamed PSEA into all media briefs by the senior leadership for partners and communities to appreciate WHO’s role in safeguarding, as such, radio talk shows, and public briefs are used as a platform to sensitize communities about WHO’s PSEA policies. At the community level, the state’s offices have PSEA policies posted in offices, vehicles, and public boards with clear steps for reporting. Staff, contractors, and volunteers do mandatory PSEA courses and participate in regular town halls on PSEA and they have also signed PSEA policies as part of their contractual agreements. Through these channels, WHO informs its staff on the immediate and confidential mechanisms to report abuses including for WHO staff members and WHO collaborators or any other person who may have been a victim of PSEA or may have witnessed or otherwise been informed of a case of PSEA involving WHO, as such, all staff members at all levels organization-wide, collaborators and beneficiaries who have reason to believe a SEA situation may have occurred, by a WHO staff or collaborator, or another UN international civil servant, must report either by contacting the PSEA focal points, the ethicsoffice@who.int. or through the Integrity Hotline, where they will receive confidential advice.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Feedback on WHOs health interventions is anticipated to come from established formal and informal complaints and feedback mechanisms e.g Mobile Team briefings during mobile clinics, Post distribution monitoring exercises for medical supplies, age, and gender structured Focused Group Discussions (FGDs), meetings with community leaders, health facility committees, health cluster partners, Boamah Health Teams, representatives of vulnerable members, and local authorities as well as participatory joint monitoring missions. Community feedback obtained during the implementation of mobile clinics will be used to inform the design of the clinics to suit the unique context and needs of the population e.g selection of locations that take care of protection concerns of at-risk groups, the timing of activities, disability-inclusive health programming among others. PDM feedback will be used to inform the design and implement quality core pipeline activities e.g allocation of IEHKs to communities based on the need to achieve humanitarian imperative, improve warehousing, storage, handling, rational use to avoid wastage. Feedback from joint monitoring missions will be used to design capacity-building initiatives for communities and health care workers and ensure the project is achieving its intended objectives.

Yes

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

The project will continue to advocate for a resilience-building approach in its investments in the health sector to ensure durable solutions are sought to the flood-related displacements. Besides its contribution to disease outbreaks ,floods has had severe effects on the health infrastructure and supplies affecting the delivery of health services. WHO will continue investing in the health systems resilience and advocating through ICCG with other clusters to ensure dams, water ponds and environmentally friendly approaches to development is pursued to address the flooding

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

WHOs Mobile Medical Teams provide health response to SGBV cases during mobile team deployments especially clinical management of rape, addressing the effects of physical violence due to SGBV such as injuries, provision of onsite psychosocial support and referral of cases to relevant support centers. WHO will also train health care workers on Clinical Management of Rape to ensure existence of capacity in the target locations to provide health services even beyond the project. WHO also works with local health workers to provide the needed medical reports to support victims to access legal services. As a result, these activities will contribute to overall GBV response and reduction

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

As part of mobile medical outreaches, the project will aim to provide nutritional screening of children under 5 years old with the view of identifying malnourished children as early as possible. The identified malnourished children will be linked with the existing nutrition intervention in targeted areas. Those with medical complications will be referred to stabilization centers for further management.
