Project Summary

Following the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development (2015), the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015), and the One Humanity Shared Responsibility: Report of the Secretary-General for the World Humanitarian Summit (2016), as the Charter on Inclusion of Persons with Disabilities in Humanitarian Action, the IASC Guidelines for the Inclusion of persons with disabilities in Humanitarian Action, 2019, inclusion has become key in the humanitarian response. However, despite commitments to leave no one behind, persons with disabilities remain amongst the most likely to be left out of humanitarian assistance. Simultaneously, the vicious cycle of poverty, conflict, and barriers contribute to an accumulation of insurmountable barriers in accessing information and essential services and prevent de-facto the full spectrum of protection mainstream principles when it comes to humanitarian response. Protection concerns in the occupied Palestinian territories (oPt) range from the consequences of the long-lasting occupation to recurrent severe issues in protecting civilians in Gaza. At the same level, Palestinians living under full control of the Palestinian authority (PA) have experienced several protection concerns linked to intra-political tension and shrinking of civic space. The Multi Sectorial Needs Assessment (MSNA) 2022 preliminary finding and analysis on protection concerns and barriers to aid, reports that in West Bank, 6.8% of interviewed households (HHS) have difficulties accessing services, while in Gaza percentage rises to 17.8%. Barriers to access range from the cost of accessing services (transportation), distance from it (specifically in the West Bank), and lack of physical accessibility of the services, especially in Gaza. In terms of satisfaction with the services received, 41.1% of the HHs in Gaza reported dissatisfaction, with a high prevalence of HHs with members with disabilities (49.7% out of the 41.1% overall), while in the West Bank percentage is 16.6%, reporting most of the issue on the quantity of aid. The MSNA 2022 reports as well a striking prevalence of disability across Gaza, with 21% of the population reporting some form of difficulties, 1 out of 4 being under the age of 18; the percentage is 6% higher than the global average and close to ex in-conflict or in-conflict areas such as Iraq and Syria. In Gaza, the population reports as main constraints in terms of equitable access, barriers to accessing education services, health and shelter services; among coping mechanisms, HH with disabilities, 24.3% report reducing food consumption.
The project aims at promoting the concept of disability Inclusion as part of protection mainstreaming. The project will consider the intersectionality between humanitarian crisis and protection risks, with a specific focus on persons with disabilities, operating on the four pillars of protection mainstreaming: 1. Prioritize safety & dignity; 2 –Meaningful Access; 3 – Accountability; 4 – Participation and empowerment. Humanity & Inclusion (HI) proposes targeted advocacy, training, and coaching to better identify and include persons with disabilities in the humanitarian response. By capitalizing on the cluster’s commitment, HI will specifically advocate for collecting data on disability using the Washington Group Questions (WGQs). Advocacy efforts will be complemented by training and providing technical assistance to humanitarian actors on integrating WGQs into needs assessments and dedicated support on the operationalization of disability inclusion. The above will be achieved by building the capacity of members of 7 main clusters operating in Gaza and the West Bank. The action will be implemented in coordination with the disability working group (DWG) in Gaza and will guarantee that representatives of persons with disabilities are involved and taking an active role in mainstreaming disability.

1 - Needs

The MSNA 2022 has identified 21% (of whom 9.3% is reported a minor 18) of the population of Gaza reporting one or more difficulty, followed by 9.7% in West Bank (of whom 4% is reported a minor 18). Across these percentages, in the West Bank, 95.6% reported a barrier to accessing healthcare, 42.1% of the overall HHs in need of shelter are more likely to suffer damages and defects, and 3% of the unemployed population report to be because of barriers to work. 88.9% of households with children with disabilities face additional challenges accessing education services. In Gaza, 99.7% of the 21% HHs reporting a member with disabilities has had difficulty accessing health care, 11.2% report barriers to livelihood income/activities, and 93.5% of HHs with children with disabilities report barriers to education. Particularly critical is the situation on education, where 26% of male and 33% of female is a risk of drop-out or has already dropped out, entering the risk of early marriage and/or child labour (HI, Gender Study, 2021). According to the last Protection analysis for West Bank and Gaza, out of the total 2,009,843 people in need identified (1.021.561 in West Bank and 988.282 in Gaza), 148.242 are persons with disabilities. Protection trigger factors vary from one location to another. In the West Bank, factors are driven by the Israeli military occupation, the separation wall, excessive use of force, eviction, displacement, restriction of movements and settlers’ activities. In Gaza, protection factors are triggered by recurrent escalation, lack of economic pathways and freedom of movement. All these factors result in a large portion of the population needing protection activities and services of different natures, including FSS, Health, Shelter, WASH, and Education. In both contexts, rising concerns are associated with inter-family violence, GBV and child abuse. When it comes to protracted humanitarian crises, persons with disabilities, specifically women and children, are disproportionately affected and exposed to heightened risks, and remain among the most likely to be left out of humanitarian assistance as they encounter multiple environmental, institutional, and attitudinal barriers. Awareness and policy developments have led to more concrete changes and commitments to disability inclusion; however relevant gaps remain in ensuring no one is left behind. For this reason, disability inclusion is essential, starting at the coordination level and extending to targeted action by humanitarian agencies. Keeping in mind the four ‘must-do’ actions outlined by the IASC Guidelines, emphasis must be placed on collecting data on disability via active outreach and identification; barrier removal via adaptation of services, infrastructure, and communication; and increased participation via meaningful engagement of persons with disabilities. Within the past year consistent improvements in data collection and evidence-based analysis were achieved at the level of HNO, yet, based on evidence from HI’s work on Inclusive Humanitarian Action on a global consultation to collect best practices on disability inclusion, and on recent learnings of humanitarian planning processes through a Focused Group Discussion (FGD) conducted with humanitarian actors across the Education Cluster and protection consortiums, the following gaps and challenges remain 1. Lack of reliable data and analysis at Humanitarian partners' level to inform programming 2. Turning guidelines and standards into practice: humanitarian actors willing to mainstream disability often lack the technical know-how to turn guidance into practice to provide an adapted response. 3. Access to mainstream humanitarian services and assistance is not systematically monitored, and interventions for persons with disabilities are often perceived as being limited to specialized services without consideration of their mainstream needs. 4. Clear advocacy pathways on inclusive budgets that allow adaptability of services.

2 - Activities

The overall objective of the project is to contribute to protection mainstreaming in the humanitarian response in the oPt both at the coordination level - Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO) and Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP), and at the operational level. This will be achieved via capacity building of estimated 200 non-protection professionals’ practitioners representing humanitarian organizations in Gaza and the West Bank at different levels, including local and international non-governmental organizations. The capacity building will be throughout a specific series of disability-related training that entails essential disability inclusion, interaction with persons with disabilities, and disability identification by using WGQs; this will be followed by data analysis and disability-inclusive indicators and ultimately operationalizing inclusion. The logic of intervention relies on three outputs. Output 1: Humanitarian Response is able to operationalize disability inclusion throughout all sectorial responses. Activity 1.1 Establish a Technical Working Group on Disability Inclusion co-led by Protection Cluster and HI, with the membership of a diversity of clusters and organizations with various technical backgrounds; Activity 1.2 Design a TOR and Action Plan for the working group; Activity 1.3 Conduct consultations and needs assessment of partners concerning their technical support needs with regards to inclusion mainstreaming; Activity 1.4 Develop a minimum package of training to support on disability Inclusion; Activity 1.5 Advocate for the inclusion of persons with disabilities towards Humanitarian Leadership and in relevant coordination fora and processes (cross-result 1 and 2 via elaboration of specific messaging including around the concept of inclusive-budgeting in the humanitarian response). Output 2: The HNO and HRP structure adapts their tracking tools and skills to better identify transformative inclusive actions for persons with disabilities. Activity 2.1 Capacity building of clusters members on disability-sensitive indicators and the use of WGQs; Activity 2.2 In collaboration with clusters and their partners, produce contextualized chapter notes and general guidance on the relevant sectors for HNO and HRP process; Activity 2.3 Support the development of disability scoring system considering gender with age and disability markers; Activity 2.4 Support the cluster(s) to monitor the inclusion of persons with disabilities in assessments as well as assistance and service provision upon request of specific review. Output 3: Local organizations of persons with disabilities are able to conduct non-technical accessibility assessments (NTAA) via capacity building, ensuring their ongoing participation in humanitarian efforts; Activity 3.1 Capacity building of Organizations of Persons with Disabilities (OPDs) on conducting NTAA.The project upholds Community engagement (CE), specifically by building the capacity of 200 non-protection practitioners on inclusion mainstreaming; this includes also interaction with persons with disabilities. Through this strengthened interaction, persons with disabilities will be able to meaningfully receive and provide information, as well as feedback through an existing Feedback Complaint Mechanism (FCM). The proposed project will benefit from the current HI’ presence in both area, Gaza and West Bank, and its collaboration with clusters in addition to the established networking and coordination with local and international actors in different sectors including protection, education, and health. All of the actions will be implemented in coordination with disability working group (DWG) to ensure that representatives of persons with disabilities are involved. This will be ensured by involving them, as well as OPDs, in the implementation of needs assessment, development of action plans, supporting the implementation of training courses, and disseminating disability inclusive good practices. It will enrich the proposed capacity building and enhance new awareness and knowledge on how to propose inclusive responses during the implementation of humanitarian responses. In Gaza, the activities will be channelled in coordination with the DWG.

3 - Indicators

HI adopts a monitoring system that will ensure proper progress tracking and capture any challenges in the implementation in order to deliver the requested support on time. This system is composed of: I) a monitoring tool (PMBox) developed at the project’s inception phase and regularly monitored during the project’s implementation period. This tool tracks the progress of different activities, achievements, and indicators; it also allows for identifying the challenges and delays within the project activities and identifying potential solutions. II) A project review meeting is scheduled and conducted every four months according to HI standards. During this meeting, all the departments and the project team discuss the progress of the project’s activities and the achievement of results. Additionally, recommendations for improvements are identified and followed to ensure the quality and timely implementation of activities. III) Technical monitoring can be provided by the Regional MEAL and Technical Units based in Jordan, as required.Concerning the indicators of the project, HI will report against the following standard indicator: number of humanitarian partner organisations who received specialized training in disability inclusion. The above indicator is the one identified by the protection cluster for disability mainstreaming training responding to one activity which is "specialized training in disability inclusion". The monitoring exercise will be pre & and post-tests. In addition to the above protection cluster standard indicator, HI identified a set of custom indicators to better measure the impact of the intervention. This includes; 1) % of 7 clusters members integrating and strengthening the disability concept within their framework of the actions. 2) # clusters members’ receiving training on disability sensitive data. 3) # of disability scoring system developed. 4) # local organizations of persons with disability receiving training on conducting Non-technical Accessibility Assessments (NTAA). The measurement of the afore-mentioned indicators will be conducted through pre & post tests and activity reports. The M&E of the project is designed in accordance with HI Planning, Monitoring and Evaluation Policy, which is derived from HI institutional policies on Child Protection (CP), Protection Against Sexual Exploitation Abuse and Harassment (PSEAH), Prevention of and Fight Against Bribery (PFAB), Code of Conduct (CoC), and Safety and Security (SaS). The M&E system complies with IASC guidelines on the inclusion of Persons with Disabilities in Humanitarian Action through the application of Common Humanitarian Standards (CHS) Commitments 4 and 5, SPHERE Standards, and Humanitarian Accountability Partnership (HAP). A Feedback and Complaint Mechanism (FCM) exists and will allow different stakeholders to raise their concerns through different available modes of reception such as complaining via: a phone line, email, boxes, HI staff, and data collection exercises. Data confidentiality protocols are in place. All HI Palestine staff have received training on the HI safeguarding approach by the Safeguarding focal point. HI also has internal (via intranet, email) and external (website, email, post) complaints and whistle-blowing mechanisms with the clear investigation and disciplinary procedures. A PSEAH toolbox is available on HI’s intranet to implement the existing policy. Beneficiaries with and without disabilities are able to fill complaints if needed.

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

Feedback will be analysed and interpreted to ensure proper learning and improvement of interventions provided. The feedback received through different channels (such as phone line, monitoring exercises, training evaluations, field staff, etc.) will be recorded once received in a secured database that is accessible only to concerned staff. 10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

The proposed intervention considers gender, age, and disability during the process of design, implementation, monitoring, and evaluation. The HI policies and DGA marker exist to ensure that the proposed intervention contributes to non-discrimination practices. The capacity-building activities rely on the experience of HI in the field of implementing training on disability mainstreaming. Considering the nature of the project - no direct beneficiaries - HI recognize the importance of promoting and reinforcing the do no harm principle. As such, HI commits to sharing information on the promotion of do no harm principle through the different capacity-building activities and indirectly enhance humanitarian action in some of the fundamental dimensions of the do no harm approach such as the need for identification among the vulnerable community and tailored activities in a way that positive change of inclusion does no harm by inadvertently reinforcing existing inequalities and discrimination. In such regard, it is expected that the capacity on the matter will be reinforced by analyzing major driving factors that may present harm for persons with disabilities and tailored the capacity building of partners in addressing them. However, the full adherence of this principle in daily practices and operations can solely be guaranteed by partners and their commitment towards this goal.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

Inclusion and as such equal and impartial access to services is at the core of the project and will be ensured to all indirect beneficiaries including persons with disabilities considering theirs needs, age and gender, through supporting the HNO process collecting and analyse disability sensitive data that will surely support partners to effectively operationalized the concept of inclusion while they are developing their interventions. In addition, the HRP process will be supported to include more inclusive and responsive actions and indicators. Additionally, specific support will be provided to the humanitarian actors on the identification of barriers and adaptation of services that will allow equal access to services, hence operationalizing the concept of disability inclusion, with attention to children and women, to guarantee equal access to aid. In such regard, HI, together with the selected partners, will develop a minimum package of training to be delivered to support on disability Inclusion with the aim of adapting partners’ activities under an inclusive lens, hence guarantee equal access to services by final beneficiaries. The training package will include as much specific modules on SOPs related minimum indicators on access, allowing partners to monitoring the impact of their activities in terms of reach out to persons with disabilities and report based on the disaggregation on gender, age and disability.
