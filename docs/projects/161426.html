Project Summary

Through the proposed intervention, Coalition for Humanity (CH) will continue giving integrated lifesaving nutrition responses to conflict affected communities in two locations of former Jonglei state namely Fangak and Nyirol County. The proposed intervention will provide life-saving nutrition support targeting children <5 years of age, PLW, and patients with chronic diseases as the most vulnerable categories from acute malnutrition due to their high bio- physiological demands through management of moderate and severe acute malnutrition, at existing health facilities. Coalition for Humanity will promote prevention activities to address the immediate and risk factors of malnutrition through mother support groups and community nutrition volunteers whose capacity will be strengthen and tasked as entry point to reach and support caregivers/mothers. The nutrition staffs and County Health Department teams will be capacitated and equipped with knowledge and skills to respond to nutrition emergencies on time to manage moderate and severe acute malnutrition cases.
Through the proposed humanitarian response and intervention plan, Coalition for Humanity (CH) will targets to reach 2,500 children under five and 1200 PLW beneficiaries with acute malnutrition services in Fangak and Nyirol County in former Jonglei State. The intervention will provide lifesaving nutrition services to the most vulnerable, especially PLW and children < five years in the existing functional health facilities as well as mobile nutritional support services as outreach services for hard to reach distant community populations in coordination with humanitarian partners providing WASH, FSL and ensuring that the caregivers/mothers receive additional services at facility level.
CH will carry out nutrition assessments that provide robust information on the nutrition situation of the population, this information will be key in responding effectively to nutrition needs.
Coalition for Humanity will encourage the affected population to participate fully in the project implementation to ensure communities have information as well as provide feedback to continuously shape programing decisions. Community consultative meetings will be conducted on a quarterly basis by field nutrition officers in coordination with the M&E officer, to receive regularly feedback from the community and set an action plan on areas to improve project implementation at the supported sites.

1 - Needs

Generally in South Sudan, the increasing effect of six years of conflict, destroyed livelihoods has led to a humanitarian emergency of high demands. The revitalized peace agreement if implemented can give high chances and promises to offer new opportunities in the coming years for South Sudan’s women, men and children. However, Maternal and child malnutrition is a significant public health, social and economic problem faced by communities in South Sudan. The recent FSNMS round 24 results indicated prevalence of GAM of 16.2%, implying the country has crossed the emergency threshold of 15%, with the highest prevalence observed in Unity and Upper Nile were Coalition for Humanity South Sudan operates. Therefore, the current nutritional status of the county is classified as very high according to the new UNICEF/WHO thresholds. In the same survey of July 2019 data on IYCF shows that exclusive breastfeeding and early initiation of breastfeeding are 91.2% and 92.5%, respectively. Continued breastfeeding at one year for age 12-15 months is 92.7% while continued breastfeeding at 2 years (20-23 months) is 81.4%. However, introduction of solid or semi solid foods is still low (53.6%). hence, the need to continue and scale up both curative and preventative nutrition services is pertinent striving to minimize and alleviate the suffering of under five children. The country has been experiencing a deteriorating nutrition situation in the last five years (2014-2018). The burden of acute malnutrition remains significantly high, with over 1million children under five acutely malnourished in 2018. Results from the latest Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) May –July 2019 analysis show that 59% of population is expected to face acute food insecurity. This indicates that acute malnutrition remains a major public health challenge in South Sudan.August 2019 IPC report indicated that an estimated 1.25 million people, representing 64.8 percent of the state population are facing Crisis (IPC Phase 3) and Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity with 1,301,000 children 6-59 malnourished in need of treatment with 292,300 SAM cases, 1,008,700 MAM cases and 352,000 Pregnant and lactating women acutely malnourished in need of treatment. Nyirol County among other counties are in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) while Fangak County among others is in crisis IPC phase 3. This crisis has persisted due to broadly seasonal flooding which destroyed crops, limited food and income sources among displaced households and disrupted delivery of assistance and market functioning. Additionally, insecurity, cattle raiding and intercommunal conflict involving revenge killings have led to loss of lives, and disrupted livelihoods and restricted access to wild foods and fish.The overall nutrition situation during the lean season of 2019 is Critical and worse compared to same period in 2018. Based on the IPC AMN, all the 79 counties were included in the analysis of which 58 counties are classified as IPC AMN Phase 3 and above (GAM prevalence above 9.9%). 43 counties were classified as Critical (GAM prevalence of 15.0 – 29.9%, IPC AMN Phase 4). Most counties in Jonglei inclusive of Fangak and Nyirol are in critical levels of acute malnutrition.The worsening nutrition status and food security situation demonstrates that this is an area of concern requiring continuation of focused humanitarian assistance. The high rates of malnutrition in the proposed counties are attributed to several factors including high levels of food insecurity (IPC AFI Phase 3 and above), sub-optimal child care and feeding practices, lack of safe water and sanitation, internal displacement and conflict. Poor quality and diversity of food and high prevalence of diseases (up to 40%). Furthermore, poor access to health and nutrition services during the rainy season due to poor road infrastructure constrain service delivery in these areas already afflicted by high malnutrition levels.

6.6 - Will beneficiaries' rights be legally recognised?

Coalition for Humanity as humanitarian organization places the right of vulnerable households to ensure that they do not get harm in line with the DO NOT HARM principles. We ensure that the project activities are designed with the interest of the beneficiaries in mind and that they have say in how the project is going to be implemented.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

Yes. This project is designed to assist the IDPs, returnees and host communities and ensure their children have access to basic primary education in the selected locations. All displaced persons, returnees and host communities in the selected locations will be assisted to take their children to the selected schools without any discrimination.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

The project will be implemented under humanitarian response principles, however, with close consultation with all stakeholders including government and other clusters. Communication will be established well with all relevant actors including beneficiaries, local government to ensure that the nutrition project is aligned and work in complementary to all efforts by the other stakeholders working in education sectors and other areas. The temporary learning structures shall be made manner that they last to ensure long term improvement of education service to people in dire need especially in the targeted counties.

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

While the project is primarily going to meet urgent humanitarian response, the overall objective of this response are aligned to long national objective of education where IDPs, returnees and host communities are provided with support that could be built on to transit to long term development.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

The project locations are slowly returning to normalcy and basic nutrition and health systems are operating minimally although the areas still remain fragile. The local leaders, parents understand the importance of nutritional and health service therefore striving support humanitarian response in every way possible. This nutritional initiative is launched and coordinated with other partners and clusters in areas of WASH, Health and Food security to ensure there are maximum benefits to the children who have been affected by war for so long time. The project will provide a foundation for durable solutions, which will be realized progressively with efforts from different development partners. However, the general atmosphere is so far safe for the vulnerable persons including the IDPs, returnees and host communities.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

Yes. The project is designed with sustainability and clear strategy in mind. At the design stage of this project, thorough consultations were done with various groups of leaders to ensure that they understand the intervention, take ownership and take responsibility over the activities once the project is winded up.

8.18 - Please indicate how the risks selected above will be mitigated.

Coalition for Humanity take seriously the risks raised above and as it has done in previous programmes. CH will first consult with affected communities/beneficiaries to continuously map out risks and concerns affecting every different category of people benefiting from the intervention by coalition for Humanity. Local authorities and other partners including relevant clusters will be involved in mitigating any risks perceived or known. Secondly, CH’s planning will be done in manner that risks, perceived or known are identified and measures taken to address them promptly. For instance, distributions are to be done in places or areas where women and girls are not likely to get exposed to GBV related or protection concerns. Disabled or people with special needs (PSN) needed to be considered when it comes to location of assistance sites, distances travelled, arrangement at the site that is safe and support everyone according to the specific needs such as lactating and pregnant mothers to minimise disruption of childcare activities. CH takes proper assessments of risk before any intervention is carried to ensure safety of the beneficiaries.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

As a practice, our programme staffs carry out periodic focus group discussions and key informant interviews with separate groups (women, girls, boys and men) to find out how they feel about our service and how they would like to us to improve them. Separate FGDs for women and girls and one boys and women will be conducted. Some sensitive information where groups might not be comfortable to share will further be obtained through key informant interviews organized privately by our respective staff (males and females). In order to make everyone comfortable and free to speak, a thorough mobilization will be carried to explain the background of the exercise so that people will be free to speak and share their experiences without fear of being victimized. The beneficiaries might be illiterate in term of English but some might able to communicate in other languages such as Nuer, Dinka and Arabic. We would encourage those to provide feedback in the language they are comfortable with as we can understand or translate the feedback later on.
