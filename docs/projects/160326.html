Project Summary

Gender-based violence (GBV) is alarmingly present in all corners of South Sudan. GBV – including rape, sexual assault, domestic violence, forced and early marriage, sexual exploitation and abuse, abduction and other harmful practices – is a widespread, chronic and persistent violation of the basic human rights of girls and women. GBV is both a human rights and public health issue. GBV prevention and response is a precondition for equitable and inclusive recovery, sustainable development and human rights, as well as an important development objective.
Hence, CARE seeks to implement GBV prevention, response and risk mitigation activities by providing quality and timely age appropriate case management and psychosocial support to GBV survivors in Yambio, Upper Nile, Unity (Koch, Rubkona, Leer, Mayom, Abiemnom) Jongolei (Twic East, Panyagor, Uror, Duk, pachola, Pibor) Eastern Equatoria (Lafon, Ikwotoes, Magwi, Nimule,Torit and Lopa). As conflict in South Sudan enters its seventh year in 2020, ongoing fighting and surges of violence in some parts of the country have led to widespread human rights violations, forcing people to flee their homes (many multiple times). The compounding effects of widespread violence, recurring famine and sustained economic decline have further diminished the capacity of people to face threats to their health, safety and livelihoods and exacerbated gender based violence. In the recent What Works report up to 65% women experience physical and sexual violence in their life time in areas of conflict and 33% of women in the POC's experience sexual violence from non-partner and many of the incidents are associated to raids, displacement or abduction. Across the country there is increasingly desperate situation for women and girls, with an increased presence of soldiers and numerous checkpoints leaving women and girls fearful and at risk of GBV. Safety audit reports show that women and girls are beaten, raped, abducted, tortured and looted when collecting firewood and when accessing markets for basic needs. The proposed project contributes to the CARE 2020 and the Strategic Action Plan (SAP) for South Sudan strategy outcome, “strengthening individual voices” by supporting people of all genders and ages to make choices assert their voices and realize a life free of gender based violence. CARE will encourage health seeking behaviours among GBV survivors facilitating referrals, and creating supportive structures to prevent GBV. CARE will work in collaboration with local partners and will establish and operationalize women and girls’ safe spaces and provide periodic individual and group psycho social support for women and girls. This project will support the healing process of GBV survivors by providing community based case management and psychosocial support services and engage men and boys in community dialogues and awareness raising sessions on GBV, its effect and consequences and their roles in prevention. Protection interventions will be integrated within CARE’s health, nutrition and FSL programmes to complement holistic assistance to communities through static and mobile.

1 - Needs

From the outbreak of conflict in December 2013, nearly 7.5 million people are in need of humanitarian assistance and protection across the country. The number of people uprooted since the start of the conflict has reached more than 4 million including 1.9 million internally displaced people, with up to 85 percent estimated to be women, girls and children. The humanitarian and protection needs are compounded by high rates of malnutrition and severe levels of food insecurity. More than half of women aged 15–24 have suffered some form of gender-based violence. According to a 2017 study on what works to prevent violence against women and girls’ study by GWI, IRC and CARE, GBV is pervasive in these conflict zones with up to 65% of women and girls experiencing physical and/or sexual violence in their lifetime. These are among the highest rates in the world. Women and girls have experienced either physical or sexual violence from a partner or non-partner in the course of their lifetime. The Gender-based Violence Information Management System data for 2018 recorded around 95% of reported GBV survivors were women and girls (South Sudan GBV Sub Cluster Strategy, 2018). A 2017 CARE study on trends of change in a turbulent context; social norms and barriers analysis for women and girls in Jonglei and Eastern Equatoria, indicates that about 52% of girls in South Sudan are estimated to be married by the age of 18. In Jonglei, marriage related conversations begins either at menstruation even earlier at 7 or 8 years of age. A survey carried by IOM in 2017 found that in one out of two households (50% of households surveyed) a woman or girl had experienced some form of GBV in the past 12 months. The deepening economic crisis have forced women and girls to risk their lives to fend for their families including resorting to negative coping mechanisms such as sex for food “transactional sex” (CARE; HCT Protection Strategy Baseline Assessment Survey (2016). The continued practice of dowry payments perpetuates the belief that girls are both a source of wealth for their families and property of their prospective (CARE, 2017). There is a complex relationship between gender roles, social norms, food insecurity and GBV and a persisting phenomenon of ‘hyper-masculinity’ amongst men often exacerbated by the conflict of risk and insecurity. This reinforces harmful power differentials between men and women (and children), often leading to GBV and other forms of violence. Patriarchal societal norms that promote gender inequality (including men minimizing the occurrence of violence, and both men and women accepting the use of violence) are common, (CARE, 2017). While the crisis has dramatically increased the challenges that households face, the division of labour has not shifted, but rather has strengthened with women facing immense additional burdens when trying to feed, clothe, and protect their families. Women and girls are seen as assets in obtaining bride price, a social function closely linked to income security and male dominance and status with minimal decisions from women while boys are seen as heirs to the family wealth, property and family name (CARE, 2016). Evidence gathered by CARE, GWI & IRC on Lessons from South Sudan in a September, 2018 study on intersections of violence against women and girls with state-building and peace-building suggests that war and conflict also increase other forms of VAWG – including forced marriage and that other forms of endemic violence, such as intimate partners violence are more prevalent during conflict compared to conflict-related sexual violence. It also confirms that violence against women and girls, gender inequality and conflict are often mutually reinforcing and that during times of conflict, gender inequality remains the core cause of GBV. Despite the prevalence of GBV, there is still insufficient services to respond to the needs of survivors compounded with limited knowledge on availability of services.

2 - Activities

Provide comprehensive quality and timely survivor- centered case management services to individual GBV survivors. Provision of basic financial support to survivors of GBV to meet some basic needs (transport, meals and other services related costs) in case of referrals - Refurbishment or Construction and equipping of women and girls’ safe spaces. Conduct information education sessions for women and girls at Women and Girls’ Safe Spaces (WGSS). - Age appropriate individual and group psycho social support activities which includes skills and life skills building for women and girls. - Adolescent girls learning initiatives to mitigate risks of early and forced marriages through using tailored curriculum. - Establish and update coordinated GBV referral pathways. - Conduct capacity building trainings for state holders including women leaders on GBV Basic concepts, psychological first Aid, GBV Guiding principles, women's leadership and advocacy and context specific referral pathways. - Conduct capacity building training and mentoring support supervision for CARE staff, implementing partner’s staff on case management and psycho social support. - Conduct regular community mobilization and awareness raising on consequences of GBV, community awareness raising and sensitization on women’s rights. - Conduct GBV assessment and quarterly coordinated safety Audits to identify the protection risks and concerns for women and girls. - Establish and support community-based protection. Strengthening participation of women’s groups and women-lead organisations in protection coordination structures). - Provide protective livelihood programming for women like training women and girls on alternative fuel strategies like construction of fuel efficient stoves and briquettes. - Conducting gender analysis of community relations and norms affecting peaceful co-existence.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

CARE has established multi-sectoral Community Feedback and Response Mechanisms in all of its operational sites, including in the proposed project locations. Regular listening sessions will be conducted with women and girls, which are community dialogue sessions where women, girls and the target community can express any concerns regarding services provided. Women’s protection concerns will be taken into consideration in particular, and all feedback will be used to ensure responsive programming. CARE will implement a complaints mechanism for the cash-based intervention in order to create a positive environment which is characterized by mutual trust. The complaint and feedback mechanism will be set up during the project initiation stage, with beneficiaries being sensitized on beneficiary accountability standards. Communities will also be informed about the focal point in CARE responsible for handling any complaint report. The feedback and reporting procedure will be displayed in a visible place in the project location, and complaints and feedback boxes will be placed at each WGFS and project site office for those who are able to write and someone will help those who can not write.Views and feedback will be collected from women, girls, other community beneficiaries and local authorities will be collected regularly. Feedback and complaints will generally be collected through 3 main approaches (although this may vary depending on a community’s preferences): FGDs with groups of women, men, girls, and boys; during community sensitization and awareness sessions; and through the use of suggestion boxes. CARE ’s experience shows that most feedback and complaints emerge during FGDs. CARE ’s community feedback mechanism allows for a range of complaints to be received and managed, including a mechanism for confidentially fast-tracking concerns of a serious nature such as sexual exploitation and abuse.
