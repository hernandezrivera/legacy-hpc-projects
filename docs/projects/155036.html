Project Summary

1. Recruitment, training, deployment of 120 CHVs, including 20 for host community, in compliance with CHW-WG TORs for CHVs, including data collection and reporting requirements. Locations are in accordance with designated blocks identified by the CHW-WG mapping exercise 2. Activation of 840 womens' care groups with trained MHPSS CHVs, with an emphasis on MHPSS and GBV, in accordance to MHPSS WG gap analysis and TORs 3. Activation of a community based referral system at block level for 24-7 accompanied referral to 24-7 maternity services. 4. Appropriate training on CHV duties, ANC, PNC, MHPSS, GBV, referral, and data collection, with periodic refresher training. 5. Reporting as per IOM/UNHCR CHW guidelines. Rokeya Foundation (Formerly Rohingya Womens' Welfare Society has partnered with Community Partners International (CPI) to to link the continuum of care from household to CHVs to available facility based maternity services. The foundation of the program is the establishment of Womens' Groups at the household level, with an initial focus on creating a supportive environment of trust and mutual respect, and for group oriented MHPSS. Rokeya Foundation engaged over 568 women in block groups that meet weekly with a CHV group coordinator. This group is the key to establishing new norms regarding utilization of services, case identification for SGBV and MHPSS for one-on-one counselling, as well as sharing information regarding SRH and a suite of facility services focused on maternal and newborn health. The overall approach leverages the womens' groups in a program of community health extension, building from our established presence of CHVs and Women’s Groups. This model incorporates local Rohingya women with established roles in the community, training them as Community Health Volunteer (CHV), links them with trained Bangladeshi midwives at the facility level, and provides a pre-ANC visit at the household level. This is followed by accompanied referral to birthing facilities, and in-home provision of significant post-natal care for newborns, as per CHW-WG ToRs. Chlorhexidine treatment has proven -through random controlled trials in Nepal, India, and Bangladesh -to reduce post-natal mortality by 23-30% in low resource settings. Tetracylcine has similarly proven effective for preventing blindness if given at birth, and additional education and training of mothers can meaningfully improve early referral for sepsis in both infants and mothers. Additionally, weighing of the baby will improve early identification of low-weight and/or early term pregnancies that are at highest risk for neo-natal mortality.
A key provision of this approach is increasing the capacity of facility linked Bangladeshi Midwives and CHVs for the care of recently newborn babies, including neo-natal massage, helping babies breath, Kangaroo Mother Care, and hygiene for the prevention of sepsis. This suite of skills is especially important for those 68% of births where, despite concerted effort, a mother has not been able to refer to a 24-7 facility- but still has the right to qualified medical care for herself and her baby. In addition recognizing the danger signs for sepsis, lack of eating, lack of drinking, ARI, and strongly emphasizing the imperative of early referral will greatly reduce neo-natal death. Lastly, home based post-natal visits can support greater uptake of family planning; peer reviewed studies by JHPIEGO in Bangladesh show a 15% increase in family planning uptake in the post natal period. CPI is currently working with CDC and other parties to design a realistic, impactful protocol for post-natal care.

Needs

Preventable maternal and neo-natal deaths continue to persist within Ukhiya FDMN camps. Despite the investment in facility-based services, only 32% of refugee women give birth in a facility. The prevelance of home based births- in some camps, over 90%- leads to the '3 delays' in seeking, accessing, and recieving appropriate maternal care, thus leading to maternal mortality. In addition, the provision of post-natal care, even with facility births, is often low, with the majority of women receiving insufficient post natal care after leaving the facility. This leads to an excessive rate of neo-natal mortality, in some camps 2x the SPHERE standard for neo-natal mortality in an emergency. Simple post-natal care, such as the use of chlorhexadine for umbilical cord care, tetracycline for eye care, and even weighing of the baby, is under utilized. Similarly, provision of CMR has not resulted in significant use of services, and the same is true regarding MHPSS for SGBV survivors. At the root cause of this is low availability and acceptance for facility care, especailly for maternity services, post-natal care, CMR, SGBV, and MHPSS. Combined with limited geographic access due to the limited (25% of target) 24-7 care, cultural preferences, and service delivery that has not prioritized the care of mothers and newborns at the household level. One part of the solution to the root problem is the establishment of womens groups that have acceptance, trust, empowerment, education, and mutual interdependance at their core. Leveraging the peer group social capital of the womens groups is a key to social and behavioral change. Combined with a program of health services extension, provision of quality services with high acceptance will change the social and behavioral norms regarding uptake of maternal, newborn, SRH, SGBV, and MPHSS serivices. Outputs will incude partial CHV coverage in 5 camps, creation of womens groups, 120 trained CHVs meeting minimum standards for the role, appropraite service delivery at household level in accodance with the CHW-WG ToRs for trained CHVs, accompanied referral, and improved community surveillance and response at household level. Outcomes will be increased facility based births, increased utilization of MHPSS and SGBV services, and reduced maternal and neo-natal mortality.
