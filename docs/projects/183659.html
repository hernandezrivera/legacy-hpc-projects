Project Summary

Action Against Hunger (AAH)’s proposed intervention, together with the partner Volontariato Internazionale per lo Sviluppo (VIS), will focus on safe and equitable access to quality education for children in Area C and H2 area of Hebron city in the West Bank. Specifically, the proposed project aims to provide emergency response to the most vulnerable schools in Area C through integrated and targeted activities, aiming to increase the protection of 1185 people (students; girls: 593, boys: 495, teachers: 62 females, 35 males). The intervention will also be responsive to the negative impacts that COVID-19 has had and is continuing to have on education. The response will be implemented by upgrading the educational environment in schools through the rehabilitation of classrooms and providing key support to enhance healthy educational environments. It will also supply and construct new classrooms in overcrowded schools to facilitate separate spaces for students from different grades. Activities will also target the schools’ staff and parents to improve their capacities in delivering knowledge as well as dealing with risk reduction; risk mitigation (settlers attacks, clashes); and to give the students the needed support in the face of these risks and hazards (evacuation procedures, first aid, etc.). Simultaneously, the project will respond to psychosocial issues faced by students and teachers alike as a result of the coercive environment they are subjected to. The proposed psychosocial response will be innovative; various educational and recreational tools targeting students, teachers and parents, will be adopted to ensure the maximum effect on the targeted students. The proposed interventions are in line with Education Cluster 2021 Strategic Objective 1 for the occupied Palestinian territory (oPt) “Strengthen advocacy and reporting on education-related violations across Gaza and the West Bank and ensure that school staff and students affected by the education-related violations and COVID-19 pandemic benefit from protective environment”, the Strategic Objective 2 "Increase equitable safe access to inclusive, quality educational services for vulnerable children” and the Strategic Objective 3 “Children and school staff in vulnerable schools are supported in preparing and responding to emergencies”, and aligns with the 3 Strategic Objectives of the oPt Humanitarian Response Plan.
The project uses a community-based approach, with students (girls and boys) and teachers (women and men) participating in all stages of the project including in the assessment, design and implementation stages. Hence, the students, teachers as well as parents’ fears, needs and ideas will be discussed and analysed to inform effective technical and psychosocial activities considering gender and age disaggregated data, resulting in adapted assistance. As outlined, the proposed intervention ensures protection mainstreaming as it is guided by protection principles and considers safety and dignity; meaningful access; accountability to affected population as well as participation and empowerment.

1 - Needs

The ongoing protracted conflict in the oPt has had a significant impact on all aspects of people’s lives, particularly on the right to access to basic needs. Access to education in the oPt continues to be compromised due to the occupation and recurrent conflicts. Education related violations include attacks on schools, military use of schools, threats of attacks against school staff and students and interferences with access to education. According to the 2022 HNO, 579,173 people need Education in Emergencies humanitarian assistance across the oPt. These include 556,000 children (244,640 M; 311,360 F) of whom 13,398 are children living with a disability (CWD), who face challenges in accessing quality education in a safe, child-friendly environment. In the West Bank, including Jerusalem, checkpoints and the Barrier impede access to education for children living in the “Seam Zone” and in East Jerusalem communities isolated by the Barrier. Based on the Education Cluster, recent years have witnessed an increased number of Israeli attacks on schools located in Area C and Hebron H2. The REACH multi-sectoral needs assessment in 2021 has identified that 45% of HHs reported challenges to accessing education services in the West Bank. While 18% of HHs in the oPt reported safety concerns about travel to and from study in the education facilities, the most frequently reported safety issues were traffic/crossing roads (66% in WB), military presence around schools (14% in WB), firing tear gas on schools and students (11% in WB) and violence/harassment travelling to school (11% in WB). Alongside these challenges, the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic continues to disrupt normal school services, with 85% of HHs with school-aged children report that at least one child was continuing teaching and learning activities remotely (similar in West Bank and Gaza Strip) and 72% of HHs with school-aged children reported a need for their children to participate in catch-up learning programmes because of missed learning due to COVID-19 related school closures. Schools remain at the front line of facing demolishing orders, 5 incidents of demolition orders and/or stop-work orders served in 2019 in addition to 2 orders in 2020, and 3 schools partially or fully demolished in Tubas, Hebron and East Jerusalem governorates. The Palestinian Authority has been unable to significantly intervene in these communities as they are in Area C, i.e. under Israeli occupation. Additionally, all the existing school structures have been implemented under emergency conditions following minimum standards aiming to ensure an access to education and keeping space for further upgrading works in future. Consequently, all the schools have become uncomfortable spaces for students who are already struggling to reach them due to access restraints, particularly during winter. Poor school conditions and access have driven many families to adopt negative coping mechanisms such as withdrawing their children from school or displacing part of the family. AAH’s multi sectorial needs assessment and protection analysis involving communities in Area-C indicated that communities in the E1 zone, Firing Zone 918, Jordan Valley and Road 317 clusters are the most affected by Israeli restriction policies, affecting the educational sector in the targeted communities. Girls’ school dropout, access to schools and schools ’unsafe environment were the main concerns for the children in these communities. The intervention is coordinated with different organizations (including Medecins du Monde, UNICEF and NRC) acting in the area to support these students to access and enjoy school spaces.

2 - Activities

The continuous assessment of AAH’s interventions in 2021, in addition to the previous field assessments, provide a full picture of the required actions to address vulnerable community’s needs, particularly the unsuitability and inadequacy of school facilities, unsafe educational environment as well as the associated psychosocial problems faced by students and their families. Therefore, based on its experience in implementing over 45 educational interventions in Area C, AAH has proposed the needed technical solutions for each school in order to create a more healthy, safe, and friendly educational spaces for students and considering ICA construction restrictions in Area C. It will do so by rehabilitating the existing infrastructures to ensure the basic and humanitarian needs that will thereby support students in continuing their education. To complement, VIS, who has extensive experience in conducting psychosocial activities at both community and school level, has conducted different focus group sessions with students, teachers and parents from different Area C communities. Throughout these sessions VIS’ social experts identified the psychosocial problems that targeted groups suffer due to school access and environment; out of which they were able to propose technical solutions and share them with relevant stakeholders (Ministry of Education (MoE) and school headmasters, etc. AAH and VIS have experience in implementing educational activities in coordination with (MoE). For this project the coordination with MoE will be on two levels, Ministry level: to ensure that all the project activities will be implemented according to the Ministry strategies and priorities, and on Directorate’s level: to ensure that the education Directorates in each area have been consulted regarding the assessment, design and even during the implementation of the intervention. Furthermore, the activities will be handed over to the MoE as the service provider of the educational sector. PSEA was built into the activities by adopting safe feedback and complaint mechanisms for beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries. There is a clear process for referrals of complaints and the project staff, with engagement in the HTC oPt PSEA network.The following activities will ensure meaningful access to education and psychosocial support, prioritizing safety and dignity: 1) Provision of emergency education supplies: targeting schools that suffer overcrowding or merging students from different grades into one classroom; schools that face a deficit in power supplies, affecting the safety, health and educational attainment for students. Under this activity simple classrooms will be added, and power supply solutions will be developed (including solar systems for isolated communities). 2) Basic emergency rehabilitation to schools: to enhance the students’ learning environment, classrooms and other facilities will be rehabilitated/upgraded, creating a friendlier, more enjoyable, and safer space for children. The upgrading of sub-standard structures in the most vulnerable communities will ensure a more protective educational space and the improvement of facilities in schools. 3) Provision of school-based psychosocial support to vulnerable school children and staff: the activity will target the most vulnerable schools that suffer from a variety of threats including settler violence, natural hazards, and those with a generally unsafe and unhealthy environment. Detailed psychosocial assessments will be conducted in each school and consequently VIS and psychosocial support teams will identify the appropriate psychosocial intervention in each school. They will use various educational and recreational events and sessions with students, teachers and parents, including activities focusing on stress management, trauma and class communication. 4) Conducting hygiene awareness raising sessions and campaigns, to improve student’s hygiene practices and help them facing COVID-19 possible waves and impacts.

3 - Indicators

Technical and psychosocial baseline surveys will be conducted by VIS and AAH to identify the target groups’ needs; the survey will facilitate the supervision, monitoring and evaluation of the activities. Teachers and students (girls, boys and persons with disabilities) will be involved in the design, monitoring and evaluation of the activities to ensure the full participation of all groups. A post line and KAP surveys will be conducted to assess and evaluate the satisfaction and impacts of the activities on all groups, including an evaluation of improved access to schools and the students’ psychosocial status. Project activities will be monitored by 1) Working field staff; 2) Community representatives; 3) MoEHE visits and participation in the activities; 4) Baseline and post-implementation reports; 5) Donors and cluster monitoring visits; 6) Project progress and impact reports. The communities’ representatives and village councils will support AAH and VIS to provide ongoing field monitoring of the rehabilitated schools and advise AAH on the security status to determine optimal timing of field operations. In order to ensure accountability, AAH has a Feedback and Complaint Mechanism (FCM), which allows beneficiaries to provide their comments and complaints on the activities and is designed to increase their protection, in compliance with sensitive data management and adhering to the “Do No Harm” principle. The knowledge of this system has been promoted through several field assessments and monitoring visits. In addition, a service phone number will be distributed to the population in the proposed target communities enabling them to report feedback or complaints relating to project implementation by AAH and its partners. AAH will process and treat all information collected through an ad-hoc committee composed of project staff to understand the situation, identify appropriate solutions and include technical adjustment or modifications if needed. Output 1: 6 schools (548 students and teachers, of whom 503 are students and 45 are teachers; 277 girls, 226 boys,26 female teachers, and 19 are male teachers) benefited of the emergency education supplies (caravans, solar systems, educational tools). Output 2: 7 schools (637 students and teachers, of whom 585 are students and 52 are teachers; 316 girls, 269 boys, 36 female teachers, and 16 are males) received emergency rehabilitation for the school building and facilities. Output 3: Psychosocial support through using various artistic, sport activities, theatres, and recreational tools (26 events) provided to 1185 (students; girls: 593, boys: 495, teachers: 62 females, 35 males) students, parents and teachers received. Output 4: All targeted schools received hygiene awareness trainings. The expected objective verifiable indicators and targets from the project are as follows: Outcome1: 50% of the targeted students reported that the schools became safer. Outcome 2: The total educational attainment improved by 20% in the targeted schools. Outcome 3: The average absenteeism rate decreased by 30%. To further ensure protection is mainstreamed throughout the project implementation, the indicator “% of communities/project beneficiaries reporting that humanitarian assistance is delivered in a safe and dignified manner” is added and will be measured through an end line survey of the beneficiaries.

9. - Feedback and complaints mechanisms

Yes

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

Beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries alike will be able to reach out to the project with complaints, suggestions, and any other feedback through the mechanism managed by AAH MEAL Unit. AAH actively promote the existence of its feedback mechanisms through information sessions and information material (e.g., flyer) handed over during projects’ activities (e.g. assessments, distribution, training session etc.). Our programs maintain fluid communication with all rights holders regularly and extensively throughout the project management cycle. Our team consults and meets with rights holders when carrying out needs assessments in relevant beneficiary areas and employs a series of methods to ensure that their perspectives and priorities are addressed in the project design. These methods used to receive feedback include interviews with key informants, home visits, group discussions (separating men and women to ensure a safe space when discussing sensitive subjects), questionnaires and regular monitoring sessions to ensure that the project is appropriate and relevant to their needs. The information collected through complaints and feedback mechanism or directly in the field will be regularly fed into our programming to ensure no harm is done and to guarantee continuous improvements of assistance delivered respecting internal humanitarian standards, including informing the budget. Beneficiary feedback will be analysed and presented in a systematic way, including to project partners that we work with. Learnings following this feedback can often be used across projects and we make sure this is shared among relevant stakeholders so that complaints are taken seriously and can be anonymous where necessary, to make beneficiaries feel safe in expressing their opinions. The AAH MEAL unit will be in continuous contact with the Program and Coordination teams to analyse the data, address concerns, respond to complaints and work with the project team to make adjustments to the project.

9.1d - Explain how you have ensured that mechanism are accessible to all population groups?

AAH pays particular attention to the way vulnerable groups are included in our work. Information on the feedback and complaints mechanism is given to every beneficiary individually when distributing assistance at household level. To ensure that our beneficiary feedback mechanisms are accessible to all, AAH have a variety of different methods so that people can choose which they prefer (e.g. email, phone, field, office) but AAH promotes mainly the use of a phone to give feedback and complaints to avoid having vulnerable population spending time and money in coming to AAH offices. 10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

Through the whole project cycle AAH ensures protection mainstreaming and Do No Harm. AAH considers safety and dignity, meaningful access, accountability as well as participation and empowerment of paramount importance. AAH has mechanisms in place to prevent and respond to sexual exploitation and abuse by all staff. AAH has in place focal points at base and mission levels, as well as safe and confidential ways to report abuse. All AAH staff have the obligation to adhere to the organisation code of conduct and our held accountable if they do not respect it. AAH has made use of cluster specific Protection and Gender Mainstreaming Tip Sheets, along with learnings from various interventions and external evaluations. Overall AAH’s aim is to take decisive humanitarian action with attention to risk mitigation thereby avoiding doing harm to the greatest extent possible. Risks assessments and analysis of the protection context are let for all the projects, and even more so when implemented with at risk groups (e.g. minors) or in areas where risk of doing harm is exacerbated (e.g. Area C in the West Bank or inside health facilities in COVID-19 times). In order to inform its assessments and implementation AAH also continuously works on understanding gender roles and how its interventions might impact them without aggravating existing power relations between genders. By understanding gender roles and household dynamics, AAH believes that the organization mitigates the risks of doing harm. In 2019, AAH conducted a masculinity study in both Gaza and the West Bank, which focused on identities/roles of men in order to fully understand men, women, girls and boys and gender dynamics. As part of AAH accountability to affected populations, a feedback mechanism emphasizing fluid and transparent communication will be in place. COVID-19 protective measures and equipment will be applied throughout all interventions to further prevent harm being done to beneficiaries, communities and staff members.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

AAH upholds the humanitarian principles of Humanity, Impartiality, Neutrality, and Independence. During the course of the project, AAH will ensure all projects are easily accessible to those most at need, avoiding risks to the beneficiaries. AAH is committed to operate in an ethical manner and on a programmatic risk mitigation strategy for risk of fraud, waste, and abuse within the proposed activities. At a global level, AAH relies on the organizational Policy on Anti-Bribery, Corruption and Abuse of Power and Code of Conduct. Organizational whistleblowing mechanisms and procedure are in place, `ZERO TOLERANCE ́ towards fraud, bribery or corruption is applied. Reporting mechanisms have also been implemented to report these behaviours to headquarters. Ethical Risks related to any kind of abuse(sexual, power, etc...) are mitigated by providing compulsory briefing to each new staff member on ethical policies. AAH Code of Conduct is aligned with IASC principles. AAH has a Policy for Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) and in 2019, AAH conducted in its Middle East country offices a Protection Mainstreaming Internal Evaluation for which the consultant spent March in oPt. Particular attention was paid to the way AAH includes vulnerable groups in its work, for instance through inclusive participatory assessments. As one of AAH’s core values, AAH thrives to prevent discrimination and to ensure equal and impartial access though transparency and accountability to affected populations. AAH aims for all community members to be involved and consulted as much as possible in project design and implementation, as well as evaluation, including through household and community consultations and focus group discussions, accessible to all. In order for their participation to be meaningful, AAH focuses on ‘qualitative inclusion’ meaning safe spaces for beneficiaries to participate. AAH is reporting under the HRP disaggregated data by sex and age; though pending the reporting format, AAH usually includes further disaggregation on vulnerability such as data on people with disabilities. Random checks will be carried out by the MEAL team members to ensure transparency, neutrality and objectivity and spot potential bias or incongruency.
