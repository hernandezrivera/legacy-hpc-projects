Project Summary

Markets in Iraq are functioning and accessible, and vulnerable households have a variety of critical basic needs. CCI PDM surveys show 79% of vulnerable households that receive cash transfers reduced their usage of negative coping strategies. Meanwhile, 99% of beneficiaries prefer cash to in-kind assistance. Accordingly the CCI will utilize MPCA as an appropriate, relevant and preferred humanitarian response modality to meet the critical basic needs of vulnerable households in conflict-affected out-of-camp communities across Iraq in 2019 and contribute to the CWG objectives:

Obj.1: Provide MPCA to extremely vulnerable households affected by armed conflict and to vulnerable households affected by a shock generating humanitarian needs that cannot be covered by existing national services, and;
Obj.2: Facilitate vulnerable households access complementary and critical humanitarian and national services including non-contributory social protection programs

Although needs may change and the CCI will pivot accordingly, priority areas of intervention for this program currently include the following: Mosul, Tel Afar, Tilkaif, Baaj, and Sinjar in Ninewa; Ana, Al Qaim and Ru’ua in West Anbar; Heet, Fallujah, Khaladiya, and Ramadi in East Anbar; Shirqat, Tikrit, Touz, Balad, and Baiji in Salah al-Din; Kirkuk, Daquq, and Hawija in Kirkuk governorate; and Khanaqin and Ba’quba in Diyala. However, with operational presence across 11 governorates (including Baghdad, Erbil, Najaf, Dohuk and Sulaymaniyah) the CCI has access to all CWG priority areas of intervention if required and in coordination with other CWG actors.
CCI partners will aim to reach 60,000 vulnerable households (360,000 individuals) with MPCA in accordance with CWG guidelines. In addition, CCI partners will also utilize established mechanisms to facilitate access to free in-house legal assistance and support to access barriers to Iraq’s SSN and other services. The CCI anticipates that at least 8,000 households will require support to obtain ID and/or support to register with the PDS, MoDM, or MoLSA (as feasible). CCI partners will also refer 5,000 households into other critical humanitarian services they have on offer including CfW, tailored protection support, shelter assistance, MHPSS, etc. Moreover, the consortium structure provides value and opportunity as follows:
The inter-agency CCI governance structure is already established and coordination is mandated by the MoU. The CCI coordination mechanism is proven to work, and provides an important cross-sector forum to coordinate and harmonize tools. Shared ways of working are established, partners trust each other, compromise, pivot and work together to promote a more effective overall response.
The shared database enables rapid and efficient two-way data sharing, and prevents duplications with CCI and non-CCI partners (five DSAs are already established with ACTED, TDH, CARE, Tearfund, and PIN, while a DSA is under development with ICRC and Reach Iraq). An automatic referral mechanism is established and automated which ensures consistent access to services on offer at different CCI partners. The CCI provides free in-house legal counsel and support to obtain legal documentation and, if required, register with the SSN. Moreover, CCI partners refer households into other CCI and bilateral programming including tailored protection, shelter, CfW services etc.
With 50 offices across 11 governorates, CCI partners have expansive infrastructure to promote reach and access for comprehensive, and coordinated, community-based programming at scale.
All CCI partners are engaged in the national and subnational CWGs, report on ActivityInfo and 4Ws, and actively participate in the CWG-led JPMI, the JRAMs, as well as the TVTF and MMTF, and the CWG-MoLSA SPF. The CCI’s common fund enables partners to rapidly respond to unanticipated shock and needs and promotes preparedness.

Needs

Across the entire CCI portfolio, in all intervention areas, CCI partners have witnessed an acute humanitarian situation over the past year in line with the CWG’s Humanitarian Needs Overview, and plan to respond in 2019 with an MPCA program to assist these households in meeting their critical basic needs. According to available data, approximately 2 million households remain displaced across Iraq; however, there are widespread reports of secondary displacement within areas of return where families are unable, or unwilling, to return to their homes. Although recovery needs are emerging, the Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP 2019) and Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO) evidence that relief needs remain acute. Households continue to be characterized by a variety of critical basic needs. Analysis of CCI data intarget governorates (Ninewa, Anbar, Salah al-Din, Kirkuk and Diyala) shows that households typically allocate just over half (53%) of their expenditure to food, rent, and healthcare, with the remainder covering other basic needs such as small shelter repairs, electricity, fuel, debt repayments, education costs, clothing, hygiene items, and water. Certain household characteristics contribute to households’ inability to consume, such as the head of household being disabled (15% of cases) or chronically ill (44% of cases). Among the disabled heads of households, their disability prevented 91% from working, likewise forthe chronically ill heads of households. However, across all vulnerable households, 71% of households said no member of their household had workedduring the previous 30 days. Only 24% on average across target governoratessaid at least one member of their household had even temporary work and 5% of households reported having regular work. CCI data shows that households spend on average more than 300% of their monthly income, ranging from 260% in Diyala to 339% in Ninewa. The inability to earn income, combined with thehousehold-level vulnerabilities, mean that many households both accumulate debt to meet basic needs and resort to a range of negative coping strategies. The average total household debt is IQD 2,435,389 - or 14 times the average income; 83% of households said they had resorted to buying basic goods on credit, 82% had resorted to reducing spending on non-food items, 79% had needed to consume smaller meals, 77% relied on their relatives for basic goods, and 75% reduced their access to healthcare to cope. The use of negative coping strategies is worse among female headed households, as well as young households that tend to have even lower access to income and are either unwilling or unable to access credit. Moreover, Protection concerns are also pervasive and acute across the CCI’s proposed areas of intervention. There is now a reversal in population movement, with voluntary returns occurring at an increasing rate since October 2017; however, many families are returning to their areas of origin under duress due to the increasing prevalence of both forced evictions and forced returns. Meanwhile, secondary and tertiary displacement continues, catalyzed by security concerns, tribal dynamics, and persecution of households with perceived affiliation to extremist groups. The primary protection threats and gaps going forward are myriad. Continued threats to civilians’ physical safety and security due to the continued presence of armed groups is first and foremost. Equally, the confiscation of documents and property continues to plague vulnerable Iraqis as evidenced by the REACH Multi-Cluster Needs Assessment, which indicated at least 8% of out-of-camp Iraqis are missing at least one form of civil documentation. Additionally, the needs of many vulnerable, conflict-affected households are not being supported by socialprotection systems, with only 28% of households assessed by the CCI stating they are already registered for government assistance (including assistance provided by MoDM, MoT-PDS, or MoLSA). Sources: CCI vulnerability assessments; CCI PDM survey data

Activities

CCI partners identify underserved out-of-camp areas where there is limited access to other types of assistance, shocks have occurred, or households face persecution. Partners communicate with local leaders and other members of the community, to obtain access. A security assessment, cash feasibility assessment are conducted followed by a Market Assessment utilizing the JRAM methodology. CCI partners also ensure JPMI is conducted.Blanket door-to-door vulnerability assessments promote conflict sensitivity, inclusion, and on-site verification, by male and female staff. Partners incorporate UNOPS referred households. The CWG-endorsed PMT eligibility model is applied to identify the most vulnerable male and female headed households. Following the scoring, CCI M&E guidelines dictate verification surveys (95% CI) must be conducted for inclusion/exclusion error. This rigorous targeting in hard-to-reach areas, CCI partners maximize the likelihood of achieving the CWGs objective 1 including all vulnerable households.The CCI centralized database enables efficient duplication checks and 5 DSAs are signed with non-CCI partners, with two more underway. CCI partners also take referrals for cash assistance from non-CCI partners, or provide referrals for cash assistance or other services, on the basis of DSAs to promote greater coordination. CCI partners have an average response time of 32 days. CCI partners communicate eligibility 24-48 hours before a distribution, arranged at safe/accessible centralized location with access for women, and minority groups including people with disabilities and chronic illness. MTAs continue to provide reliable, accessible, trusted, access to liquidity for cash delivery. At distributions, households are briefed on the process, accountability mechanisms as well as other services on offer. Separate waiting areas and separate lines enable CCI partners to be sensitive to gender norms. Water is available, seating and shelter. Male and female staff are on hand to ensure equal access to support and communication. Accountability desks are available. Meanwhile, CCI partners can conduct tailored MTA cash distributions at beneficiaries’ homes if they are unable to attend the group distribution. Members of CCI staff that verify ID, ensure the transfer is paid in full, and no personal data is shared with the MTA. All CCI partners adhere to the CWG guidelines on transfer value and duration. UNOPS cards are distributed with cash transfers to all beneficiaries to ensure access to information and accountability. CCI partners conduct regular and longitudinal PDM (95% CI) one month after transfers have been delivered and in accordance with the CCI M&E guidelines to gauge impact as well as quality of process.CCI partners will also utilize the MPCA modality to facilitate legal support for vulnerable households to obtain official identification and access to government assistance, in line with the CWG’s strategic objective 2. CCI legal support teams also enable households to register with the SSN (MoDM, MoT-PDS, and MoLSA). In addition, CCI partners will use the pseudo-PMT to provide targeted offline support to those potentially eligible for MoLSA assistance. CCI partners are developing new referral pathways for non CCI-CWG members to pilot accessing the CCI’s in-house legal support. This will require technological development already underway with the shared database, as well as revised informed consent statements to be added to the CWG vulnerability assessment, and revisions to the script used by the CCI’s legal teams, in order to provide safe, secure and well communicated access to the CCI legal referral mechanism based on need and consent. Pending funding, this process will be piloted with CWG partners on the basis of DSAs and findings of the pilot will be reviewed by the CCI and shared with the CWG, to inform next steps as well as potential extensions to other CWG members. CCI also refers households for other types of support partners have on offer including shelter, tailored protection support, CfW, MHPSS etc. All CCI partners participate in the CWG initiatives. The CCI funds the IMO, and will continue to deliver training on the eligibility model, as well as CALP certified training, when required. The CCI will work with the CWG to implement the CWG-MoLSA action plan. The CCI is developing a mentorship programme to build MPCA capacity at local organizations. Pending funding, a detailed capacity building plan will be developed as per the needs, and on-the-job training will be provided to enable quality MPCA delivery and preparedness.

Protection Risk Analysis

*Armed groups and/or local authorities may block access to target populations or proposed geographical areas.*Tensions may arise in targeted communities between those who have received MPCA, and those who have not*Duplication of efforts between CCI partners and other cash actors; duplication of assistance to beneficiaries*MPCA may disrupt local markets that are not prepared for the additional demand generated by MPCA distributions*Local authorities and other stakeholders may cease at any time to cooperate with humanitarian organizations providing assistance to vulnerable populations*The Iraqi government’s processes and procedures to access the Social Safety Net (SSN) may change*Corruption, bribery and abuse/exploitation of affected populations CCI partners promote do no harm, conflict sensitivity, and adherence to all humanitarian principles as enshrined in the MoU. Protection is also mainstreamed through each component of the CCI program cycle-- including assessment, distribution, M&E, and legal assistance. CCI partners are currently in the process of rolling out inter-agency protection mainstreaming training developed specifically for cash actors, which will certainly benefit this program. Moreover, the provision of in house legal assistance integrates protection support to MPCA. The types of documentation beneficiaries require assistance in obtaining typically include nationality certificates, Civil ID (Jensiya) and housing cards. It is through obtaining these documents that vulnerable clients can attain legal identity, which enables them to prove their date of birth, area of origin and Iraqi citizenship, amongst other key identifiers. Clients are then able to fully realize and exercise their civil rights including registering for services, registering to vote, enjoying freedom of movement and fully partaking in their civic lives. Beneficiaries are also connected to the SSN whenever possible.

Mitigation Measures

CCI partners recognize the potential negative consequences that cash distribution programs can have on local communities. With this in mind, CCI partners mainstream conflict sensitivity throughout MPCA programming by ensuring that there is no real or perceived preferential treatment given to any one group. This is achieved through a number of strategies. CCI partners conduct blanket vulnerability assessments, making it clear to the populations served that all households within certain geographical areas would be assessed, and that there will be no additional privileges for any one group. To mitigate community tensions between those who have received cash and those who have not, CCI partners ensure that the beneficiary selection criteria is understood by the community. In order to minimize complaints from ineligible beneficiaries, they are communicated with respectfully according to CCI guidelines and receive a standard CCI regret message regarding their ineligibility. To promote more effective beneficiary data management, the CCI developed a shared database. This database enables duplication checks across CCI awards, and data sharing agreements have been signed with Terre des Hommes, the Agency for Technical Cooperation and Development, Tearfund, and People in Need to prevent duplication among the cash community. To further coordinate with other cash actors, all CCI partners participate in the CWG at the national and sub-national levels. The CCI’s cash response has been aligned with the CWG recommendations in terms of transfer value, beneficiary categorization and vulnerability targeting. All partners use harmonized tools for vulnerability assessments, beneficiary verification and post-distribution monitoring designed by the CCI and endorsed by the CWG. Partners regularly coordinate in terms of geographical locations, duplication checks and results through the CWG’s ‘Activity Info’ platform and 4Ws. Information and updates are regularly shared with the CWG. In an effort to mitigate inflation to the local communities, CCI partners conduct market assessments in all locations (or used the information in market assessments conducted by other organizations) to determine whether the local markets are functioning well and have the capacity to meet additional demand generated by cash distributions. CCI partners use harmonized market survey tools and utilize the CWG’s methodology based on the International Committee of the Red Cross’s Rapid Assessment of Markets to drive the wider adoption of the CWG tool and build a stronger evidence base. Throughout the project, in all areas of intervention, CCI partners will work hard to communicate clear messages with all relevant stakeholders such as mukhtars, local authorities, and community leaders about the transparency of the selection process, the equal access of all beneficiaries to such services, confidentiality of beneficiaries’ data, and the responsibility of each actor in charge with this process. Before intervening in a given location, CCI partners conduct a detailed security assessment and a proper risk analysis to understand the conflict dynamics, identify potential risks associated with cash transfer programming and put the mitigation measures in place. Legal assistance teams of the three CCI partners providing legal assistance, will continually monitor the Government’s processes and procedures to access the social safety network. Offline registration systems will be explored until MoLSA cash assistance registration reopens. The CCI MOU enshrines commitments to humanitarian principles as well as safeguarding and anti-fraud. Each partner has all the safeguarding tools in place to ensure no harm is done to beneficiaries, staff and other people involved in project implementation. Each partner’s sub-agreement across the CCI portfolio contains clauses for code of conduct, accountability to beneficiaries, anti-corruption and anti-bribery.

How is your project accountable to affected population

CCI partners coordinate local officials in all areas of intervention, in order to understand local contexts, actors, and promote good communication to explain the efficacy of the CCI approach and ensure the CCI’s methodology can be implemented without interference. The eligibility model was developed by the CCI in collaboration with UNHCR, and endorsed by the CWG. The context specific evidence base is the cornerstone of our accountability to affected populations. The model is informed by them, and tailored to their needs, to ensure accurate targeting for socio- economically vulnerable households that require cash assistance to meet their critical basic needs. Moreover, an integrated package of assistance, with safe and protected internal referral pathways to other CCI and non CCI programs, better enables CCI partners to respond to the myriad of relief and recovery needs that are identified during blanket assessments. This comprehensive community-based approach is key to the CCI’s accountability. CCI partners use a variety of feedback mechanisms, including phone hotlines, in-person complaints registration, and locked complaint boxes at distribution points for beneficiaries’ use, and appropriate protection methods are taken to ensure confidentiality, with the option of anonymity. Feedback and response mechanisms are a vital component to this project which reflects accountability towards beneficiaries and fosters trust around program implementation locally. All CCI partners report a summary of information related to all complaints to the CCI on a quarterly basis in order to enhance accountability and promote analysis of trends at the central level. According to recent CCI post-distribution survey data (PDM), 87% of beneficiaries reported that they were aware of the various mechanisms through which they could give feedback. During PDM, over 97% of beneficiaries were either satisfied or very satisfied with the selection process. Ninety-five percent of beneficiaries reported that they were provided information about the distribution process.CCI partners receive complaints and feedback from both beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries via the various mechanisms mentioned above. The majority of complaints (60%) are regarding beneficiary selection and targeting. These are generally households that had been assessed but had not met the vulnerability threshold, or households that had not yet been assessed but still wanted assistance. Second to complaints about not meeting the vulnerability criteria, 30% of the complaints are households requesting to be assessed for cash assistance. Three percent of registered complaints were requesting additional information and another 3% were households who had received one-off cash distributions and were requesting to be considered for additional (multi-month) cash distribution. For all complaints/feedback received, necessary action is taken and feedback through phone calls is given to those who had raised complaints. According to recent CCI data, the average response time for a complaint was 12 days. CCI partners also coordinate closely with the UNOPS IDP Information Centre. At distribution sites, CCI partners pass out cards with the UNOPS complaints hotline number at cash distributions. The complaints/feedback that UNOPS receives is then shared with the CCI, and partner staff review, assess and respond to the complaints. When feasible, partners refer potential protection cases to their Protection teams for further investigation. CCI partners also accept referrals for cash assistance through the UNOPS IDP Information Centre. Partners assess these referrals per the CCI guidelines, and the referrals are MPCA if deemed eligible. However, some referrals are from locations where CCI partners are not operational, inhibiting the partners’ ability to assess those households’ eligibility.
