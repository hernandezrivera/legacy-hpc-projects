Project Summary

Food Security Sector (FSS) helps to strengthen the Nutrition and Health of the Rohingya refugees and the host communities of the Teknaf and Ukhiya Upazila of Cox’s Bazar district. Under the ongoing global climate crisis and rapidly changing local climate, an effective FS programme reduces climate vulnerabilities and protection risks and enhances peace and stability.
BRAC's Food Security Sector in Cox's Bazar will undertake a holistic humanitarian approach, incorporating development nexuses as the crisis has entered a protracted mode. As per sectoral objective 2, BRAC FS sector will provide support to develop the food security resilience of 20,967 Rohingya refugees in 25 camps through climate-sensitive food production in the camps. Moreover, to promote sectoral objective number 3, BRAC will work on the adjacent host communities to strengthen the food security of the 6,152 individuals in 6 unions of Ukhia and Teknaf Upazila through climate-smart agricultural production, agro-processing, creating market linkages, resilient income opportunities and disaster response preparedness with early actions. BRAC has piloted and developed a “Resilience Framework” considering its impact on host communities in Ukhiya. While developing this matrix, BRAC ensured the participation of all age and gender groups and persons with disabilities from the targeted communities.
From the learning of responding to this crisis since 2017, BRAC will focus on sectoral integration and prioritise localisation by developing the capacity of local NGOs, CBOs, youth clubs, and government stakeholders through training and supporting them in implementation activities. BRAC will also work for land stabilisation, environmental restoration, and local awareness raising to protect the climate, natural resources and local ecology. BRAC aims to emphasise building resilience against environmental risks and hazards. The FS programme will have a contingency plan to protect and replenish agricultural products so that the affected households and individuals most adversely affected by the influx can recover and rebuild their food security and livelihoods.
25% of the project resources will be allocated for cash transfer interventions, including conditional cash transfer and cash-for-work (e.g., plantation, nursery preparation) in the host community. BRAC will aim to cover 85% of project participants to be adult females from both communities, prioritizing widows, female-headed families, transgender, LGB communities, people with long-term illnesses, GBV/SGBV victims and persons with disabilities across all age groups.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

The Food and Nutrition security of Rohingya and the people of the host communities is a growing concern, especially in the current global context. With limited or no access to self-reliance or income-generating opportunities, the refugees remain highly reliant on humanitarian assistance. Almost all households have unmet multi-sectoral needs, most commonly related to food security and livelihoods (J-MSNA, 2021). Besides, access to food is the first priority needs as reported by the 75% Rohingya, 33% host community households (J-MSNA, 2023).The Resilience Index Measurement Analysis (RIMA) study conducted by FAO found that in 2022, 50% of the Rohingya households had acceptable food consumption patterns, 47% had borderline consumption, and 3% had poor consumption, whereas 66% of the host community households were found with acceptable food consumption patterns, 31% have borderline consumption, and 3% had poor consumption (RIMA, Nov’2023). Since the Rohingya influx in 2017, there has been increasing economic pressure and intense competition in the host community for local resources, like in the food labour market. The host communities already living below the poverty line have struggled to meet their basic needs, as most have lost their livelihoods due to the influx. These are local farmers, native foragers, and fishing communities. The study of Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) on chronic food insecurity (CFI) analysis of Bangladesh observed that in Cox’s Bazar, a significant portion (70%) of the people are in food insecurity, either mild, moderate, or severe. Among them, 25% are moderately and at least 10% people are severely food insecure (CFI, 2022). In addition to that, as per IPC acute food insecurity analysis, high inflation, coupled with reduced incomes and repeated climatic shocks, are putting extreme stress on the poorest households and driving acute food insecurity in the analyzed population. Urgent action is required to protect their livelihoods and reduce food consumption gaps (IPC, 2023). Moreover, the impact of infectious diseases, including Dengue, conjunctivitis, etc. and local commodity price hikes caused by the global crisis pushed them further into destitution. Therefore, it is vitally important for the Host Community to have access to alternative income to reduce vulnerability. The food allocation for refugees is based on family size instead considering needs of differential individual in a family, especially of children, pregnant, lactating women, growing children and Persons with Disabilities. Refugees and host communities have strong patriarchal cultures, limiting women's participation in income generation, leadership and decision-making. As a result, these vulnerable groups have more nutritional deficiency and related diseases than their male counterparts. In both communities, the major dietary share goes to the male members of the family, while inadequate amounts of food consumption, poor dietary diversity and hierarchy in families keeping male adult members at the top of the consumption pyramid are causing malnutrition, especially among children, pregnant and lactating mothers and girls. Therefore, despite targeting women and girls, they still lag in food security and nutritional deficiency (Gender & intersectionality report October 2020, UNHCR).

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

This proposed programme is aligned with the rationalisation principles of the FSS. The programme will ensure usages of common strategies, systems, protocols, and tools across all the camps. It will lead towards a standardized and cost-effective approach to providing equitable services to refugees and also ensure a minimum standard of service delivery by different partners. BRAC will follow an integrated approach to ensure effective and impactful project design and implementation outcomes. Based on BRAC's experience in this crisis context of the last six years has shown that an integrated approach incorporating relevant sectors like Nutrition, Protection, Education and WASH will have a greater impact on the response to Rohingya and host communities.In this regard, FSS plans to integrate with BRAC's other relevant sectors including WASH, Site Management Support, Protection, etc., for a robust comprehensive intervention.Moreover, BRAC has been ensuring stability in the refugee operation and improving relations with refugees, the Government and other partners in the camps. In addition, BRAC is committed to serving in the FSS in the same areas for several years, which is mentioned in its 5 years humanitarian action plan that will support strengthening relations with government agencies, the refugees, and other implementing partners in the area. All activities are aligned with the JRP following current Sectoral/Working Group objectives and strategy. BRAC also actively participate in the relevant Sector and/or Working Group and coordination meetings. BRAC regularly shares the 5W reports with the FSS, which helps them to understand the types of activities provided by BRAC, as well as to help understand the gaps. BRAC complies with the FSS guidelines and strictly maintains, avoiding any duplication and overlapping in the intervention areas. Notably, BRAC has a strong government Liaison team to obtain the relevant approvals from the government promptly.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

As a PSEA network member, BRAC has compliant policies prioritizing protection and safeguarding with an emphasis on 'Do No Harm' to prevent unintended negative effects of any programme. BRAC ensures meaningful access, participation and empowerment of its beneficiaries while its programme design, planning and implementation embed accountability at all levels. Additionally, BRAC has a strong safeguarding policy including child safeguarding. BRAC has safeguarded its participants and employees in the first place by making their safety a primary consideration at the level of the design of programmes and enterprises. The prevention of sexual harassment, child and adolescent abuse, intimidation, violence, bullying, humiliation and discrimination, neglect and exploitation are, as far as possible, built into the work practices. Where the design and its implementation cannot reduce risks sufficiently, BRAC has established secondary safeguarding mechanisms, like selecting and training personnel, supervising, monitoring, establishing multiple reporting channels, investigation, and punishment.

3.3 - Age & Disability

BRAC will conduct periodical community-based inclusive approaches (e.g. PRA) and gender analysis, using sex, age and disability disaggregated data to analyse and tailor to food security needs, understand capacities, barriers and the differential food and nutrition requirements for different groups, especially vulnerable groups of women, children, elderly, persons with disabilities and female-headed households. BRAC will strive to address the barriers faced by vulnerable genders, mainly women and girls, older people, LGBTQ, and persons with disabilities by sensitising the male members of the communities through community male leaders and volunteers. Through the programmes, they will be linked to critical services in protection and health, ensuring equal representation and prioritisation of the most vulnerable members of the communities.

3.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

Accountability to affected populations (AAP) is an active commitment to use power responsibly by taking into account giving account to and being held to account by the people humanitarian organizations seek to assist. BRAC will provide beneficiaries with a meaningful influence over decision-making in inclusive, non-discriminatory arrangements for diverse communities. Besides this, BRAC will also put individual feedback mechanisms and innovative approaches to joint feedback mechanisms that can reinforce transparency and mutual accountability and have a positive impact.Project information will be shared with the beneficiaries effectively and transparently. BRAC will ensure the inclusion of persons with disabilities and special needs through the programme activities, which include the participation of the persons with disabilities, taking opinions during the designing and planning of any activities in the programme and ensuring the transparency of the programme.

3.5.1 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) is a key principle and is integrated systematically into emergency preparedness and throughout all stages of the response. Besides, BRAC is a PSEA network member. It always prioritises ensuring that forcibly displaced and stateless persons are aware of their rights, services and where/ how to access them, that sexual exploitation is not tolerated, and how they can report any concerns related to SEA. BRAC will also ensure that victims of SEA have access without delay to the assistance and support that they require in line with a victim-centred approach. This project will ensure that effective, safe, and accessible feedback and response systems are in place to report any suspicions of SEA of forcibly displaced Rohingya and host community people. It will systematically integrate PSEA in information campaigns, awareness-raising, training and meetings with project participants of the Rohingya and host communities.

3.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

The proposed program aims to tackle land stabilization and environmental restoration in host and Rohingya communities. It will introduce climate-smart agriculture to enhance soil carbon absorption, reduce greenhouse gas emissions, mitigate the adverse impacts of climate change, and enhance agricultural production. Within these communities, BRAC has an established Emergency Operation Centre (EOC) and Emergency Response Team (ERT) structure, featuring representatives from all sectors to promptly respond during emergencies. Moreover, BRAC's Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) program delivers crucial training in Disaster Risk Management and Emergency Response to project staff, including community volunteers, in order to enable them to respond effectively to emergency crises. As part of their skill development, first aid training is also provided to all staff and volunteers.In addition, BRAC has implemented an Environmental and Social Safeguard Framework (ESSF) that takes a scientific precautionary approach to project design and implementation. BRAC's interventions in the Rohingya context are proactive in anticipating and addressing potential harm before it materializes. In situations of threat of serious or irreversible damage, the absence of scientific certainty will not be used to avoid cost-effective measures to prevent environmental degradation in the project implementation process.
