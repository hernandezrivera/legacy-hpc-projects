Project Summary

In order to provide more sustainable protection for children in return areas and for those remaining in camps, UNHCR will increase the investment in national child protection systems provided by the State and national civil society partners, based on an analysis of the existing systems, access of IDP children to these systems and their acceptability, affordability and quality. This includes child-sensitive legal procedures for custody, legal guardianship and child victims of violence, child-sensitive social welfare services and access to vital civil status documentation. UNHCR will provide technical support and advocate with authorities to increase non-discriminatory access for children associated with extremist groups to national child protection systems and advocate for the removal of barriers for these group of children to access services. In IDP camps, UNHCR will continue to provide integrated prevention and response services for children who are at risk of or victims of violence, neglect, abuse or exploitation or who have specific needs that require an urgent response.

Needs

Assessments conducted in IDP camps, non-camp settings and liberated areas in 2018 reveal that most children remain highly vulnerable and protection risks remain significant. During conflict and displacement, many children were exposed to traumatic events and subjected to sexual violence, child marriage, detention and child labour. As noted in the 2018 MCNA, rates of psycho-social distress among returnee and non-displaced children appear higher than among IDP children, possibly due to the lack of access to services, livelihoods opportunities for their families, and insecurity in the areas of origin. There are more than 15 districts across Iraq where 30-50% of children show signs of psycho-social distress. Large numbers of children formerly associated, or perceived to be associated with ISIL face stigma, discrimination and security risks. Older boys have been routinely detained when suspected of association with ISIL (on anti-terror charges), with huge obstacles to reintegration and risks upon release, including revenge killing upon return to their communities. Children related to alleged ISIL members are similarly discriminated against, with access to civil documentation, such as birth certificates, a major concern. Across IDP camps and host communities, child marriage and child labour have reportedly increased as negative coping mechanisms due to multiple factors including challenging economic circumstances and lack of educational and livelihood opportunities. As noted in the 2018 MCNA, violence at home is reported both in IDP and returnee locations nationally, which indicates the need to strengthen family and community based care for children. Girls and boys living in and out of camps are increasingly out of school, mainly due to the lack of financial means, exposing them to higher child protection risks.

Activities

UNHCR’s project will be aligned to the strategic objectives and approaches agreed upon by the Child Protection Sub-Cluster. In line with the systems strengthening approach, UNHCR will increase the investment in national child protection systems provided by the State and national civil society partners, based on an analysis of the existing systems, access of IDP children to these systems and their acceptability, affordability and quality. UNHCR’s interventions to support child-sensitive legal procedures for custody and legal guardianship and child victims of violence, and civil documentation (birth registration) will support the strategy and interventions of the CP Sub-Cluster, but will be budgeted and reported under the Protection Cluster. UNHCR’s activities will include: (i) As Protection Cluster lead and as an agency, UNHCR will advocate with authorities to increase non-discriminatory access for children associated with extremist groups to national child protection systems and advocate for the removal of barriers for this group of children and youth to access services; (ii) in IDP camps, UNHCR and partners will continue to provide integrated prevention and response services for children who are at risk of or victims of violence, neglect, abuse or exploitation or who have specific needs that require an urgent response. UNHCR will provide specialised programmes to ensure that, at a minimum, all vulnerable children receive immediate quality case management services in line with their best interests; (iii) UNHCR and partners will support children, families and communities to ensure that those children identified with signs of acute psychosocial distress are assisted to overcome the effects of conflict and displacement and that they are subsequently protected from further violence, abuse, neglect and exploitation; (iv) UNHCR and partners will promote positive values and practices that protect children and build their resilience among families and communities through enhanced community-based protection networks, outreach volunteers and committees in camps and return areas. Parenting skills programmes aimed at reducing harsh forms of physical discipline and parental stress will be run through community-based child protection responses such as Child Protection Committees (CPCs) who will also be supported to identify and respond to child protection issues at a local level; and (v) UNHCR and partners will convene trainings for frontline staff from other organizations and relevant government agencies, where appropriate, building upon UNHCR’s best interests procedures; (vi) UNHCR and partners will also ensure that their community-based protection work and engagement with community leaders has a strong focus on child protection, supporting community leaders to identify and respond to child protection issues in their area, and where possible, engaging adolescents and youth in youth groups that support them to address their own protection issues, and developing their capacity to be active members of their community; and (vii) UNHCR will continue contributing to the MRM mechanism in Iraq, including through participation in coordination fora and gathering reliable information on grave violations. The prioritized response will focus on case management, psychosocial activities and capacity building of a number of government line ministries.

Protection Risk Analysis

UNHCR continues to receive complaints of SEA and aid diversion, and continues to investigate all complaints in line with the PSEA Network SOPs and its own internal oversight and control mechanisms. In order to address this protection risk, UNHCR requires that all partners sign the UNHCR Code of Conduct, and UNHCR monitors compliance with the Zero Tolerance Policy on SEA and UNHCR SGBV policies and guidance. As co-chair of the PSEA Network, UNHCR will also actively engage to share key messages on complaints mechanisms and build capacity of partners on PSEA and investigations, while continuing to directly investigate any complaints of SEA, fraud or aid diversion. Through its leadership of the CwC Task Force, UNHCR will support IDPs and returnees to make informed decisions about solutions and to better understand how to access their rights and entitlements, and ensure that they are aware of complaints and feedback mechanisms. UNHCR is committed to localization, and will ensure that national partners are supported to strengthen their capacity to develop and implement protection projects that effectively mainstream prevention of GBV and PSEA, and that they implement a community-based approach that integrated AAP. UNHCR will also maintain regular meetings with partners implementing protection projects, in particular projects that may subject partner staff to harassment and/or threats (e.g. legal aid partners representing persons with perceived affiliation), and follow-up directly any allegations with relevant authorities to ensure that humanitarian protection partners are able to safely support IDPs and returnees.

Mitigation Measures

The Government of Iraq declared in December 2017 it had regained control of all Iraqi territory from extremists. However, the scale and severity of humanitarian needs remain high and returns and rehabilitation have not taken place at the scale and speed anticipated. Many of the 1.87 million internally displaced persons (IDPs) and 4.11 million returnees face constrained access to basic services as well as security and protection risks while contending with destroyed properties and infrastructure, lack of livelihood opportunities and financial resources. This has led to secondary displacement or re-admittance to camps where return was not possible or sustainable. IDPs and returnees continue to face serious protection risks including unlawful and disproportionate restrictions on access to safety and freedom of movement, confiscation of documents, abduction and illegal detention, forced encampment, forced and premature returns, and increased risk of sexual gender-based violence. Some IDPs are barred from returning to areas of origin by security actors, tribal leaders or communities over alleged affiliation with extremists. This will result in challenges in identifying sustainable solutions for IDPs, and may require longer-term engagement of UNHCR and humanitarian partners in the provision of protection and assistance programmes. UNHCR continues to receive complaints of SEA and aid diversion, and continues to investigate all complaints in line with the PSEA Network SOPs and its own internal oversight and control mechanisms. In terms of mitigation measures, UNHCR will undertake the following actions: (i) capacity building interventions for Government and civil society actors on the Principled Returns Framework and the Guiding Principles on Internal Displacement and relevant international and national human rights legal frameworks with a view to strengthening their capacity to implement sustainable solutions in line with the principles of voluntariness, safety and dignity and so that Government actors gradually assume their responsibilities for providing protection to conflict-affected populations; (ii) UNHCR requires that all partners sign the UNHCR Code of Conduct, and UNHCR monitors compliance with the Zero Tolerance Policy on SEA and UNHCR SGBV policies and guidance. As co-chair of the PSEA Network, UNHCR will also actively engage to share key messages on complaints mechanisms and build capacity of partners on PSEA and investigations; (iii) Through its leadership of the CwC Task Force, UNHCR will support IDPs and returnees to make informed decisions about solutions and to better understand how to access their rights and entitlements; (iv) UNHCR, through the Protection Cluster and directly, will also continue to actively engage in protection advocacy with Government actors and the international community; (v) UNHCR will also strengthen its internal tools to gather and analyse protection information through increased IM capacity and through continuous support to partners using CHAT and ASSIST; and (vi) With some multi-year grants concluding in 2018, the UNHCR operation will proactively engage with donors to underscore the necessity of continued support, including looking to non-traditional sources of funding.

How is your project accountable to affected population

UNHCR is a member of the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) Task Team on Accountability to Affected Populations, including Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (the AAP/PSEA Task Team). AAP is reflected in UNHCR's Revised Age, Gender and Diversity (AGD) Policy (2018), which aims to ensure that all sections of populations of concern have equitable and non-discriminatory access to protection and assistance programmes, and a say in decisions that affect their lives. The AGD Policy defines key elements of accountability with respect to affected populations to which UNHCR is committed. They include: communication and transparency; feedback and response; participation and inclusion; and learning and adaptation. The policy also standardizes the language used in reporting; informs priorities; supports assessments of the extent to which UNHCR achieves its AAP commitments; and provides criteria when evaluating learning and improvements. UNHCR’s programmes integrate three key elements that support AAP: (i) an AGD approach: An AGD approach is a rights and community-based approach according to which planning, programming, implementation, and monitoring and evaluation should be guided by principles of equality and participation; (ii) Community-based protection: this requires UNHCR to integrate community engagement in all aspects of its protection work, and recognize the capacities as well as needs of communities in humanitarian crises, in order to avoid harm and ensure that humanitarian programmes do not inadvertently leave people and communities worse off. A core tenet of the AGD policy, community-based protection employs a rights-based approach that puts persons of concern at the centre of decisions that affect their lives; (iii) Participatory assessment: this tool builds partnerships with IDP, returnee and host community women and men of all ages and backgrounds by promoting meaningful participation through structured dialogue. Separate discussions with women, girls, boys, and men, including adolescents, enable UNHCR and partners to gather accurate information on the specific protection risks that different groups face, identify underlying causes, understand community capacities, and hear their proposed solutions. It helps communities to take collective action to enhance their own protection, and makes it possible to implement a rights- and community-based approach. In Iraq, UNHCR will implement the above approach by ensuring integration of the Revised AGD Policy in all of its partnerships, including through the organization of its annual Participatory Assessment to guide participatory planning, and to provide feedback to IDPs and returnees on project implementation. As lead of the CwC Task Force, UNHCR will support partners to integration community-based approaches in its programming and to ensure that two-way feedback is incorporated into protection programmes. In the specific area of GBV, an internal expert is currently deployed in Iraq to assist UNHCR to develop a National SGBV Mainstreaming Action Plan. The plan will include mainstreaming SGBV and PSEA into UNHCR’s sectoral programming and response, including child protection and work with adolescents and youth. UNHCR is also supporting the Protection, CCCM and Shelter/NFI Clusters to integrate SGBV into their planning and implementation. UNHCR will continue to closely coordinate with IDP Call Center to ensure that UNHCR follows up on reported issues, and challenges are addressed and appropriate referrals made.
