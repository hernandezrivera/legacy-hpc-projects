Project Summary

The main aim of this project is to address the challenges associated with access to and utilization of essential lifesaving emergency healthcare services by all people in need and endeavour to improve the same in Nyirol and Uror Counties. This is in line with the Health Sector Response plan 2022 for South Sudan which emphasizes three objectives i.e. to improve equitable access to life-saving essential quality health care services including maternal, child and adolescent health, sexual and reproductive health, treatment common illnesses, disability, MHPSS and GBV health services to crisis affected populations, reduce excess morbidity and mortality by timely detection and coordinated response to epidemic prone diseases, enhance resilience and promote humanitarian-development linkages to strengthen health system recovery and coping mechanisms. The design of this project is emphatic on provision of basic essential health services in line with the established protocols and guided by the epidemiological profile of disease outbreaks, seasonality and trauma related emergency requirements. Prevention, preparedness, response and mitigation activities responding to people in need, with attention to access for vulnerable groups like women, children and person with disabilities is paramount in the delivery of this project. Therefore, the emergency that this project will address is in respect of the high rate of mortality from the highly prevalent epidemic diseases. In the volatile context of Jonglei, the project will target Returnees, IDPs recently displaced by conflict and flooding, and host communities in areas with limited access to health services. Whereas the 2022 HC PIN is 62,246 and 105,919 in Nyirol and Uror respectively, this project targets 80,000 beneficiaries out of the HC target of 115,859 consisting of Host Community, IDPs and Returnees and disaggregated by County, age, gender and disability i.e. U5 =M7,689-F7,758, 6-17 yrs=M17,049-F13,798, 18-60yrs.=M14,267-F15,039, >60yrs=M1,601-F2,799. Of these, the project targets a total of 550 who are PWD (U5-M8-F12, 6-17=M20-F40, 18-60=M175-F245, Over 60=M19-F31).
The flooding, limited food and income sources all have food insecurity and health implications. In order to achieve the expected results, the project has prioritized PHCC, PHCU and mobile team clinical packages with various strategies namely recruitment and placement of adequate staff in key cadres to provide key services such as provision of OPD consultations, BeMONC and CeMONC services in key health facilities, Initiating and sustaining IPC measures in health facilities, establishment and deployment of mobile response units and medical teams, renovation and equipping of health facilities, Conducting monthly supportive supervision of health facilities jointly with the CHD, Provision of MHPSS and CMR including training of staff on the same based on standard treatment protocols as well as rehabilitation services for disabled, referral of the survivors to safe houses and lastly, establishment and maintenance of feedback mechanism at health facilities. It is envisaged that a successful implementation of these activities through scaling-up access to integrated quality essential health services would significantly result in the overall decrease in morbidity and mortality arising from the epidemic prone diseases. It will further increase access to services for SGBV survivors, disabled individuals and people with mental health disorders. Besides improving resilience among vulnerable populations, the activities are further expected to benefit from an integrated response involving key sectors such as health, WASH, nutrition, FSL and protection. In order to ensure AAP, a mechanism will be provided for affected populations to receive the information needed to make informed decisions on accessing health services, provide feedback on the interventions, share concerns and submit complaints by involving all stakeholders.

1 - Needs

In spite of the signing of a peace agreement and the formation of a unity government, the situation in South Sudan remains fragile with key sectors such as health care bearing the brunt of the conflict and hence suffering some of the worst health indicators globally (WHO 2020). The health care situation is worse in states such as Jonglei which have borne the brunt of conflicts and other natural crises. In Nyirol and Uror Counties, for instance, poor access to healthcare services has been attributed to multiple factors including, conflicts, perennial flooding and displacement. Ultimately these conflicts have caused continued breakdown of health facilities and resulted in interruption of essential services and remains one of the main drivers of morbidity and mortality in the counties. Currently, the two counties have a disease burden characterized by health cluster PIN of 168,165 against a target of 115,859 and the project targets 80,000. Indeed in these counties limited health facility functionality and capacity, and poor coverage, have made it difficult to meet additional health needs posed by floods, food insecurity and frequent conflicts. Women, children, elderly and persons with disabilities are particularly vulnerable and most frequently face limited access to healthcare. Whereas nationally, only 59% of the population can access health facilities in one hour’s time (2022 FSNMS+HNOi survey), this proportion is drastically lower in the two counties due to various barriers in accessing health services. The extremely low immunization coverage e.g. Penta 3 has made children more vulnerable to vaccine preventable communicable diseases (WHO Immunization Data portal)Malaria is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality, accounting for 52.6% of all morbidities and 58.2% of all mortalities in 2021. In spite of the effects of conflicts in the targeted counties, access to Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) remains inadequate. WHO prevalence estimates of mental disorders in conflict settings to South Sudan, is approx. 2.5 million cases, emphasizing the risk of mental health burden in the affected population WHO (2019). Research study by L. C., Lopez, B. et al (2017) shows 41% of probable occurrences of Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) in six states. Attack on health care workers/facilities continues to negatively impact provision of and access to health services. Additional stress on the PHC system is due to malnutrition. Similarly, seasonal outbreaks of communicable diseases including cholera and measles, and the continued endemicity of malaria pose additional challenges. On the other hand, perennial flooding in the targeted counties has exposed people to Malaria, Diarrhea and other waterborne diseases. Unfortunately, flood-response activities have been considerably constrained by infrastructure damage and reduced physical accessibility and insecurity. A critical analysis of gaps in the health sector in the two counties, therefore, points to the dire need for the provision of essential lifesaving basic health services. Besides the need to put in place adequate prevention measures there is urgent need for disease outbreak detection and timely response and enhancement of the referral pathways for MPSS, SGBV survivors as well as maternal and child health emergencies. With recorded cases in the counties, there is need to mainstream COVID-19 prevention and response activities to mitigate the spread and threat to communities. The rise in SGBV has heightened the need for comprehensive care of victims of SGBV. Women, adolescent girls and children are most at risk. This reality coupled with conflict, insecurity and flooding causes severe restrictions to access health services. Besides static services through health facilities, special medical outreaches and community-based protection measures are needed to expand equitable services and also ensure PLW and children have seamless access to adequate health services.

2 - Project activities

This project is aligned to the 2022 health sector response plan and therefore the activities proposed are aimed at facilitating the achievement of the Strategic, specific and sectoral objectives. By implementing these activities, CMA seeks to improve people’s access to and utilization of essential health care services to reduce excess morbidity and mortality in Nyirol and Uror Counties. It is envisaged that scaling up of the proposed activities will ensure that disease outbreaks will be detected early, responded to in a timely manner and supported by adequate surveillance systems through the Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) and Early Warning Alert System (EWARS). Further this project has an inherent plan to provide health services adapted to the current COVID-19 context and ensure COVID-19 prevention and appropriate response activities are integrated while continue providing essential lifesaving health services. In alignment with the HRP and health cluster objectives, the project has been planned around PHCC, PHCU and mobile team clinical packages. Therefore, 1) to improve equitable access to life-saving essential quality health care services including maternal, child and adolescent health, sexual and reproductive health, treatment common illnesses, disability, MHPSS and GBV health services to crisis affected populations, CMA plans to carry out the following activities: 1.1 Recruitment and placement of adequate staff in key cadres namely midwives and clinical officers to provide key services, 1.2 Capacity building for health care workers, 1.3 Provision of outpatient consultations, 1.4 Provision of BeMONC and CeMONC in key health facilities, 1.5 Initiate and sustain Infection prevention and control (IPC) measures in health facilities, 1.6 Provision of MHPSS and CMR services in health facilities,1.6 Provision of rehabilitation services for disabled, 1.7 Capacity building of health personnel for providing CMR services following the standard treatment protocols, 1.8 Referral of the survivors to safe houses, 1.9 Capacity building of health personnel to provide MHPSS services, 2) Similarly, in order to reduce excess morbidity and mortality by timely detection and coordinated response to epidemic prone diseases, the project plans to focus on the following activities: 2.1 Emergency preparedness and contingency planning for epidemic diseases, 2.2 Case management for selected diseases (Malaria, ARI, AWD, ABD, Measles); 2.3 Immunization against vaccine preventable diseases, 2.4 Disease surveillance including outbreak investigations and response, 2.5 Epidemic disease prevention and control measures, 2.6 procurement of outbreak response commodities and emergency antigens for immunization and cold chain commodities. 3) In order to enhance resilience and promote humanitarian-development linkages to strengthen health system recovery and coping mechanisms, the following activities will be undertaken in the project,: 3.1 Renovation and equipping of health facilities, 3.2 Conduct monthly supportive supervision of health facilities jointly with the CHD, 3.3 Support targeted number of static integrated facilities, 3.4 Establishment of mobile response units and deployment of medical teams, 3.5 Provide technical and operational assistance to heath facilities through supportive supervision visits to ensure quality provision of health services and accountability to the affected population 3.6 Conduct community consultative meetings within the community, 3.7 Promote humanitarian-peace-development nexus bringing humanitarian, peace and development actors together to promote synergies and optimize use of available resources, 3.8 Establishment and maintenance of feedback mechanism at health facilities.

3 - Project indicators

Indicators are markers of a project’s achievements since they describe a value that an entity seeks to achieve and informs stakeholders about the success or progress of the project. They are especially important in the monitoring and evaluation as tools for measuring success versus failure in a project. Therefore, in this project indicators will be assessed to establish if the project requirements have been delivered and success criteria set met as promised in terms of service delivery. The important factor here will be to measure if indeed this project did deliver on the set objectives. This will be measured by focusing on the review of planned activities and the operational and performance indicators. Some of the key indicators of success include: 1) Case fatality rate for malaria by age and gender, 2) % of alerts, rumors and outbreaks investigated within 48 hours of notification, 3) # of reactive measles vaccination of boys and girls between 6 months – 15 years, 4)% of health facilities assessed using Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) scorecard, 5) Coverage of PENTA3 in <One-year-old by administrative unit, 6) # of community health workers per 1,000 people, 7) % of deliveries assisted by a skilled birth attendant 8) # of consultations per person per year disaggregated by sex, age, and disability 9) # of persons receiving MHPSS services disaggregated by sex, age and disability 10) # of SGBV survivors receiving CMR services by age and sex and disability # of persons with disability receiving rehabilitation services disaggregated by age and sex 11) # of community health events conducted for behavioural change communication 12) # of people reached by health education and promotion during disease outbreaks disaggregated by sex, age and disability 13) % of facilities with community feedback mechanism, 14) # of community consultative meetings conducted to identify needs, participate in response, and reduce impact on their lives disaggregated by men, women, boys and girls and disability, 15) # of functioning health facilities provided with supportive supervision quarterly 16) # of meetings conducted with humanitarian, development and peace partners 17) # of pregnant women who attended 4 ANC visits. Further, the two health parameters of PIN and targets, will provide a basis for the measurement of the progress attained. In terms of accurate, repeatable, and verifiable data, this project will collect and store the same for purposes of measuring success, and facilitating decision making regarding adjustments or modifications in implementation and delivery. CMA will use various tools to monitor activities: (1) FGDs to monitor protection, impacts of awareness outreaches and access to health facilities; (2) Regular consultations with affected populations of all cadres to ensure participation in planning and monitoring the program, access to services, implementation of a complaints mechanism and raise awareness on its process, and ensure a system of AAP is in place; (3) Monthly HMIS, weekly IDSR reports and immunization campaign reports from health facilities; (4) Monthly activity reports from health facilities providing data not included in the HMIS reports; (5) Quarterly project reports to donors; (6) Quarterly field monitoring and evaluation reports. Feedback from the key stakeholders will also be used to measure success wed data will be used to ascertain their satisfaction with the delivery. Instruments shall be administered to selected respondents to rate various success factors and subsequently the data availed will provide evidence for strengths and weaknesses and inform improvement options. Similarly, based on the contractual agreements between CMA and the donors, guidelines on reporting will be strictly adhered to as stipulated i.e. either per mid-term or end of project submission. Where necessary, joint participatory evaluation exercises will be conducted by CMA, health cluster team, CHD and donor representatives.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The Facility lead officer will be responsible for coordinating the engagement with communities and actively seeking their feedback. The consultation activities will be recorded and reported monthly to the County Officers and the project management team, highlighting what new actions (if any) need to be taken. These measures will ensure that an effective system of representation of affected populations is in place for the purpose of improving project results. Further, the Project Manager will visit and supervise the project (once per quarter) to conduct monitoring and evaluation so that regular assessment of beneficiary knowledge on project progress and their satisfaction with project performance in conducted, and new measures put in place in response to beneficiary feedback. Further, the Project Manager will conduct meetings with local leaders of host and IDP communities and local authorities to ensure effective implementation and robust monitoring of the complaints mechanism is in place so as to achieve effective accountability to the populations being served.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

CMA’s on-ground personnel will undertake focus group discussions (FGDs) and key informant interviews (KIIs) as part of the planning and monitoring processes to ensure ongoing effective feedback. CMA’s Facility lead officers will ensure that the most vulnerable disabled, illiterate, women and children are included. At all sites, the affected population groups that CMA consults in the planning and feedback processes are as follows: (1) local women and men leaders, focus groups and key informant women and men; (2) Mother to Mother Support Groups, other social groups representing the most vulnerable including individuals living with disabilities and (3) discharged patients women, men, girls and boys including individuals living with disabilities. In all the deliberations held with these diverse groups, their concerns and complaints will be recorded and analyzed further by the project management team. Ultimately, this team will be tasked with the responsibility of coming up with strategies which are friendly and therefore able to address the concerns of the most vulnerable members of the targeted beneficiaries.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

CMA has completed the rollout of its revised PSEA policy with training of all management at Juba and County levels and the Facility lead officers at the facility level. The County level officers and the Facility lead officers are now skilled to implement the PSEA training at the facility level which includes training of facility-based, mobile team and outreach workers on: (1) gender, age and disability appropriate methods and content for disseminating information about PSEA; (2) available channels for reporting abuse; (3) confidentiality rules in reporting abuse cases; and (4) the mechanisms and locations available for victims of abuse to access support and treatment. CMA has already also developed a whistleblower policy which has been shared with senior staff waiting to be shared with all staff at various levels including the field to facilitate timely reporting of PSEA related cases. Similarly, adequate communication materials have been developed including posters and banners which would be used to sensitize and create awareness among the beneficiaries regarding their role in Prevention and/or Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA). In this endeavor, the overall strategy will include use of these materials for awareness-raising campaigns to sensitize targeted communities on sexual exploitation and abuse; reporting mechanisms and procedures for ensuring confidentiality and the rights of survivors; and mainstreaming of sexual exploitation and abuse in any workshop and training conducted by CMA. CMA continues to employ a three-tier ToT approach in building the capacity of staff on PSEA issues and also for dealing with the field based challenges associated with PSEA. Under this strategy, CMA ensures that training on PSEA is conducted at Juba level then the same is extended to the project implementation team in the various field locations who finally have the responsibility to conduct awareness raising sessions with the project beneficiaries and the community members. For instance, CMA key staff recently attended the UNOCHA coordinated training in PSEA and Fraud targeting various implementing partners. After benefiting from such trainings, CMA ensures that the same is rolled out to the field teams through sessions facilitated by the original beneficiaries.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Based on its longevity in programming in various sectors, CMA has an inbuilt mechanism of involving project beneficiaries in key project activities from the planning stage through to the launch, implementation as well as monitoring and evaluation. For instance, the emphasis on needs assessment on entry to new locations on an ongoing basis thereafter ensures that project activities are tailored to community needs. Information is disaggregated in respect of gender, age and the needs of different population groups to improve suitability of planned interventions. In locations already served, CMA has established mechanisms to ensure accountability to affected populations. In new locations, CMA will apply its established strategy and implementation model to ensure accountability to affected populations. The structures and mechanisms established enable affected populations to participate actively in planning services, receive information they need to make informed decisions on accessing services, provide feedback on interventions, voice and submit complaints and participate in program decision-making. CMA’s programs are implemented in collaboration with local authorities and leaders of host communities and IDPs. The integration approach adopted through sectors such as health, nutrition and WASH ensures that information about the activities of each sector is widespread and therefore readily available in the community. At all locations, monthly meetings with leaders, Boma Health Committees, Mother-to-Mother Support Groups and other key social groups are conducted to report on project output and obtain community feedback. The Facility lead officers or supervisors, (typically a Clinical Officer or Team Leader) conduct discharge interviews with a sample of the clients/patients to obtain feedback from those who receive services. These consultations provide the most vulnerable of affected populations full awareness and opportunity to utilize the complaints mechanism, the opportunity to actively participate in planning and monitoring the project, learn their entitlements for accessing project services and render complaints CMA’s on-ground teams will ensure that affected populations are aware and able to utilize the complaints mechanism by practicing the principles of transparency, communication and provision of information linked to the complaints mechanism, and engaging the target populations to actively seek out their feedback.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

8.1 High Risk Planned Mitigation Measures: Beneficiaries face exposure to violence while accessing facility-based services because of long distances to locations where project services will be centered. To mitigate this risk, CMA will employ mobile and outreach approaches to go to the locations where at-risk populations live in order to reach beneficiaries in a safe way. Further, the project will promote community-based strategies and practices to provide protection for the most vulnerable community members (children, PLW, IDPs and disabled). The project will engage men and women leaders to take responsibility for mobilizing protection for disadvantaged and vulnerable individuals, so that they have unimpeded access to services. To adhere to the principles of “Do-No-Harm”, the project will strive to deliver services in a balanced manner so that IDP and host community populations will have equal access to project services. To achieve this balance, CMA will implement a strong awareness campaign to engage both IDP and host community leaders.8.2 High Risk Planned Mitigation Measures: Women, girls and boy with and without disabilities feel unsafe and are at risk of sexual violence and abuse. CMA will conduct community assessments for delivery of services from static, mobile and outreaches, and apply the “do-no-harm” approach to reduce the risk sexual of exploitation and abuse. The project will conduct safety audits at all static and mobile services, and implement all recommended measures to improve safe access to services. The safety audits will be led by CMA Safety Audit Focal Person, and implemented by female staff members in respect of common protection protocols. These measures will ensure access and minimize exposer of women and girls to violence, including sexual exploitation and abuse as they access to project assistance. 8.4 High Risk Planned Mitigation Measures: To address the need for available and effective protection assistance and referral services for abuse survivors, project sites will be linked to health services where CRM service will be provided for victims of SGBV including psychological support for the trauma experienced by child survivors of conflict and of SGBV. Through promotion and awareness sessions, both those in treatment and the wider community will be made aware of the appropriate referral pathways for the services that survivors need. Further, local authorities and community leaders will be made aware of available protection assistance and the GBV referral pathway for CMR and psychological support for survivors of SGBV.8.5 High Risk Planned Mitigation Measures: Exclusion of marginalized communities/minority groups from consultations and decision making on project approaches and interventions will be mitigated by adopting strategies to ensure communities: (1) have information they need to make informed decisions on accessing services, (2) can provide feedback on the project intervention and submit complaints. As women, girls, boys, the disabled and elderly are primary targets of this project, and to ensure they are all included in consultations and decision-making, CMA will organize specific forums (KIIs, FGDs) and meetings to engage these vulnerable groups. This will include engaging women’s groups, and women community leaders in consultation and decision making in the planning, throughout the implementation and at evaluation and this will also include discharge interviews. To reach the disabled and elderly, CMA will conduct case seeking interviews and consultation with these groups to ensure their situational context, needs and limitations/barriers are understood and accounted for in project planning and implementation decision-making.

12 - Contribution to Collective Outcomes
12.1. Does your project contribute to any of the three Collective Outcomes, i.e. Improved Food security; Enhanced support for prevention and response to needs of survivors of Gender-Based Violence; and Flood displacement?

Yes

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

The health project will be implemented along with nutrition services in all the targeted two counties of Nyirol and Uror to ensure timely and inclusive medical care, mental health and psychosocial support are available to target populations especially at risk women and GBV survivors. Where Protection partners are co-located within the county and operating in the vicinity of this project’s catchment area, CMA will seek the assistance of these partners to support women at risk and GBV survivors with legal matters. Where Protection partners are not available to assist in a direct way, CMA will refer women who need protection services to capable service providers and assist these vulnerable women and adolescent girls to access required legal, mental health and psychosocial services.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

Through health education and awareness campaigns within the communities, they will be sensitized on the effect of floods in all the sectors including health. In consultation with community leaders, sites for the health facilities shall be situated in high grounds which can not be easily overrun by flood waters. Similarly, the community members will be sensitized on the need to situate their homes/houses on high grounds. All these measure are likely to help mitigate against the impact of and the ctual flood related displacement.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

Nyirol and Uror counties are classified as severity level 4 with and in IPC 4Critical for malnutrition. It is a known fact that food production is dependent on a healthy population which is able to participate effectively in agricultural production. To ensure that production is not affected, this project will endeavor to reduce morbidity and mortality from many communicable diseases which make most people lethargic and unproductive. Similarly, in order to protect both patient and highly needed health staff, this project will encourage and support full implementation of IPC/WASH measures including COVID-19 prevention and management activities as COVID-19 is expected to have a long-term impact on, not only health service provision, but also food security. Furthermore, CMA will adopt an inter-sectoral coordination mechanism at all levels which is envisaged to work towards comprehensive integration and collaboration with other sectors including nutrition, food security and livelihoods, WASH, and protection implementing partners.
