Needs

A total of 8,941,401 people are in need of Food Security and Livelihoods (FSL) according to the 2019 HNO for Syria. More than 90% of households report spending over half of their income on food (FAO, 2017). Trends like hyperinflation, high food prices, and difficulty earning income have left many Syrian households unable to afford enough food to cover their needs and has limited their access to basic services. These gaps in economic access manifest in irregular food consumption patterns, a lack of dietary diversity, and a heavy reliance on coping strategies. In October 2018, WFP and FAO reported that half of all households surveyed reported buying food on credit. Reliance on this coping strategy was even more widespread among vulnerable households, particularly those that are IDP (60%), female-headed (57%), and rural (54%) (CFSAM, 2018. Highly vulnerable categories such as women-headed households, the elderly, and people with disabilities face particular challenges physically accessing food, often stemming from protection risks, such as threats to physical safety, or because of a lack of mobility. Pervasive income vulnerability encourages reliance on negative coping mechanisms such as taking on debt, early marriage, and child labour (source: DRC social counselling data, 2018). Disrupted education means that youth have been left without means to develop vocational skills enabling them to access livelihoods, while repeat displacements and destruction of economic capital has led small business owners to lose their livelihoods. Support to access sustainable livelihoods is therefore needed to increase capacities of the affected population to access basic services and increase their capacities to self-protect. The crisis in Syria has rendered different age-gender groups highly vulnerable. Women compelled to become heads of households because of the death or absence of husbands may face unsafe and/or inappropriate workspace conditions including lack of lockable WASH facilities and harassment from other workers and/or employers. Meanwhile, they may be paid less than men working the same jobs. Both girls and women may face pressure to marry, including marry before the ages of 18, to obtain a livelihoods or relieve pressure on resources in their own household. Stress and low psycho-social wellbeing due to income vulnerability and inability of boys and men unable to meet expected gender roles by generating income has been widely reported by DRC beneficiaries. Pressure to engage in unsafe income generating activities, including begging and survival sex the case of women and girls, also heightens vulnerabilities. Sources: HNO 2018 and HNO data 2019; DRC social counselling data, 2018.

Implementation of activities

To deliver skills development activities (vocational trainings and internships) DRC has field staff present in all areas of operation who will directly implement activities and supervise activities implemented by private contractors (including local trainers/workshops). Trainings and internships delivered by private contractors will be monitored on at least a weekly basis, if not multiple times per week, with DRC field staff speaking separately with beneficiaries and using structured feedback forms to gain beneficiary feedback. Selection and referral of beneficiaries is conducted through comprehensive livelihoods counseling enabling DRC to gauge each beneficiaries’ unique capacities, needs, and situation informing referral to the most suitable training/internship placement. Market analysis in each area of intervention will ensure responsiveness of skills development support to local market gaps and opportunities. The trainings/internships will last between two and three months each. In regards to micro-grants, DRC will support beneficiaries to create a comprehensive business development plan, including providing business development skills (BDS) trainings. The business development plan developed by the beneficiary will be assessed by a panel of DRC staff who ensure businesses are responsive to market needs, operationally and financially sound, and sustainable. The technical skills of beneficiaries are also assessed in a separate technical test. Should beneficiaries pass both tests the micro-grant is disbursed and followed-up with three months of coaching delivered directly by DRC staff. The community infrastructure rehabilitation projects will be based on detailed needs assessments conducted by DRC in close consultation with the local community and will utilize expert consultants to advise on strengthening inclusivity of the infrastructure to vulnerable groups (including people with disabilities). Works themselves will be implemented by local contractors and supervised by DRC engineers. Women field staff are available should women beneficiaries prefer to interface directly with women. Where possible, DRC will coordinate with other ERL sector partners to maximize complementarities and synergies, for example by restoring links along supply chains. Kindly note that the total target number of beneficiaries does not match the breakdown for the following reasons. Firstly, the beneficiary number includes household members of people who have received micro-grants. Secondly, the total target number factors beneficiaries who receive more than one service. Also, all beneficiaries provided with vocational skills training and/or support to start or re-start small businesses will be aged 17 or above; approximately 3,000 beneficiaries per community infrastructure rehabilitation project are envisaged, hence overall approximately 880 young children will benefit based on average population breakdowns in target areas. Where appropriate DRC will use activity groups separated by age group (young adults, middle aged adults, and older adults). DRC works with specialized consultants to ensure curriculum and pedagogical techniques are tailored to the needs of older adults who may require additional support. Beneficiaries directly influence the project through the following mechanisms. Firstly, DRC staff informally consult with local community members, including IDPs, small business owners, and heads of households to gain feedback on needs and market opportunities in the local area at the project design stage. Secondly, DRC uses phone surveys conducted on a three monthly basis to gain beneficiary feedback on activities and tailor activities accordingly.

Protection Risk Analysis

PR1: Women’s participation in vocational training, income generating activities, creates tensions in the community or family and exposes women to GBV. Risk: high; impact: high. Mitigation measures: private, in depth counselling with applicants before referring them to services to ascertain risk to applicant; referral where appropriate to trainings in vocations that can be done from home (e.g. sewing); provide transparent and clearly communicated information about the nature and objectives of activities. PR2: Inter or intra community tension due to service delivery locations causing perceptions of discrimination or favoritism. Risk: Medium; Impact: High. Mitigation measures: DRC’s community centres in Damascus, Rural Damascus, Homs, Dar’a have longstanding presence in the local community; selection of new locations for Homs and Dar’a centres, selection of new locations for satellite centres, and of new service delivery points for mobile teams, in line with shifting severity of needs across areas done according to transparent selection criteria, targeting the most vulnerable and selection criteria clearly communicated to the community , and ensuring adequate service mapping, needs assessment, and coordination with local authorities and local humanitarian actors, as well as humanitarian leadership on the Damascus level, has been conducted before entering into new locations. PR3: Population targeted by the intervention is put at risk due to location of DRC’s facilities; including lack of accessibility, lack of consideration to local/contextual factors. Risk: medium; Impact: high. Mitigation measures: location chosen according to transparent selection criteria, targeting most vulnerable, and ensuring adequate service mapping, needs assessment, and coordination with local authorities and local humanitarian actors, as well as humanitarian leadership, has been conducted before entering into new locations. Continuous situation monitoring by DRC Safety unit, standard SOPs in place and applied in case of security incidents, closure or reduction of opening hours in centres if necessary. PR4: Exploitation/abuse by staff or other service providers. Risk: medium; Impact: high. Mitigation measures: clear and regular communication with beneficiaries, including reinforcing the message that humanitarian services are free of charge; regular consultation with beneficiaries through group discussions; operation of complaint mechanism, including regular advertisement of the system to beneficiaries; training to all staff on code of conduct and PSEA; adherence to all of DRC’s global minimum standards and guidelines for protection.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1. Should any of the planned areas of intervention become inaccessible due to safety concerns, DRC would consider alternate locations in line with its standard targeting criteria and procedures. 2. Should exchange rate fluctuations between donor currencies and the Syrian pound mean that DRC had less actual funds available in Syria; and/or, should this project not be fully funded, DRC may be unable to deliver all activities as planned. DRC would prioritize those communities in most urgent need of Protection assistance according to verified data from the field. 3. Should all necessary approvals not be received from relevant stakeholders for new activities such as establishment of satellite centres, DRC would as a last resort return the related funds to donors.
