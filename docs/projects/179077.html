Project Summary

Building on the rehabilitation and re-equipment of PHC facilities in Medair’s health programming, integrated preventative and curative nutrition services will be provided at each facility and wherever possible in the community through community health workers (CHWs). Medair will contribute to the following strategic objectives set by the nutrition sector;
- Provide facility and community-based one-to-one counselling on WHO recommended Infant and Young Child Feeding and maternal nutrition practices
- Provide Micronutrient supplementation to pregnant women and children
- Building partners capacity in Nutrition program, coordination and management
- Early identification and referral of Acute Malnutrition cases
- Ensuring adequate coverage of treatment of Acute Malnutrition for children, pregnant and lactating mothers
- Provision of Quality services in CMAM centers
- Strengthen/Establish Nutrition surveillance system
Medair will contribute to these objectives through the activities outlined below;
Facility based activities will include malnutrition screening for CU5 and PLW, treatment of severe and moderate acute malnutrition, provision of micronutrient supplements for CU5 and PLW and iron supplementation for pregnant and postnatal women. IYCF counselling including breastfeeding support and promotion of appropriate complementary feeding will also be provided, both in health facilities and in communities. Relevant clinic staff will receive both formal and on the job training. Therapeutic food and supplements will be provided through a well-established partnership with UNICEF and WFP. In total, Medair expects to support around 720 beneficiaries with SAM or MAM.
CHWs will work in pairs in the clinic catchment area, delivering targeted relevant nutrition messages to households and providing malnutrition screening to CU5 and PLWs. They will also deliver health, hygiene and disability messages and act as a link to the PHC clinic, referring any malnutrition cases identified. In line with MoH guidance the CHWs are currently given a performance related quarterly gift in kind incentive worth up to approximately 60 USD.
Medair will target locations which are newly accessibly, hard to reach or have high severity needs. Planned locations include Rural Damascus, Deir-ez-Zor, rural Aleppo, Hama, rural Homs, southern Idleb and Dar'a and Quneitra. Medair has established access and initiated multisectoral programming in these areas. In addition possible expansion of activities into Raqqa is being actively explored.

4 - Needs

The deterioration of socio-economic conditions remains the primary concern for large parts of the population, suffering food insecurity, bread and fuel shortages, rapid price hikes and lack of availability of basic food items in local markets. According to security reports, people in many areas in DAR, QUN, RD and HOM have on several occasions gone for more than three consecutive days without bread, an occurrence not recorded since the start of the conflict. National average prices of standard reference food baskets are also rising rapidly; between November and December 2020, this rise was recorded at 13% and reached the highest average since WFP monitoring began in 2013. In its health activities, Medair observed a rise in malnourished children and PLW, likely linked to the economic crisis; a trend supported by anecdotal evidence from other partners in the Health sector and one which Medair will continue to monitor. A key factor in acute and chronic malnutrition is poor IYCF practices: a SMART survey in 2019 showed exclusive breastfeeding rates among children <6 months old of only 28%. In rural Dar’a, Medair found women usually returned to manual work 4-6 weeks after giving birth due to acute economic pressures, and did not breastfeed due to separation from their infants. Use of inappropriate breastmilk substitutes was widespread (FGD, 2019). Early initiation of breastfeeding in Aleppo city was only 57% despite 89% of deliveries taking place in a health facility (Medair LQAS survey, 2019), suggesting additional training of health staff is needed. The capacity of health and nutrition practitioners in specialized IYCF skills needs to be strengthened on a national scale.

5 - Implementation of activities

Integrated nutrition activities will be implemented in PHCCs already rehabilitated and reequipped in Medair’s health programme. Activities include screening and treatment of SAM and MAM in all CU5 and PLW, IYCF counselling of PLWs and provision of appropriate micronutrients. Cases of severe malnutrition with complications will be referred appropriately.Clinic staff receive an initial IYCF/CMAM training and on the job training. Provision of key messages and counselling in clinics will promote appropriate IYCF behaviours, particularly breastfeeding and adequate complementary feeding. Therapeutic supplements are expected to be supplied as GIK from UNICEF/WFP. Monthly supervision visits will monitor quality of implementation and counselling and data on outcomes. Through the CHP, CHWs will provide house to house malnutrition screening and IYCF promotion. Malnutrition cases identified are referred to the PHCC. About 20 CHWs/clinic are recruited (predominantly female) working in pairs in the catchment area, conducting 15-20 HH visits/week. In line with MOH guidance, CHWs receive quarterly gift worth around 60 USD.CHWs will receive a training, followed by bimonthly refreshers. Topics include IYCF, malnutrition screening, communication skills, first aid and disability. All activities will be closely coordinated with MoH/UNICEF/WFP. Medair/DoH are planning monthly joint supervision visits to the supported PHCCs, dependent on DoH availability and security clearance. Wherever permitted community members will be consulted during assessments. Beneficiary feedback will be sought through exit interviews if permitted and complaints boxes. Monitoring visits by UNICEF/WFP will be supported.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

• Confidentiality of sensitive data is breached, risking beneficiary stigmatisation: train staff on data protection; clinic staff to ensure privacy during consultations. • Reluctance of community leaders to accept GBV awareness-raising: involve influential parties from start of health programmes. Clearly communicate issues and encourage community contribution. Support social structures. • Inequity of access to clinics between MWBG: Awareness of wider community context e.g. transport availability, market times. Ensure service providers available at suitable times for women and men. Train clinic staff/CHWs. Monitor data for adverse trends. HH visit awareness-raising of importance of access for all children.• Head of HHs or PLWs reluctant to be screened, access services, or receive HH visits: CHWs mainly female (85%) to achieve acceptance for HH visits.• Inequity of access to clinics for people with mobility impairments: accessibility adaptations to be installed during rehabilitation; disability awareness and inclusion training to all staff and CHWs• Children at risk from accidents during building works: Explanation in advance to communities; Contractual requirements for tidy work sites. Site engineer to oversee contractors. Spot checks.
