Project Summary

When conflict is ongoing, measures need to be taken to protect vulnerable children and to maintain the provision of education. Schooling is an important institution in the lives of children and its continuation can assist them and their communities to cope with the effects of conflict.
The project targets the most disadvantaged population groups in areas facing the highest severity of need, categorized as severe to catastrophic (severity areas 4-6).
The project is In line with the No Lost Generation (NLG) Initiative, the Education Cannot Wait (ECW) Investment for Syria and the Education sector. It follows the three objectives identified in the Education 2019 HRP chapter.
Thus, this project aims at increasing the access to a protective, safe, and conducing learning environment among crisis-affected children and teachers.
It builds on a two-pronged strategy. The first pillar seeks to provide protective and safe learning environments through small-scale rehabilitation, including gender-sensitive WASH facilities (boost hygiene promotion and good practices among children), combined with the provision of learning/teaching supplies and student kits. Gender equality considerations are integrated into project design with a specific focus on ensuring gender sensitive WASH facilities. The second pillar works in parallel and aims at ensuring conducive learning environments through capacity building of teachers on pyscho-social support. Schools and teachers can facilitate children’s recovery from trauma, having a significant impact on their student’s well-being. The purpose of the psychosocial component of the project essentially, is enhancing the skills and competencies of teachers to support the psychosocial recovery of crisis -affected children; to understand, identify and refer children with symptoms of various psychological and behavioral problems to trauma therapy. This effort is crucial to allow longer-term progamming. As outcome, access to protective, safe, and conducing learning environments for 20,000 crisis-affected children (10,200 girls and 9,800 boys) and 500 teachers (255 women and 245 men). Finally, the project will be implemented directly by AAH, which has the capacity to start the implementation immediately, counting on the full-access to the facilities targeted. The overall focus of the outcome is placed on the children and teachers rather than the physical rehabilitation of school and educational facilities.

Needs

Education-related needs are tremendous. 2.1 million children in Syria are out of school, and a further 1.3 million are at risk of dropping out. 245,000 education personnel are in need of education assistance inside Syria, 61% of whom are in acute and immediate need. More than one in three schools are damaged or destroyed, while others are used for purposes not related to education such as shelter for IDPs (HNO 2019). Schools in IDP/returnee receiving communities have limited absorption capacity for newly arriving students, causing overcrowding. Lack of School Safety remains a critical barrier to children’s access to education. 762 attacks on education have been reported and 426 verified since the crisis started in 2011. Researches revealed the long-term neurological impacts of children’s repeated exposure to violence, which can lead to toxic stress and then the necessity to increase access to a protective, safe, and conducing learning environment among crisis-affected children and teachers. Schools and teachers can facilitate children’s recovery from trauma, having a significant impact on their student’s well-being. Through capacity building activities, teachers can identify and refer children with symptoms of various psychological and nutritional problems. For example, children in North-East Syria suffer from deteriorated conditions in schools due the absence of public services. Assessments done by AAH have indicated that, currently, schools do not have sufficient psychological counsellors to address the significant psychological needs of the school children. Psychosocial distress remains a major issue of concern for both children and caregivers. Children’s experiences of violence in their community, schools and homes, potentially together with repeated displacements, loss of or separation from family members and friends, dramatic deterioration in living conditions, divisions in their communities, and lack of basic social services is profoundly affecting children’s wellbeing and development. As most children are traumatized, teachers need to be trained on how to deal with children in wartime and how to behave during disasters. Because of the conflict, teachers observed that students are suffering psychological problems that affect their normal learning. At the same time, it was assessed that the 80% of teachers have not attended psychological support training courses.In Al Hasan Bin Al Haitham Elementary School in Hassakeh, the assessment conducted by AAH indicates that only 15% of the children are able to focus on classroom activities and a high degree of traumatized behavior and aggression is observed (Rapid Needs Assessment conducted in Al Hasan Bin Al Haitham Elementary School (Hassakeh), Action Against Hunger, June 2018). In Al Hasan Bin Al Haitham Primary School, as an example, it was observed that rehabilitation works are urgently required to provide a safe, protected and secure learning environment for traumatized children.

Implementation of activities

For almost nine years, children have been the direct targets of violence, witnessed violence, or committed violent acts themselves. This chronic condition increases their risk for cognitive and emotional distress, undernutrition and therefore behavioral difficulties later in life. Schooling is an important institution in the lives of children and its continuation can assist them and their communities in coping with the effects of conflict such as violence, psychological trauma, disability, and undernutrition among other effects. AAH will provide protective and safe learning environments through small-scale rehabilitation, including gender-sensitive WASH facilities, combined with the provision of teaching/learning supplies, and student kits. Small-scale rehabilitations will include standard basic works such as supply and installation of doors, repairing the walls and ceilings, walls painting, repair and installation of WASH facilities. Particularly the last action will also boost hygiene promotion and good practices among children, as preliminary visits to potential target schools evidenced poor hygiene practices among the pupils. Gender equality considerations are integrated into project design, and a specific focus on ensuring gender sensitive WASH facilities is placed, thanks of the sectoral expertise of AAH. In parallel AAH also aims at ensuring conducive learning environments through training to the teachers in psycho-social and nutritional support. School and teachers can facilitate children’s recovery from trauma, having a significant impact on their student’s well-being, promote peer interactions and boost care in the most natural support system outside of the family. The purpose of the psychosocial component of the project aims at enhancing the skills and competencies of teachers to support the psychosocial recovery of crisis affected children, and to understand, identify and refer children with symptoms of various psychological and behavioural problems to trauma therapy. This effort is crucial to have the long-term impact and feed into the current institutional system and settings. AAH has significant capacity to implement this project, with offices in Damascus, Hassakeh, and a presence in Aleppo. Moreover, a MoU has been signed in November 2018 with the Ministry of Education for the rehabilitation of schools, including gender-sensitive WASH facilities, and to build the capacity of educational-staff in phyco-social support for crisis-affected children.Throughout the project-cycle, beneficiaries will be consulted and involved. During the initial needs assessment, data are collected from community representatives. Then, the vulnerabilities and capacities of different people and groups are provided to the project teams to fully adapt activities to the gender and the diversity of the beneficiaries. Several mechanisms are used by AAH to foster accountability to beneficiaries. Traditional systems such as hotline or a feedback box are not used due to issues in terms of cultural and context relevance, safety and operational feasibility. Instead, AAH has developed informal mechanisms.The project gives priority to 20,000 crisis affected-children (IDPs, host communities and returnees) and 500 teachers (255 women and 245 men).

Protection Risk Analysis

AAH develops risks and mitigation measures following AAH Security Guidelines, Standard Operations Procedures and long experience and knowledge in the field. Possible risks that might encounter in project implementation have been identified based on context analysis, daily monitoring and observations. The analysis takes into consideration a number of stakeholders that might influence the context, access as well as the planning and implementation of activities. Small-scale rehabilitation/construction of class rooms/schools (including gender-sensitive WASH facilities): Risk that construction of education infrastructures degrades the local environment (likelihood: Low, impact: Low);Risk of insufficient safety and health standards at schools (Medium, High), Attacks on schools (High, High); risk of children being out of school due to unavailability of school furniture upon completion of school rehabilitation and construction of prefabricated classrooms (Medium, Medium); risk of not having enough WASH facilities for boys and girls, this might cause lack of privacy to the children (Medium, Medium).Mitigating measures: AAH will check environmental impact has been adequately considered prior construction/maintenance; Sector/cluster agreed minimum standards with members and local authorities commitment; regular communication with local education authorities, community leaders, schools, etc. ; awareness raising on safety for students, school staff and organization; AAH will follow Do no harm approach and Code of Conduct; ensure wash facilities rehabilitation is part of intervention, if not AAH will coordinate with other organizations on priority needs; A new MoU has been signed in November 2018 with the Ministry of Education.Training to the teachers in psycho-social and nutritional support:Increased risk of adding harm to children if knowledge is not applied appropriately (Medium, High).Mitigating measures: AAH will ensures that teachers are trained properly with pre and post assessment for validation.Provision of teaching/learning supplies, and student kitsRisk of Interference of armed groups, including confiscation of sensitive materials (laptops, phones) (Medium, Medium); Increased risk of theft from children receiving kits (Low, Medium); Increased risk of inter-community tension due to varied assistance packages across education sector members (Medium, Medium) (High fluctuations in prices of items needed (if procurement happens inside Syria) (medium, Medium), Lack of suitable markets (Medium, Medium), Risk of not being able to access HTR areas (Medium, Medium), Delay in delivery (Medium, Medium). Mitigation measures: Coordination and communication with different stakeholders on activity; all materials should be delivered by organizations following security protocol and procurement procedures; Students and teachers kits should be per standard agreed by sector and MoE;

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risk 1: The security situation in targeted areas is unstable and AAH has no access to implement the activities; there are additional unforeseen administrative procedures imposed, and restrictions on access to beneficiaries. Likelihood: medium. Impact: highMitigation: AAH works and coordinates with authorities and Ministries to maintain access to target areas and beneficiaries, and the capacity to implement activities. AAH will reach out to local authorities and line ministries with trust building activities to further develop relationships and capacity to avoid or mitigate restrictions in AAH capacity to operate in Northern and Southern Syria. Advocacy and coordination with the humanitarian community and humanitarian diplomacy activities on the local level are also considered to contribute to this mitigation measure. Risk 2: Substantive devaluation of the EURO or USD against the Syrian pound; International bank transfers to Syria are fluid and timely.Likelihood: medium. Impact: highMitigation: The eventuality of significant changes in the value of the Syrian currency will be closely monitored and, if materialized, timely communicated to donors and options discussed. To overcome the lack of liquidity that affected several INGOs in 2018, AAH opened a new bank account, and resulted in successful completion of an international transfer. Risk 3: Aid diversion, fraud, and corruptionLikelihood: medium. Impact: highMitigation: diversification of supplier, triangulation of information, standard monitoring procedures in place, security plan and risks assessments tables updated and followed. Risk 4: Information gathering activities hampered by suspicion or misunderstandingLikelihood: low. Impact: medium Mitigation: Transparency, trust-building, developing relationships with municipalities, district authorities and facilities managers
