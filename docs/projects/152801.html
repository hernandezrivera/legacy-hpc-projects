Project Summary

The proposed project is a graduated approach to support the protection of displaced and conflict-affected populations through static, mobile, and emergency responses.
The project will offer the following services:
• Protection context assessments in all locations and, where possible, community-based protection monitoring, in which community focal points will be trained to report on protection needs and risks. NRC will prioritise working with women, youth, and vulnerable persons as monitoring focal points. Protection incidents will be documented and analysed to understand trends and evolution in the protection context, and results disseminated to communities and relevant stakeholders.
• Provision of individual protection assistance (IPA), to persons with urgent protection needs. This will include providing a tailored intervention to an individual or small group of individuals. It will t include facilitated referral, material assistance, or unconditional cash transfer.
• Support the creation of community-driven protection (CDP) mechanisms in hard to reach, underserved areas. NRC teams will work with communities to establish a series of local committees, including marginalized groups, through careful selection of representatives to ensure effectiveness and inclusiveness.
• Facilitate improved access to legal identity documentation for displacement affected persons who cannot access basic rights and services, especially school-aged children who have challenges applying for state exams. NRC will provide both awareness raising sessions to at-risk populations and individualized assistance in accessing national identity documentation.
• Housing Land and Property (HRP) rights. The HLP activities will include: 1) organizing dialogue between government and women leaders, host community’s leaders and for land allocation for persons returning from camps (if needed) to discuss HLP rights for returnees. 2) HLP rights trainings targeting chiefs, youth groups, older people, opinion leaders, customary authorities, local government authorities to enable fair allocation of land to returnees 3) Free legal counselling to those in need of it.
INTEGRATION: The project will be implemented in an integrated way so that IDPs and returnees don’t only receive protection support but also a source of Food, WASH and Shelter through other NRC projects in the planned locations as well as referrals to other agencies. This process will start with multi-sectoral assessments, joint trainings and deployment of integrated teams to respond. NRC is already using this approach of protection mainstreaming and integration in Education where NRC works with school management committees on prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA) of children.
COORDINATION: The project will be implemented in coordination with other players in the sector especially UNHCR, IOM for information sharing and use of the cluster core pipeline supplies as well as other Shelter cluster members for joint assessments and response when appropriate. The project will also be coordinated with the Protection cluster especially in prioritization of locations and needs assessment.
GENDER: In both CDR, HLP and other components of the project, the implementation will be preceded by the Gender and vulnerability analysis so as to best understand the needs of boys, girls, women and men as well as the social structures and resources to advance inclusion of all categories of people including the elderly and PWDs.
RISKS: The risks associated with HLP work are lack of acceptance of the IDPs and returnees by the Host communities as well as politicizing the land allocation to returnees. NRC will not be directly involved in land allocation discussions and all the activities will be done in a transparent and participatory manner with all stakeholders. The project will limit its scope to training, advocacy and facilitating dialogue and acquiring legal documents.

Needs

South Sudan is first and foremost a protection crisis. More than half of respondents in NRC protection needs assessments reported that food insecurity, lack of WASH services, and absence of livelihoods opportunities were due to protection risks. Unintentional and deliberate attacks on civilians will continue, including conflict-related sexual violence, targeted killing and destruction of property. The conflict exposes all segments of the population to protection risks. Men and boys are at risk of forced recruitment, targeted detention, ill-treatment and killings; indeed, in NRC assessments, targeted killing and assault of men and boys was the leading protection risk identified by all respondents in Akobo. Girls and women of every age are at heightened risk of displacement; in most locations targeted by NRC, women and girls account for more than 60% of displaced people. They also face abduction, injury and killings in addition to all forms of gender based violence in their homes and public spaces. Domestic and intimate partner violence is consistently identified as a common risk facing women and girls across all parts of the country and displacement status, with some 50% of women and 19% of men in Akobo suggesting physical violence against women occurred regularly in their household and 43% of all respondents in Wau reporting gender-based violence as a serious issue in their community. In Yei, women respondents noted not only was sexual assault of women and girls common, but that deliberate deprivation of resources, especially HLP rights, and targeted neglected disproportionately affected women. Approximately one in three South Sudanese have fled their homes and are either internally displaced or have sought refuge in neighbouring countries. IDPs inside POC sites continue to be at risk of attack from outside threats as well as increasing levels of violence and decreasing psycho-social well-being within the sites. 2019 is also likely to see a heightened risk of forced and secondary displacement from POC sites. IDPs outside of POC sites face limited and uncertain access to humanitarian services including specialised life-saving services and psycho-social support. Large IDP populations strain the limited resources of host communities and are often sources of inter-communal tension, particularly in areas of mixed ethnicities; in Wau, 45% of newly arrived IDPs reported hostility from the host community. As a result of the insufficiency of services and near-perpetual nature of shocks, people are forced to resort to negative coping mechanisms, like family separation, child marriage, child labour, self-restriction of movement, and survival sex, all of which were reported in recent NRC assessments. Access to accurate and actionable information is scarce, while response remains mostly focused on remedial action to address the consequences of violence rather than preventing risks for affected populations. There is a need for more and better resources dedicated to protection monitoring to inform subsequent protection interventions. Acknowledging the importance of protection monitoring in successful protection coordination and programming, the Protection Cluster has previously prioritised protection assessment and monitoring to better inform protection and humanitarian response. Achieving this objective requires the creation and maintenance of a robust protection monitoring system; thus, NRC takes protection monitoring as the foundational activity of its protection programme upon which to build further evidence-based programming.

Activities

NRC will implement a community-based protection monitoring project, allowing access to timely and accurate data. NRC will synthesize actionable information products relevant to the protection and assistance of displacement and conflict-affected populations and inform further decision-making, prioritisation, advocacy, and programme design for both NRC and the Protection Cluster. Alongside NRC protection monitors, the project will train community focal points to collect data and report on protection incidents. Utilisation of focal points will result in a greater immediacy of information gathering, as volunteer enumerators will be able to collect and report data in near real time. Delegating some data collection to focal points will allow NRC teams to utilise more complex data triangulation approaches, the overall impact of which could be a more accurate data analysis. NRC will offer individual protection assistance (IPA) persons with urgent protection needs, particularly vulnerable women. IPA focuses on addressing a central, immediate protection risk through one-off support with limited follow-up. It is not a long-term intervention, nor is it designed to assist generalized vulnerability. IPA support should be directly related to a specifically assessed need of the beneficiary, with a clear ameliorating and protective outcome. IPA interventions will engage a client-centred approach aimed at empowering the client – and where appropriate the caregiver – by giving them increased awareness of choices they have in dealing with protection risks, and assisting them to make informed decisions about how to address the risks they face. IPA will usually include some element of material assistance, including facilitate refer, procurement of NFI, or unconditional cash transfer. NRC will support the creation of community-driven protection (CDP) mechanisms in hard to reach, underserved areas. CDP programming will comprise of a series of inclusive local committees who will be empowered to identify and analyse protection risks in their own communities, addressing negative coping and promoting positive community-wide coping mechanisms through engagement with community leaders, awareness-raising sessions and sensitization campaigns. CDP will reduce community members’ vulnerabilities and enhance community risk preparedness and resilience through community contingency planning and projects overseen by the community committees. CDP will provide opportunities for NRC to raise awareness and disseminate key information on fundamental rights and protection issues identified and prioritised by targeted communities, as well as piloting innovative community-based referral and accountability mechanisms. Due to NRC’s thematic expertise and the high need, NRC will also offer a suite of information, counselling, and legal assistance (ICLA) interventions, including: (a) awareness raising regarding access to legal identity documentation, including nationality certificates, complimented by targeted individual assistance to vulnerable cases; (b) improved understanding of and ability to exercise HLP rights and responsibilities for individuals and groups affected by displacement with priority to the most vulnerable, including female and child-headed households, the elderly, individuals with physical and mental disabilities, and those who are extremely poor; and (c) training and mentoring in collaborative dispute resolution (CDR) mechanisms. ICLA interventions will target conflict-affected areas and areas of potential return. In its HLP response, NRC will compliment awareness raising sessions with tailored individual counselling to vulnerable persons requiring further support including free legal counselling. Individual counselling will be complemented with a cash based intervention to support extremely vulnerable individuals to purchase items that will enable them to construct suitable shelters.

Consultations with affected people

Yes

Provide breakdown by women, men, boys and girls

NRC believes that all projects must be informed by and reflect the voices of the communities in which we work. To that end, NRC conducted several gender-sensitive protection needs assessments in areas that will be targeted by the project in both static and mobile locations. Assessment teams conducted focus group discussions and Key Informant Interviews. Purposive and convenience sampling was used, depending on the location, timing of the assessment, and capacity of the team. Persons that were targeted for inclusion included people with disabilities, older persons, recently displaced people, and female-headed households. Across these assessments, NRC teams spoke with 76 adult women and 23 adolescent girls, and 53 men and 22 adolescent boys. NRC teams did not interview children younger than 15 for these assessments.

Does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

Yes

How does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

NRC will ensure that the project will systematically engage with all beneficiaries and relevant stakeholders, including the most marginalised, to deliver information about assistance to be provided in such a way as to maximise meaningful participation and consultation. Information shared will include the scope of the proposed interventions, entitlements of those involved, clarify any targeting of vulnerable groups, and information essential to accessing companion services, as well as other relevant information. NRC will consult with communities about the optimal way to share information, and provide information in forms accessible to those with limited literacy and in local languages, to the extent that it is possible. Taking precautions to make the project as transparent and inclusive as possible will both service to make the program more inclusive and contribute to a sense of local ownership, as well as mitigate possible negative consequences of targeting or prioritising vulnerable persons and minority groups. NRC has utilised a variety of approaches to provide beneficiaries with information, including holding community awareness raising sessions, both general and targeted at specific groups according to gender, age, and other considerations. IEC materials used and messages shared will be tailored to meet the needs of these unique groups. NRC has also training community mobilisers to go door-to-door in targeted communities and share information, as well as sharing information over the radio. These and other approaches would be used in the current project, following consultation with communities about the best way to reach affected groups, and depending on the duration of the intervention (i.e. static versus mobile).

Are messages context-specific or/and tailored?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Messages are both context-specific and tailored for gender and age-groups. In particular, NRC revisits all protection messages after each protection needs assessment, to ensure that messages are addressing identified needs and targeted to the most vulnerable groups. Messages are particularly tailored to reach children, people with disabilities, and older persons. NRC strives to include local context in its messages, such as alerting beneficiaries to existing referral pathways for complementary services. NRC also considers messages from a Do No Harm perspective, moderating messages as appropriate.

Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse

Yes

Briefly describe how

NRC takes prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse very seriously. All NRC staff engaged in the project will be trained in PSEA; further, staff will be trained in PFA and identification of potential survivors of SEA, and receive training and mentoring in how to respond appropriately and with empathy. All communications with affected populations will stress their right to receive services without coercion or exploitation. NRC will work with communities to develop confidential, appropriate complaints and feedback mechanisms, and respond to any complaints promptly and transparently, to the extent that we are still able to assume both the survivor and perpetrator of their safety. NRC will strive to recruit gender-balanced teams and take every effort to ensure that vulnerable women are able to work directly with competent woman staff.

Nature of the consultations

Yes

Briefly describe the nature of the consultations

NRC believes that all projects must be informed by and reflect the voices of the communities in which we work. In preparation for this proposal, NRC conducted several gender-sensitive protection needs assessments, targeting not only community leaders, but also people with disabilities, older persons, recently displaced people, young mothers, and female-headed households. In all communication with communities, NRC stresses the importance of inclusion and seeking out a diversity of respondents. It is essential that those affected by the programme also have the means to affect it in turn, and NRC teams actively seek to consult a wide range of persons, most explicitly those from vulnerability groups we target with our programming.

Feedback /complaint mechanisms?

Yes

Briefly describe the mechanisms

The project will offer several complains and feedback mechanisms, depending on the needs of the community. In initial assessments, NRC will consult with targeted persons about the best approach to establish CFMs and strive to be responsible and flexible in our implementation.

Community Engagement?

Yes

Briefly describe how

In line with NRC’s global commitment to ensure that all aspects of humanitarian intervention – including monitoring and evaluation – are informed by and reflect the voices of the communities with which we work, NRC will ensure that monitoring and evaluation activities are inclusive and accessible. Whether utilising IPA case closure and evaluation forms, PDM tools, or more general community evaluation FGDs and KIIs, NRC teams will seek to engage a range of community members from both sexes, all relevant ages, and across a range of diversity characteristics. In assessment stages and throughout implementation, NRC teams will discuss with communities the ideal mechanisms for monitoring and evaluation to ensure inclusive approaches. In hard to reach areas, or those with emergency responses that might mitigate the kinds of monitoring and evaluation approaches possible in more static locations, NRC will take particular care to discuss with communities safe, realistic options for monitoring and evaluation and means of improving accountability to affected populations.

Protection Risk Analysis?

NRC conducted protection needs assessment in several locations targeted by the project, both those intended to be served by static and mobile activities. Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Based on NRC’s protection needs assessment and protection risk analysis, we carefully considered a range of possible barriers to meaningful access during the design of the implementation, including security, financial, and perceived discrimination. NRC’s analysis reveals that it is likely beneficiaries will face a diversity of barriers, particularly according to AGD-related issues and local conflict dynamics. For example, a newly arrived IDP women in Wau, a single young educated young man in Akobo, and an unaccompanied elderly person Yei are all likely to face sever access constraints, if for different reasons (assumption of discrimination, fear of physical assault, mobility limitations). NRC will approach its implementation with nuance, recognising that its target groups are likely to encounter a range of access constraints that NRC teams will need to be aware of and reactive to. Teams will regularly re-evaluate access constraints throughout the intervention to ensure that beneficiaries are consistently able to reach, receive, and benefit from services.

Addresses inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

In recognising that every individual faces a unique array of protection risks, depending upon their own vulnerabilities, capacities, and external threats, NRC conducted a gender-sensitive needs assessment, consulting more than 100 women, girls, men and boys in targeted areas, including older men and women, female-headed households, and persons with disabilities of both sexes. The assessment identified a range of individualised protection threats and potential inequalities that NRC is considering in programming interventions and approach modalities. An example of one such consideration is that that men must be explicitly targeted and significantly engaged. In all target locations, a majority of respondents reported that men enjoy sole control over assets and decision-making in households At the same time, respondents across gender and location reported high rates of GBV. A failure to proactively engage men will likely result in significant harm to women and girls.A second is the diversity and inter-related nature of protection risks facing different members of the same community. For example, in Akobo, numerous respondents noted that a lack of livelihoods opportunities for displaced men and male youth and an inability to cultivate land due to insecurity led adolescent girls tended to travel long distances to collect firewood to sell, exposing themselves to heightened risk of sexual violence. The intersection of needs in communities should be carefully considered; what might at first appear to be a livelihoods opportunity for men could in fact have the positive outcome of reducing gender-based violence and empowering women. A final potential inequality from NRC's assessment has to do with freedom of movement; older persons and those most concerned with threats of physical abuse - often young men - were the most likely the least likely to access services. Thus, special care must be taken to reach diverse and often unexpectedly vulnerable groups in NRC programming.

Does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

NRC will evaluate three pillars of meaningful access – ability to reach, receive, and benefit from services – during assessment, throughout implementation, and during monitoring and evaluation stages. Questionnaires utilised at individual, household, community level will ask about actual and perceived barriers to access, and project teams will make all possible adjustments to intervention modalities to mitigate challenges as the arise. Especially in static or recurrent mobile locations, NRC will seek to verify access concerns with service providers and maintain and up-to-date, quality-based service maps.

Does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

NRC prioritises the safety of beneficiaries in all activities. Thus, all assessment and monitoring tools take questions of community and individual safety as mandatory. Likewise, all M&E tools will ask about improvements in safety generally, and related to the intervention. Beneficiaries will be asked, before, during, and after an intervention what steps would contribute to feelings of safety, and if they felt that NRC was failing to account for safety in meaningful ways. Beyond perceptions of safety, protection monitors will be asked to report on specific instances of rights violations, both related to NRC interventions and those otherwise occurring in targeted communities. NRC will also include an internal incident reporting mechanism for staff to monitor our own observations regarding beneficiary safety.

Does the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

Yes

How the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

As with issues of safety, NRC prioritises the dignity of beneficiaries in all activities. Thus, all assessment and M&E tools take questions of community and individual dignity as mandatory. Beneficiaries will be asked, before, during, and after an intervention what steps would contribute to feelings dignity and self-worth, and if they felt that NRC was undercutting feelings of dignity in meaningful ways.
