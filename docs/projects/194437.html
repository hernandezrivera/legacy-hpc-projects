Project Summary

The aim of this project is to increase the percentage of Men, women, boys, and girls accessing GBV risk mitigation, prevention, and response interventions in Ayod and Fangak Counties. The project targets 32,628 direct beneficiaries: 29,693 women and girls as the risk of facing GBV and/or survivors of GBV. The beneficiaries will be drawn from host communities, IDPs, and returnees. 10% of the project beneficiaries will be people with disabilities and 7% will the elderly. To identify the most vulnerable and deserving beneficiaries, the implementing organization (UNKEA) will engage local authorities, community representatives, and key stakeholders in the targeted counties as well as establish a project management committee at each of the selected counties. The Project Management Committees (PMCs) and community organizations supporting protection and needs of other marginalized and vulnerable groups will help in identification of Bomas with the highest protection needs as well as in beneficiary identification and registration. The project will collect and adapt project activities to the feedback of the beneficiaries. UNKEA will establish various feedback mechanisms and regularly monitor beneficiaries’ input and integrate them to better meet the expressed needs. The key interventions of the project are self-defense training to vulnerable women and girls, male community members’ engagement to challenge patriarchal views and social norms, and training of community social workers and women groups to provide psychosocial support services to children and caregivers in distress as well as to survivors of GBV. The project will also facilitate public awareness meetings on the availability of child protection and GBV support mechanisms and referral pathways. The trainings and public meetings will be facilitated in local dialects to promote message comprehension. Verbal messages will be complemented by short dramas and songs to promote message retention.

1 - Needs

In 2022, it is estimated that 5.6 million people in South Sudan need protection services of which 3.5 million are in need of critical child protection services while 2.6 million people risk facing gender-based violence (HNO, 2023). Child Protection NeedsIn 2021, the number of children in need of child protection services was 2.3 million but in 2022, the number has increased up to 2.9 million children. This increase is associated to the COVID-19 pandemic, protracted displacement, and limited access to basic services which continued to significantly affect the mental, physical, and social wellbeing of children, adolescents, and caregivers. Continued violence, food insecurity and floods in some parts of the country have weakened the protective environment for children while lowering the capacity of families to effectively care for children thus putting them at the risk of violence, abuse, exploitation, and neglect. The Child Protection Information Management System (CPIMS+) shows that the number of documented child protection cases including unaccompanied and separated children increased from 26,009 in 2020 to 30,441 by the end of 2021. IRNA (2022) indicates that child labour has become a routine in towns in South Sudan where children have resorted to child labour and begging as an alternative to support their families. In other communities, lack of access to basic services such as food is forcing parents to forcefully marry their children and at an early age so they can acquire bride price. The report highlights the need for sensitization on child protection, child rights, available services, and the negative impact of early and forced marriages. The HNO (2022) report indicates that although there has been progress in the provision of psychosocial support to children and their caregivers, the vulnerability of children has been worsened by limited availability of mental health and psychosocial support services and the low level of core child protection services such as family tracing and reunification, case management, and reintegration services for children affected by armed forced and groups. GBV Protection NeedsHNO (2022) reveals that 48% of the households in South Sudan have concerns about GBV while 57% of IDPs have greater fear/concern about GBV especially rape and forced marriage. Women and girls are exposed to different forms of GBV in their routine activities and as a coping strategy they avoid areas they consider unsafe including humanitarian distribution areas, latrines, farmlands, water points, firewood collection sites, and markets among others. Findings by FSNMS+ reveal that 32% of households face an increased risk of GBV. The 2022 HNO highlight an urgent need for GBV prevention, risk mitigation, and scaling up of specialized GBV response services and in particular the need to strengthen GBV case management, medical care, psychosocial support, safety and security, legal service, safe houses, livelihoods, economic support, and GBV referral pathways. GBV cases in Ayod and Fangak most times go unnotified even though such are being faced by women and girls in the 2 counties. Due to the various shocks experienced in both Ayod and Fangak, people have lost their most valuable assets such as cattle to the floods, food insecurity levels have risen, malnutrition and ill-health have increased. Several people have resorted to early girl-child marriage, Forced and early marriage, domestic violence, physical assault, and rape cases appear to be rampant in the 2 counties. These increases the insecurity related to GBV in the 2 counties that needs immediate support for the victims, families and community needs awareness actions to impede the current state. Ayod and Fangak counties of Jonglei State are among the counties in South Sudan with the highest GBV and child protection needs.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

UNKEA will establish several channels to collect feedback from diverse beneficiary groups. These channels will include suggestion/complaint boxes, enquiry/complaints desk, two-way face-to-face communications, key informant interviews, focus group discussions, project’s focal point person’s contacts, and the open-door communication channels. UNKEA will also encourage peer-to-peer conversations and group discussions during the project interventions to help the beneficiaries identify areas they need more information or areas they feel they disagree with the project. At the end of the peer-to-peer and group conversations, the beneficiaries will be provided an opportunity to ask their questions and seek clarifications to their concerns. Providing a variety of communication channels will ensure beneficiaries are able to use a means they prefer to give feedback or express their concerns during the project implementation period. UNKEA will engage community organizations supporting the interests of the most vulnerable groups such as people with disabilities and the elderly in the identification of the needs of these marginalized groups and in encouraging the marginalized beneficiaries to express their concerns. UNKEA will have the activities separated for various beneficiary categories to ensure maximum participation by various categories. For example, training sessions for girls will be separated from training sessions for women to encourage meaningful participation and engagement of girls.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

At the project launch level, UNKEA will hold inception meetings with local authorities, key stakeholders and community representatives who will be informed of the project goal, objectives, target beneficiaries, and the proposed interventions. The meetings will be consultative in nature allowing the audience to contribute to the project design and finalization of the interventions while proposing the best approaches to ensure the project reaches the most vulnerable persons in the communities. During the meetings, printed project briefs will be given to the audience to complement the verbal messages disseminated. UNKEA will encourage two-way communication to ensure that the audience has a feedback and/or complaint mechanism. Additionally, UNKEA will emphasize that the view of every person is respected and valued in making the project success thus welcoming all attendees to contribute. For those that might not be bold to verbally give feedback, UNKEA will offer them an opportunity to write their questions and pass them forward or put them in a suggestion/complaints box that will be provided in the meeting room. UNKEA will also introduce the project focal person and provide his/her contacts for the audiences to reach will questions or suggestions about the project. At the beneficiary selection level, UNKEA’s team will introduce themselves to the targeted beneficiaries upon their visitation. The team will them provide the household/beneficiary information about the project including its aim, objectives and the assistance to be provided. Each household will be informed on the beneficiary selection criteria and given an opportunity to suggest the most deserving person(s) in the household to be recruited into the project: the rationale for the preference of a certain person must be explained. Communication to beneficiaries will be done verbally in local languages to ensure the message is delivered as was intended by UNKEA. During the mass public meetings, information will be communicated verbally but a public address system will be used to ensure the sound is high enough to be heard by all attendees. UNKEA will also distribute visibility and IEC materials during public meetings as well as use short songs and dramas around the intervention theme to promote message retention. The songs and dramas will be played in local languages. During every meeting, UNKEA will give community members a chance to ask questions or express their concerns to any of the project team members. The received questions and concerns will be addressed publicly.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

UNKEA has an elaborate policy on PSEA: every employee of UNKEA is taken through training on it during the employee induction training and signs a commitment to protect vulnerable people and employees against SEA. During beneficiary selection, UNKEA will explain to every individual that the beneficiary selection process is free and fair and no one should ask of any sexual favors to be recruited into the project. Additionally, UNKEA will inform beneficiaries that selection into the project is a guarantee that the individual will freely receive the explained assistance. UNKEA will verbally explain that all community members have the right to receive humanitarian assistance in a respectful manner and in a safe area and time. To protect beneficiaries from SEA during this project, UNKEA will have its project team recommitted to the PSEA policy, sensitize the beneficiaries on SEA and UNKEA commitment to PSEA, and welcome beneficiaries’ input in meeting venues and meeting time deemed safe for protection services delivery. UNKEA will emphasize that every beneficiary has the right to receive protection services from the project team without coercion or exploitation. The beneficiaries will be encouraged to report to UNKEA, the PMC, or the local authorities anyone asking for sexual favors in return of provided protection services to the beneficiary.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

In any meeting with the beneficiaries or other stakeholders, UNKEA will leave open feedback loops and encourage the participants to be free to air their concerns or ask questions. The received feedback will be noted down and response provided instantly in the case of face-to-face meetings. In cases where decisions are made and action points defined, UNKEA will note down the action items, which will further be discussed with the PMCs and implemented. In cases where instant response cannot be provided, UNKEA will note down the questions and discuss them with the project team and the PMCs to agree on the best way forward. UNKEA will open the suggestion/complaint box on a daily basis to ensure beneficiaries’ input is received in a timely manner. The suggestions and complaints will be discussed with the PMCs and response given to the beneficiaries during the subsequent meeting. UNKEA will regularly evaluate beneficiaries input against progress to find out if the project responds to beneficiaries needs as expressed. UNKEA will conduct regular beneficiary surveys to determine in the beneficiaries are contented with the extent to which UNKEA adapts their project to the needs and requests of beneficiaries. The findings of the surveys will be used to further adapt the project assistance but bearing in mind the budgetary limitations: in cases where beneficiaries’ feedback cannot be incorporated due to budgetary limitations, the beneficiaries will be informed and the inputs documented for future projects.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

The project will participate in the mobilization of community members in mitigating measures driven towards controlling floods and reducing the effects of floods such as digging of dikes, drainage channels, identification of high grounds, etc. In an event of displacement due to floods, the project will move and continue service provision among the displaced population who need services.

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

The project directly implements GBV and child protection prevention, risk mitigation, and response interventions targeting 25,000 beneficiaries. The project raises awareness on available support services while training more personnel to provide support services.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

The project will provide women and girls with self-defense skills to fend off sexual attackers, which will potentially increase their willingness to go to area they formerly avoided as they considered them unsafe. Such areas include farmlands, distribution sites, and markets: this implies that tic this project, women and girls are more likely to engage in income generating activities such as businesses and agriculture, which will promote their livelihoods. By being confident to go to distribution areas, women and girls will receive support to engage in sustainable practices such as smart-agronomics, which will enhance their resilience.
