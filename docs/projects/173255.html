Project Summary

The project aims to 1) improve the quality, accessibility and safe referral of existing response services for survivors, especially linkages between case management, medical and legal services 2) mitigate the risk of both sexual and gender-based violence through multi-level and cross-sectoral capacity building (including on PSEA) and community-led (especially by women and girls) initiatives and 3) prevention through sustained, evidence-based programming that changes the social norms undergirding violence against women. Highly vulnerable, underserved populations will be targeted.
UNHCR and partners’ approach is community-based and survivor-centred, with refugees—especially women and girls—engaged as active agents and emerging leaders within their communities. We will increase women and girls’ participation in decision-making and leadership by building their confidence and capacity with skills building and training, engaging female refugees in our community-based protection work and engaging men and boys as allies in promoting gender equality. Key components include safe spaces, life and vocational skills, PSS, community volunteers, male role models, women-led initiatives, women’s leadership training and SASA! Together.
UNHCR and partners will continue to provide case management and psychosocial support services for survivors of sexual and gender-based violence, with special attention to strengthening the survivor-centred care practices within the multi-sectoral referral network and increasing accessibility for, and capacity to serve, target populations. Service delivery modalities have been adapted for risk mitigation, including for COVID-19 and natural disasters. Key components include capacity building for case management, PSS, health, legal and law enforcement service providers.
Promoting the centrality of protection and an age, gender and diversity approach, UNHCR and partners will invest in AAP with community-based complaints mechanisms, SEA risk mitigation through protection mainstreaming across sectors and targeting highly vulnerable populations. Target populations include child and adolescent survivors, male survivors, adolescent girls, gender diverse populations and individuals engaged in sex work in host and refugee communities, and persons with disabilities.

1 - Identified Needs

Rohingya refugees who fled Myanmar in 2017 were escaping violence that included widespread gender-based violence and sexual violence against both females and males, which inflicted lasting traumas on the population. These and more recent arrivals joined an existing Rohingya refugee community in Bangladesh, which share social norms that maintain and reinforce gender inequalities that enable and perpetuate violence against women and girls within their communities.Pre-COVID-19 research conducted in 2020 by the University of New South Wales found a clear pattern in the types of GBV risks faced by women and girls that tracks with life stages. The risk of rape and sexual abuse was the top concern for girls 18 and under, followed by child marriage and human trafficking. Although risk of rape and sexual abuse remain high across all age groups, the risk of intimate partner violence (IPV) increases as a concern with age, including for LGBTI women and women with disabilities. The threat of trafficking is greatest for young women and girls.Refugees feel unsafe both within the community and inside their shelters, particularly women and girls. In MSNA’s, women reported violence in the home (26%) and violence in the community (25%) as their top two perceived risks(2019). COVID-19 related movement restrictions have left men in their shelters and communities during the day, exacerbating existing risks. Inside shelters, the stresses of lost income and cramped quarters frequently explode into violence, with IPV now accounting for over 84% of reported GBV incidents(GBVIMS). In public, the temporal segregation of public facilities like latrines has been disrupted, increasing existing risks related to camp design. The COVID-19 pandemic reduced the number of available response services, while simultaneously increasing the barriers to access what remains. The reduced number of humanitarian actors on the ground correlates with an increase of violent crime within the camps, heightening fear and a sense of insecurity. Refugees face risks of sexual exploitation and abuse at the hands of humanitarian workers, particularly as COVID-19 restrictions have simultaneously increased reliance on volunteers while limiting UN monitoring presence.Parents fear for the safety of their adolescent daughters. Facing closed schools and failing livelihoods, parents see marriage as a protective measure, even for girls under 18. Men in the camps are opportunistically practicing polygamy, while men in countries such as Malaysia and Indonesia are arranging smuggling of young brides, despite the journey’s grave risks. Human smuggling and trafficking in the region have become ‘feminized’, with more women and girls than ever forced into dangerous journeys.Pre-existing risks have been further exacerbated for key populations in host and refugee communities, including gender diverse populations and individuals engaged in sex work. The discrimination, risks and lack of services for these populations were identified prior to the pandemic(UNHCR assessment) and assessed as worsened since COVID-19. Male survivors of sexual violence, including conflict related sexual violence, are similarly underserved, but their existence is highlighted by the increasing trend of male survivors seeking services(GBVIMS). Despite protective legislation, access to justice for GBV survivors—both refugee and host—is limited by justice system inefficacy and distrust. In 2020, Bangladesh faced a national ‘rape crisis’, focusing attention on legal failures. Rohingya refugees are reluctant to seek remedies in the formal legal system and 74% of surveyed households reported that they would refer a friend who was sexually assaulted to a Majhi (J-MSNA, 2020). Informal, gender-biased resolution mechanisms further entrench unequal gender norms and disempower women. Success in women’s empowerment has also been threatened by backlashes targeting female leaders and volunteers.

2 - Activities or outputs

1.1 Prevention through community-based protection and social change. Prevention of incidents will be strengthened through sensitization and awareness-raising on GBV (including PSEA), information on available services and sustained, evidence-based programming that addresses the social norms at the root of violence against women (ex. SASA! Together, Male Role Models).1.2 Multi-level GBV risk mitigation (community level). In cooperation with refugee community members—particularly women and girls—identify risks and mitigation/prevention measures and implement these measures through community-led initiatives. 1.3 Communication with communities, community-led messaging and awareness raising. In 2021, more focus on participatory message development and inclusive targeting of people with disabilities, gender diverse populations and other key populations. COVID-19 messaging will continue to be integrated. 2.1 Inclusive, survivor-centred response services and safe referrals. Given UNHCR’s refugee mandate, and the presence of UN agencies with GBV expertise and development mandates, UNHCR will target the refugee population for response services. Maintaining and strengthening multi-sectoral GBV response services with attention to increasing inclusivity of case management (child, adolescent & male survivors and survivors with disabilities, from gender diverse populations, or engaged in sex work) and promoting a survivor-centred approach by health, PSS, legal and safety/security service providers. 2.2 Safe shelters. The number of community-based safe shelters will be increased from 9 to 20 to provide immediate support to persons at risk of GBV or with physical protection needs. The partner-run safe shelter in Cox’s Bazar city will continue to be made available, including for males. 2.3 Women and girls’ protection and empowerment. Continue efficient operation of 18 Women Friendly Spaces (WFS), 13 community centres with GBV services, 9 counselling centres, 5 community engagement centres and 3 integrated community centres providing services to women and girls as well as male survivors. The WFS provide case management, PSS, safety planning, skills-building, NFI distribution, recreational activities. Targeting adolescent girls with protection and empowerment programming through our WGFSs and other community-based structures. 2.4 Emergency preparedness and response including disaster risk reduction. Continue to mainstream GBV interventions in its emergency preparedness and response plans, through specialized capacity building for partners and refugees, contributory stocks of dignity kits, targeted awareness raising sessions, safety audits for disaster prone areas, enhanced case management and follow up for GBV survivors living in zones at heightened risk. 3.1 Coordination & protection information management. Membership of the GBV Sub-sector and contribute technical expertise to development of strategic direction. Leadership in the principled, systematised, and collaborative processes of collecting, processing, analysing, storing, sharing, and using data and information to enable evidence-informed action for quality protection outcomes. 3.2 Multi-level GBV risk mitigation (sector level). In coordination with the GBV Sub-sector and in partnership with UNICEF, mainstreaming of GBV risk mitigation at the sector level across all sectors with priority given to WASH, Health and Site Planning / Site Management. Activities include training and creation of sector-specific action plans. 3.3 Accountability to affected populations & protection from sexual exploitation and abuse. Reporting and feedback mechanisms in place include a 24-hour protection hotline, protection desks, info hubs, letter boxes, face-to-face and a direct line to UNHCR’s global level Inspector General’s Office. Community-based complaints mechanisms will be further developed in 2021, considering inclusion and accessibility.
