Project Summary

To foster stronger socio-economic resiliency, RI will work to support enhanced alternative livelihoods opportunities for vulnerable Rohingya and host communities, namely youth, women and persons with disabilities (PwDs), in camps 8W, 12, 24, Teknaf Sadar and Palong Khali. First, RI will establish farmers' aggregation centres to support host community suppliers' access to markets, prioritizing under-represented women farmers for inclusion under the program. This will be designed based on RI’s 2020 needs assessments which found key gaps in farmer product quality, hygiene, scale-up capacity, and inclusion of women. Aggregation centres will serve as training outlets and act as supply centres for farmers to aggregate produce to supply markets with the higher quantities of fresh produce demanded. Second, RI will establish social innovation centres (SICs) to provide skills and livelihood training for cohorts of youth, PwDs and vulnerable women and men, tailored to different participants' needs and capacities. Based on RI’s assessments of the targeted locations, general market and training needs of targeted camps and host communities include fish drying, vegetable production, shop management and handicrafts. However, specific training modules will be designed and tailored based on trainees’ feedback. These sites are also intended to provide physical sites for exchange on community issues, fostering improved inter-community empathy. Lastly, RI will work through trained host community members to map out community restoration projects which will be funded through a cash-for-work (CfW) modality. The program will embed regular feedback from program participants, with particular focus on women, girls and PwDs, through community outreach and facility-based surveys, to enable program adaptation and ensure continued relevancy of its interventions. To this end, RI will disaggregate key indicators (1.1, 2.1 and 2.2) by age and sex, as well as disability (1.1 and 2.1) to evaluate the project’s intended contributions toward gender and ability inclusion.

1 - Identified Needs

In 2021, with compounding stressors of COVID-19 and mounting negative coping, effective targeting of vulnerable populations for livelihoods support will be increasingly key. Based on current data, these populations will include persons with disabilities (PwDs), adolescents and women, including renewed emphasis on these demographics in host communities.As the crisis continues amid growing tensions in HC over land use, depletion of natural resources, economic distress and access to social services. HCs – primarily the youth – have begun to express resentment towards the FDMNs, causing tension between communities. As the crisis becomes more protracted and the prospect of safe return less likely, tensions between host and FDMN communities will continue to rise. For HCs, the upsurge in cheap labor and resale of humanitarian assistance by FDMNs has distorted local markets and weakened livelihoods for the poorest. An influx into fishing and related activities has been noticeable, as this sector offers immediate income opportunity. However, it is unreliable and characterized by risks such as loss of lives, disaster, and fishing bans imposed by GoB. Furthermore, the depletion of the local forests as a result of the influx has severely impacted HCs as many previously relied on planting and harvesting trees as sources of livelihoods and cooking fuel. GoB restriction on education and livelihoods for FDMNs negatively impact youth in the camps. Many are at risk of growing up without basic capacity for self-reliance. Youth hold negative perceptions of the future and are at the greatest risk of engaging in illicit activities. Adolescent girls, in particular, are affected by this as cultural norms curb their ability to build confidence, identify shared interests and goals, and be included in community activities. Among limited opportunities, youth are left exposed to a greater risk of violence, sexual abuse, and exploitation. Persons with disabilities (PwDs) and woman-headed households, who have always lived at the margins in the Cox’s Bazar context, are perceived to be increasingly among the most vulnerable in the community (REVA 3, 2020; ACAPS, 2020). The situation of these vulnerable groups is further exacerbated by the current COVID-19 pandemic and the subsequent social and economic shocks. Even though numerous strides have been taken to ensure inclusion of these vulnerable groups, the REVA 3 conducted by WFP and partners found that households with a disabled family member were more likely to utilize more severe negative coping mechanisms such as reducing meal portion sizes (34%), borrowing from neighbors to cover living expenses (34%), and reducing the number of meals per day (26%). These higher rates of negative coping among households including a PwD were reportedly largely as a function of these family members’ higher expenses, including medical ones, higher dependency on their family members and fewer available income generation opportunities tailored to their different abilities. Approximately 14% of all Rohingya households have at least one member with disabilities. So the impacts of socioeconomic dependency and shocks disproportionately affecting families with PwDs will be felt widely across the camps. To build economic resilience and ensure food security for these highly vulnerable individuals and their families, RI will engage them in inclusive, center-based livelihoods programming. Since households with a member who is handicapped or elderly face significantly greater economic vulnerability, the need to identify relevant opportunities and training unique to these groups’ abilities is imperative. To ensure greater independence for PwD and address persistent biases against PwDs in business, unique training curriculums and livelihood opportunities must be established and identified to ensure the rights of all individuals.
