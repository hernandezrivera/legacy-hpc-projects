Project Summary

The project aims to deliver child protection services and longer-term protection solutions (i.e., durable solutions) to 182,105 Rohingya refugee children (89,063 girls; 93,041 boys) in 16 camps and 30,717 host community children (15,017 girls and 15,700 boys) in six host community sites in Bangladesh, comprising a total of 212,822 children (104,080 girls; 108,741 boys). The programme includes case management, structured and sustained psychosocial support, strengthened community-based child protection structures, child protection recreational activities, life skills for adolescents, positive parenting, system strengthening and capacity building for child protection and non-child protection partners, and the operation of facilities and safe spaces. Activities and facilities are designed and implemented using a community-based approach and considering gender equality, AGD and disability inclusion to ensure that vulnerable children can access services. Community-led initiatives will be supported according to the needs identified by community members. Children, adolescents and youth will be engaged in designing, implementing, monitoring and evaluating child protection prevention and response activities for their community with the aim of strengthening resilience and fostering a spirit of development. A special initiative will be implemented in coordination with the local authorities and community, especially adolescent girls, to support efforts to tackle child labour and child marriage. UNHCR will uphold core guiding principles and protection standards, AAP and PSEA to guide its interventions to ensure adherence to national standards and quality of services. Child protection interventions will consider disaster risk reduction (DRR) measures to prevent recurrent disaster risks occurring due to cyclones, landslides, fires and floods. Child protection case management services will respond to incidents of abuse, neglect, exploitation and violence towards children. Structured and sustained psycho-social support (PSS), including child protection recreational activities, will be provided to children at risk. Evidence-based approaches will be applied to strengthen children’s own knowledge, skills and capacities for self-protection, and those of their parents and families. In coordination with the Child Protection Sub-Sector (CPSS) and partners, the existing Community-Based Child Protection Mechanism will be provided with technical and material support in identifying solutions to address the protection needs of children. UNHCR will enhance its coordination and partnership with different humanitarian actors to raise awareness amongst community members, volunteers and social workers on child protection prevention, mitigation and response through the provision of capacity building for the purpose of mainstreaming the best interest procedure (BIP) and child safeguarding. In collaboration and coordination with the CPSS, UNHCR will improve the quality of services provided to targeted children, particularly with regard to case management and child protection intervention systems by enhancing the capacity of partners, target communities and Government through trainings. Positive parenting sessions will reinforce the capacity of parents to care for their children. Structured curricula life skills, GBV awareness, adolescent engagement, social cohesion and peaceful co-existence sessions will be delivered to support adolescents and youth. Early Childhood Development (ECD) activities will be initiated and supported, with linkages made with child protection activities to tackle Adverse Childhood Experiences. UNHCR will work closely with the Government, including but limited to the justice systems, Department of Social Services, Office of the RRRC and Ministry of Women and Children Affairs to coordinate interventions such as safety and security, legal protection, case management and protection coordination.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

Rohingya refugee children continue to face child protection issues, with child marriage, child labor and exploitation, and child neglect continuing to weigh heavily on the resilience and capabilities of families to protect their children. Rohingya refugee children, comprising 52% of the registered Rohingya refugee population in Cox’s Bazar District, face protection risks and threats to their safe and healthy development, including violence, exploitation, neglect and abuse. Girls are particularly vulnerable to gender-based violence including sexual abuse, sexual harassment and child marriage. Supporting community-based structures and an enabling environment where children and adolescents are able to reach their full potential and make informed decisions on issues that affect their lives is a key priority for UNHCR. According to the CPIMS+ June 2023 report, the top five child protection concerns for refugee children in CXB comprise neglect (35.91%), psychological distress (11.73%), physical abuse (9.88%), child labor (8.07%), and emotional abuse (7.94%). Cases of child marriage were observed as underreported (4.18%), with approximately 98% cases involving girls. Children with disabilities (9.03%) face stigmatization and considerable barriers in accessing critical services, including child protection services. According to the 2023 Multi-Sector Needs Assessment (MSNA), 60% - 80% of interviewed households stated that their children did not participate in recreational activities during the past 30 days, while less than 25% confirmed that the children are able to access safe areas without adult supervision and 50%-60% responded that their children (aged 3 – 17 years old) are attending learning facilities. As a result of harmful gender norms and practices, young teenage girls face challenges in moving around the camps safely, having limited access to school and protection services and being exposed to early pregnancies and intimate partner violence. Families frequently resort to child marriage as a mechanism for their protection. Meanwhile, young teenage boys face specific risks of being recruited into drug and gang-related activities. Boys are also at risk of child marriage, forcing them to abandon development activities such as education. According to the CPIMS+ June 2023 report, 7.14% of the total reported child protection cases comprised separated children, with the majority left in the camp by parents/caregivers who previously departed in search of livelihood opportunities or other means of survival. This situation results in a lack of accessibility to food and appropriate alternative family care, resulting in child labor and exploitation. The low level of awareness of various dangers and hazards in the camps results in incidents such as drowning and road traffic accidents, while children are also vulnerable to trafficking, abduction and violence. The Government and humanitarian actors have combined forces to tackle child protection issues, but more efforts are needed to address the challenges facing children in their development to help ensure the full enjoyment of their rights. Thus, in collaboration with other partners, UNHCR will implement holistic child protection prevention, risk mitigation, and response interventions to address the priority needs of refugee and host community children as per the identified needs, protection principles and standards.

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

The project is aligned with the rationalization principles and process. UNHCR has worked with Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh for over 30 years, including providing child protection services. UNHCR’s international protection mandate underpins its continuous commitment as a first responder and provider of last resort, alongside the Government, to ensure refugees’ access to protection, including child protection. UNHCR works hand in hand with all humanitarian agencies to ensure that Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh have equitable access to basic services in a predictable, efficient and timely manner and that the transparency and accountability of the humanitarian community is enhanced. UNHCR has participated in and contributed to Child Protection Sub-Sector (CPSS) coordination meetings to advance the rationalization process, also applying its expertise in refugee protection to ensure that the principles of the rationalization process are achieved, and providing a matrix of its child protection interventions in different camps. UNHCR has advocated for its own interventions, while at the same time avoiding the compromising of interventions implemented by other actors, as well as for some actors to be considered to intervene in the camps to reinforce the child protection response according to their capacity. In line with the ISCG and CPSS rationalization guidelines, UNHCR facilitated rationalization discussions for 21 affected camps in 2023 with the CPSS, child protection actors and local authorities. Geographical demarcation at sub-block level was applied to define areas of responsibilities of each child protection actor to prevent duplication and ensure full coverage of comprehensive child protection intervention activities. UNHCR recognizes the authority of different sectors and working groups in coordinating the humanitarian response to the Rohingya crisis and remains open to constructive discussions to ensure the continuity and efficiency of interventions.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

In coordination and collaboration with the Protection Sector, Child Protection Sub-Sector (CPSS) and other sectors, UNHCR has ensured that the core principles and practices of protection are mainstreamed and integrated throughout all aspects of child protection activities applying a comprehensive and proactive approach to safeguarding children’s well-being and rights. The holistic child-centred and AGD approaches are used to design prevention and response activities to prioritize the child's best interest and to focus on their safety, development and well-being. Disability inclusion and a child-friendly approach are integrated into the project to increase meaningful access to child protection services by children and caregivers through developing the knowledge and capability of staff and volunteers to communicate and deliver services in a child-friendly manner. The rehabilitation of child protection facilities to increase access for children with disabilities, as well as outreach activities to deliver services for vulnerable children who have difficulty with movement, are included in the project. Community-based child protection mechanisms, local authorities and humanitarian workers - comprising both child protection and non-child protection actors – will be strengthened and empowered through technical support, capacity building and provision of materials. Community-based child protection structures and adolescent clubs are being engaged to identify child protection risks and develop a prevention and response plan together with UNHCR’s partners. The existing feedback and complaint mechanism will be regularly monitored and assessed jointly to ensure two-way communication between refugees and UNHCR, and that this remains efficient in strengthening services according to the needs of the community and upholding the accountability and integrity of service providers. Finally, a zero-tolerance policy will be emphasized throughout the project cycle.

3.3 - Age & Disability

The first principle of the Convention on the Rights of the Child is non-discrimination. As such, UNHCR is responsible for ensuring the inclusion of children of all ages, regardless of their gender or status, in child protection programs and services and ensure that their voices are heard. UNHCR and its partners have collaborated and coordinated with various sectors, sub-sectors and working groups to put in place children’s and adolescents’ clubs and groups, ensuring inclusion of children of all backgrounds. This includes ensuring their rights to voice needs and concerns, and that their contributions are considered as part of decisions made on services provided. Child protection community-based structures, comprising groups of men and women in the community who implement child protection intervention activities, have been established and receive support from UNHCR. Child-friendly spaces, learning centres and case management reception rooms are designed in consideration of the diverse backgrounds of children to foster a sense of belonging and consideration amongst Rohingya refugee children. UNHCR also conducted disability inclusion training for its partners in 2023, including outreach services for children with disabilities who may face barriers in accessing multi-purpose child and adolescent centres or child-friendly spaces, which will continue in 2024. UNHCR and partners will continue to make efforts to ensure that spaces are accessible and adapted for all children and caregivers and to provide tailored services for children with disabilities.

3.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

UNHCR is a member of the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) Task Tam on accountability to affected populations (AAP) which includes a task team on PSEA. UNHCR has developed and implemented AAP-related policies and a guideline including UNHCR’s Code of Conduct (2004), Accountability Framework for Aged, Gender and Diversity Mainstreaming (2007) and AGD policy (2018). UNHCR has advocated and mainstreamed the key elements of the AAP policy - comprising communication and transparency, feedback and response, participation and inclusion and learning and adaptation – in its child protection programming. In coordination and collaboration with the Protection Sector and Child Protection Sub-Sector, UNHCR and child protection partners are committed to ensuring that the best interest procedure is integrated into the design, implementation, monitoring, assessment and evaluation of the programmes and activities for Rohingya and host community children. The best interests of individual children are prioritized during the provision of assistance and delivery of case management services. UNHCR will ensure that AAP principles are put in place throughout the project period as per IASC and CPSS AAP principles and standards. As such, UNHCR’s child protection interventions involve the different segments of target communities, partners, government and donors for the purpose of ensuring accountability and transparency in its interventions, such as community-based complaint and feedback mechanisms (hotlines, complaint boxes, face-to-face interactions) to ensure that services and assistance to be delivered to targeted children will not result in negative consequences and do not go against the principles of “do no harm”. UNHCR has zero tolerance for sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) and sexual harassment. To address any incidents, UNHCR has its own internal reporting and accountability mechanisms in place, applicable to UNHCR’s operations in Cox’s Bazar.

3.5 - Is your agency/organization and are your IPs members of the PSEA Network?

Yes

3.5.1 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

Yes. Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse and sexual harassment (PSEA) is a core component of UNHCR’s work to ensure the protection and well-being of refugees and other displaced persons. In collaboration and coordination with PSEA network in Cox’s Bazar, of which UNHCR is a member, UNHCR ensures the mainstreaming of PSEA, including by integrating measures and practices to prevent, respond to and address any incidents of sexual exploitation and abuse across all aspects of UNHCR’s projects. UNHCR has also identified a PSEA focal point for the Bangladesh operation and established clear policies and codes of conduct that explicitly prohibit SEA by staff, partners and refugee volunteers involved in the operations and activities of child protection partners to ensure that strong PSEA measures are in place and partners are held accountable for maintaining these standards. Awareness raising activities and capacity programs are included in UNHCR's chid protection programme for UNHCR staff, partners and the affected population. The refugee community is also engaged in the development of community-based awareness and prevention strategies. UNHCR ensures the availability of accessible and confidential reporting mechanisms to enable individuals to report incidents of SEA, such as helplines, complaint boxes and community-based focal points. UNHCR works with the GBV and Child Protection sub-sectors to ensure that survivors of SEA are able to access multi-sectoral support through GBV and child protection referral pathways.

3.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Yes. UNHCR facilitates the protection-led inter-agency protection emergency response (PERU) in the targeted camps and host communities and is guided by an emergency preparedness and response plan (EPRP) to address the risks faced by refugee children during emergency situations in the targeted areas. UNHCR includes monsoon, cyclone, landslide, fire, and other hazard/disaster preparedness aspects in its communication with communities activities, and in community initiatives. When disasters occur, UNHCR implements protection services, including child protection, to respond to the needs of the affected population, with a specific focus on women, children and persons with specific needs. UNHCR and its partners assess and respond to specific needs which include temporary safe shelter, family unification, psychosocial support and referrals of affected children and caregivers, facilitated both directly and remotely according to the needs assessment. UNHCR's commitment to disaster risk reduction (DRR) and sustainable and environmentally friendly principles is aligned with global efforts to address climate change and reduce the environmental impact of humanitarian activities. This approach is applied during the design and construction facilities for refugees, to enhance their safety and resilience, including through the use of sustainable and locally sourced materials when possible.
