Project Summary

The response is designed to provide humanitarian assistance to 20000 most vulnerable people (30% IDPs and 70% are host-community ) in North-west Syria, to alleviate suffering in addition to increasing the resilience of affected communities by improving multi-sectoral access to integrated assistance, over a period of 12 months.
The project will aim at addressing the life-sustaining response, enhancing the resilience of conflict-affected persons (host -community, and IDPs). The action will also improve access to public services and facilities on community level and revive the local economy through community utility projects that will benefit rather the entire community (although focusing on the most vulnerable people in need). The project is aiming at improving the public infrastructure, supporting up to 20000 individuals at community level, through the rehabilitation of essential public infrastructure implementing 10 different community-projects.
To include as a key elements in our social cohesion program design, the project will include a conflict analysis to help us understand what the relationship between or amongst different groups is, a conflict sensitivity analysis to help us implement programs in a way that does not cause or increase harm to project staff and project participants, a stakeholder analysis to help us understand the different stakeholders in the area of implementation, and how the stakeholders could affect the project and what we should to manage stakeholders so that the project is successful.
Identification of specific groups is undertaken to better target the beneficiaries groups, based on findings of a conflict analysis in their area of implementation. Dialogue is also a key approach across projects, including integrating dialogue processes into the community mobilization process.
Finally, with the Income Generating Activities (IGA) part of the project, SARD aims at supporting families to restore their livelihood by restoring their own income-generating activities through micro/small businesses, with tailor-made suitable support on how to establish and manage their IGA.
IGA project will focus on the skills, capacities, and interests of adult men and women as well as youth in terms of potential IGAs to be restored and run within the community. 1000 IGAs will be selected where only activities and businesses that were existing before the war and/or lost due to the conflict will fall under this project (damaged workplaces, loss of material and light machinery, etc.).

4 - Needs

Civilians living in NW Syria have been enduring a drastically worsening humanitarian situation as hostilities continue to intensify since November 2019. From 1 December 2019 to 31 January 2020, 616,248 people – around 80% of whom are women and children – were displaced in northwest Syria, mostly from large population centers such as Saraqab and Ma’arrat An Nu’man. Many have been displaced multiple times, with each new displacement compounding experienced risks and vulnerabilities. Since 15 January, heavy aerial bombardment and artillery shelling has been affecting western Aleppo, leading to 38,295 people moving from the area, with 10,700 IDP to Atareb and some 4,500 to Dana. In total, 162,000 people were displaced in northwest Syria between 15 and 26 January. Of these, some 121,000 remained in areas of NW Syria, while some 41,000 moved to areas such as Afrin and A’zaz in northern Aleppo governorate, with many reportedly unable to return due to restrictions on movement and risks of explosive hazards. Additionally, against the above-mentioned backdrop, there are 9.9 million people inside Syria requiring Early Recovery and Livelihoods support given the cumulative impact of crisis on the population and broader macro-economic developments (2019 MSNA Community Level Assessment). While areas exposed to active hostilities have shrunk over time, populations continue to experience poor living standards 25% of basic and social infrastructure is destroyed, which strains availability and accessibility of services. For instance, only 50 % of surveyed communities have access to less than 12 hours of electricity per day and at least 5.2 million people are receiving less than 3 hours of electricity per day. The local context analyses indicate that poor economic conditions, physical destruction of infrastructure, and large-scale displacement have led to competition over scarce livelihood opportunities and overburdening of available social services.

5 - Implementation of activities

The project will support 10 communities benefiting 3000 families in restoring their basic infrastructure through public infrastructure repairs to 1) improve community safety and economic activity (such as, access ways to market or cultivable lands, drainage to reduce risk of flooding, light rehabilitation of water, sewage, and electricity systems) 2) improve access to facilities such as 2 running schools (in coordination with the Education cluster), 2 health centers ( in coordination with the health cluster or 2 social infrastructure . These projects will contribute to social cohesion, based on conflict, sensitive and stakeholder analysis. Prioritisation of activities is done on the basis of the findings of the above analysis and a) Provide an opportunity for interaction between groups who experience tension or conflict, b) result in mutual benefit for groups who experience tension.The action will be also integrated with Income Generating Activities (IGA) aim at supporting 1000 families (5000 individuals) to restore their livelihood by restoring their own income-generating projects through micro/small businesses, with tailor-made suitable support on how to establish and manage their Income Generating Activities (IGA). The IGA assistance will be in form of in-kind assistance (IGA kit) and cash grants based on Bills of Quantities (BoQ) for the proposed IGA. BoQs will be prepared with selected families by SARD staff based on a market assessment on IGA related tools and business development plan for each case. Triggers for selection will pertain to the family vulnerability (for instance child/female/widow headed households, people with special needs), the shelter status (poor and undignified), the safety and security of the shelter (weather protection and privacy) and access to basic facilities (sufficient water and sanitation facilities). Technical aspects of the project will be referred to the SNFI cluster Shelter Repair and Rehabilitation Guidelines.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

Activities are monitored by SARD’s M&E team on a regular basis. SARD has a strong complaint response mechanism (CRM) that is advertised to local communities and offers multiple channels (desk in SARD’s office, face-to-face field visits by the CRM team, complaint boxes and WhatsApp) to beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries alike. Community tensions/crowd management: SARD has built strong relationships with local authorities over the years to ensure the buy-in of activities with local communities. SARD –whenever feasible, offers door-to-door distribution. When this is not possible, distribution sites are chosen in coordination with local authorities and offer gender-disaggregated lines. Distribution of relief is staggered to facilitate crowd management. To avoid the risk of inter-community tensions due to varied assistance packages, SARD follows the Cluster guidance on minimum standards and recommended content and ensures that distribution of kits is based on needs and not status. Bias or exclusion errors in selection: Selection criteria are transparent, and communities are informed of selection criteria through sensitization sessions. Community members are encouraged to report any complaints – including on selection, to SARD’s CRM. Criteria are clearly explained to local authorities who are actively engaged in identifying and preparing beneficiaries list. PSEA: Staff were trained on protection principles, including on PSEA, child protection and GBV. In 2019, SARD has implemented its PSEA-Child Protection policy. All staff have been required to read and sign the new policy along with SARD’s Code of Conduct. PSEA focal points were also appointed.

7 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Assumptions:- Locations of project activities are secure and accessible.- Supplies, contractors, commodities and materials are accessible.- No interfering from armed groups and no divert aid.- Women are equally included in the decision-making process.- IDPs population are not excluded and/or neglected.- Protection is mainstreamed throughout the response by promoting meaningful access, safety, and dignity with "do not harm" as a key principle. Risks and Mitigating measures • Insecurity conditions result in accessibility problems and exposure to risks to staff and beneficiaries.• Escalation of conflict exacerbates insecurity in target areas affecting the SARD ability to operate and access.- Ensure the operational context is secure enough to ensure access.- Staff and volunteers from the local community are hired.- Re-direction of programming into other accessible governorates.- Security of staff remains of utmost importance and work will be suspended in case of serious threats endangering the staff and the program.- Local Security Plan (LSP) for operations inside Syria is established and shared among all staff. LSP is a document summarizes all Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) as well as contingency plans. It gives field staff and SARD management team guidelines how to mitigate risks during the field operations inside Syria, and how to behave in a certain incident situation.- Risk assessments, risk matrix, situation scenarios, and mitigation measures are being reviewed quarterly a year and on ad-hoc bases.- The field team releases a monthly security report. It includes security & safety recommendations for the staff.- A monthly security map is produced; this map contains layers of locations of residence of staff, areas of operation, conflict areas and air strikes.- All routes of transportation vehicles of field staff are mapped, and the routes are modified in accordance with security reports.- SARD coordinates with OCHA Access Working Group (AWG)
