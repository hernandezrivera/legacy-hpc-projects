Project Summary

Since its initiation by the Syria Strategic Steering Group in 2017, the Whole of Syria (WoS) Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) Program has supported the interagency humanitarian response in the joint implementation of international PSEA commitments. The WoS PSEA program is a multi-donor, comprehensive response, designed to address the risks of SEA posed by the complex and protracted humanitarian emergency in Syria. The PSEA program is designed to cover diverse contexts.through an established, robust interagency PSEA Network consisting of more than 300 focal points from senior staff in UN agencies, INGOs and NGOs, the program strives to decrease the risk of crisis‐affected communities experiencing incidents of sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) by humanitarian workers and provide technical and operational support to humanitarian actors to prevent and respond to SEA cases.
The WoS PSEA Network operates an inter-agency complaint hotline in NWS which offers humanitarian staff, beneficiaries and communities a way to make complaints related to SEA and other misconduct by aid providers. The PSEA Network receives and in a timely, confidential way coordinates the referral of the complaint to the relevant organization or agency for follow-up. In cases where there is access to the survivor, survivor assistance is always offered and if consented by the survivor, referred to GBV coordination, with outcomes closely monitored and followed up in coordination with the PSEA Network and GBV sub-cluster. The PSEA Network also offers technical advice to organizations in this process of following up on and investigating the complaint.
Through its more than 300 focal points, the PSEA Network also produces resources such as operational checklists to be used by staff during humanitarian activities to prevent SEA, codes of conduct for humanitarian staff, organizational guides to ensure accountability/reporting mechanisms exist within an organization, as well as providing mandatory PSEA Training-of-Trainers, and refreshers to all focal points, to ensure that all organizations can ensure that 100% of staff are trained on PSEA. The development of these tools, training, resources and free sharing of them, enable significant preventative measures to be in place among all actors engaged in the provision of humanitarian assistance.
The PSEA Network also directly strengthens PSEA interventions through the implementation of IP Projects focused on conducting awareness-raising sessions with both staff and beneficiaries, as well as contributing to PSEA capacity-building materials that are made accessible to other humanitarian actors. The greater community engagement with PSEA Network IPs and partner organizations also results in improved feedback provision from the community related to PSEA programming needs and preferences. Network organizations internally carry out surveys in their areas of operation and with the communities, they are working with to better understand their level of awareness on PSEA and knowledge and access about complaint mechanisms, which allows them to identify and fill gaps and results in PSEA related activities resulting from community input and consultation. In 2020, through its IPs, the PSEA network directly supported the distribution of more than 40,000 awareness-raising materials, to widely disseminate knowledge of the interagency PSEA hotline across NWS.
Finally, the PSEA Network intends to further strengthen evidence-based programming through regular sharing on PSEA related trends and findings obtained through internal data statistics of the PSEA Network NWS inter-agency hotline, and support of an annual household survey on PSEA throughout Syria. These data-backed approaches allow the Network to identify challenges and barriers that require greater levels of advocacy and focus.

4 - Needs

SEA by aid workers directly contradicts the principles upon which humanitarian action is based and represents a protection failure on the part of the aid community. SEA inflicts harm on those whom the humanitarian community is obligated to protect, as well as jeopardizes the credibility of all assistance agencies. Despite the significant progress made in instituting international commitments on PSEA in the Syrian Response thus far, needs remain high, as found in the 2021 household survey carried out jointly by HNAP/PSEA. Key findings from the survey continue to show that affected populations remain highly reliant upon humanitarian assistance for survival. At the national level, 5 percent of households (HH) that received humanitarian assistance stated that they or members of their HH were asked for favours in exchange for humanitarian assistance or services (including requests for cash, labour and personal relationships, or SEA), with female-headed HH 9 times more likely to receive these requests in comparison to male-headed households. SEA in Syria continues to be underreported due to its sensitive nature and the number of actual violations is expected to be higher. Unfortunately, the majority (83 percent) of Syrian HHs who receive requests of favors in exchange for assistance do not report these incidents, largely due to concerns over confidentiality and fear of losing their eligibility to assistance.

5 - Implementation of activities

Since its inception in 2017, the Whole-of-Syria PSEA Network has been supporting the country-wide humanitarian response in the joint implementation of international PSEA commitments. Through its established PSEA Networks of more than 300 focal points across 165 UN agencies, INGOs and NGOs, the program aims to decrease the risk of crisis‐affected communities experiencing incidents of SEA and provides technical and operational support to humanitarian actors to prevent and respond to SEA allegation. The Whole of Syria PSEA Network, consisting of three hubs (NWS, NES, and Damascus), carries out capacity and commitment building to protect against sexual exploitation and abuse through a system of network focal points. These focal points act as a bridge between the respective PSEA network and the organization. They receive PSEA related training from the PSEA Network and are provided with a package of PSEA related tools and resources that the focal points introduce to their organizations to build PSEA related internal capacities. 9 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risks: 1) Physical security risk due to conflict activity and barriers to access; 2) If community messaging is not culturally sensitive and suitable for communities, it could cause a reduction in community members’ trust in existing complaint mechanisms or implementing agencies; 3) lack of timely referral to requested survivor assistance may create additional protection and health risks for the survivor; 4) lack of confidentiality and safe information-sharing protocols may result in additional harm or retaliation towards survivor, perpetrator and/or community members Mitigation Measures: 1) Through interagency collaboration, monitor security risks and barriers to access to ensure the safety of staff and beneficiaries; 2) provide agencies’ access to culturally sensitive and tested PSEA awareness-raising materials created by the PSEA network and review other PSEA related materials used by network partners to ensure culturally sensitive and consistent with standards for PSEA messaging; 3) ensure timely referrals of requests for survivor assistance received by the network coordination to GBV experts for assessment and assistance provision and follow-up with service providers and/ or survivor on the outcome; 4) follow information sharing protocols when sharing information about SEA allegations, and ensure that network partners are familiar and also utilize these protocols and practices


10 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

The PSEA Network ensures that all activities operate on a “Do No Harm” basis. New actions, activities and materials are only implemented following consultations with coordination fora, beneficiaries, and community consultations. In order to ensure appropriate interactions among staff, the network ensures that all staff are trained on PSEA, aware of IOMs internal reporting mechanisms, strictly follow its Code of Conduct, and have received training concerning confidentiality and engagement with vulnerable populations and SEA survivors. Furthermore, the PSEA network ensures that all partner organizations have the following standards in place:1) Internal SEA reporting mechanisms and organizations have a code of conduct that includes a comprehensive section on PSEA. 2) The organization adopts the Humanitarian Code of Conduct (CoC) for the Syria Cross-Border Response (2020) and/or ensures that their own CoC is consistent with it. All staff, including contractors, sub-contractors, daily wage workers and volunteers have understood and signed the CoC3) All staff are trained regularly on PSEA.4) The organization follows up on received complaints with due diligence, with respect to consent, confidentiality, and time-sensitive, survivor-centred approaches5) All field activities are supervised by humanitarian staff, including female staff members to mitigate PSEA and other protection risks.


11 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks: • Deteriorating political/security situation with repercussions for access • Insufficient funding Assumptions: • Safe and secure access to communities to allow for awareness-raising activities amongst the field staff and beneficiaries • Beneficiaries have access to multiple reporting mechanisms (such as an interagency hotline and organizational complaint mechanisms or CBCMs) that are easily accessible and of consistent standards of operation • Inter-agency collaboration facilitates communication and planning • Funding is sufficient for sustaining of PSEA activities • Inter-agency coordination will mainstream PSEA monitoring, prevention and response consistently • The PSEA Networks in all hubs will sustain high participation of agencies and effective PSEA mainstreaming within their policies and programming. Mitigation measures: • Continued advocacy on the importance of PSEA among all relevant stakeholders including donors, the Government of Syria, and humanitarian leadership andsectors/clusters. • Open and transparent dialogue on PSEA amongst the humanitarian community and with key stakeholders • Implementation using a consensus-based, andinter-agency (PSEA Network-centered) approach.
