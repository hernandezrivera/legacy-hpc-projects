Project Summary

This project aims to mitigate and respond to the critical mental health crisis faced by young people in oPt, which results in increasing high-risk coping mechanisms. The focus of this project is on young people (10-29 years), who are often left behind, as interventions often target children or older adults. In emergencies, young people are particularly vulnerable to the risk of mental health and psychosocial consequences and destructive behaviors. Young people in oPt are exposed to high levels of traumatic experiences and violence through e.g. the Great March of Return, escalations in violence, harassment (GBV), movement restrictions, house demolitions, imprisonment, etc. More recently, the outbreak of the coronavirus pandemic has added to the complexity and increased critical needs among young people of mental healthcare services to be addressed.
The continuous exposure to traumatic and violent experiences, and the impacts of COVID-19, leads to progression in high levels of mental, behavioral and emotional issues among young people that urgently need to be addressed. There is a humanitarian responsibility, to both protect and address young peoples’ needs and to engage them in humanitarian preparedness, response and recovery, where they play a vital role. MHPSS is continuously underdeveloped at a national level and in most instances, life-saving MHPSS services and information are out of reach and sparse, especially those offering adolescent and youth-sensitive services for the unique needs of young people, which has increased based on the disruption of health services delivery during COVID-19. It is especially vital during the ongoing pandemic and its restrictions that alternative methods do deliver adolescent and youth-sensitive services and information are developed, implemented and sustained by offering services and information through different physical and virtual platforms (digital health). It is of importance to consider the existing mistrust in terms of confidentiality and stigma hindering from seeking assistance. Capacities of communities need to be built to ensure critical access to MHPSS by tackling cultural and societal barriers leading to stigmatization and misconceptions associated with mental health and COVID-19.
UNFPA plans to respond to the growing need for MHPSS among young people during COVID-19, covering communities in Gaza, Area C, H2 and EJ. It will be complementing WHO's previous work with MoH with a focus on providing services by focusing on NGO and university health clinics and health providers.
The interventions will cover the following key areas:
Provision of physical and virtual adolescent and youth-sensitive mental health services through digital health platforms and university and non-governmental clinical sites, including both fixed and mobile settings, to increase availability and accessibility of mental health services to young people.
Conduct mentoring and in-service training for health providers (NGO and university clinics) to increase capacity building in managing and providing adolescent and youth-sensitive MHPSS services through detection of symptoms, assessment and treatment as well as referral pathways and triage of mental health cases.
Delivery of critical community-based support awareness and education sessions (physical and virtual) on adolescent and youth’s mental health, MH, stigma, negative/positive coping mechanism and misconceptions around depression, suicide, violence, SRHR, GBV and similar.

1 - Needs

The protracted humanitarian crisis in oPt is characterized by high levels of traumatic experiences, leading to a widespread crisis in mental health, which is underreported, under-treated, and underfunded. Essential for mental health and wellbeing is to feel safe and secure, and the loss of psychosocial security has led to an inability to satisfy needs. More recently, the outbreak of COVID-19 adds to the complexity and increased critical needs of mental healthcare services to be addressed. The Palestinian population is young, with 69% under the age of 29 years and the majority between 10-19 years (PCBS, 2019). Young people (10-29 years) are among the most vulnerable and continuously exposed to traumatic and violent experiences, and impacts of COVID-19, leading to progression in high levels of mental, behavioral and emotional issues (UNFPA). Gender and age-differences, form of vulnerabilities and residency influence exposure and risk to traumatic experiences affecting the mental health and psychosocial challenges, coping strategies, accessibility to services and responses by the health sector and society which determine the needs for MHPSS interventions. The multidimensional impacts by COVID-19 on young people ranging from health, safety and protection, education, economic stability etc. are amplified in humanitarian contexts (COMPACT; IANYD Statement on COVID-19 and Youth). Routine health services are disrupted, especially those delivered through schools and universities. Lockdown measures enforce and extend faced challenges which result in negative coping mechanisms, such as self-harm (OCHA Bulletin), substance abuse and other harmful behaviors, based on the economic deterioration and limits to community initiatives and services, sports, formal and non-formal education (COMPACT). Based on OCHA Bulletin (August-September) a drastic increase is reported of hotline calls and phone counseling, which is especially confirmed in SAWA’s monthly helpline reports. Therefore, the need has increased for critical adolescent- and youth sensitive MHPSS services and information and the development of methods to remotely reach people (HNO 2021; PCBS, 2020; SAWA reports emergency hotline; OCHA Bulletin). How young people are supported in the preparedness and response is critical and have lasting impacts on mitigating risks and the recovery process of individuals and communities. MHPSS including psychosocial first aid and community-based support must be integrated into the humanitarian response to meet the urgent need in providing equal and impartial age and gender appropriate services and information. Health services are often not seen by young people as “friendly”, limiting how many seek services and number of successful treatments. MHPSS for young people must be integrated into fixed and mobile clinics, including expanding and investing in digital health platforms (especially based on lockdown restrictions and mitigating risks), and strongly emphasizing the developed referral systems and directories. Therefore, in-service training and mentoring in delivering adolescent and youth-sensitive services and information (incl. mhGAP and other standardized guidelines) and guidelines for COVID-19 response, are critically needed and required through specialized capacity building of personnel to ensure advanced, safe, non-judgmental and disability sensitive individualized case management. As well, strengthen and expand delivery of community awareness and empowerment sessions and community-based psychosocial support services by trained youth educators (already started to be integrated into mobile clinics) on MHPSS, MH, COVID-19, SRHR, GBV, stigma, misconceptions connected to vulnerable groups with a focus on those living with disabilities, coping mechanisms are of crucial importance in increasing accessibility and acceptability of services and building a crucial protective and self-helping environment with high levels of preparedness and resilience.

2 - Activities

Activity 1. Delivery of critical MHPSS for young people in Gaza Strip, Area C, H2 and EJ. MHPSS for young people will be provided by integrating adolescent and youth-sensitive MHPSS into university youth-friendly health centres, NGO fixed and mobile health clinics, and home visits. However, the service delivery will be adapted to COVID-19 restrictions and be flexible towards offering young people with digital health (phone, email and mHealth application, and other virtual platforms) to support the growing urgent health needs and to mitigate the disruption of services. These interventions will follow WHO’s guidelines e.g. Digital Implementation Investment Guide (DIIG). For example, since universities are closed due to COVID-19, the focus will be on strengthening and offering young people with digital health services and information through alternative platforms and make use of existing referral systems. Adapting and offering alternative ways of receiving services, will reach a wider set of young people by striving to overcome and mitigate barriers in form of restrictions, diagnosed with COVID-19, no access to fixed PHCs due to remote location, vulnerabilities (ensuring services are available and accessible for PWDs) and the existence of cultural norms hindering seeking life-saving care e.g. within post-operative outreach programs for those with new disabilities as a result of the GMR. Activity 2. Conduct mentoring and in-service training for health providers (at universities and NGO) to increase capacity building in managing and providing adolescent and youth-sensitive MHPSS services and information (4 Gaza and 4 West Bank). The training will focus on health providers (NGO and universities), complementing WHO’s previous work with MOH PHC-services. It will cover how to provide both physical and virtual services and information in an adolescent and youth-friendly manner, guidelines for COVID-19 response, individualized MHPSS for young people, including meeting the specific needs of PWDs by incorporating guidelines. NGO health care workers also need to be trained on how to train others e.g. using mhGAP guidelines and manuals, they can conduct community group sessions and disseminate the information in partnership with community workers. Activity 3. Conduct 6 trainings for geographically spread community workers and mobilizers on community-based support services and MHPSS (Y-Peer Educational Network and PMRS volunteers). Trained community workers to provide services and tailored sessions through e.g. community workers joining mobile clinics’ teams and conducting virtual interventions. The training will incorporate guidelines for digital health, COVID-19 response and as per mhGAP guidelines will include, PFA, PSS, mitigation of coping mechanisms and stress management, usage of mhGAP algorithm to increase engagement in detection, monitoring and assessing early signs. Also, trained on how to identify and assess the needs and communicate with PWDs. Further, gain local perspectives on stigma and misconceptions and messages and principles. Delivery of community awareness sessions will address young peoples’ mental health, SRHR, GBV, incorporating specific needs of PWDs, specific MH gender and age-related needs, coping mechanisms, detection of early signs, stigma and misconceptions of vulnerable groups and MH, etc. By offering virtual sessions, a wider set of young people will be reached erasing the factor of location.

9 - Protection mainstreaming
9.1 - Do No Harm

Due to the ongoing pandemic, it is crucial to integrate COVID-19 protection and prevention measures, such as risk mitigation measures and adapts interventions. UNFPA will act upon the gained understanding with the priority to consider and uphold the rights and to not cause any further negative impact and harm. Safety and security will be guaranteed by upholding dignity and protection in the provision of services and information in friendly spaces and focusing on and strengthening alternative methods during lockdown such as mobile clinics and virtual platforms. Health providers will be trained and mentored to deliver non-judgmental, disability-sensitive and high-quality adolescent and youth-friendly services ensuring confidentiality, privacy and respecting the wishes about the care of those seeking help, including meeting the specific needs of PWDs. As well, trained on COVID-19 response guidelines. Safeguards are to be put in place in ensuring confidentiality to not be compromised when mobility is restricted. At the same time, the specialized personnel needs to be supported in delivering services and upholding their right and dignity through being treated equitably and fairly. Based on PSEA, to include related activities such as conduct training on PSEA guidelines for involved partners and staff who will be directly in contact with targeted population. In combination with carrying out PSEA-related awareness and sensitization activities (physical or virtual, developing and handing out soft and hard copy materials) for the targeted population. Within the project, a safe and accessible reporting system needs to be in place and everyone involved or targeted to be aware where and how to seek assistance for reporting and/or information. In relation to health facilities (both fixed and mobile), safety and protection will be reached by identifying and securing surrounding areas minimizing threats and vulnerabilities, especially following and implementing guidelines on COVID-19. Infrastructure adaptations will be made when needed e.g. ramps, railings, braille signs to health facilities in order to make services more available and accessible for everyone. If, needed providing separate waiting areas for males and females, specific service hours and the availability of both female and male staff. At least one qualified health provider will be working in the clinics (mobile or fixed) and on virtual platforms. Including taking relevant measures for locations for community-based support sessions. The priority is to conduct virtual sessions, however, where applicable to conduct physical following procedures. Sessions need to take into consideration different needs, in particular PWDs (accessibility to and within facilities, sign language translator, accessible and available information to all). For young people, it is important to provide a physically, emotionally and socially safe and supportive environment both physically and virtually. Inequities and divides among them and their communities must be considered, and ensure the participation in sessions or receiving care don’t backlash from community or family. Meaning, culture and customs bust be respected throughout the whole cycle in order to ensure that people are not put at risk. Further, negative effects on the local economy and livelihoods have been addressed by the project not building a parallel system for the provision of health services, instead, strengthening local capabilities, by utilizing existing fixed resources. The responses through this project will, therefore, be carefully coordinated to aim for complementary structures e.g. utilizing existing referral systems. Further, it considers the impact on the environment that can affect safety, rights, and dignity of people, aiming for a low level of carbon footprint and to handle materials and equipment in an environmentally friendly manner based on available options throughout all activities and the whole implementatio

9.2 - Equal and impartial access to aid

As mentioned under ‘Do no harm’, due to the ongoing pandemic, it is very crucial to integrate COVID-19 protection and prevention measures, such as risk mitigation measures. Which will require adapting interventions and to especially promote meaningful and equal access of vulnerable groups. It will take into consideration needs based on gender, age and vulnerabilities and disabilities since the need for MHPSS and accessibility to services are different. Primarily, ensuring that selected communities get needed MHPSS services and will gain a better understanding and knowledge about MH and psychosocial wellbeing/issues, coping mechanisms, available MHPSS, vulnerable groups etc. This project is designed to provide equal and impartial accessibility without barriers and discrimination through all planned activities. Impartiality is upheld through striving for a large outreach and accessibility by coordination with other operating actors in combination with continuously conducting needs assessments. It is of importance to highlight and promote young people’s rights to health care and how/where to obtain it by comprehensive awareness and informational campaigns e.g. utilizing existing referral systems. The project is based on the needs of young people, but take into consideration possible differences in urgent needs, faced risks and faced traumatic experiences, which are gender-or age-specific, vulnerabilities (including specific needs of PWDs) and connected to location of residency. It will primarily target identified vulnerable young people at risk by providing crucial MHPSS, including considering the arrangement of peoples’ access and availability to services including adapting services and information (developing a specific alternative communication strategy in order to ensure reaching everyone, especially those at risk of exclusion). That is achieved by offering different alternatives in order to be able to widen the reach and met needs, through existing health clinics (fixed and mobile) and home visits when applicable and enhance digital health services and interventions through other channels such as phone and virtual platforms. According to the Household survey for information and communications technology covering 2019, results showed that 86% of youth (18-29 years) have access to internet (90% West Bank and 79% Gaza Strip). This will allow barriers to be tackled that may mitigate disruption of health service delivery, restrict freedom of movement and/or people from seeking help, for instance, making sure all disabilities are considered (people with reduced mobility and non-mobility-related disabilities), and those who cannot access services due to for instance restrictions related to COVID-19, no access to internet or technological device, socio-cultural norms and restrictions by tackling stigma and misconceptions at all levels. Mobile clinics and virtual platforms will especially have the role to enable services and information and community-based support interventions to reach people in more vulnerable communities. Further, community action is in focus by delivering tailored sessions and initiatives based on different outreach strategies by community workers (peer educators and volunteers) within communities targeting different population groups.
