Project Summary

OCHA plays a critical role in the leadership and coordination of humanitarian action in South Sudan based on a people-centred approach. OCHA brings together different stakeholders to facilitate coordination and analyse the evolving needs, and adjust response operations where needed. This includes assessing and documenting humanitarian needs, negotiating humanitarian access, and compiling and synthesizing qualitative and quantitative data to produce a wide range of information products.

1 - Needs

Throughout 2022, people in South Sudan continued to face increased humanitarian challenges because of compounding shocks, including the impact of intensified conflict and sub-national violence; four years (2019-2022) of higher-than-average erratic rainfall and resulting flooding; and the impacts of COVID-19, and the evolving risk of Ebola. People’s food security situation continued to be precarious, with some communities potentially facing catastrophic needs. Interconnected and compounding shocks increase people’s vulnerability and erode coping skills. As such, more children are expected to experience acute malnourished. Women and girls continue to face extreme levels of gender-based violence and psychosocial trauma. The economy remains unstable, and access to livelihoods is a challenge for many. Access to essential services, including health care, education, water, and sanitation, as well as protection and legal services, is limited for many. over 2 million people remain internally displaced, and more than 2 million people are refugees in the region. An estimated 9.4 million women, men, girls, and boys will need humanitarian assistance and protection services in 2023, according to the need analysis captured in the 2023 Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO). This is a significant increase from 2022.

2 - Project activities

OCHA contributes to principled and effective humanitarian response through coordination, advocacy, policy, information management and humanitarian financing tools and services with partners. In South Sudan, these activities include: • Coordination of the various stages of the Humanitarian Programme Cycle, including front-line coordination activities as close to the affected population as possible, facilitating the development of the Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO) and Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP); Coordinate and as relevant, management of humanitarian financing instruments such as the CERF and SSHF; Champion and advocate for Accountability to Affected Populations, gender, age and disability, PSEA, cash as a modality supporting humanitarian-development-peace nexus, and protection ensuring that each phase of the humanitarian programme cycle reflects these commitments. Co-chairing the National Advisory Group on Solutions. • Provision of the secretariat and other necessary support, including chairing as relevant the Humanitarian Coordination Forum meetings, Humanitarian Country Team, Inter-Cluster Coordination Group, Information Management Working Group, Access Working Group, Needs Analysis Working Group, and the South Sudan Humanitarian Fund Advisory Board, informed by timely, relevant, evidence-based information to guide advocacy positions. • Support and build consensus guided by IASC standards to develop policy approaches to best serve people in need. • Provide Information management services to the humanitarian community to inform timely, effective, needs-based principled responses. • Leverage timely information on people’s needs and gaps, mobilize and engage the spectrum of financing instruments, and mechanisms, including the country-based pooled fund (SSHF), and as relevant CERF, to ensure that humanitarian needs are met, humanitarian leadership and coordination mechanisms are promoted, and are complementary among themselves, coherent with development funding when possible. • Support humanitarian hubs and operational centres to facilitate access to hard-to-reach locations, bring humanitarian assistance closer to the people and enhance inter-sector and inter-agency coordination, and Support decentralization efforts to ensure coherent leadership and coordination as close to affected population as possible

7.4. Community Engagement Strategy

Yes

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

OCHA liaises with clusters to gather feedback channelled through feedback mechanisms to inform the inter-sectoral needs analysis for different demographic population groups, which will support the designing of response strategies tailored to the needs of these population groups. OCHA will leverage its role to ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members of the affected community informs advocacy messaging.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

OCHA works with the inter-agency Communication and Community Engagement Working Group to strengthen two-way communication with affected people across the response system. For example, OCHA staff interact directly with crisis-affected people to hear from them about the challenges they face and to understand their needs. OCHA plays a key role in promoting accountability to affected people approaches are integrated into humanitarian activities, mainstreamed through humanitarian planning and programming, and reflected in humanitarian funding, specifically through the South Sudan Humanitarian Fund. This includes ensuring that the HCT engages in timely, strategic discussions on PSEA and Communication and Community Engagement Working Group, undertaking missions to hot-spot areas across the country and supporting the Humanitarian Coordinator’s advocacy efforts. OCHA promotes that communication with the affected community with appropriate feedback mechanisms and referral pathways are in place and leveraged to inform all humanitarian activities with timely and accurate information, including through timely needs assessment with data collected directly from the affected population. OCHA supports the HCT to meet its commitment to ensuring that affected people are at the centre of any humanitarian response, that collective mechanisms are in place to ensure that they can provide feedback on their own priorities and concerns around humanitarian action and that these priorities and concerns are considered and addressed in humanitarian action in a meaningful way, as outlined in the HCT Compact.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

OCHA continues to disseminate information to its staff members on the zero tolerance for SEA and sensitize them regularly about the need to prevent any SEA. This includes facilitating all staff to take the mandatory online training on PSEA and Sexual Harassment, including regular refresher training; facilitating that all staff sign their commitments on zero tolerance; Conducting regular briefings and internal dialogue with all staff on PSEA to ensure ongoing awareness.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

OCHA leverages all feasible opportunities to remind affected populations of their right in terms of zero tolerance for Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (SEA). OCHA is a member of the PSEA Taskforce and supports their efforts to carry out assessments to contribute to risk mitigation and risk management; and support feedback mechanisms, especially for community communication on issues they identify.
