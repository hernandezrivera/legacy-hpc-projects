Project Summary

Heartland Alliance International’s (HAI) humanitarian response encompasses the provision of legal services, psychosocial support services (PSS), protection monitoring and assessments, capacity building for government officials on protection, and advocacy for protection of participants against human rights violations. HAI’s humanitarian response will reach approximately 47,873 IDPs, returnees and host community members in Ninewa, Salah Al Din, Anbar, Kirkuk, Diyala, Sulaymaniyah and Duhok. HAI’s humanitarian response program is directly in line with the Protection Cluster objective 1: Provide specialized protection assistance, and community-based support, to vulnerable conflict-affected individuals, thereby enabling them to secure their rights, access humanitarian assistance and public services, and overcome barriers to achievement of durable solutions; and objective 2: Support and advocate with authorities, humanitarian and recovery actors to address protection issues in accordance with protection norms and applicable legal standards, thereby contributing to a favorable protection environment.
HAI’s protection response interventions are carried out through a mobile response team composed of legal officers, psychosocial support officers, protection monitors and case managers. The mobile teams operate in Ninewa (Mosul District, Airstrip camp, Hamdaniyah (Bashiqa, Bartella), Anbar (Ramadi, 18 Kilo Camp, Heet district), Salah Al Din (between Sammara and Tikrit, Tuz Khurmatu), Diyala (Khalis and Muqdadiya), Duhok (Amedi and Dawdiya camp), Sulaymaniyah (Barznja, Surdash and Tazade camp), Kirkuk (Kirkuk and Hawija), as well as the juvenile detention facility in Bagdad.
HAI’s protection response will include provision of legal services to approximately 12,767 individuals, including legal services for civil documentation, detention cases (including for children detained for suspected affiliation to terror groups), sexual and gender based violence (SGBV), property and asset protection, arbitrary arrests, and criminal cases. Protection services will include protection monitoring, identification of protection needs, and referrals to specialized services. Legal awareness raising efforts will include distribution of “Know Your Rights” (KYR) brochures to target populations on legal rights, and civil documentation, compensation and land registration procedures. A 24/7 hotline will be made available to request HAI assistance in Ninewa and Kirkuk. HAI will respond immediately to ensure safety, full legal and psychosocial services, and appropriate referral in cases of SGBV, and for at-risk LGBT persons.
HAI’s team will provide basic PSS including psychological first aid (PFA), individual consultation and group consultations and referral to specialized mental health services to HAI’s newly established trauma treatment and rehabilitation center in Mosul to approximately 8,026 individuals.
HAI will continue the local level advocacy mechanism it has created through the Ninewa Security Steering Committee and the Kirkuk Steering Committee. The committees will be engaged through the project implementation to ensure protection and security issues are brought to high level stakeholders in Ninewa and Kirkuk.
Furthermore, HAI will work on increasing the skills and capacity of 385 government staff to support IDPs and returnees in target governorates, including conducting protection principles training to government officials, and basic psychosocial support training to Ministry of Education teachers.

Needs

The humanitarian crisis in Iraq reached a peak immediately following military operations to retake areas formerly held by the Islamic State of Iraq and the Levant (ISIL).The fight against ISIL shifted to low intensity levels mainly focused in rural areas, and the humanitarian situation in Iraq is entering into a protracted crisis. For the first time since 2014, the estimated number of people returning to their areas of origin exceeded the number of people displaced. The number of returnees reached four million across Iraq, while the number of those displaced is 1.9 million. Approximately 85% of the displaced people are coming from Ninewa, Anbar, Kirkuk, Diyala, Salah Al Din, and over two thirds of IDPs in Iraq are hosted in Kurdistan region major cities of Erbil (11%), Dohuk (18%), Sulaymaniyah (8%) and Ninewa (31%) (IOM Reasons to Return November 2018).59% are displaced outside of governorate of origin,41% are displaced within governorate of origin, and 70% and 30% are displaced outside camps and in camps respectively (ibid). HAI is planning to respond to the needs of IDPs located in the governorates of Kirkuk, Sulaymaniyah, Ninewa, Anbar, Salah Al Din, Dohuk and Diyala with context specific interventions for legal and MHPSS services. Common barriers for return include damaged homes and lack of social services in the place of origin (IOM Reason to Return, November, 2018). Approximately 30% of IDPs combined for both in camps (16%) and out of camps (24%) report not knowing the state of their house in their places of origin, which may limit informed decisions towards resolving their displacement status. (IOM Reasons to Return, November 2018).Multi Cluster Needs Assessment VI suggests most IDPs in Iraq are planning to stay in their current location for the next 12 months (IOM November, 2018) and actors need to plan responses to address their needs, fears and concerns. 8% of all IDPs outside the camps, who represent the largest proportion of all IDPs in Iraq, report missing civil documentation, and 1% of experienced movement restrictions during daytime (REACH, MCNA, and September, 2018).For those in camps (30%), 10% reported missing documentation and 2% experienced movement restrictions during daytime hours.The magnitude of civil documentation needs and effects of restricted movement contribute additional risks for 18% of out of camp female headed households and 21% of in-camp female headed households.Based on the assessment, 40% of the households surveyed were in need of humanitarian assistance with equal severity (19 out 100 on weighted score across 8 sectoral indicators) for both in and out of camp IDPs. The effects of conflict have negatively impacted children and their psychosocial wellbeing, with most children in out of camp settings exhibiting signs of psychosocial distress.In both in and out camps households, 6% had a child aged 6-17 years old working, and approximately 3% of the households had a child aged 12-17 years old married. Based on Risk Assessment Site Priority findings, key informants from 27% of these sites reported the presence of unaccompanied children in the area, and child headed households were reported in all the assessed six governorates’ sites, except in Anbar.In all the seven governorates targeted by HAI, context-specific needs and concerns have been reported in MCNA reports. In all the governorates except Diyala that were prioritized for risk assessment, 93% of the surveyed sites, 241, (18 in Sulaymaniyah, 37 in Salah Al-Din, 115 in Dohuk, 7 in Anbar, 49 in Ninewa and 15 in Kirkuk) were reported to have inadequate mental health and psycho-social support services available for persons in the site. Out of the 241 sites, 79.2% had no adequate facilities and/or services available for persons in the site with physical, intellectual or mental disorders. In 31 of the 241 sites where data was collected in the governorates, there were reported cases of households being forcibly evicted. Mid-2018, HAI conducted a protection assessment in Ninewa that included a sample of 833 household heads (43% women and 57% men respondents).The majority of the surveyed households indicated that they or someone in the household is need of legal assistance. While 34% were in need of legal advice, 31% and 36% were in need of legal representation and documentations. A majority (53%) of those in need of documentation (765) needed civil ID cards.17% had boys and girls in need of birth registrations and nationality certificates.The survey found high level of experiences of trauma (3 out of 4 respondents).14% of the households noted that they would not seek services even when they are available.This is linked to stigma;30% noted that it is deemed culturally and religiously inappropriate within their community.

Activities

HAI will implement key activities through implementing partners in Anbar, Salah Al Din, Diyala, Duhok, Sulaymaniyah, Ninewa, and through direct implementation in Mosul District and Kirkuk. Services will be implemented through mobile teams and statics teams composed of lawyers, PSS officers, protection officers, and case workers to maintain flexibility in reaching IDPs and returnees in hard to reach areas in the targeted locations as well as respond to changing needs on the ground. HAI will provide legal services (legal consultations and representation) for civil documentation, detention, arbitrary arrests, SGBV, property and assets protection, family law cases, and advocate with authorities to prevent confiscation of identity documents. Static teams will be placed at court houses in Kirkuk (Kirkuk, Hawija), Salah Al Din (Tuz Khurmatu) and Ninewa (Mosul, Qaraqosh, Ba’shiqa and Sinjar). Legal assistance will include provision of services to children and youth who are at risk / in conflict with the law; assist in tracking and identification of unaccompanied and separated children (UASC), and assist in family reunification, in coordination with KRI and GOI officials; and identify children transferred to detention and provide their families with updates as to their status, treatment, visitation and reintegration into the community. HAI will also respond to reports of arbitrary arrest and detentions, to investigate and ensure that those who are arrested are accorded basic protections. HAI will respond immediately to ensure safety, full legal and psychosocial services, and appropriate referral in cases of SGBV, and for at-risk LGBT persons. HAI will conduct household level protection monitoring (in Kirkuk, Hawija, Tuz Khurmatu, and Mosul District), identify protection needs and refer cases of vulnerable individuals with specific needs to specialized assistance. A hotline will be available for IDPs and returnees to request services and referrals. HAI will advocate for a protection response with government officials and NGOs through continuing its work with the Ninewa Security Steering Committee created in 2017 and the Kirkuk Steering Committee created in 2018. The committees will be engaged through the project implementation to ensure protection issues are brought to high level stakeholders. HAI will expand referral networks with relevant stakeholders, including UN agencies, local and national NGOs, INGOs, and government authorities to ensure that IDPs are able to access legal assistance; and provide referral system awareness sessions within target IDP, returnee, and host communities. HAI will increase awareness sessions on rights, entitlements, and availability of services through distribution of “Know Your Rights” brochures and awareness sessions. The KYR sessions and brochures will include information on legal rights and acquiring civil documentation procedures, compensation and land registration procedures, and sessions with at risk IDP youth and parents. HAI will build the capacity of government staff and authorities on protection approaches and issues. HAI will develop the capacity of judges, public prosecutors, legal officials, and detention center staff in child protection principles for youth; hold training sessions for teachers, law enforcement and community leaders on child rights and protection issues; provide psychoeducation training to teachers in understanding the impacts of conflict and displacement on children and adolescents and supporting positive development; provide WHO Mental Health Gap Action Programme capacity building training for MOH primary healthcare staff in the identification and management of priority mental health conditions. Mobile teams will also provide PFA, basic PSS, and referrals to all target beneficiaries in need. Services will be conducted in secure spaces in existing camp public health centers or community centers to ensure confidentially. The PSS workers will conduct small psychosocial group sessions intended to help to educate communities on traumatic stress and depression and reduce stigma, teach basic relaxation techniques, and provide a structured format for community members to collaborate on reducing the impact of violence on their communities and families. PSS workers will also be trained to detect SGBV, child abuse, and other issues requiring emergency response. PSS workers will provide individual psychoeducation and PFA sessions to IDPs who are experiencing distress. As part of its ongoing response, HAI will establish higher-level mental health services at a new trauma rehabilitation centre in Kirkuk and expand services at an existing newly HAI established center in Ibn Sina Hospital in Mosul for IDPs with debilitating depression and/or anxiety as a result of their traumatic experiences. Both treatment centers will be linked to the Ministry of Health and will primarily assist IDPs or recently returned individuals.

Protection Risk Analysis

Large scale humanitarian operations are transitioning from emergency humanitarian relief to early recovery and development. This transition brings a number of new challenges, including the shift from urban combat operations to low intensity hostilities, primarily in rural areas. Due to HAI’s willingness to work with families perceived to be associated with ISIL, staff working across project areas could face hostility from communities and security forces. In providing legal consultation, representation, and referrals to program participants, legal officers may face threats for representing individuals who have family members who are suspected to be affiliated with ISIL and face accusations of supporting terrorists. HAI anticipates similar challenges when implementing programming in Ninewah, in addition to Anbar, Diyala, Salah Al Din, and Kirkuk governorates. Local staff from NGOs are often harassed, intimidated, or accused of affiliation themselves when they try to support perceived affiliates. Additionally, access to detention facilities to work with juveniles both in the Iraq Kurdistan Region and federal Iraq may be limited on a varying basis. Moreover, HAI has experienced delays in program implementation due to administration and security approvals from the government, and anticipates this obstacle in upcoming projects due to the nature of the various security bodies and associated bureaucratic processes.HAI staff could face access issues with various security forces in providing training sessions in program areas for teachers, law enforcement, detention center staff, and community leaders. HAI’s staff and partners will continue to provide psychoeducation to IDP and returnee parents and caregivers and to teachers with a similar level of risk. When conducting “Know Your Rights” awareness sessions, HAI staff have faced risks associated with accessing participants in areas that are controlled by armed groups not associated with the government, especially when getting access to the camps. There is also the possibility of government officials requiring a breach of participant confidentiality for security reasons. IDPs may require protection assistance in cases of gender-based violence (GBV). Many children and adolescents who were forcibly recruited by ISIL or subsequently detained have been subjected to sexual assault. LGBTI individuals across Iraq are also at extreme risk of violence and exploitation. GBV including sexual violence also affects Iraqi men and boys. Reports are emerging of boys and young men being sexually assaulted in ISIL captivity or when detained by security forces. GBV against males carries with it a tremendous stigma in Iraqi society, and caregivers require special training and awareness to respond appropriately to victims. Women who were forced to marry ISIL members face ostracism and risk of retaliation. IDP populations in general require legal assistance in cases of divorce, child custody, guardianship, inheritance and paternity, and in recovering property and assets. IDPs displaced by conflict in Mosul, Salah al Din and Anbar require immediate protection from arbitrary arrest and detention, with children as the highest priority. Most IDP families assisted or identified through protection programs are affected by multiple war-related stressors and many are in need of comprehensive mental health and psycho-social support services (MHPSS). In particular, children and adolescents have been exposed to trauma and faced serious protection threats in displacement. HAI works with local implementing partners who have better access to participants living in high risk, hard-to-reach areas. Working with local partners and providing remote management and oversight can result in additional risk related to financial compliance and avoiding conflicts of interest.

Mitigation Measures

HAI monitors the local security environment through open source intelligence and an active security network. High staff member involvement in the local aid community and long-standing experience in the area enables the HAI team to respond to changing events and curtail operations to prevent staff members from entering any unsafe areas. Staff members are required to adopt a “low profile approach” to mitigate the risk for kidnapping, petty or violent crime, and targeted assassinations. To carry out legal, MhPSS, and Know-Your-Rights sessions, HAI staff advocates with security and government entities to ensure access to program sites for staff and partnering organizations. HAI is compliant with local access-related regulations in each proposed governorate. HAI puts an emphasis on hiring local staff within each programmatic governorate; if access is limited or denied, HAI still has staff directly on the ground who are continuing services without interruption. If there are issues with security, HAI will immediately pull staff out or ask officers to stop implementing project activities until the security risk is mitigated. Ensuring confidentiality of participant information is of utmost importance. Local authorities may require HAI to provide sensitive participant information; HAI continues to take appropriate measures to protect the confidentiality of participants and their information, unless given explicit consent by the participant. At the same time, HAI recognizes and will continue to inform participants that if information is criminal in nature, it sometimes cannot remain confidential. HAI will continue to advocate with MoLSA to secure access to provide services for juveniles at detention centers and collect data on torture and abuse in these detention centers. HAI will advocate with local and national authorities to respect and support the work encompassed in this project to ensure successful project implementation and aid in securing staff safety in program areas. In the past, HAI has advocated with human rights, security, and legal actors to discuss concerns about staff being threatened and obtaining materials staff are legally allowed to access. Resolutions have been found to address problems. HAI has proven its ability to respond swiftly to cases of subaward non-compliance and will continue to do so. Allegations of fraud and political participation were handled immediately by HAI management, and subawardees were provided training and support to strengthen their organizational policies to prevent allegations or non-compliance to international standards of operations in the future. When corrective measures recommended by HAI are not taken by the subawardees, HAI has terminated subawards. This is a last resort action as HAI believes in supporting local partners and building capacity as part of its sustainability plans. HAI will perform regular monitoring on subgrantees to ensure compliance. The proposed HAI activities are designed to address heightened vulnerabilities posed by age and gender, as well as ethnic and religious background. Female-headed households, survivors of GBV and unaccompanied or separated children will be prioritized. HAI will train its staff and partner staff to be sensitive to the needs and unique trauma of male survivors of GBV. Persons at risk of retribution or violence will also be prioritized, including persons forced to collaborate with ISIL such as child combatants and victims of coerced marriage, ethnic and religious minorities and LGBT persons. HAI will provide legal representation for divorce and support relocation of women facing immediate risk from family members.The protection teams will address mediation in cases of GBV or early marriage, and in responding to crises. HAI collaborates with other organizations to relocate and protect victims of severe GBV and LGBT individuals at immediate risk or others who are in crisis and in need of protection.

How is your project accountable to affected population

HAI has set up internal processes that guarantees accountability to affected populations as described in the IASC guidelines on implementing accountability to affected populations (IASC, 2012) in line with the five commitments endorsed by the IASC in 2011. HAI refers to affected men, women, boys and girls as participants because HAI acknowledges that we are allies with the individuals we serve. To ensure participants are well informed and can hold program staff (both HAI and partners staff members) accountable for their actions, HAI provides information to participants on their rights throughout the entire project design, implementation, and monitoring of service delivery process. Information on ways to share feedback, concerns or complaints is also available to participants. HAI actively seeks participant feedback to improve policy and practice in programming, ensuring that feedback and complaint mechanisms are streamlined to address any alleged breaches in policy or instances of stakeholder dissatisfaction. HAI will implement a client satisfaction survey, provide a hotline number on all printed materials, and administer a quarterly evaluation to ensure IDPs have access to, and are satisfied with, the services provided to enable maximum and equal protection under the Iraqi and IKR laws. To ensure objective analysis of complaints and feedback, HAI has recruited an accountability, monitoring and verification assistant who will work under the guidance of the country M&E focal to conduct telephone interviews to assess satisfaction level and seek feedback on the quality and conduct of HAI and partner staff while interacting with affected population. Focus group discussions will be held on a quarterly basis with affected populations in areas of operation for programming review purpose. Focus group discussions will be held with boys, girls, men and women and disabled participants to assess program quality and any concerns related to services. Feedback and concerns will be analyzed on a monthly basis for internal discussions and decision making. A database of satisfaction surveys will be developed to track the nature of complaints, feedback and commitment to address concerns on monthly basis during monthly review processes. In responding to GBV survivors, HAI adopts a Do No Harm principle and a survivor centered approach to services that ensure confidentiality, respect, participation and safety of participants. HAI has a country level case manager who handles severe SGBV cases which are maintained in a centralized and encrypted database. The case manager follows IASC guidelines for case management protocols and tools. All staff are trained on how to refer SGBV cases to specialized services, how to treat the survivors and to maintain confidentiality. HAI recognizes its obligation and responsibility to operate in a manner consistent with the fundamental principles of human rights and is dedicated to the prevention of and response to sexual harassment, exploitation and abuse (SHEA). HAI has a zero tolerance policy to sexual harassment, exploitation and abuse by its staff members or its partner staff. All staff sign the SHEA policy upon employment, and the SHEA policy is included in the partner subawards and partner staff are trained on SHEA policy and reporting mechanism. HAI outlines SHEA reporting and response mechanisms in its policy. HAI’s reporting and response mechanism to SHEA includes assigning a dedicated a trained reporting focal point in the country office, and providing a reporting email (abuse@heartlandalliance.org). Complaints will be received by a representative of Heartland Alliance in Chicago who has expertise in handling cases of SHEA. Sensitive information related to incidents of SHEA whether involving partners, colleagues, program participants or others in the communities in which HAI works shall be shared only with individuals of the appropriate seniority or function who need to know such information.
