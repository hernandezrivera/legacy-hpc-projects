Project Summary

Plan’s International Bangladesh (PIB) Child Protection Program will focus on two interlinking components: case management and community based child protection (CBCP). The services will ensure boys, girls and adolescents in Cox’s Bazar are safe and protected from violence, abuse and exploitation.
The activities will provide support to vulnerable children and adolescents in the Rohingya camps and host communities in Ukhyia and Teknaf. Case management activities will target unaccompanied and separated children (UASC), child-headed households, children in psychosocial distress, children with specific needs, child victims of trafficking and child survivors of GBV. In this regard, PIB will mainstream child protection in education by ensuring safety and dignity of beneficiaries and avoid causing harm to the children. The EIE unit will refer children with protection concerns to child protection for case management as well as for life skills opportunities.
CBCP activities will be structured around establishing and supporting CBCP mechanisms embedded in the refugee and host communities. These activities will be structured through feedback from community consultation and participation to ensure inclusion and ownership by both the host community and the refugee community. PIB will work with a range of stakeholders in the community, including children, adolescents, caregivers, community and religious leaders, as well as government representatives. The interventions ensure inclusion of children, adolescents, persons with disability and the youths in the CBCP mechanism and their empowerment. Community-level interventions will include working directly with the refugees and host communities to promote social cohesion, supporting families and strengthening self-protection of children. In order to address harmful practices and attitudes related to child rearing and parenting, PIB will invests in active community participation, empowerment, inclusive feedback and in providing support to caregivers and families.
PIB is accountable to the communities that it serves and will ensure that system of complain and child friendly feedback mechanisms are in place with strong emphasis on PSEA and Child Safeguarding. Specifically on child safeguarding, PIB has a Safe Guarding Specialist totally dedicated to the response in Cox’s Bazar and continuously linked to the Global Safe Guarding Unit of PI and to the Country Safe Guarding Advisor in Dhaka. Regarding to the specific prevention and combating Sexual Exploitation and Abuse, Plan International has a PSEA Focal Point in Cox’s Bazar who is here on a long term and continuously participates in the activities of the PSEA Network in Cox’s Bazar. PIB also has a code of conduct to ensure protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse as well as Child Safeguarding and the humanitarian principle of DO NO HARM. PIB will ensure its partners have PSEA policies and the same is implemented to the core taking into consideration measure in case of breaches.
PIB is committed to coordinating the response through active participation in Child Protection Sub-Sector, Case Management Taskforce, other sectors and working groups such as Education, GBV, CwC to ensure meaningful access, prioritization of safety and integration of services. The response will also be coordinated on the field level both with partners and other stakeholders including government bodies and local communities.
The services offered will be in line with Child Protection Minimum Standards in Humanitarian Action, Child Protection Sub-Sector localized Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) for Bangladesh, interagency case management tools and referral pathway. This will be ensured by provision of structured training to PIB staff to enhance competencies in providing quality gender-responsive and timely services to children and adolescents.

Needs

Since August 2017 approximately 744,000 Rohingya sought asylum in Bangladesh joining approximately 170,000 who sought refuge prior to the 2017 influx. Boys and girls represent 55% of all refugees. Refugee children report feeling unsafe and vulnerable to violence, including sexual violence (Rohingya Refugee Response Gender Analysis, August 2018, Joint Agency Research Report). Child marriage with all its accompanying consequences remains of great concern in the camps. (Adolescent Girls in Crisis: Voices of the Rohingya, June 2018, Plan International) approximately 5-10% of Rohingya households engage in Child marriage and two-thirds of these households consider it a normal practice, while approximately one-third use it as a coping strategy to either protect girls or reduce economic burdens on the household. The perceived risk of abduction and trafficking remains high, with households reporting fear of kidnapping of boys and girls at 49% and 44% respectively. Agencies continue to monitor the worst forms of child labour, involving children as young as 7 years old; 3% of 5-17 year old children are reportedly working; with 51% of those working exposed to the worst forms of child labour. By Sept 2019, 8,596 unaccompanied and separated children (UASC) were identified and are deemed to be at risk of multiple CP risks. There therefore remains a need to establish and monitor family-based care arrangements for UASC or those unable to live with their primary caregivers due to abuse or neglect. Conducting family tracing and reunification and providing support to vulnerable families is also essential. Access to education remains a challenge, with only 13% of boys and 2% of girls aged 15-18 attending learning centres; out-of-school children may also be exposed to further risks such as exploitative labour, drugs, and criminal activities. Given the ongoing nature of the crisis, the need to ensure sustainability and reinforce accountabilities, capitalizing on opportunities to harmonize CP humanitarian work with national child protection systems is essential. Like refugees, host communities living in Cox’s Bazar District, in particular the rural less-developed Unions of Ukhiya and Teknaf Upazilas, have unaddressed basic needs and are also exposed to protection risks due to a combination of factors, including poverty, limited access to livelihood opportunities, limited availability of public services and inadequate infrastructure and markets. Recent assessment findings confirmed the presence of child protection issues in host communities, with 6% of assessed households reporting at least one member under the age of 18 already married or about to get married; 8% reporting the presence of at least one child working; and 52% of working children reportedly engaged in hazardous work for more than 40 hours per week and working with machinery/ lifting heavy objects. Moreover, 7% of recently assessed host community households reported relying on children to collect firewood; exposing them to further protection risks. Recreational space for children in host communities is limited and the pressure on limited space attributed by some host community members to the large number of refugees has been reported as a factor contributing to inter-community tensions.

Activities or outputs

SO 2: Promote a community-based approach to the response, support community self-protection mechanisms and facilitate meaningful access to specialized services for persons at heightened protection risk, including girls, boys, women and men of all ages and with diverse needs and vulnerabilities, with the ultimate aim of mitigating exposure to protection risks, strengthen the resilience of affected communities, and place communities at the center of the response in line with AAP principles. Output 2.1: Community based child protection mechanisms are established and functioning to create a protective environment for boys and girls.2.1.1. Support and mentor existing Community Based Child Protection Committees (CBCPCs) in their work to advance the protection of children through monitoring, identifying, responding, mitigating and referring children cases in their blocks in the camps and the host communities.2.1.2 Strengthen the adolescent and youth clubs through mentorship, life skills training and discussion sessions on child protection in both the host community and the camp.2.1.3. Provide training and raise awareness on child protection to parents/caregivers, community members including community and religious leaders, provide them with key messages on risks facing children in the host community and in the camp.2.1.4. Strengthen Disaster Risk Reponses (DDR) capacities among adolescents and youth including projects that target inclusive, social cohesion and strive for sustainability.2.1.5. Strengthen the Community Based Child Protection Complaint mechanism for monitoring child protection risks and to develop community led intervention plan.2.1.6. Explore opportunities for increased community ownership including possibility of handing over activities as appropriate based on developed exit strategy. SO 4: Boys and girls including adolescents facing life-threatening risks of abuse, neglect, violence, exploitation, and severe distress have access to well-coordinated and gender-sensitive quality child protection services by 2022.Output 4.1: Boys and girls have improved access to child protection services including FTR, psychosocial support, GBV and referral to services that address their protection needs.4.2.1. Identify, support and refer children with protection concerns/at risk of abuse, including survivors of gender-based violence (GBV), children with specific needs, unaccompanied and separated children.4.2.2. Strengthen psychosocial support in the child friendly spaces and multi-purpose centers to enhance children resilience and coping mechanism.4.2.3. Capacity building and knowledge enhancement of staff and department of social services, police, military and other stakeholders on case management and response initiative while dealing with children survivors of violence, child trafficking and early marriage.4.2.4. Mainstream and integrate protection with Education sector to ensure that adolescent girls and boys have access to life skills and individual case management services as well as WASH to ensure access and prioritization of safety and dignity in their implementation.
