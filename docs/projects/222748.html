Project Summary

The purpose of this project is to protect the livelihoods of rural and urban populations, as well as returnees and improve food security through the provision of emergency agriculture support during the main planting and dry season (crop and vegetable seeds, tools, fishing kits, livestock vaccines and drugs), strengthen absorption capacity and build resilience at the household and community level, and support food security information, analysis and coordination.

1. Needs

Although South Sudan has been independent for thirteen years, the country continues to face ongoing and violent conflicts at the local and subnational levels. South Sudan has experienced a large displacement of people from Sudan, most of whom are highly vulnerable and likely to exert pressure on resources in their re-settlement areas. The November 2024 IPC analysis projects that 7.7 million people, or 57% of South Sudan's population, will face crisis-level or worse acute food insecurity (IPC Phase 3+) during the lean season (April-July 2025). Of these, 63,000 are expected to experience Catastrophe (IPC Phase 5). The worst conditions will occur in Unity, Upper Nile, Jonglei, Northern Bahr el Ghazal, Warrap, Lakes, and Central Equatoria, with over 50% of their populations at risk. Particularly severe conditions (IPC Phase 5) are anticipated in Pibor and Uror (Jonglei), Malakal (Upper Nile), and among 31,000 returnees nationwide. This food insecurity is driven by economic crises, violence, flooding, dry spells, and the Sudan crisis, which disrupt supply chains and worsen vulnerabilities. Agriculture plays a pivotal role in South Sudan, serving as a crucial source of livelihood and income. The sector accounts for approximately 10 percent of the nation’s GDP and serves as a source of livelihood for about 80 percent of the rural population. This significance is anticipated to grow, considering the pressing need for economic diversification from the current over-reliance on oil production that has been jeopardised by the ongoing conflict in Sudan and associated risks to the oil pipeline that runs through the country to Port Sudan. South Sudan’s agriculture sector is characterized by traditional practices, is rainfed, and is vulnerable to climate change-related shocks and disasters; for example, in 2021, an estimated 800 000 livestock and 38 000 tonnes of cereals were lost to flooding . Additionally, over the past twenty years, slow-onset climate change impacts have eroded cereal crop yields by 11-26 percent; a process that is expected to continue, with yields of the major cereals projected to decline a further 17-25% by 2050. Of the 33 million acres of prime arable land in South Sudan, only 4 percent is currently being cultivated with the country’s net cereal production hitting an estimated 1 014 000 tonnes in 2023 . The cereal gap of 388 250 tonnes is one of the key drivers of food insecurity and malnutrition and is compounded by endemic poverty, conflict and insecurity, the economic crisis, climatic shocks (floods and/or prolonged dry spells), and low private sector participation in economic development that has resulted in limited employment opportunities for the majority of the population. The most recent analysis indicates that food insecurity and malnutrition continue to be high in South Sudan, driven by climatic shocks and disasters, low agricultural production, the economic crisis, and limited employment opportunities, among others. Support to livelihoods and food security is vital to South Sudan’s future, and FAO will continue to work closely with humanitarian and development partners to assess shifting needs across the country, throughout 2025.
