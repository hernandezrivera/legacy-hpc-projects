Project Summary

Women often endure extreme hardships, such as increased violence and insecurity, restricted mobility and additional care, domestic and livelihood responsibilities during crises. It has been reported that during natural disasters the likelihood of rape, sexual exploitation and risky behaviour greatly increases the likelihood of unwanted pregnancies, sexually transmitted infections and complications regarding reproductive health. Studies have also shown that women disproportionately suffer the impacts of drought because of cultural norms and the inequitable distribution of roles, resources and power. According to the 2016 ZIMVAC Supplementary Gender Report more female headed households (4.5%) reported experiencing marital and other social disharmony/gender-based violence when compared to male headed holds (1.9%). 56.3% of the female headed households compared to 39.8% male headed households were unable to recover from the impact of marital and other social disharmony or gender-based violence. Drought has a significant impact on securing household water, food, and fuel. The 2016 ZIMVAC Supplementary Gender report revealed that food insecurity was higher among female headed households at 36% compared to 29% male headed households. During a drought situation access to water and sanitation for women and girls is essential for menstrual hygiene. Drought increases the burden on women and girls as they assume additional responsibilities as heads of households. This creates additional vulnerability to sexual exploitation and abuse, exposure to HIV, early/forced marriages, and trafficking. The 2016 Supplementary Gender Report noted that 20.8% of female-headed households reported that they had received no humanitarian support compared to 14.6% male headed households.
UN Women will proactively work with humanitarian actors to ensure their emergency response plans adequately integrate gender equality and women’s empowerment; that women and girls are equally consulted to understand and address their needs and vulnerabilities; that women’s leadership is leveraged; that gender is integrated into humanitarian assessments, reporting and monitoring tools; and that gender experts are included in the humanitarian teams responding to the crisis. UN Women will be working specifically to ensure the protection of the most vulnerable populations, especially women and girls from exploitation and gender-based violence which often increases during emergencies. UN Women will thus focus on affected districts that are high transient areas and known to have high levels of transactional sex. It will facilitate mobilization and information efforts targeting at risk women and girls. Moreover, the Entity will coordinate UN efforts to integrate gender within the response, support the collection of sex-disaggregated data and support the training of the different humanitarian actors to ensure compliance to the humanitarian code of conduct on sexual exploitation and abuse. Within the prioritised locations, UN Women will specifically work to strengthen the capacity of community protection systems led by women’s groups to provide urgently needed leadership and support the overall humanitarian assessment and response in a manner that ensures that the needs of women, men, boys and girls, especially those most disadvantaged, are equitably considered. It will also establish women’s groups for monitoring sexual exploitation, abuse and trafficking of persons in the communities. Gender sensitive men will also be recruited to promote active engagement of women in the response as well mobilizing communities through gender-based violence prevention and awareness activities at the community level in targeted communities. Community outreach and information will be promoted to inform the communities especially women and girls of their rights and services available.

Needs

• Data on differential impact of drought on women and menLack of adequate gender analysis based on accurate and timely sex and age disaggregated data and consultation with women on forms of assistance and protection they need and the role they can play undermines the effectiveness of humanitarian response. There is need to gather evidence on the differential impacts of drought on women and men. The 2016 Supplementary Gender Reports recommended the further exploration of links between GBV and food insecurity within households and exploring the vulnerability of women and girls to sexual exploitation and transactional sex during humanitarian crisis, exploring the intersectionality with GBV and HIV.• Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse, survival sex and GBV• Safe access to assistance• Right to participation and decision makingAlthough there is growing recognition of the importance of women’s participation in humanitarian action, women’s leadership roles as early responders and promoters of community resilience continues to be underutilized. In rural communities women are deprived of having a voice when it comes to decision making power. They are underrepresented in community politics and thus have little influence over community strategies for response. Research shows that when women are involved in prevention and crisis response, it leads to better outcomes and lowers risk.
