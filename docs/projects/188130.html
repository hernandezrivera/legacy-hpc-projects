Project Summary

UNFPA will continue to reduce maternal and newborn morbidity and mortality, support IPC during COVID-19, provide family planning (FP) commodities, and provide medical assistance and care to GBV survivors, including CMR. This will be achieved by (1) ensuring the availability of, access to, and utilization of integrated quality SRH services and information for women, men, girls, and boys; (2) building the capacity of health workers and CHWs; (3) reactivating and activating services in areas where no SRH services exist; (4) ensuring availability of essential medical, PPE, and supplies while mitigating COVID-19 and other infection risks; (5) ensuring delivery of complementary comprehensive services, including for MHPSS and non-communicable diseases; and (6) strengthening referral mechanisms and improving national and subnational SRH coordination.
UNFPA will continue to follow the Whole of Syria approach, with implementation from Damascus and cross-border from Turkey, targeting all 14 Syrian governorates. Vulnerable population groups targeted include women and girls of reproductive age, IDPs, host communities, returnees, adolescents, and PwDs.
Key objectives include:
Increasing access to lifesaving and life-sustaining coordinated, equitable health services for the most vulnerable and in need persons.
Increasing access to primary healthcare by providing essential primary health services to cover the reproductive, maternal, newborn, and child health needs of vulnerable populations and repairing facilities. Improving access to secondary and tertiary healthcare for high risk pregnancy, malnourished pregnant and lactating women (PLW), EmONC cases, and acute cases which cannot access treatment in their area, particularly rural areas.
Supporting access in areas with service gaps through integrated mobile teams and the midwifery network in Aleppo and Deir-ez-Zor.
Building the capacity of health workers and CHWs on health and GBV principles, COVID-19 IPC and treatment, and to address any technical gaps.
Supporting essential infrastructure maintenance and equipment repair and supply to health facilities, in particular for areas of most acute need (directly or through referrals).
Strengthening and consolidating medical supply chains, ensuring availability of safe and quality medicines for essential primary healthcare, IPC supplies and PPE, obstetric care, infectious diseases including HIV to prevent mother-to-child transmission, and FP methods.
Pre-positioning emergency supply stocks of RH kits, medicines, and supplies in line with contingency scenarios for reduced access and increased hostilities.
Strengthening health sector coordination and HIS, emphasizing enhancing protection, increasing access to health services, and continuing coordination within and across hubs, including thorough joint contingency and preparedness planning.
Engaging in activities that support continuity of care, strengthen community resilience, respond to IDP movements and shifts in control, and beneficiaries’ participation in programming.
Supporting resilience through the midwifery schools in NWS and Deir-ez-Zor, and empowering and supporting the full implementation of DHIS2 in NWS and later at the national level.
Adapting, updating, and adopting the national curriculum for non-physician clinicians, including nurses and midwives, with the MoH, and developing training packages for academic and practical training, which can be subject for potential accreditation.
Continuous monitoring of implementation based on the five AAP principles and UNFPA’s global results-based monitoring framework. UNFPA staff or third party monitors and IPs will jointly conduct monthly supervision visits, subject to access and approvals. Standardized tools including the supported services and supervision checklist are used to assess service availability/quality. Clinic registration data will be collated monthly. Beneficiary feedback (complaints box, FGDs, exit interviews) will be collected.

4 - Needs

Health needs are exacerbated by access constraints, COVID-19, facility damage, economic deterioration, insecurity and conflict, displacement, and insufficient staff, contributing to heightened risk of child labour, GBV, early/forced marriage, and other forms of exploitation. More than 40% of health facilities and institutions are partially or fully damaged. The number of clinical health workers is less than 11 per 10,000 people in almost half of all sub-districts (GHO 2022), half of the emergency standards. Limited CMR services is identified as a service gap by health providers and communities. Of the 12.2m people in need of health assistance, WRA are a particularly vulnerable group, making up 28% of the 2022 Health PiN, including 288,010 expected pregnant women (2022 HNO). In some rural areas, health services are up to 100km away, and transportation is often unavailable and unaffordable. In NWS, up to 25% of deliveries will be via C-section. ANC services target 20% of girls below age 18 in NWS and 5% in GOS; of those in GOS, 20% have severe anemia (UNFPA-supported facilities). The 10 MoH nursing schools graduate 300 nurses and 45 midwives annually. Around 4,077 midwives are working in the public sector in all governorates (CBS, 2019), the majority work in central cities, leaving gaps. In NWS, each midwife should cover about 2,400 WRA. Breast cancer incidence has increased from 30% in 2015 to 39% in 2020, and cancer screening and treatment is insufficient.

5 - Implementation of activities

UNFPA prioritizes improved maternal and newborn health through integrated SRH and GBV services, supporting 17 CEmONCs, 14 BEmONCs, 85 PHCs and 124 IMTs in GOS and NES; and 5 CEmONCs, 1 BEmONC and 1 mobile clinic in NWS. UNFPA provides partial support to 923 MoH PHCs; capacity building; essential RH equipment, medicines, and supplies; strengthens referral mechanisms to specialized health, SRH and GBV services; and improved coordination.UNFPA IPs implement the MISP, supporting the EHSP. UNFPA ensures social inclusion of PwDs and older persons, providing transport, accessible facilities and mobile clinics. UNFPA builds capacity on lifesaving protocols; C-sections; CMR; cancer screening; MISP; MHM; GBV risk mitigation/referral; and COVID-19 service adaptations. UNFPA builds resilience through midwifery schools, updating the national midwifery curriculum, and rolling out DHIS2 in NWS. UNFPA Syria will continue to partner with WFP on the e-voucher programme to support vulnerable PLW (150,403 reached in 2021), who can purchase food and hygiene items and access RH and GBV services. UNFPA will explore expanding this partnership in NWS. UNFPA ensures meaningful participation of beneficiaries throughout the programme cycle, monitored through AAP and UNFPA’s framework. UNFPA refers to different levels of facilities, including EmONCs and GBV facilities; patients may be referred to advanced services not available through UNFPA. All health facilities have referral tools to track patients.

9 - Protection Risk Analysis

Violence against Health Facilities and Healthcare Providers: • Increase advocacy efforts, by the health sector, with all parties to the conflict to engage them on health protection topics and respect of international humanitarian law. • Monitor and report on the health situation in reconciled areas. • Consult with UNDSS, the health sector, and local communities in the event of escalating violence regarding how to safely respond.Gender Based Violence: • Prevention and management of sexual and intimate partner violence is a life-saving intervention. Affected populations should have access to the RH services of the Minimum Initial Service Package as part of the essential health package. • Train specialized health staff to provide first-line support, CMR, identification of GBV, and safe referrals. • UNFPA and GBV partners have identified safe referral pathways to ensure comprehensive management of GBV cases connecting health services and clinical management with social services, MHPSS, in a manner which guarantees safety, confidentiality, and privacy of the survivor. • Recruit female staff in health facilities in order to increase access of women and girls to health services, and provide integrated, accessible, private services.Provision of Healthcare for Vulnerable Groups and Equitable Access to Health Services: • Inclusion of vulnerable and minority group needs throughout the program cycle through gender, age, and disability disaggregated analysis of beneficiaries


10 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

UNFPA enhances the prevention and mitigation of protection risks through mainstreaming the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) into all activities, where UNFPA’s partners integrated PSEA policy into the code of conduct for the facilities staff. Risks: - The COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated PSEA risks for displaced populations. - The deteriorating economic situation inside Syria has led to more human rights abuses, including SEA. - Ongoing conflict enables a context where SEA cases are inadequately addressed. Mitigation: - UNFPA organizes specific trainings on PSEA for staff and IPs- UNFPA conducts PSEA assessments of all its IPs and supports them in developing and validating a PSEA policy. - UNFPA has established a hotline to report any SEA related incidents. - UNFPA has a zero tolerance policy with regards to SEA. UNFPA ensures that implementing partners apply the following:1. Embed PSEA into policies, contracts, and code of conduct; in the health facilities UNFPA ensures that all HFs staff are aware and signed the codes of conduct.2. Embed PSEA into MEAL toolkits; UNFPA is committed to integrating the PSEA and protection mainstreaming components into the design of all monitoring tools, which is applied by the TPM.3. Enhance the awareness of both the HF staff and the beneficiaries regarding the prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse through displaying PSEA posters in the supported health facilities.


11 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks: Escalation of violence targeting of health facilities, increasing displacement and limiting access to services. Lack of access makes it difficult to monitor programs and provide services in insecure areas. Large population displacements overburden partners. Weak and insufficient partners in Syria can require more resources to ensure quality services. Partners have restricted movements, relying on approval from authorities. Staffing limitations from delayed Visa/long recruitment procedures, increased outward migration and internal displacement of service providers. High turnover of staff, particularly key government staff, results in the delay of activities. Procurement risks as sanctions make it difficult to procure locally and long lead time of international procedures affect delivery. Fraud/Aid Diversion as the economy deteriorates. COVID-19. Lack of acceptance of sexual terminology in SRH programs, as sexual health is a taboo subject and is often not prioritized in emergencies. Potential non-renewal of cross border resolution will affect services in NWS. UNFPA maintains strong contingencies for the above risks and is leading efforts to develop contingency plans within RH/GBV coordination groups, including adapting to changes in access through regular monitoring of partner activities and the security situation in its areas of operation via UNFPA staff and third parties. It also includes the establishment of UNFPA’s own internal risk management framework.
