Project Summary


Over the past year the education sector supported over 160,552 refugee children to access education. A total of 35% (55,744) of children are enrolled in the Learning Competency Framework and Approach (LCFA) Level I (children aged 3-5 years), and 64% (102,753) in Level II (6-14 years). Furthermore, the sector developed intermediary materials for Level I and Level II, recruited over 3,208 teachers, provided foundation teaching and learning as well as constructed 2,077 learning spaces. As part of the overall response, UNHCR and partners directly guaranteed access to over 40,820 learners (20,745 girls and 20,075 boys). The interventions included the construction of 344 learning spaces in 16 camps, recruitment, training, and deployment of 842 (424 male and 418 female) teachers. Development, production and distribution of teaching and learning materials as well as mobilizing and working with over 673 School Management Committee Members to facilitate and ensure community engagement and involvement in education services delivery. Furthermore, UNHCR supported Disaster Risk Management (DRR) activities in schools by training teachers, forming school-based DRR committees, and disseminating critical messages among parents, teachers, and students. Additionally, in collaboration with partners UNHCR established six Community Technology Access (CTA) centers in six different high schools, college and social institution targeting 3,660 host community children and youth.
It is noteworthy that in Bangladesh since the 1990s, UNHCR and partners made significant progress in advocating for the use of the national curriculum which has been taught in schools in the registered camps since 2007 and access was extended to secondary levels in recent years, with classes up to grade 8 by 2016. Certification was however still not provided, and children do not sit for exams. Nonetheless, the system provides structured teaching and learning using materials vetted and approved by the National Curriculum and Text Book Board (NCTB) with predictable progression essential for academic stimulation.
As part of JRP 2019, UNHCR will expand its education interventions in collaboration with partners in 2019. Through the sector UNHCR seeks to strengthen and expand equitable quality lifelong learning opportunities aligned with the education sector standards, Ministry of Primary and Mass Education (MoPME) as well as Ministry of Education (MoE) standards. Moreover, UNHCR will seek to expand its intervention through the maintenance of 464 existing education facilities (344 classrooms and 120 home based learning center), constructing 242 classrooms with adequate Water Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) facilities, establish 418 home base learning facilities, recruit 958 teachers and facilitators, train 1800 teachers and establish 280 community education committees. Further effort shall take a series of project targeted at adolescents and youth through Massive Open Online Courses (MOOC), engaging peer and volunteer in youth clubs in non-formal education programming and addressing lack of skills training opportunities. Emphasis will be given to enhance school community governance and participation mechanisms, including risk reduction activities; integrate school community-based participatory planning and community awareness. Additionally, host community children also will be benefited through classrooms renovation in host community schools and continuing Community Technology Access (CTA) services.

Objectives

As part of the 2019 JRP, UNHCR’s overall strategy seeks to strengthen its partnerships with national, international non-governmental organizations, government entities as well as academic institutions to optimize complementary capabilities as enshrined in the Global Refugee Compact and the Solidarity Approach for the Rohingya refugees. Specifically the project seeks to: 1. Expand and strengthen immediate access to equitable learning opportunities, in a safe and protective environment, to crisis-affected refugee and host2. Improve the quality of teaching and learning for refugee and host community children and youth, aligned with Ministry of Primary and Mass Education (MoPME), Ministry of Education (MoE) and education Sector standards, and increase teaching-related professional development opportunities3. Increase refugee and host community participation and engagement in the education of children and youth.

Needs

Over 47% (253,000) of the children aged 3-14 years still have no access to education and up to 98% (117,400) of adolescents and youth (15-24 years) are out of school. Despite the need for 4,984 Learning Centres (based on the October 2018, Education Sector Gap Analysis) sector partners have only established to date established 2,077 centers (with an additional 178 under construction). Furthermore, according to the Joint Education Needs Assessment (JENA) in June 2018, and the UNHCR concluded Multi-Sector Needs Assessment (MSNA) in July 2018, at the Early Childhood Development (ECD)/Learning Competency Framework and Approach (LCFA) Level I (aged 3-5 years), there are more girls than boys participating in Learning Centers. However, the number of girls in other levels- LCFA level II is less than boys with the gender gap widening. Similar findings emerged during the UNHCR initiated Madrasah Education Survey in August 2018, where at a younger age, there are more girls than boys taking part in the Madrasah (3-5 yrs. 47% girls; 35% boys, 6-14yrs 50% girls; 46%, 15-18yrs. 3% girls; 14% boys) but significantly decreasing as they grow older.Moreover, current education services address the education competency needs of learners at grade two level, leaving out a significant majority (up to 97%) of adolescents and youth. The situation is further complicated by the low levels of teacher competencies with the majority either under-qualified or unqualified, with insufficient ability in basic child-centered methodologies as well as content matter mastery, leading to poor learning outcomes. Additionally, many national non-governmental organizations (NGOs) with the country and local context knowledge support the response; however, very few of the organizations have experience supporting education in emergencies (EiE) or for displaced population. As such, the need for continued capacity development remains a critical concern. On the other hand, international non-governmental organizations (INGO) undergo difficult statutory accreditation processes, often facing significant delays limiting the scope of the response. The above notwithstanding, concerns regarding the adequacy and appropriateness of the available education services for the refugees is increasingly emerging from the community. For instance, during community meetings, concerned parents have asked progression pathways and opportunities available and why their children were only allowed to study languages, basic mathematics, and life skills. During focus group discussions (FGD) held with youth as well as feedback obtained through the adolescent clubs the desire to obtain functional practical skills remains apparent. The value of appropriate education is obvious, but more so situations of displacement. Meaningful education services serve as a fundamental protection tool. Moreover, learners acquire essential skills, both academic as well as well as life skills such as disaster risk reduction (DRR), skills that promote psycho-social well-being, health and hygiene promotion, as well as awareness on vital elements on environmental protection, as well as the development individual and collective resilience. Education also forms a core component of the durable solutions for refugees. Skilled refugees not only play an active role as essential partners in the humanitarian response but are also better placed to contribute to their host communities. They also play a critical role in rebuilding their communities upon return. As such, a lack of appropriate educational opportunities presents a real danger of a lost generation. Additionally, unengaged adolescents and youth are predisposed to significant protection risks including worst forms of child labor, forced marriages, sexual gender-based violence, the risk of human trafficking, negative coping such as drug abuse among others hordes of risks.

Activities or outputs

UNHCR seeks to strengthen and expand equitable quality lifelong learning opportunities aligned with the education sector, Ministry of Primary and Mass Education (MoPME) as well as Ministry of Education (MoE) standards. UNHCR will continue extending technical support and contribute towards the overall improvement of education services for refugees and host community children, co-leading the Strategic Advocacy Group (SAG), facilitating retention of learners already enrolled and expanding access to reach a total of 87,803 children and youth aged 3-24 years in Kutupalong Registered Camp (RC), Camp 1E, Camp 1W, Camp 2E, Camp 2W, Camp-3, Camp 4, Camp 5, Camp 6, Camp 7, Camp 4 (EXT), Camp 17, Nayapara RC, Camp 21, Camp 26 and Camp 27. UNHCR will expand physical access through renovation and maintenance of an existing 344 classrooms and constructing of 242 new classrooms with gender segregated and adequate Water Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) facilities. Additionally, UNHCR will recruit 958 teachers and facilitators for new learning facilities. Support host community schools by renovating classrooms, toilets and continuing support for ongoing Community Technology Access (CTA) centers to benefit 21,000 students. Procure school, teachers, and students kits for 1,004 learning spaces. Moreover, to enhance teaching learning processes and facilitate quality education, in-service teachers and learning facilitators shall undergo continuous teacher training and newly recruited teachers will be enrolled on basic teacher training. UNHCR will work closely with the government to strengthen pedagogical approaches for teachers already in-service but also newly recruited through a standardized teacher training strategy based on the assessed teachers’ skills profile to provide suitable education and create a positive and safe learning environment. Additionally there will be intensive English language programs targeting educators to assist with English language learning, with additional an focus placed on the English language as medium of instruction in the camps. Improving teaching and learning materials developed in 2018 is a priority, ensuring that both the core and supplementary materials meet sector standards but also address cross-cutting issues including positive gender relations, promoting positive conflict resolution, raising awareness on environmental protection and fundamental protection principles. Efforts will include foundation skills training for 1,300 teachers, training on psychosocial support (PSS), disaster risk reduction and management (DRR) and sanitation and hygiene (S&H) , continuous teachers professional development (monthly refreshers, cluster meeting, peer support meeting etc) activities for 1,800 teachers and functional English literacy training for 1,600 teachers. Efforts to provide training to understand and help mainstream gender-sensitive and inclusive education principles for all teachers is a priority. Attention to teachers’ code of conduct (CoC) in addition to critical Education in Emergencies (EiE) initiatives, including PSS, sanitation and hygiene (S&H)promotion, DRM as well as provision of extracurricular activities to ensure that learning environments remain both socio-emotionally protective and safe is key. A series of pilot projects will target adolescents and youth through Massive Open Online Courses (MOOC) to address lack of skills training. Other efforts shall include engaging peers and volunteers in youth clubs to support adolescent and youth non-formal education programming for host communities and refugees (including peacebuilding, leadership, and voluntarism). Enhance school community governance and participation mechanisms, including risk reduction activities, integrate school community-based participatory planning and community awareness. Emphasis will be on targeted training, activities promoting peaceful co-existence and social cohesion such as co-curricular activities and community events.
