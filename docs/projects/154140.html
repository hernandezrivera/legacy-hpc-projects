Project Summary

Conflict affected boys and girls in the central and the northern Mali have access to and receive quality education that links in to the wider humanitarian response and that is empowering and reduces inequalities. Project targets 5,000 children that will have access to non-formal education and 26,000 children into formal schools. Project will support 500 teachers and 3,500 parents including members of school management committees.

Needs

Needs The education system is highly affected by the crisis. During the school year 2017/2018, it was reported that 735 schools (primary and secondary education) in the center and the north of Mali (for Gao: 67, for Timbuktu: 88 and for Mopti: 464) were closed (Education Cluster, May 2018). Compared to beginning of 2017, it is reported that this year (2018), 235 new schools have closed in the center and the North of Mali (resulting in an increase of 70,000 more out-of-school children compared to a baseline of 150,000 out-of-children at the beginning of 2017). It is important to note that in certain cercles of Mopti the situation is even more difficult for children; 17% of the schools in the cercle of Tenenkou, 19% in the cercle of Mopti, 16% in the cercle of Koro and 23% in the cercle of Douentza were reported closed. As a result of these closures in the northern and central regions, over 220,000 children (51% of girls) were driven out of the school system, and approximatively 3,000 teachers have left the affected areas (Education Cluster, HNO 2018). Moreover, the return of teachers is scarce in these regions. The absenteeism of qualified teachers, a consequence of insecurity and lack of infrastructure, has heavily affected the quality of the education and the school attendance. The qualified teachers, recruited by way of the Ministry of Education, are as such acknowledged as representative of the state and hence can be targeted by non-state armed groups. Indeed, most of the non-state armed groups are opposed to the principles of formal education. Meanwhile, among the formal schools, which remain open, there exist substantial deficiencies in overall capacity, contributing to absenteeism. Formal schools which continue to operate in Mali suffer a lack of community involvement, deteriorated infrastructure, deficits of learning and pedagogic materials and a lack of school canteens; a phenomenon in and of itself which contributes heavily to absenteeism in the Malian context. Formal schools open in areas of refuge; communes which are considered safe and attract high numbers of IDPs are often over-crowded and unable to accommodate the quantity of children in the area. These schools are thereby unable to act as the host-schools for accelerated education programs, which are functioning in temporary learning spaces nearby, accommodating out of school children affected by conflict and displacement. Children are severely psychologically affected by the conflict. Child protection issues in school environments as well in the communities (corporal and/or physical punishment, early and forced marriage, sexual violence, absence of birth certificate inhibiting enrollment of children into schooling, child exploitation and gender-based violence) are negatively affecting the quality of education, school enrolment, and attendance, in particular for girls. There is an urgent need to reopen schools and give children, parents and teachers an opportunity to engage in child-friendly educational environments. School management committees including student mothers’ associations are designed to support the education of their children. In the current context, they are unable to uphold their responsibilities, influence the quality of learning or ensure that schools and leaning spaces are protective environments. NRC’s emergency education response in Mali seeks to provide boys and girls with life-saving and life-sustaining education activities in response to the effects of the conflict crisis. Across all impact areas, activities have been informed by the INEE Minimum Standards for Education and are in-line with the government strategy and with the NRC Education policy. Program activities have been developed in coordination with Education Cluster strategy, as well as other NRC program sectors to ensure maximum integration of the emergency response.

Activities

Result 1: Children, teachers and community access diverse opportunities of quality and relevant education• Develop accelerated education classes for out-of-school children • Train teachers on psychosocial support module• Train animators from accelerated education classes• Support catch-up classes• Distribute of students kits, recreational kits and teacher kits• Develop community learning approach• Rehabilitate and equip schools as needed (providing school materials, didactic materials, school desks, school manuals, etc.)• Establish and equip temporary learning spaces with appropriate WASH facilities (including separate latrines for boys and girls) Result 2: Communities and local partners support education activities and develop a resiliency for future crises• Engage all sectors of communities (both host and displaced) throughout the program planning and implementation • Work with school and Committees of school management to establish remedial classes and tutoring, mentoring, activities to keep children engaged in learning, and involve them in monitoring of learning activities and child referrals• Promote community based efforts to restore access and to provide safety from violence, particularly among marginalized groups including girls, people with disabilities and minorities• Facilitate the re establishment of Children’s Governments to promote dissemination of key messages of education, monitoring of school activities, and development of contingency plans• Conduct social cohesion and culture of peace activities
