Project Summary

The project is to provide life-saving emergency healthcare to an estimated 70,000 crises affected beneficiaries in Bay, Bakool and Hiraan regions. This action will deliver comprehensive reproductive health care to women of reproductive age among the target beneficiaries and promotion of health seeking behaviour including adoption of preventive practices through campaigns, health and hygiene education sessions. Emphasis will be on maternal, neonatal and child health including emergency immunization to the host communities and Internally Displaced People (IDPs). This action will focus on provision of both preventive and curative health services at primary level integrated with nutrition and WASH activities to provide holistic and integrated support. Through deployment of trained health workers including midwives and community health workers in facilities and mobile teams, essential primary care services including treatment of common childhood illnesses, as well as emergency obstetric and neonatal care for pregnant and lactating women will be provided. HIDIG an actor in the target regions will leverage on existing support & activities to ensure the gains made in the post-drought response are not rolled back. Building the capacity of health workers through training on Basic Emergency Obstetric Care (BEmONC) will be done to equip health workers with the required skills to deliver quality health care services to the affected population. The project with the technical guidance of WHO and the Health Cluster will also integrate surveillance and emergency preparedness in the program so that enough steps are taken in ensuring prompt response. The project will also contribute to strengthening early warning disease surveillance through weekly submission of eDEW reports from all the functional supported health facilities. Referral system for patients seeking secondary healthcare services to Baidoa and Beletweyne regional referral hospitals will be strengthened focusing on pregnant women requiring emergency services, and GBV cases referred to secondary care immediately.

Needs

According to the 2019 Somalia HRP, more than 3 million people need lifesaving emergency healthcare services. The lasting impact of the 2017 pre-famine situation is still evident. This has increased a burden on a population with already poor health, leading to increased levels of malnutrition across the country. Somalia still has one of the highest fertility rates in the world and every two hours a mother dies due to complication of pregnancy (732 deaths/ 100,00 live births). Out of 1,300 children born every day, 45 are still born which equals 16,425 deaths yearly. IDPs, both long-standing and newly displaced, as well as civilians in conflict-affected areas, are among the most vulnerable, with many lacking access to essential basic services such as clean water, sanitation and primary health. Across Somalia, 2.6 million exist, most of them in areas Classified as Crisis (IPC Phase 3). More than 294,000 acutely malnourished children, including 55,200 who are severely malnourished require urgent treatment and nutrition support in Q4 of 2018. A recent health facility found that the health facility density in Somalia is less than 0.74 per 10,000 population [international minimum standard 2/10,000] and medical staffing including nurses, midwives and doctors 4 per 10,000 population [standard 23/10,000]. Health service coverage for the IDPs is poor with only 25% of IDP camps having access to a health facility. With over 2 million people in Somalia currently displaced, this constitutes a major gap in basic primary health care service provision. Since the start of 2018, over 6,424 cases of AWD/cholera have been reported in 22 districts and 7,765 cases of suspected measles. Due to multiple risk factors and highly compromised nutritional status of children under 5, malnourished children are 9 times more susceptible to morbidity. Therefore, sustained humanitarian assistance is needed to prevent and timely address health and malnutrition crisis in these regions.

How is the project linked to durable solutions/resilience?

The project is meant to build community resilience by strengthening local healthcare infrastructure and thus provide timely action, maintaining momentum of the response, and expanding reach in underserved areas promoting equity. Based on the cluster objectives, the response strategy includes the need to deliver a core package of integrated services, where priority will be given to the delivery of life-saving services, while simultaneously addressing underlying causes of exposure to the risk of malnutrition, disease outbreaks and poor sanitation and hygiene. To increase community uptake of provided services, community level demand creation will be strengthened. HIDIG will strengthen facility level quality service delivery by training staff to deliver quality services. Communities will be involved in project designation and will be part of the facility level committees supporting district level health committees. Working with other actors will ensure leverage amongst themselves and providing maximum support to target communities strengthening their capacities in various aspects.

Protection risk analysis & Mitigating measures
Preventing risks of exclusion based on displacement

This project targets areas with high concentration of IDPs including Baidoa and Beletweyne, therefore possible community tensions between IDPs and host communities is a risk for displaced populations who may face animosity from host communities who already feel overburdened and with limited services. With the IDPs living in camps, many of them not legalized, constructing health facilities may be a challenge exposing them to lack of comprehensive and timely care. To overcome these challenges, HIDIG will ensure IDPs have access to a comprehensive package of health services within the IDP camps by establishing health facilities in consultation with the host communities and health authorities. Where not possible outreach and mobile teams will provide needed services. To promote communal cohesion, host communities will be provided with access to camp health facilities without any impediment. Based on the Health Cluster Guideline on inclusion, both displaced and host communities will be consulted about health needs and assess whether health interventions could cause tension, harassment or conflict between the IDPs and host communities.

Preventing risks of exclusion based on gender and age

Lack of desegregated data impedes well planned interventions based on needs. HIDIG intends to overcome this by having a need based assessment and disaggregated data so that the special needs of aged people and those with special gender needs are taken care of. Due to poor health infrastructure, many facilities do not have proper planning so that aged patients and those with disabilities can easily seek services. To overcome this, HIDIG will ensure that our facilities can support aged patients and those with disability. Our referral system will promote assisted delivery of services to ease their challenges. Sexual and reproductive health needs of women of child bearing age will be prioritized. Also, services will be ensured that they reach the most vulnerable through identification and prioritization so that discrimination and exclusion is avoided. Location, distance and access routes to the health facilities and services will be accessible to all members of the community, including the most vulnerable groups. To support women seeking services, female workers will be recruited. To ensure equity, outreach teams will be used to reach rural communities.

Mitigating the impact of armed conflict and violence

Building community structures including local health committees will ensure tension is minimized. Staff will be trained on human rights based approach where all people should be treated equally, especially those who ran from conflict and violence. Communities fleeing violence will be immediately supported through mobile and outreach teams. Where IDPs are involved, facilities that support them will be built in consultation with them and the host communities. Marginalized groups will be prioritized so that they don’t feel excluded, which can otherwise aggravate their situation if they have run from violence.
