Project Summary

The intervention aims to ensure increased civilian population’s security and sustainable returns enabled by protecting lives and civilian infrastructure and productive assets and supporting livelihoods and income-generation. The project focuses on the reduction of impacts by explosive hazards and is a contribution towards a lesser dependency of the population in Al Hasakah Governorate on humanitarian aid and supporting their long-term resilience. The proposed area of operations are former agricultural areas or contaminated arable land in Al Hasakah Governorate, primarily along the Khabur River or to the west of Hasakah City towards the Iraq border, where limited access to vital assets due to explosive hazards contamination is limiting people’s abilities to rebuild their lives. Operations could also expand to other areas, most notably to the south towards Ash Shaddadi and Deir ez Zour, and possibly to the north towards Tal Tamer. Precise locations, however, will depend on the developments in the field, priorities highlighted by local communities/authorities, and complementary activities of other HMA operators in the region.
In order to remove and mitigate the threats against lives and limbs of the civilian population, Team ITF proposes an integrated humanitarian mine action approach in close coordination with the relevant local authorities, communities and other humanitarian actors. The project envisages the continuation of non-technical survey and battle area clearance conducted on suspected or confirmed explosive hazards contaminated former agricultural land or infrastructure in ISIS liberated areas, return of cleared land to local population for their safe and productive use. Estimated at approximately 3.5 million m2 of cleared land in areas assessed as priority in a participatory process with local authorities and communities. These activities shall be augmented through explosive ordnance risk education / community liaison, targeting approximately 5,000 at-risk boys, girls, men and women with safety messaging, and providing the ‘eyes and ears’ for the clearance teams on the ground. Through continuous outreach, the latter shall ensure that the team’s efforts are genuinely community-based and community-focused. When combined, this package of efforts shall help revitalize the social fabric in the community and bring a sense of normalcy to a war-ravaged and impoverished region.
This intervention will build on first-hand knowledge and understanding of the specific local context in the Al Hasakah Governorate, and on experience gained during explosive hazards clearance activities that are being implemented in the region since 2020. The intervention will aim to encompass the ethnic and religious diversity and secure equal opportunities in terms of employment, project implementation structure, and decision-making to all, regardless of their social status or personal background. To the greatest extent practicable, it will specifically address the needs of the most marginalized groups. By aiming to benefit the residents of Al Hasakah Governorate through this specific program, this intervention is prioritizing assistance to the most vulnerable groups, as defined by the 2020 Syria Humanitarian Response Plan, namely 1. People currently living in areas that have seen high intensity hostilities or have high levels of contamination with explosive hazards; and 2. Over-burdened communities, where, due to the large number of IDPs and/or spontaneous returnees and/or prolonged interruption of basic services, the entire population (both host communities and IDPs) is exposed to increased challenges in accessing services, livelihoods, and economic opportunities.

4 - Needs

In Syria, including the Northeast, levels of explosive hazard contamination continue to be considered as very high and even massive, especially in areas where there has been intense fighting, aerial bombardments, and ground shelling like Ar Raqqa, Deir ez Zour, and Al Hasakah. According to the 2020 Syria HRP, 11.5 million civilians live in 2,562 communities reporting explosive hazards in Syria. UNMAS reports that the destruction or contamination of key infrastructure, such as hospitals, has deprived civilians of basic services, and the presence of explosive hazards is a lethal barrier to movement, the delivery of humanitarian aid, and to those seeking refuge from violence, as well as to those wishing to cultivate their land. In 2018 1,465 mine/explosive remnants of war victims were recorded in Syria by the Landmine and Cluster Munition Monitor from landmines and ERW, though due to underreporting the true figure is thought to be significantly higher. The majority were caused by improvised victim-activated devices or IEDs. In addition to the loss of civilian life, explosive hazards are the cause of life-changing injuries, trauma, loss of livelihoods, for instance through farming, or hindered access to medical provision or education. Further compounding the needs of people in NES, are the COVID-19 pandemic and recent economic collapse, leading to increasing poverty rates and major food insecurity. The project is specifically addressing the needs of farmers and shepherds whose livelihoods have been affected by explosive hazards contamination, preventing the cultivation of arable land.

5 - Implementation of activities

Non-technical survey (NTS) will assess land and provide categorization as a suspected or confirmed hazardous area (SHA/CHA) and will serve as the foundation for subsequent processes of cancelling, reducing or clearing land for productive use. Battle Area Clearance (BAC) teams will be deployed using various methodologies to release land to safe and productive use by local residents. Technical survey (TS) will be the primary method for defining accurately and with confidence the extent of areas that require clearance. TS will be implemented hand-in-hand with BAC as the systematic and controlled clearance of hazardous areas where the risk is known not to include mines, including former military positions and temporary storage ammunition storage positions, or munitions simply dropped or abandoned, munitions that have been primed, fuzed, armed or otherwise pre-pared for use but have not been fired (or fired but failed to function, inclusive of sub-munitions), and booby traps (failed, or abandoned IEDs left behind after hostilities have ceased. Explosive ordnance risk education (EORE) will be embedded with the survey and clearance teams to share safety messaging within local communities. They also take on the role of community liaison (CL), and will work on training a number of local Community Focal Points.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

R1. Presence of explosive hazards limiting access and posing a threat to the physical safety and security to both those delivering and those benefitting form services; R2. Creation of wrong expectations among beneficiaries, who could wrongly attribute to risk education teams the ability of removing explosive ordnance, leading to risk of tensions and frustration between beneficiaries and service providers;R3. Risk of interventions not reaching all vulnerable groups because of lack of understanding of social norms, work patterns / gender considerations; R4. Familiarization with messages delivered at risk education sessions resulting in better understanding of the explosive hazard threat may lead beneficiaries to become overconfident and engage in risky behaviours.R5: Damage to housing, land or properties or eruption of HLP disputes due to survey/clearance. M1. Delivery of risk education training to humanitarian personnel to increase awareness of the explosive threat; include first aid component in risk education sessions; delivery of training of trainers (ToT) to ensure large scale awareness and sustainability of efforts; M2. Clear explanation of intervention and objectives to beneficiaries; close cooperation between clearance and risk education/community liaison personnel;M3. Involvement of community focal points in the planning and delivery of risk education sessions; tailoring of messages and risk education materials according to the audience; M4. Involvement of survivors of explosive hazards as role model for behaviour change; linking risk education with clearance interventions. M6: Clear explanation of intervention to local communities/beneficiaries; implementation of land remediation when possible.

8 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

R1: Disinterest of local authorities/lack of community acceptance. M1: maintain communication with local authorities and communities, employ lessons learned from previous projects in the area, deployment of community liaison. R2: Inability to ensure relatively safe access of staff to the areas and inability to operate in a manner that does not disproportionately endanger the lives of the project staff. M2: Contingency planning, scenario development for remote management with enhanced capacity mentorship of local staff. R3: Non-compliance of project personnel with SOPs or lower than expected knowledge and competencies of the staff. M3: Re-training provided to staff failing to live up to the expectations; disciplinary processes for repeated failures to comply; feedback and complaint mechanism in place.R4: Deteriorating trajectory of the COVID-19 pandemic, resulting in the imposition of renewed travel bans or lockdowns. M4: Adopting social distancing and other recommended measures during the implementation of activities; strict adherence to health prevention measures by the work team and the provision of appropriate PPE, testing kits; encouraging beneficiaries to use prevention methods and social distancing, and include COVID-19 messaging in activities.R5: Unexpected weather patterns and conditions related to climate change, which may render land clearance activities impossible. M5: Maintaining flexible working patterns and adapting the timing of mine action activities to the weather conditions or pausing activities temporarily (e.g. starting activities earlier in the morning and breaking off during the hottest hours during the day) with flexible work patterns.
