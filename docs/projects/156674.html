Project Summary

The purpose of the Shelter and NFI intervention is to provide life-saving and life-sustaining shelter and NFI support and contribute towards the resilience and cohesion of communities and households by improving housing and related community/public infrastructure in Syria. NRC will respond to the diverse problems and needs faced by vulnerable populations throughout Syria through the delivery of a multi-faceted Shelter response. The different components of NRC’s Shelter project are intended to contribute to HRP Shelter & NFI Sector Objectives 1 and 2.
NRC will deliver this project through a combination of direct, partner-led and hybrid modalities. Partner names have not been included in this submission for reasons of confidentiality
Geographic areas will be targeted based on the severity and scale of needs, inter-agency coordination at hub-level, and access. NRC will provide assistance to vulnerable conflict and displacement-affected populations, including IDPs, refugees, members of host communities, IDP and refugee returnees, and non-displaced households suffering the effects of the conflict. All interventions will seek to prioritize the most vulnerable households. Where appropriate, multi-sectoral vulnerability scoring techniques will be used that integrate vulnerability indicators
In line with the NRC Syria Country Strategy, the Shelter programme will include emergency, transitional and recovery response options. These options are not intended to be “standalone” and may be delivered in conjunction with one another. The proportion of emergency, transitional and recovery related outputs will vary between different parts of Syria, depending on context, needs, access and implementation modality in those locations. Shelter and WASH activities will be delivered as a fully-integrated joint programme where relevant. The implementing modality (direct, partner-led or hybrid) will be defined by the Area Office based on access constraints and NRC/partner capacity.
The Shelter programme will contribute to NRC’s:
- Emergency response through the provision of one-off support as part of an integrated, multi-sector response in accordance with the Emergency Preparedness and Response Plans (EPRPs). Comprises of three Result Areas: Temporary Shelter(with an overall target of 1600 assistance reaching 9600 ind), Household Assets (which includes shelter NFI kits of Blankets, mats, Jerry cans, plastic sheets and kitchen set, the overall target = 7,352 kits reaching 38,360 individuals) and Support to Education in Emergencies.
- Provision of medium-term shelter support at household and settlement-level to populations living in and out-of-camps. Comprises of three Result Areas: Community Infrastructure and Services in Camps, Temporary Shelter and Support to Education Pathways.
- Provision of longer-term assistance at household and settlement-level in urban and peri-urban out-of-camp areas. Comprises of two Result Areas: I-WISH (i.e. Housing Rehabilitation) targeting 976 Households reaching 4,880 ind), and school rehabilitation (see NRC’s Education project submission).
All “permanent” Shelter activities require a full HLP Due Diligence process to verify ownership and usage rights to a reasonable level of certainty.

Needs

The 2019 HNO estimates that a total of 4.7 M people are in need of shelter assistance and 4.4 M people need NFI assistance. This represents an increase of 18% for Shelter and a decrease of 6% for NFIs, relative to the 2018 estimates. Whilst there are needs throughout Syria, inter-agency assessments indicate particularly high scale and severity of needs in Aleppo, Idleb, Rural Damascus, Damascus, Homs and Homs governorates. Amongst IDPs, 64% live in rented accommodation, 10.7% are hosted (with no rent), 9.6% live in camps/ informal settlements, and 6.4% in unfinished or abandoned buildings. However, Shelter conditions vary considerably between the different areas of Syria, as indicated by NRC’s own assessments. In south Syria, 60% of respondents stated that they live with host community members, 23% live in camps/informal settlements and 9% live in some form of collective shelter. However in northwest Syria, NRC identified that 21% of respondents stated that they are living with host families, around 44% are living in camps/informal settlements and 18% were renting private accommodation. Continued displacement, coupled with the economic impact of the crisis & conflict damage have resulted in over-crowding, escalation of rent relative to incomes, occupancy of sub-standard shelters, poor security of tenure, and a lack of access to services in many areas. Findings by WoS Shelter Sector indicate that approximately 7.4 M Syrians cannot afford rent, 1.5 M are hosting other HHs. 5.3 M are living in inadequate housing & 3.7 M are unable to conduct shelter repairs. The World Bank estimates that approximately 7% of housing stock has been destroyed & 25% has been partially damaged. Based on RNAs conducted by NRC’s field teams throughout 2018, many newly IDPs lack core HH assets and are often obliged to live in shelter options of last resort (e.g. camps/informal settlements, collective centres, etc.). These include co-habiting with other HHs in over-crowded conditions and in extreme cases, sleeping in the open without shelter. The NE Syria INGO forum estimates that there are 74,570 individuals (including approximately 24,000 Iraqi refugees) residing in 6 camps in northeast Syria. These camps were established by or with the approval of the Kurdish self-administration. The nature and conditions of the “camps” vary considerably from ad-hoc Informal Settlements housing IDPs in northwest to effectively formal camps hosting a combination of IDPs & Iraqi refugees in northeast Syria. These provide limited climatic protection & privacy and have a limited life-span. FGD and PDMs conducted by NRC have identified a lack of communal lighting of public areas & lack of personal privacy in their shelters as particular protection concern amongst women and girls. In addition to lack of adequate physical access for people with impaired mobility. Despite continued new displacement in parts of Syria,other areas are experiencing consolidated political control & high levels of return. UNOCHA estimates that approximately 987,000 individuals returned to their area of origin between January and August 2018 inclusive and it is anticipated that the number will grow.It’s anticipated that the majority of Syrian refugee returns will be to urban and peri-urban areas. This may accelerate the trend of rapid urbanisation of the Syrian population that was observed prior to 2011 and is considered by some analysts as a component in the socio-economic causes of the conflict. The 2019 HNO estimates that the population in urban areas represents around 76% of the population in Syria – a significant increase relative to pre-crisis. NRC’s assessments found that 41% of IDPs in northwest & 50% of IDPs in south Syria reported that their original residence had been destroyed or damaged beyond repair, whilst only 25% of IDPs in northwest & 9% in south Syria still have their title deed with them & in good condition. Available data indicates that rental costs are higher in urban areas

Implementation of activities

NRC and its partners will contribute to the inter-agency objectives of “Provide life-saving and life-sustaining shelter and NFI support” through the following HRP outputs:1. Targeted crisis-affected households, including those in HTR and Besieged locations, have their core and essential non-food item needs met. This will include the provision of one-off basic assistance through the most appropriate modality to newly displaced and/or highly vulnerable households. This will comprise of a combination of in-kind NFI kits and vouchers/restricted cash. Whilst NRC is considering multi-purpose cash assistance in its 2019 plans, this has been included under other sector submissions. NFIs ONLY.2. Targeted crisis-affected households are provided with timely life-saving and life-sustaining shelter support (medium-term/temporary shelter). This will include the provision of temporary shelter assistance (tents and sealing-off-kits) and medium-term (transitional) shelter assistance (light shelter rehabilitation) to vulnerable households. NRC and its partners will contribute to the inter-agency objective of “Contribute towards the resilience and cohesion of communities and households by improving housing and related community/public infrastructure” through the following HRP outputs:1. Increase in adequate housing stock available to targeted households and communities. This will include the provision of medium/heavy rehabilitation of sub-standard housing units. Where appropriate, this will be delivered using conditional cash based modalities.2. Reduced risk of Housing, Land and Property (HLP) issues emanating out of shelter interventions. NRC/partners will protect its Shelter beneficiary’s security of tenure and contribute to avoiding potential violations of HLP rights through following the HLP Due Diligence process for the relevant Shelter activities. I.e. NRC's Shelter programme will work in collaboration with its ICLA (Information, Counselling and Legal Assistance) department to determine basic information about HLP. This includes understanding: a). Who is the rightful owner of the property? b). How can it be used? c). Who has the right to use it? d). How long can it be used for?NRC will provide Shelter assistance to conflict and displacement-affected populations, including: IDPs, non-displaced and host populations, Syrian IDP or refugee returnees, Iraqi refugees. Priority will be given to the most vulnerable of the affected population living in sub-standard living conditions and in geographic areas where other NRC sector teams are implementing projects. Project site selection will include an analysis of gaps and needs for all response options.Where feasible and appropriate, NRC will use a vulnerability-based scoring system for beneficiary selection for household-level assistance packages, consistent with the Protection Cluster Guidance “Protection, Vulnerability and Prioritising the Most in Need”. This methodology has been developed in collaboration with other NRC sectors. It considers a number of dimensions of vulnerability, including: household composition/demographics (child-headed HHs, female-headed HHs, HHs with disabled members, HHs with elderly members, single-headed HHs, etc.), food consumption, coping strategies, living conditions, etc.NRC/partner projects will incorporate consultation with diverse representatives (i.e. male, female, youth, elderly, etc.) of the affected population throughout the project cycle (i.e. design, implementation, monitoring, etc.). NRC/partner interventions will be adapted to respond to the particular concerns, needs and priorities of men, women, girls, boys and other specific groups (i.e. age and gender).

Protection Risk Analysis

NRC has conducted a Shelter-specific protection risk mapping as part of its 2019 strategy development process. Major identified protection risks include (this is not exhaustive):1. Requirements for formal civil documentation (e.g. IDs) in order to receive humanitarian assistance has been identified as one of the main barriers to equitable access to assistance. 2. Status-based provision of assistance may lead to/exacerbate social tensions and distort consistent needs-based targeting of assistance. 3. Centralised, large-scale distributions create security risks for beneficiaries and staff/partners. Also, they may not be physically accessible for people affected by impaired mobility or those unable to leave the house.4. PDM indicates that in-kind NFI distributions lack appropriate items for women and children and/or the quality/type is inadequate resulting in unmet needs.5. FGDs conducted with camp/informal settlement populations indicates that physical access to/within the site is limited for people with impaired mobility and others unable to leave the family tent, creating a barrier to equitable access to services.6. FGDs indicate a lack of privacy in camps as a major issue, particularly for women and girls. 7. A lack of adequate understanding of HLP rights risks breach of do-no-harm principles and may increase the risk of eviction or other lack of security of tenure for vulnerable populations. 8. NGO activities may have unintended impact on local market (e.g. cost inflation) or contribute to local ‘war economies’ and do harm.9. Unsafe construction practices expose beneficiaries to avoidable risks of physical harm, particularly to children. 10. A lack of gender balance and gender awareness amongst project teams has been identified as a barrier to equitable access to assistance and the provision of gender-sensitive assistance. Mitigation Measures for identified protection risks:1. Alternative means of verification of beneficiary identification will be used, subject to donor compliance requirements. 2. Beneficiary selection will be vulnerability-based, rather than status-based, to ensure that assistance targets the most vulnerable.3. Prioritisation of household-level distributions where feasible and appropriate. Where centralized distribution sites are required, selection will include risk assessment and alternative distribution method for those unable to access centralized distribution sites.4. Where possible, market-based approaches will be used in place of in-kind assistance. In-kind assistance packages will be adjusted based on PDM findings.5. Programming in camps will include consultation with diverse representatives from the affected population. Interventions will take measures to improve physical access where possible.6. Design of shelter interventions will include improving beneficiary privacy as a priority consideration. This will include ensuring tent materials are sufficiently thick to avoid becoming translucent when lit from within and the provision of partition within tents where relevant.7. All relevant interventions will be contingent upon a full HLP due diligence process conducted by specialist staff.8. Systematic market monitoring to understand market dynamics and the impact of programming.9. Code of conduct and training for safe construction practices, based on risk assessment. Plus regular monitoring of construction sites to ensure compliance. In addition, construction works will be phased in order to reduce disruption and risks for the beneficiary population. 10. Project teams will be gender balanced and trained on gender awareness. This includes removing barriers that inhibit the recruitment of women to field teams in particular.11. General: all activities will require the completion of a participation plan (e.g. accountability mechanisms), in addition to protection risk assessment, monitoring and modification as part of the project-cycle."

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1. Escalation of violence and loss of safety and security for staff, partners or beneficiaries: NRC has dedicated Risk and Safety Management staff in the Country who monitor security change triggers and maintain communication with local and regional analysts. All offices maintain updated vulnerability assessments, risk tolerance statements, as well as security plans, for all operational locations. All NRC staff receive proper security and safety training.2. Changes in territorial control. Mitigation measures include good understanding of context, contextual analysis, Security Plans/Security Structure; Community acceptance and good relationship with local authorities; “Red lines” sessions with staff. 3. Large-scale return of refugees and neighbouring states push for large-scale return of refugees to Syria (including coerced return): Collaboration and coordination with other NRC Country programmes in the region. Development of specific contingency plans. Adaptation of programmes. Advocacy efforts.4. Fraud, corruption and bribery: Enforcing NRC’s policy of zero tolerance of corruption through the continuous strengthening of procurement and operating procedures.5. Government permission to operate: Maintain regular and transparent communication with all stakeholders during all stages of the project; continue to build trust and dialogue; cautious and careful liaison work. 6. National/Local authorities withdrawn their approval to conduct activities: Maintain regular and transparent communication with all stakeholders during all stages of the project; continue to build trust and dialogue; cautious and careful liaison work. 7. Interference by armed opposition actors: Continue to engage and meet with relevant actors and ensure the humanitarian imperative and principles of NRC’s work is fully understood. Setting internal redlines and guidance on how to respond to such requests.8. Organisational acceptance: Assessments and initial contact with communities and stakeholders is done, who have good local context understanding and acceptance in addition to well-established networks; principled access. 9. Kidnapping community acceptance: recruitment of national staff and LHPs with networks, communication channels, and contacts in a place where interpersonal relationships are at a premium (trust and acceptance). 10. Availability of qualified staff: Mitigation include keeping the qualified staff and advertising extensively for the new positions to be recruited.
