Project Summary

Improving and sustaining essential, equitable, quality health services at primary and community levels are pivotal for effective response to affected population needs. Protracted crisis has left just 54% of primary health care centers (PHCs) fully functional. Up to 75% of health workers have left Syria and many others were lost to COVID-19, or killed, or injured. At least 50% of the affected population require reproductive, maternal, neonatal, child, and adolescent (RMNCAH) health services with almost 90% of households requiring mental health and psychosocial services (MHPSS) in some areas. Infrastructure and referral systems remain inadequate to cope with these needs. Overall, there is a lack of available, qualified, and trained staff, with limited financial means to support service provision, supervision, and referral services. Findings from AAH assessments reveal that the risk environment is creating suboptimal community engagement with rampant poverty, poor employment, limited access to healthcare resources, stigma, transportation challenges and access to illicit substances all impacting communities’ ability to engage in healthcare, and women’s health seeking behavior and decision-making as well. Access constitutes a distinctive barrier to health care in areas with high severity scores, including those that recently witnessed changes of control like Northeast, Northwest, and parts of Southern Syria. AAH proposes joint approach to meet the priority needs during 2022-2023 HRP focusing on activating community structures for sustainable use and provision of public services preventing undernutrition and epidemic diseases and restoring essential health systems and capacity within targeted communities and where local capacity meets the different needs of women, men, girls, and boys. Vulnerability criteria is based on highest severity, needs assessment, (including prioritization of IDPs, returnees, population hosting displaced persons, and people with socioeconomic hardships), contextual volatility and strategic presence of AAH. Monitoring and beneficiary data will be disaggregated by sex, age, and marginalized groups, including disabilities. To increase sustained and equitable access to health services, AAH will identify emergency small-scale rehabilitations to reinstate safe and dignified condition of services. The intervention will be completed with the provision of basic furniture, medical supplies (including COVID-19 response) and equipment. PHC staff will be trained on integrating primary health care services (including MHPSS, nutrition, NCD, and GBV) with activated supervisory visits to ensure adequacy of implementation. Community Health Workers and Volunteers will also be trained to integrate and deliver MHPSS, GBV, nutrition and RMNCAH prevention at the primary healthcare centers, community, and household levels within the selected health districts. The project will intervene at multiple levels within the targeted health districts, including primary static and mobile healthcare facilities. A comprehensive MEAL plan is designated to support collection and tracking of sex and age disaggregated data in line with proposed health interventions and is undertaken directly by MEAL in collaboration with health and nutrition department. Monitoring methodologies include needs assessments, baseline and end line, and activity monitoring. Systematic collection of information on risk environment, gender roles and responsibilities, gender relations and decision-making dynamics, access to and control of resources, and social norms will be done with a conflict sensitivity approach. Qualitative interviews and satisfaction surveys will complement monitoring activities to examine the project’s effect on social dynamics in communities and pathways of change. Focus group discussions and key informant interviews with stakeholders, such as health facility workers and beneficiaries, at key points and midway through program cycle.

4 - Needs

Health needs continue to be driven and exacerbated by insufficient and inequitable access to healthcare due to a combination of fuel and transportation shortages, damage and destruction of health facilities, insufficient human resources, and imposed access constraints. Findings from AAH assessments revealed an increased vulnerability to disease outbreaks, especially in Northeast Syria, a serious scarcity of resources and negligible presence of health actors in key areas of intervention covering a large catchment population, mainly due to the volatile security situation, complicated mechanisms for approvals, and impending access issues. Additionally, AAH assessments reveal that the risk environment is creating suboptimal community engagement with rampant poverty, poor employment, limited access to healthcare resources, stigma, transportation challenges and access to illicit substances all impacting communities’ ability to engage in healthcare, and women’s health seeking behavior and decision-making as well. Protracted crisis has left just 54% of primary health care centers (PHCs) fully functional. At least 50% of the affected population require RMNCAH services with almost 90% of households requiring MHPSS in some areas.

9 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk: health facilities could be attacked. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: apply the designated de-confliction mechanism for health and nutrition facilities. Continue reporting attacks against health facilities. Continue advocacy efforts with all parties. Risk: gender inequality and GBV. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: train all health staff on how to supportively engage with GBV survivors and provide information in an ethical, safe and confidential manner. Ensure referral mechanisms to specialized GBV support, health care, MHPSS, and child protection. Risk: improper management of sensitive data. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: electronic data and information properly protected. Clear exit strategy about how to withdraw resources while ensuring that patients and healthcare workers are not exposed to further risk; including ways to protect and/or destroy data and information. Risk: not equal provision of and access to health services for vulnerable groups. Likelihood: high. Impact: high. Mitigation: analyze and disaggregate all data by sex, age and disability. Implementation of health services in line with global and health sector guidance, including improved quality reporting and monitoring. Review existing informed consent procedures. Ensure that complaints and feedback mechanisms are accessible and facilitate inputs for all persons.


10 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

Risk: aid workers or associated people use their power or influence to sexually exploit or abuse. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) policies in place, practiced and monitored. Polices address risks at the organizational level and the program level. The organization’s staff and communities have access to safe/effective reporting systems. Risk: any kind of abuse (sexual, power, etc.). Likelihood: low. Impact: medium. Mitigation: compulsory trainings on organizational ethical policies. AAH mission has a policy for PSEA and a reference person against harassment at country level.


11 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Assumptions: security situation allows safe response; access to target areas and beneficiaries is granted; partners are willing to collaborate on timely manner; availability of suppliers and items in the market, prices remain stable; no major displacements occur. Risk: security situation is unstable and/or worsens, AAH has no access; there are additional administrative procedures imposed on D-INGOs, and restrictions on access to beneﬁciaries; lengthy processes to obtain approvals. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: AAH coordinates with local authorities, Ministries, and stakeholders to monitor the situation, to maintain access to target areas and beneﬁciaries, to develop relationships and avoid or mitigate restrictions. Security rules and plans are in place and regularly reviewed. Re-planning of activities to recover eventual delays. Advocacy and coordination with the humanitarian community. Risk: significant devaluation of EURO/ USD against SYP; International bank transfers are not ﬂuid and timely. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: signiﬁcant changes in the value of the Syrian currency will be monitored and, if materialized, communicated to donors and options discussed. Risk: aid diversion, financial fraud, and corruption. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: strict policies and procedures in place for procurement, financial management and risk management; internal controls set up. Monthly monitoring of financial spending. Risk: markets and se
