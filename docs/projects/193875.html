Project Summary

The ongoing Israeli blockade and escalations since 2008 have had devastating consequences on all aspects of life in the Gaza Strip. It is estimated that 1.32 million people (63% of Gaza residents) are in need of multi-sectoral humanitarian assistance (MSNA, 2022). Of these, 988,282 people (of which 148,242 are persons with disabilities) are assessed as needing protection services (Protection Cluster, Protection Analysis Update, September 2022). World Bank projections (2020) had indicated that 64% of Gaza's population would live in poverty by 2021, and it has one of the highest unemployment rates in the world. By 2022, the economy had not returned to pre-Covid levels, with a growth rate of only 3.5% in 2022 (versus 7.1% in 2021) (World Bank, 2022), due to factors such as the escalation in August 2022, the crisis in Ukraine, and movement restrictions.
The toll on the well-being of residents of Gaza is high, with 40% of households reporting that at least one member of their household experiences signs of psychosocial distress or trauma (MSNA 2022). In a context characterized by prolonged strain, economic isolation and developmental decline, vulnerable families, including those with many children, female-headed households, and families with people with disabilities, have adopted different coping mechanisms to deal with the high level of poverty and vulnerability. The deteriorating socio-economic condition in Gaza has had a particularly negative impact on the realisation of children’s rights and the risk of protection violations notably gender-based violence.
Nearly 1 million (953,884) children in the oPt are in need of protection and MHPSS services, of which 499,187 are children in Gaza, and 79,936 children and 27,534 caregivers in the Gaza Strip are in need of structured protection and psychosocial support (Protection Cluster Protection Analysis, August 2022). According to preliminary findings of a UNICEF-supported MHPSS Assessment conducted by Tdh in Gaza from July -September 2022, the need for MHPSS services is very high, particularly for children who have protection concerns (Tdh, 2022).
The Protection Cluster has identiﬁed the provision of MHPSS services and case management as key interventions needed in the Gaza Strip. This Action is a response to such needs and is consistent with HRP's Second Objective. Activities will include age and gender-appropriate, inclusive child protection services, including specialized case management for GBV survivors, structured MHPSS services, awareness-raising to reduce the prevalence of GBV, and family support to assist in responding to distressed children. Overall, 12,550 persons will benefit from the project (2,500 girls, 2,500 boys, 3,775 women, and 3,775 men).
Of the targeted children, 1,000 will benefit from MHPSS services, 400 children will benefit from case management, and of them, 100 will benefit from MHPSS and case management provided to child survivors of GBV. Community and family members and caregivers will receive awareness and skills strengthening, including on CP, GBV and how to deal with distressed children (7000 persons) and CP workers will benefit from training and coaching sessions. Quality, timely protection services will be tailored according to age, gender disability and the best interest of boys and girls. As part of the case management interventions, cash support to meet urgent needs will be provided to 200 vulnerable families also benefiting from protection services.
During implementation, beneﬁciaries will be closely involved in designing and monitoring the project activities and a Project Monitoring and Evaluation Plan will be developed at the start of the Action with the participation of the five partner organizations, including indicators, assessments, and means of veriﬁcation tools deﬁned in the logical framework. The Project team will coordinate with the relevant authorities, child protection actors, the Protection Cluster, SGBV-sub-cluster, and CP-AoR.

1 - Needs

The ongoing Israeli blockade and five major escalations since 2008 have had devastating consequences on all aspects of life in the Gaza Strip. It is estimated that 1.32 million people (63% of Gaza residents) are in need of multi-sectoral humanitarian assistance (MSNA 2022). Of these, 988,282 people (of which 148,242 are persons with disabilities) are assessed as needing protection services in the Gaza Strip (Protection Cluster, Protection Analysis Update, September 2022), which has one of the highest unemployment rates in the world. Projections indicated that 64% of Gaza's population would live in poverty by 2021, while 2022 data shows that the economic growth rate remains low: only 3.5% in 2022 (versus 7.1% in 2021) (World Bank, 2020, 2022), due to factors such as the escalation in August 2022, the crisis in Ukraine, in addition to the ongoing blockade and movement restrictions. The toll on the well-being of residents of Gaza is high, with 40% of households reporting at least one member of their household having psychosocial distress or trauma (MSNA 2022). Lack of livelihoods is the main driver of needs of households in Gaza: the high rate of unemployment coupled with a dearth of economic opportunities increases dependency on aid, and drives the reliance on negative coping mechanisms (MSNA 2022). By July 2022, prior to the August escalation, 82.6% of households lacked access to adequate water, while 2.1% of households experienced severe food insecurity, and 62.9% experienced moderate to severe food insecurity (MSNA 2022). For 70% of households, affording essential food needs was difficult, with 50.5% relying on NGO or charity assistance. A total of 12% of households in the Gaza were using emergency coping strategies, while 56% of households were using crisis coping strategies (MSNA 2022). Consequently, the deteriorating socio-economic condition in Gaza has had negative impacts on women’s and children’s rights and their ability to access protection services. An increasing number of children display high levels of stress-related symptoms, requiring additional protection interventions aiming at reducing vulnerability and increasing protection. This situation has been exacerbated by the recurrent escalation in August 2022, heightening the need for protection services, for children and women, regarding psychosocial distress, mental disorders, and a higher risk of SGBV. Tdh conducted a RNA in June 2021 showing that the situation has exacerbated the need for protection for women and especially for children regarding psychosocial distress, mental disorders and a higher risk of SGBV across the Strip. During the Covid-19 pandemic, 67.7% of the participants in the Tdh survey “Impact of covid-19 on vulnerable children and their families in the Gaza Strip, Oct. 2020” reported an increase in violence against children. War leaves enduring psychological episodes in children and adolescents: during war, children face (to differing degrees) exposure to two types of traumatic events: type I (sudden traumatic event) and type II (protracted exposure to adverse events resulting in dysfunctional coping mechanisms). Children in Gaza are exposed to both, regularly, and suffer from conditions such as anxiety disorders, Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD), depression, dissociative disorders, behavioural disorders (especially aggression, asocial and violent behaviour) and substance abuse, more than their counterparts. In addition the Palestinian Multiple Indicator Cluster (2019-2020) assessment conducted by PCBS and UNICEF, more than 92% of children (aged from 1 to 14 years) have experienced at least one type of violent discipline method during the last month, including 90% psychological aggression, and 28.5% subjected to severe physical punishment and 75% subject to any physical punishment. By August 2022, nearly 1 million (953,884) children in the oPt were in need of protection and MHPSS services, of which 499,187 are children in Gaza (Protection Cluster Protection Analysis, August 2022). A total of 79,936 children and 27,534 caregivers in the Gaza Strip are also in need of structured protection and psychosocial support (Protection Cluster Protection Analysis Update, August 2022). Respondents to the Tdh RNA (2021) highlighted the differing impact of war on both genders and ages, and a fear that this could exacerbate existing gender inequalities. Further, women and girls not only face discrimination and denial of resources, but also SGBV, including early or forced marriage, psychological abuse, and exploitation (Global Protection Cluster, Gender Based Violence AoR, Gender Based Violence Secondary Data Review, September 2022.) While the data shows a higher prevalence of mental health disprders for girls and women versus boys and men, services are accessed more often by male users than by female. Women and girls are prevented from accessing services, both due to there being greater stigma related to mental health in women, and due to needing permission of family members to access mental health services (Protection Cluster Protection Analysis Update, August 2022), echoed by a report from the PCBS that showed that in 2019, only 1% of SGBV survivors asked for assistance due to the lack of confidential services and fear of stigma. Within Gaza, a Needs Assessment carried out by UN Women identified that 54% of respondents an increase in violence against women, and 45% noticed an increase in violence against girls, outside of the home (UN Women 2022). A total of 37% of respondents noticed an increase in violence against women, and 31% noticed an increase in violence against girls, within the home. These factors illustrate the importance of providing children and youth with integrated protection services, including structured MHPSS and child protection case management, and the importance of using a gendered approach for SGBV and MHPSS interventions.

2 - Activities

This Action aims to increase access to protection services including case management, MHPSS services, individual counselling to vulnerable children who have child protection concerns, including children displaying symptoms of psychological distress, child survivors of SGBV, and child victims of different forms of child abuse and violence against children, and children with disabilities. Family engagement will be an integral part of the project activities to improve the protective environment. The proposed activities will include: Activity 1: Awareness sessions on MHPSS available services that aims to decrease stigmas, increase utilization of available services, and to provide information on how to contact services and benefit from MHPSS services. In total, 4,000 persons will be targeted (1,000 boys, 1,000 girls, 1,000 women, 1,000 men). Approaches and methodologies will be adapted to suit the different needs of groups within society, especially targeting youth, women, and vulnerable groups. The provided MHPSS will be provided in line with the IASC guidelines on MHPSS and Child Protection Minimum Standards. Activity 2: Provision of MHPSS services, including structured and non-structured MHPSS support to 1,000 children (500 boys, 500 girls) and 500 caregivers (250 women, 250 men). Methodologies developed by Tdh or other specialized organizations will be used for the structured MHPSS support, based on the needs and age of the targeted groups, such as: “You create”, to create a space for youth affected by adversity to strengthen skills to lead their peers in arts-based activities that support wellbeing, resilience and social cohesion, for youth between 15 to 25; or for children from 4 to 14, the Movement, Games, Sport, and Creativity (MGSC) methodology which uses sport, games and creativity as a psychosocial tool to ultimately develop the personal and social skills of vulnerable children, and therefore improve their resilience and psychosocial wellbeing. Activity 3: Provision of case management to 400 children (200 boys, 200 girls), including 50 children survivors of SSGBV (5 boys, 45 girls). In total, the provided MHPSS services and case management will target both, boys and girls. Activity 4: A total of 30 animators (15 women, 15 men) will be trained on these methodologies and on the safe identification of signs of psychosocial distress such as behavioral changes and in the identification of patterns of exposure to protection risks affecting children. Children will be referred for case management and/or other MHPSS service providers, as needed. Activity 5: Self-care and stress management sessions will be organized on a quarterly basis for all partner’s staff and service providers’ staff (30 persons: 15 women, 15 men), as staff needs to release the pressure and vent the daily stressors they face. Activity 6: A total of 4,000 persons (1,000 men, 1,000 women, 1,000 boys, and 1,000 girls) will beneﬁt from awareness-raising for persons at risk of SGBV and more broadly awareness on women rights and PSEA, as a remedial activity to tackle the issue of SGBV, since the prevalence of SGBV is higher for girls and women. Activity 7: Referral pathways will be also done in case of specialized support is required, and 100 dignity kits will be distributed as in-kind support. Activity 8: Under this Action, awareness sessions on how to deal with traumatized children- victims of the current situation and general child protection will be conducted targeting 3,000 members (1,500 men and 1,500 women). Protection services will be provided to all children in need, regardless of their age groups and their ability status; services will be also provided to their families regardless of the gender and age of the head of the household and their localities. Activity 9: Training will be provided to 50 persons (25 women, 25 women) from Tdh and partners on PSEA and child safeguarding and protection mainstreaming. The training will be based on national and Tdh PSEA material. The training will be conducted by either Tdh expert by an external expert. Activity 10: To increase the impact of the protection services, as an activity under the case management services, a total of 200 vulnerable families (1120 persons: 360 boys, 360 girls, 200 women, 200 men) who have children with protection concerns will benefit from the cash support to meet the basic needs and to avoid resorting to negative coping mechanism such as early marriage, engaging children into labour, and withdrawal from schools. Each family will receive a total of ILS 1,000 in cash support to meet their basic needs such as food and school uniform. The needs will be identified during the assessment phase of case management. A vulnerability Grid will be developed to select the most vulnerable families, the grid will be shared with the Protection Cluster for feedback and validation. Tdh will coordinate this component with the Protection Cluster. Under this Action, the five partners will provide protection services, including MHPSS support, case management. Each partner will cover the geographical area for instance, BLDA will cover Beit Layhia, Jabalia, and Beith Hannon. Tdh will take lead in the provision of cash assistance, the referral pathways, and the capacity building for partners. Additionally, Tdh will provide technical and financial support to partners, along with overall management of the project. Tdh and partners will continue to report the project activities, including 4/5 Ws. Tdh will also coordinate the project activities with the CP AoR and GBV SC. The five partners, including Atfaluna were previously trained on case management and have been providing case management over the past 2 years.

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

During the course of the project, measures will be put in place to ensure that targeted communities can provide feedback on all the proposed services in a secure and confidential manner. Complaints boxes will be available at locations in which activities will be conducted, so beneficiaries can anonymously provide comments and feedback. Beneficiaries' feedback will be used to steer the implemented activities. The feedback will be collected on a monthly basis by Tdh teams. Mechanisms of collecting feedback will involve data collection through (1) pre and post questionnaires will be used to evaluate the effectiveness of activities implemented, (2) periodically, intervening beneficiaries to ask about the perceived quality of the provided services, and (3) conducting focus group discussions to assess the quality of provided services from beneficiaries perspectives. Protecting beneficiaries from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse will be considered a priority. With the abovementioned feedback and complaints mechanism, PSEA and reporting mechanisms will be conducted to increase community awareness about preventing and reporting Sexual Exploitation and Abuse cases.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

Tdh and partners will fully respect, protect, and full beneficiaries’ safety and fully respect their dignity. Tdh and partners will achieve this by protecting beneficiaries’ personal and sensitive data such as names and ID numbers. Tdh and partners will provide services that are trailed according to identified needs of beneficiaries, taking into account the context, and will have in place all the mitigation measures to ensure beneficiaries' safety. As a good practice for Tdh, no action would be planned and/or implemented without the child’s agreement, under the supervision of her/his legal gradient. Additionally, Tdh will not publish or report any data that could reveal any identification data, all publications/reports will include only aggregated data. Tdh and partner organizations will also involve beneficiaries in as many decisions as possible, including monitoring the project implementation. All interventions will seek to avoid exacerbating the situation for children by ensuring properly planned and implemented interventions that take into account children’s and their family's needs to access to humanitarian services. All services will be provided in a safe protected environment. Tdh and partners have zero tolerance for exposing beneficiaries to violence and or harmful practices, all measures will be taken into account to grate this, including full compliance to Tdh Child Safeguarding Policy (CSP), PSEA Policy, Code of Conduct, and confidential mechanisms. All staff members are trained on the CSP, PSEA, and the Code of Conduct and have signed the policies.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

In respect of equality and impartiality, all identified cases, regardless of their gender, age groups, and disability status will receive protection services according to their assessed and identified needs. All Project beneficiaries will have equal access to services, including beneficiaries with disabilities and beneficiaries living in the Access Restricted Areas and the most vulnerable areas like camps. Tdh will also target equally men and women without any regard to any discriminatory factors such as age. The proposed activities are based on the different needs that were identified through different methods, mainly focus group discussions, monitoring visits, and interviews with key informants. During the design phase, project beneficiaries were engaged heavily and all their needs were included in the project activities. During the project design, beneficiaries were also consulted through conducting a series of FGDs and interviews and in-depth interviews. Beneficiaries will also be part of the project monitoring. Children were also involved in the assessment and design of the project and will be actively engaged in monitoring the project activities. Finally, as with any advocacy product of this project, children will propose, design, and implement such products. Tdh and partners have a list of children with disabilities in need or previously benefitted from MHPSS services. Those children will be contacted using different blended methodologies that involve in-person modalities and remote modalities. If needed, Tdh and Partner might conduct MHPSS services in places that are accessible to children with disabilities that could involve home visits and provision of services in grassroots organizations. Also, Tdh and partners will develop the MHPSS manual that could be used by children with disabilities themselves. Atfaluna Society for Deaf Children has already developed such tools for children with hearing disabilities.
