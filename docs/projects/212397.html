Project Summary

This project aims to improve food security and increase resilience, protection, and agency to adolescent girls and boys and young women and men (ages 12-24) affected by conflict and displacement in Ar-Raqqa governorate to influence their lives. 240 young people will receive cash grants to improve their food security in the short term while their long-term resilience is improved. These youth will receive cash transfers of $100 per month over a 6 month period.
During the project period these adolescents and youth will gain knowledge and skills in financial literacy, literacy and numeracy, language competency, and employability skills. In addition, this project aims to increased psychosocial resilience, protection, and support to cope with adversity and this will achieved by increasing access to age and gender-sensitive protection services and PSS and by increasing communities' knowledge and commitment to protect and support young people.
Needs:
An estimated 530,000 people in Ar-Raqqa remain in acute need stemming from a lack of access to basic services, livelihoods and economic opportunities, combined with high levels of destruction to critical civilian infrastructure, including health facilities, water systems and schools, A dire economic situation, exacerbated by sanctions and COVID-19, has led to the devaluation of the Syrian Pound which has increased the price of food and exacerbated protection concerns. 70% of sub-districts in Raqqa are at education severity 4 (HNO 2024).
A recent Humanitarian Situation Overview in North Easy Syria reported that 98% of the key informants experienced barriers to accessing sufficient food. In Ar-Raqqa, over 84% of communities assessed through a Multi-Sector Needs Assessment (MSNA) found high rates of early marriage and sexual violence and sexual exploitation and abuse. The main reason reported by adolescents for not fully attending school was being engaged in work and economic activities to support their households. The main factor pushing adolescents to work was the inability of the head of the household to cover their household’s basic expenses despite holding a job. In Ar-Raqqa, the most frequently reported form of child labour was recruitment into armed groups for both boys and girls. The main drivers behind adolescent’s involvement in hostilities were economic in nature. According to the Children Accompanied with Armed Forces and Groups (CAAFAG) Task Force assessment, one of the key recommendations was to address the economic drivers of child labour through food security and livelihoods (FSL) and child protection programmes.
PSEA:
Risks: Women and girls not able to access services or participate in income generating activities due to sexual harassment; exploitation and abuse by aid workers; female heads of HHs and adolescent girls in particular risk of sexual exploitation in exchange to services; not reporting SEA for fear of retaliation; lack of awareness on reporting channels; stigma and fear of losing on the services making it difficult for the NGO to hold staff/partners involved accountable. Mitigations: the NGO has a Child Safeguarding policy which includes PSEA. To mitigate the risk of PSEA during the assistance delivery, the following will be included: mandatory training and regular refreshers on Child Safeguarding, Safe programming and PSEA policy; working closely with the WoS PSEA TF to strengthen feedback, reporting and response mechanisms for SEA; conducting community awareness sessions on; regular participatory safety audit to identify protection and safety concerns arising as a result of project implementation and developing action plans to mitigate identified risks collaboratively with the community; including PSEA messages and topics in all IEC materials and training sessions conducted under the project; ensuring that centrality of protection is embedded in all projects to mitigate risks and prevent potential harm.
