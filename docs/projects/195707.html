Project Summary

The project aims at enhancing the protective environment for children and access to child protection services in Terekeka and Bor South Counties of South Sudan that have been categorized as catastrophic or extreme. The primary purpose of the project is to address compounding effects of converging factors i.e. severe food insecurity, loss of livelihoods, economic decline, protection threats and lack of services which contributes and aggravates the prevalence of violence against children. The project will deliver lifesaving static and mobile services to the most vulnerable children and their caregivers affected by floods, conflict that causes displacement, diseases and famine. This comprises of returnees, Host community and internally displaced people (men, women, boys, girls, young people and adolescents). The project will reach 26,666 (Boy child: 1,809; Girl child: 1,433; Boy: 7,725; Girls: 6,335; Men: 2,933; Women: 5,354; Elderly men: 492; and Elderly women: 585) people through an integrated response.
The project’s child protection (CP) responses will be guided by the IASC guidelines for child protection, national standard operating procedure (SOP) and Child Protection Sub-cluster strategy. The response is strategic to the attainment of Cluster objectives and align to activities, indicators, and outcomes of the HRP 2022. The project activities focus on mitigating child protection risks, preventing and responding to child protection cases through; as safe healing and learning space (SHLS) programming, capacity building of frontline service providers on Child Protection in Emergencies (CPiE), case management including family tracing and reunification (FTR) services, Structured/non-structured psychosocial support (PSS), community Outreach and dialogues, and safe referrals.
The comprehensive case management service will be provided to children with protection concerns, family FTR services, and alternative care arrangements for unaccompanied and separated children (UASC) and cash assistance to meet the basic need of children. Similarly, the mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) service will be provided for children and caregivers with psychological distress. The project will use a combination of approaches such as SHLS and schools for social and emotional learning, parenting skills of caregivers, and supporting adolescents and their families in emergencies (SAFE) curriculum for life skills of adolescents. The community-based child protection (CBCP) structures will be established and strengthened as the lower structure of the community. The project will facilitate community outreach on child protection and capacity building of the frontline staff and community volunteers.
The project will build clear and precise referral pathways to enable vulnerable children to have access to the services such as nutrition centers, psychosocial support centers, case management, SHLS and healthcare. The issues of child abuses have been reported among IDP families, specifically congested areas. The project will mitigate these risks through local community leaders to support the enforcement of local laws to protect children, especially vulnerable girls and boys in the community.
SLDA will adhere to centrality of protection by reducing the threats and exposure to harm on boys, girls, men and women. This will be achieved by working together with other sectors to ensure a holistic protection approach, working with community members to understand local strengths and solutions to protection issues, coordinating with other agencies and Government and observing the principle of Do No Harm. All these interventions will adhere to COVID-19 measures and guidelines.

1 - Needs

South Sudan remains a protection crisis with civilians subject to widespread and grave violations of their rights and limited access to life-saving services. Children and adolescents continue to be exposed to a wide range of protection risks including, risks of family separation, recruitment, and use of children by armed forces and groups, psychosocial distress, sexual violence, neglect, abuse and exploitation. An estimated 2.9 million children will need immediate life-saving child protection (CP) services. Increased reports of children recruitment, family separation, and children survivors of GBV require expansion of CP services in 2022. Community displacements, flooding and ongoing conflict have exacerbated this situation; hindering adequate, safe access and full utilization of lifesaving humanitarian services in South Sudan (2022-2023 HRP Protection and Sub-Clusters Response Plan). Thousands of children are used by armed forces and armed groups, 52 per cent of all girls are married before they are 18 years old, gender-based violence is widespread and thousands of children are separated from their parents leaving them vulnerable to violence, abuse and exploitation (UNICEF 2022). According to the Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO) 2022, children take up the significant portion of people in need (PiN) in South Sudan such as 4.6 million children (53% - 2.3 million girls and 2.3 million boys). Child protection issues include the presence of separated children, child survivors of SGBV, early marriage, child pregnancy, child labour and children with disabilities with limited supported assistance. The situation is compounded by very limited access to education and lack of documentation, especially birth certificates (IOM 2021). The field-based Initial Rapid Need Assessment (IRNA) conducted on October 2022 in Terekeka and Bor South Counties of South Sudan. Specific child protection issues affecting children in the proposed Counties included: (1) Increased risk of girls and boys to recruitment, sexual violence, school dropout, neglect, child marriage, child labor and mine risks; (2) Existence of large number of children affected by separation and neglect due to population movement and stretched caring capacity of families; (3) Psychological distress and self-harm behaviors among children and caregivers due to exposure to violence, displacements, livelihood disruption, prolonged school closures; (4) Glaring gaps in child protection services available in the proposed locations ; (5) Deterioration of traditional support mechanisms for children and other vulnerable community members due to the impacts of conflict, displacement and effects of COVID-19; (6) Exposure of children to risky activities including recruitment, exploitation, robbery and street life. Children continue to expose to numerous protection risks including psychosocial distress, family separation, violence, recruitment by armed groups, and exploitation because of flooding and food insecurity. Increased cases of child marriages, psychological distress and mental health problems including suicidal tendencies have also been reported. Social structures and family bonds are breaking; children are separated from their families, and they have very limited access to basic services, which hinders their physical and mental development. There is need to urgently provide child protection services to the children in all the areas identified above otherwise continued lack of services will further worsen the protection of children and subsequently increased their suffering thereby creating generational gaps in the society. Response to child protection issues requires a multifaceted, sustainable, integrated and coordinated approach including strengthening child protection community structures. IDP, returnees and host communities require much attention from the humanitarian agencies.

2 - Project activities

SLDA proposes life-saving child protection interventions focusing on both protective and preventive modalities through static and mobile services targeting 26,666 (Boy child: 1,809; Girl child: 1,433; Boy: 7,725; Girls: 6,335; Men: 2,933; Women: 5,354; Elderly men: 492; and Elderly women: 585) most vulnerable and affected populations including children in the host community, IDP, returnees with a disability. Following activities are proposed based on practical experience and understanding of specific needs in the proposed locations: Provision of MHPSS Services including through mobile teams: The project will reach 22,667 children through static and mobile SHLS based recreational and psychosocial support services. The project will develop and disseminate age, gender and disability sensitive CP and MHPSS messaging targeting IDP, returnees and host communities through awareness raising sessions and events. Participants will include parents, caregivers, community leaders and different categories of duty bearers. Similarly, the project will provide psychological first aid (PFA) and community based PSS services for children and caregivers in need of additional support. The project will organize parenting and PSS sessions for parents/caregivers experiencing psychological stress and facing difficulty to provide adequate emotional support to their children. Strengthen Case Management approach including through cash-based assistance (including for UASC, GBV survivors, and CAAFAG): The project will identify, assess and provide case management support to identified extremely vulnerable children especially girls including referral to other service providers. SLDA will continue to provide case management services including FTR to the most vulnerable children aligned with the vulnerability and risk assessment criteria matrix and affected by the flooding and food insecurity. The customized national inter-agency case management forms will be used to manage individual cases, conduct child and family assessments, develop a case plan, provide direct and indirect services to meet needs identified in the assessments. The project will facilitate Referral Pathways and service coordination mechanisms in target Counties. More specifically, the project will provide unconditional cash support for extremely vulnerable HHs caring for children in the project locations using broader case management vulnerability criteria (HHs with child labour and disability cases, children facing neglect and malnutrition, children in female headed HHs etc) Community Based Child Protection (CBCP), capacity building and outreaches: The project will develop community based child protection action plan in consultation with relevant stakeholders including children and community representatives. CBCP Committees will be capacitated to better protect from and respond to abuse, neglect, exploitation and violence of children. SLDA will support the establishment of children and youth groups to ensure their participation in the community level CP interventions, advocacy and their voice to be heard and shape the projects. The project will recruit, train and deploy community level child protection and psychosocial support facilitators in project locations. These facilitators will be trained to facilitate participant-led life skills sessions covering key topics such as identity, teamwork, communication, peace and conflict, respect, responsibility, and others. Similarly, the project will train, mentor and support community leaders to strengthen community based child protection mechanisms in target locations. CBCPC members will be trained on CPiE, case identification, and referrals to the CP actors. Teachers, PTAs and Senior Coordinator, SHLS Facilitators will be trained on UNICEF’s PSS manual and SHLS management standards.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Involvement of all stakeholders, especially those who are most vulnerable, will enable the definite achievement of goals in a timely manner. In this response, the Community Feedback and Response Mechanism (CFRM) will be set up and made functional at community levels. The CFRM will deliver a mechanism that enables the beneficiaries and community members to provide feedback and seek responses in relation to activities of this response and other development actors in their communities, in a manner that is safe, non-threatening and accessible. Community engagement meetings will draw upon participants from all groups of beneficiaries, regardless of age, ability, gender and position in the community, including school children. Ensuring that a diverse range of perspectives, lived experiences, and voices are represented in these conversations, prioritizing all types of community members will continue be the prerequisite of accomplishing the project goal and objectives. The outreach and awareness methods will ensure that children are aware of the mechanism, understand its purpose and understand the feedback submission process of focusing on children between the ages of 6 and 18. In-person awareness-raising sessions will also be conducted by field staff using child friendly messages. Project team will develop child friendly communication channels that can be used for children: child friendly suggestion box, child friendly helpdesk service and focus group discussions. For Adolescents, the team will use more typical channels such as a helpdesk. “Trusted Caregivers” of a child will also provide feedback to SLDA though they will use the traditional adult communication channels for providing feedback. School and community meetings will be held where necessary to target specific groups separately, for example parents or teachers, (men and women), school children (girls and boys), children (and families of children) who are living with disabilities, and others as necessary. The feedback mechanism will be inclusive of children with disabilities. There are outreach and awareness methods that accommodate the hearing, seeing, and learning impaired through video, song, image-heavy posters, and leaflets, and in-person communication. The suggestion box will be accessible to physically impaired children and focus group discussions and helpdesks will beneficiary feedback from impaired children.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

SLDA will prioritize accountability to affected populations (AAP) and community engagement to facilitate meaningful participation and engagement of affected communities, ensuring adequate accountability to affected populations through monitoring beneficiaries’ feedback and information sharing while adhering to the global and government of South Sudan guidelines on COVID-19. SLDA has developed a standard operating procedure (SOP) to ensure that the target population is involved in the whole project cycle. SLDA conducts the community consultation to get the feedback of girls, boys, women, and men together with community and government leaders that informs key decisions during project design, start-up, implementation and close-out. The community sensitization and information-sharing sessions on the project will be conducted from the very beginning of the project design and will continue throughout the project cycle. Design Phase: SLDA plans the client feedback channels that will be used during implementation to inform project adjustments, with specific consideration to children’s safety and accessibility in using feedback channels. Feedback from past projects or other organizations will be used to inform project design. Start-Up Phase: SLDA plans the proactive (survey, a focus group discussion, an individual interview) and reactive (suggestion boxes, hotlines, email addresses, office walk-in) channels that will be used to collect feedback from the clients during project implementation. SLDA will use best practice guidance to plan how they will record, manage and refer the feedback that SLDA staffs collect during implementation with particular attention for reporting and responding to allegations of abuse and exploitation, including SEA, and other sensitive complaints. SLDA will inform its beneficiaries about the project and the feedback channels. Particular attention to be paid to how SLDA communicate this with children and their caregivers. Implementation Phase: SLDA proactively collects and acts on feedback on a quarterly basis. SLDA will manage at least one feedback channel through which project beneficiaries can provide feedback/lodge complaints. Particular attention to be paid to the ability of children and their caregivers to use the feedback channel. Close-Out Phase: SLDA will interpret the sex and age disaggregated feedback data identifying its implications for close-out and future projects. Through community engagement, project beneficiaries play a meaningful role in the deliberations, discussions, decisions and implementation of the project activities. The project will engage with potential existing networks, including traditional and cultural structures, government structure, schools and youth programmes, and churches and faith-based organizations. The project will give following considerations for working more inclusively and engaging different stakeholder groups: - Engage tribal leaders, community action groups, and affected populations to participate in the overall development and execution of programmatic action. - Solicit input from all affected parties to discover what is working, what is not working, and what needs to be accomplished for a successful implementation of project activities. - Engage with community and association groups as starting points for approaching and communicating with the populations targeted for engagement. - Build trust with the affected populations. - Develop a different communication approach to know the attitudes, beliefs, and behavior of the intended beneficiaries in order to craft the most effective messages to encourage community involvement. - Constructively engage with youths to widely disseminate information to others within the community.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

SLDA will commit to ensuring that mechanisms in place to prevent and respond to sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) are strengthened and meet required minimum core humanitarian standards. The project will promote quality programming with a focus on inter-sector synergies and integrated programming to mainstream protection, gender, and accountability to affected populations (AAP). Similarly, the project prioritizes the mitigation, response and prevention of gender based violence (GBV) and Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA). Trainings will be done with teachers, PTA and SMC members, and other parties engaged in the project on all issues relating to protection. SLDA is committed to beneficiaries and staff safe from harm. It has a safeguarding policy that encompasses the PSEA. The safeguarding policy mainly focuses on the protection of child and adults from exploitation and abuse - physical, verbal, emotional, mental, economic or sexual – by those working for or associated with SLDA. Specifically, the zero tolerance policy states: treating beneficiaries with dignity and compassionately is a matter of priority, no act or behavior exploitation and abuse shall be condoned. SLDA has safeguarding focal person in each field office to lead the staff orientations, community awareness and receive any allegation from the staff and clients. All the reporting lines and means are displayed in all the field offices to be accessed for all clients and staff. In addition, the program team disseminates safeguarding/PSEA information to all beneficiaries coming for the SLDA services during the community outreach programme. SLDA will put in place reporting mechanisms and guidelines to ensure that cases of sexual exploitation and abuse are immediately reported and followed up on, through its clear guidelines for reporting. Any allegation of abuse or exploitation of an adult or child associated with a SLDA programme will lead to the immediate suspension and removal from the location of the accused individuals. SLDA will work in collaboration, consultation, and coordination with other partners in the area to consolidate workable referral pathways for the survivors to access services without hindrance when needed. The project intervention’s will incorporate protection principles and promote meaningful access, safety and dignity in provision of project resources and services. Safety and dignity, and avoid causing harm for the target beneficiaries will be the priority. The interventions will prevent and minimize unintended negative effects under this project, which can increase people's vulnerability to both physical and psychosocial risks.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

SLDA recognizes the right of beneficiaries and community members to give feedback and seek response from projects affecting them. Sensitization meetings will be organised with different groups to ensure information reaches all communities in the project target areas. Specifically, feedback from parents and caregivers as well as teachers, youth facilitators, community mobilizers, and others working to support the protection of children will have opportunities to provide feedback for adaptation and implementation throughout, as was indicated in their inclusion in the needs assessment. Where appropriate, children (boys, girls and children living with disabilities) will be asked for their feedback in the current project cycle. For example, children being supported by case management will have opportunities to advocate for themselves and the type of care and support they prefer to receive, as is appropriate for their situation. All feedback will be confidentially filed and registered into the feedback registry. The designated feedback focal person will review the registry on a quarterly basis in each field location in liaison with the M&E Officer to ensure that all the feedback is appropriately addressed. According to the SLDA feedback handling cycle, the collected feedback data will be compiled, interpreted, then decisions and planning will be made on what actions to take in response to the feedback. This decision could be made in consultation with a group of local key stakeholders. Depending on the nature and content of the feedback, beneficiary feedback will then be communicated and acted upon internally or referred to another organization or institution. Beneficiary feedback that is related to the SLDA project will be communicated internally to different categories of staff and with a different level of urgency (critical, high, medium and low). Any decision will be followed by immediate action. Then those decisions will be explained and discussed with beneficiaries through different means depending on whether the feedback received was individual or collective, sensitive or non-sensitive. Confidentiality and security of the beneficiaries are of high importance and have to be considered when communicating the SLDA response. Based on the feedback provided through FGDs with children or CBCPC members or community review meetings, SLDA will integrate them into project activities to ensure effective project delivery. In essence, SLDA will approach the principle of participation from equity perspective and advocate for equal participation of different groups. Based on the cluster reporting system, the project will put in place and implement and monitoring and reporting system, progress, best practices and lessons learned will be presented in cluster meetings based on the 5Ws formats developed by the cluster. Finally the monitoring and evaluation unit will continue reviewing and monitoring the progress and impact of the action upon beneficiaries.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

Many flood-displaced people, including those who were displaced by the 2020 flood were unable to return to their homes until early 2022 (HNO 2022). Heavy rain and flooding created historical overflow of the Rivers since July 2020 in South Sudan, creating the worst flooding in 60 years. In 2021, widespread flooding across the country had a bigger impact on agro-pastoral and pastoral communities compared to 2019 and 2020 floods. The floods were due to a combination of overflow of the White Nile, Kiir and Jur rivers and other small tributaries and heavy rains that intensified between May and October 2021 especially in the Congo basin. The floods created conditions that are conducive for the outbreaks of animal diseases (e.g. lumpy skin disease) including zoonoses. In addition, flooding impacted on grazing lands, leading to inundation and preventing animals from accessing feed. The flood destroyed homes and livelihoods of people - damaged settlements, agriculture (crops and cattle), horticulture, and market access which are crucial for peoples’ livelihoods. 40% of the crops and vegetable gardens in SLDA operational areas have been affected. People are displaced and feared to turn to negative coping mechanisms such as informal borrowing, selling productive assets, and depletion of working capital which might be used to protect from the impact of the disaster. The flooding crisis came at the backdrop of very grim hunger situation in some of the target areas, where already many people were suffering from acute and severe hunger. This situation was further impacted by the current economic downturn and higher inflation rates, in addition to food price hikes exacerbated by the impact of COVID-19 pandemic. Negative coping mechanisms of poor and most affected households have been reported, such as taking fewer meals per day (mostly amongst women and girls), selling assets or borrowing money at high rates to purchase food items, causing unmanageable debt. The flooding also resulted in increased incidences of human diseases such as malaria and acute watery diarrhoea that will affect productivity and deplete savings in the form of increased medical expenses – this will further contribute to household vulnerability and reduce resources available for purchasing food. SLDA is requesting access to the HRP to cover the immediate needs of vulnerable individuals severely affected by the flooding. Prior to the current flooding SLDA programme has been implementing various flood risk preparedness and mitigation activities including: dissemination of Early Warning Information, and development of an early response strategy to mitigate the impact of flooding, stakeholders coordination, formation of flood response committees, identification of most vulnerable people and high-risk areas; distribution of empty sacks to aid dyke construction and monitoring implementation of flood response actions. This request for HRP is sought as the scale and magnitude of the current flooding and livelihood crisis it has compounded requires additional interventions to help vulnerable households meet their immediate needs, reducing the hunger gap and mitigating negative coping mechanisms. This intervention will also provide support to host communities that had to cope with people displaced.There is a significant gap in the response to these needs. The project will support households through cash or voucher assistance to address their immediate food needs, while building assets, such as constructing flood control dikes that will improve their livelihoods, reduce risks and impact of shocks, increase food productivity, and strengthen resilience to natural disasters.

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

SLDA implements integrated programme including provision of nutrition, health, WASH, FSL and protection services. The beneficiaries will receive integrated assistance as far as possible. SLDA will also coordinate with other partners to ensure that the gaps are filled and the targeting include most vulnerable. At the start, SLDA will map protection and other actors in the area and establish a referral system. Referral pathways and linkages to other service providers including those in protection, education, health and nutrition will be created. SLDA will refer vulnerable groups of women in Women and Girls Friendly Spaces (WGFSs) in target locations. This initiative will allow outreach to communities to prevent and mitigate GBV, provide clinical and psychosocial care to survivors and support women and girls to know and access their sexual and reproductive health and rights. All referrals will be logged and followed up on, to make sure that people multiple needs, especially protection needs, are met. Factors contributing to inequalities will be addressed in trainings and community level meetings to be organized by the project. Communities will additionally be supported to address access challenges through mainstreamed sensitization on existing services, how and where to access them.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

The underlying causes of undernutrition are often directly linked to food insecurity. The project will contribute to improve food security to provide access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food that meets affected population dietary needs and food preferences thus contributing toward decreased prevalence of global acute malnutrition. The project will also contribute to addressing people’s immediate food needs whilst also providing them with inputs to overcome the lean season and reducing dependencies on humanitarian assistance. This will improve people’s overall food situation and as a consequence will reduce the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero). Although children under 5 will not be specifically targets, they are included in the food assistance and will benefit from the livelihood assistance and a more stable food and nutritious intake. This is likely to decrease acute malnutrition among children under 5. Additionally, Pregnant and lactating women (PLW) will be targeted and they will receive additional food. This will have a positive impact on malnutrition among new-born and children under 2. The project will target households hosting children under 5 years who are affected by severe malnutrition, levels 4 and 5. Initial selection will involve collaboration with nutrition service providers and will be focused on those admitted to treatment. Such households will be enlisted in cash and voucher assistance to improve market access while also being supported with inputs to engage in own production. The households will be further supported to grow vegetables or engage in fishing as a way of diversifying their diets. Care takers of severely affected children will additionally undergo targeted Infant and Young Child Feeding (IYCF) trainings that shall include cooking demonstrations, breastfeeding and household hygiene. Reduction of the proportion of populations facing IPC 3, 4, 5 will be addressed through provision of inputs and training to enable them restart household food production. Additional measures will include sensitization on climate change and community-based disaster risk reduction.
