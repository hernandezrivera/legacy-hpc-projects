Project Summary

In Somalia armed conflicts are still on-going, compounded by recurrent inter-clan fighting, causing massive displacement, destruction of property and decimation of livelihoods. The country has also been facing severe drought due to the combined impact of the failed spring rains, which followed the failure of the preceding season in Oct-Dec 2018 and harsh weather conditions during the Jan-March 2019 period. During the 2020, the combined impacts of COVID-19 and the desert locust invasion worsened the already dire food security situation caused by recurrent climate shocks including the recent floods of the Shabelle river, affecting population in Lower Shabelle and Hiraan. High prevalence of malnutrition remains a key concern amongst vulnerable populations where the COVID-19 outbreak had negatively affected also the economy, with a possible and sharp increase in malnutrition levels, leading to even higher mortality rates. The COVID-19 pandemic is also having an unprecedented impact on economies and livelihood and public health measures, although deemed necessary, have resulted both in the loss of jobs and livelihood options, but also an increase in staple food prices. According to the last FSNAU report (September 2020) 2.1 million people across Somalia are expected to face Crisis or worse (IPC Phase 3 or higher) outcomes between September and December without sustained humanitarian assistance. Population groups classified as Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse require interventions aimed at reducing food consumption gaps, eradicating acute malnutrition, saving lives, and protecting and saving livelihoods. Cesvi response is in line with the HRP objective of 2020 towards a life-saving assistance focusing on resilience-building at the community level with short-term interventions aimed at preventing a deterioration of their situation leading to higher severity of needs. The action aims to improve access to food of the most vulnerable population in the selected target areas through cash intervention and asset distribution in order to increase their access to basic needs.
The Action target 30,450 vulnerable affected individuals in Mudug, Hiraan and Lower Shabelle regions with the following set of intervention:
-6 rounds of UCT and CfW addressing small scale infrastructures repair; emergency livestock assistance and agricultural input through provision of animal treatment kits/CAHWs kits in Galkayo and Hobyo districts (Oman, Jilable, Dagaari, Elaqwan, Garday, Herodhagaxley). Target 1,080 HHs=7,560 beneficiaries.
- 6 round of UCT and CfW addressing small scale infrastructures repair and agricultural farms inputs in Afgoye district. Target 1,300 HHs=9,100 beneficiaries
- 6 rounds of UCT and CfW addressing canals rehabilitation and rehabilitation of feeder roads, distribution of agricultural input, IGA and self-help group business for women in the villages in Hiraan (Lebow Bulakahin, Doon raaho, Bulamaray, Bulakaharay, Adiley Kulanta and Shimo). Target 1,970 HHs=13,790 beneficiaries
The modality chosen for the intervention is an integration of unconditional and conditional cash assistance. Beneficiaries will be provided with CfW and cash transfer for 6 months which value will be based on the DFiD/ECHO Sup-Region standard and CWG recommendation. The Action will ensure inclusion of the most vulnerable and marginalized groups, using a bottom-up approach. The intervention is built on Cesvi past experience in the same target locations where cash based actions were previously implemented under different donor programmes (DFiD, UK- Aid, SHF, DEVCO, AICS and WFP). The action is also part of a wider programme strategy planned by Cesvi which integrates multiple sectors in the same area (WASH, H&N and FSL) which complement each other’s action minimizing duplication and combining the joint effects (target, assessment, evaluation), creating synergies in order to strengthen the impact of the Action with a multi-sectoral approach.

What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

Cash transfer programming (CTP) is the most appropriate response intervention in the target locations for security, access and geographic aspects. COVID-19 had several potential impacts on Food Security situation in Somalia: 1 Reduction of Food Availability (-Reduction of local food production due to agricultural land left uncultivated –Reduction of food supply due to import restrictions and interrupted trade -Dysfunctional food markets due to production and distribution being disrupted by social/physical distancing measures). 2. Reduction of Food Access (-Rising of food prices because of increase in demand and competition -Decrease in livelihood sources due to interrupted in-country economic activity -Movement restrictions threaten to disrupt the entire food supply-chain). After the disruption in the market chain during the early periods of the outbreak of the pandemic, the supply chain is now back to normal and commodities are accessed locally. With markets functioning, its ideal to use cash transfer when responding to immediate needs of beneficiaries being a quick means of response. Transportation challenges in some location for example Lower Shabelle is rampant for humanitarian aid products since its susceptible to all kinds of risks, Al-Shabab being the major risk. Use of cash gives beneficiaries the purchasing power and they can access multiple items from the market, at their own choice. Both unconditional and conditional cash transfers, as a modality, have the capacity to empower individuals to solve a wide-array of problems from healthcare access to business training to nutrition to increased social standing, to income generation. This approach preserves people’s dignity as it gives them greater choice. Cash transfer modality is seen as an effective means to aid displaced populations, supporting a dignified assistance and value for money. In fact in the absence of an institutionalized social protection system, it can be used as shock-responsive assistance to ensure population do not fall into destitution while transitioning to recovery. In details, UCTs allow beneficiaries to choose exactly which purchases are most critical to them, and where and when they want to spend the money. UCTs are mainly used to purchase goods to cover basic needs, but occasionally beneficiaries use them to repay debt and/or to make livelihoods investments. Frequency of payment and the amount of the cash transfer can influence how UCTs are spent: one-time, larger transfers are often spent on rebuilding/ recovering livelihoods, whereas multiple, smaller transfers (like the ones implemented within the project) are often used to cover basic household needs like food, medicine and clothing. Because payment is only received after a beneficiary has completed work, CfW is often considered a type of conditional cash transfer. Its aim is to provide consistent short-term wages to a targeted group of vulnerable, crisis affected households when they are not actively employed in other activities. The project objective to provide life-saving assistance to prevent a deterioration of the situation can be implemented thought the two integrated modalities (UCT and CfW) to allow the beneficiaries to cover basic needs as shock response, in particular to cope with the economic impact of the Covid19 pandemic. Vulnerable groups who are unable to participate in CfW (due to injury, immobility, etc.) are included in the project target so they are not excluded from benefits: beneficiaries who can physically work participate in CfW, while beneficiaries unable to participate in CfW activities are provided UCTs. Also, injection of cash into the market boosts the local economy and creates a multiplier effect. The existing market is able to respond to huge cash injection, and furthermore, there is an active mobile service provider with a large coverage area in the country with good network access and financial access through the phone.

Explain how the project design considered market functionality ?

A recent REACH publication (Somalia Cash and Markets Quarterly Dashboard, April-June 2020) inform that supply chain has been a big challenge as a result of the impact of heavy Gu rains, from early-March to mid-May, to critical supply chains was aggravated by the closure of land borders and limited maritime transportation due to Covid-19. By June, ground transportation started to resume and borders were gradually reopening When compared to the same period in 2019, the second quarter of 2020 observed an even higher increase in the full MEB cost. With an improvement in the supply chain in the last quarter, markets are functional, but prices of commodities have gone up as the purchasing power of many affected population has lowered down as a consequence of the Covid19 impact in the economy. Scarcity of fruits and vegetables such as bananas, tomatoes, onions, watermelons, mangoes continue in most regions and prices remain high. Poor households are particularly vulnerable to malnutrition due to limited access to vegetable and green leaves. Also data from the “Somalia 2020 Post Gu Food Security and Nutrition Outcomes and Projections” (FSNAU-September 2020) which states that 1. prices of imported food items (rice, flour, vegetable oil and sugar) will likely be stable 2. expected below average 2020 Deyr season rainfall will likely lead to tightening of cereal supply from local production (especially maize), thus will likely put upward pressure on cereal prices and 3. Livestock prices likely to decline but remain at above average levels, mainly due to a sluggish demand for livestock, following the end of the peak export period. Also, field visit by Cesvi staff report that markets are functional and stable in the project locations and beneficiaries will have the capacity to immediate convert the cash into their daily sustenance/food items. The supply chain is now back to normal and commodities are accessed locally, after the disruption in the market chain during the early periods of the outbreak of the pandemic. The transfer amount and the activity methodology in the project design follow the recommendation of publication mentioned above and of the Somalia CWG, which took into consideration the market functionality.

Mitigation Measures

1) Risk that beneficiaries are exposed to violence during registration/distribution as a result of the distance to or location of humanitarian assistance and services. MM1. Ensure the location of and access routes to distribution points and services are safe with enough space for orderly distribution. MM2. Identify central locations: beneficiaries should not have to traverse long distances or pass through conflict zones to access distribution points. MM3. Consider door-to-door distribution. MM 4. Consider the frequency of distributions. 2) Risk of women, girls and boys exposed to sexual violence, including sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA), when accessing humanitarian assistance.MM1. Distributions should take place in daylight hours giving beneficiaries sufficient time to travel home before nightfall. MM2. Ensure the mode and frequency of distribution minimises safety risk and be aware that beneficiaries may face theft, intimidation, sexual assault and extortion whilst taking their rations home. MM3. Keep lists of beneficiaries and personal data confidential and safe from unintended use by applying effective data protection measures. MM4. Work in coordination with protection actors to put in place measures to prevent and respond to physical/sexual violence arising from food distributions. 3) Risk that marginalized groups, persons with disabilities, women, boys and girls, do not participate in consultations or decision-making processes for the project or are excluded from humanitarian services/assistance/activities. MM1. Ensure that assistance and services are reaching the most vulnerable by identifying and prioritise the most vulnerable groups in the community and prevent discrimination or exclusion of marginalised groups. MM2 Apply a systematic outreach process in registering beneficiaries, otherwise vulnerable people such as older people, may easily be excluded as other more mobile and vocal population groups are registered. MM3 Ensure lack of documentation does not exclude individuals from accessing services. MM4. Make sure beneficiaries know how to obtain food assistance and livelihood opportunities, where and when the distributions will take place, what they will get, how long it should last and how to use the items. MM5 Communicate information through various means to reach the broader community. MM6. Coordinate with civil society specialising in working with persons with disabilities to help identify such individuals 4) Risk that beneficiaries are not accessing/using complaint and feedback mechanisms. MM1. Be transparent with the affected populations by providing them with accessible and timely information on selection criteria for targeted assistance, organisational procedures and processes that affect them. MM2. Set up accessible, confidential and well understood feedback mechanisms for suggestions and complaints. MM3, Respond to complaints, regardless of whether corrective measures can/need to be put in place. MM4, Organize awareness raising sessions so that people know how it works. MM5. Consider a joint feedback mechanism with other sectors to minimize confusion.

Please provide further explanation

Having considered that without access to basic services and livelihood opportunities, durable solutions cannot be achieved and recognizing that displacement is not simply a humanitarian issue but a development and state-building challenge, Cesvi will implement a comprehensive strategy involving all key stakeholders creating synergies with other multi/integrated approach of wash, H&N and food security activities in all target location with. The urgent life-savings needs of the affected communities and the livelihoods component will be addressed together with the long-term vision targeting the needs and vulnerabilities of drought related IDPs and host communities simultaneously through a building resilience approach. This strategy together with a stable environment will allow displaced people to have the opportunity to opt for local integration solution in the areas where they took refuge. in particular Cash transfer is an important social protection modality having significant positive impacts to the whole family and also to poor and vulnerable children and their families. Findings from the Transfer project - s a multi-country research and learning initiative of UNICEF, FAO and University of Carolina started in 2009 that provides rigorous evidence on the impact and effectiveness of large-scale national cash transfer programmes. indicate that cash transfers can: increase household productive capacity and resilience; create household and local economy spill overs; increase school enrolment and attendance; improve mental health and life satisfaction; delay sexual debut and reduce intimate partner violence, among others. Through the lens of the social protection the Action aims to support shock-responsive elements of social protection systems for the most vulnerable, and in laying the basis for more durable solutions.
