PURPOSE:
To clear priority Minefields, cluster strikes and ERW to enable towns to expand to accommodate returnees building resilience by releasing land for agriculture and housing
TARGET:
The MTT will release 1,420,000m2 (142 Ha) of high priority minefields and cluster strikes and all spot tasks in Torit/Canal/Fangak. This should equate to 34 hazardous areas surveyed and cleared.
MAG’s CL team will:
1. Build local capacity by training 206 NNGO staff (M:131, W:75) (MRE).
2. Provide MRE for 7,200 residents through community sessions. (M:600, W:768, B:2280, G:3552)
3. Train 175 Community Focal Points (W:105, M:70), empowering women decision makers to participate in
prioritisation of MRE & the collection of data to aid prioritisation for clearance tasking.
4. MRE for 328 INGO staff (W:121, M:207).
SUMMARY:
MAG will deploy a multi-task team (MTT 1) to Torit, Eastern Equatoria, and Fangak/Canal, Jonglei to clear high-impact minefields and cluster munition strikes. The team will be supported by a MineWolf on minefields during the dry season and switch to cluster strikes with the support of a Bozena with a mulching head during the wet season. MAG is the only NGO using ground preparation MineWolf 370 and Bozena 3 machines in South Sudan. As such MAG delivers clearance faster and more cost effectively than manual teams. Increased clearance is needed to provide land for housing and agriculture as displaced people and refugees return to their villages. The ability to settle and farm increases the resilience of these communities and reduces reliance on other forms of aid. With access to mechanical assets to support clearance, MAG is in a position to begin to return large areas of contaminated land back to these communities in Tier 1 locations.
MAG community liaison team will support the MTT providing risk education to local communities and INGO staff. The team will also train Community Focal Points (CFPs) to provide risk education to their communities and aid prioritization of clearance tasking, as well as build the capacity of national NGOs to undertake mine risk education activities. The community liaison element of the project is expected to increase national ownership of risk education and improve the sustainability of life-saving messaging in vulnerable communities.
Inputs
MTT 1 is made up of 27 people: 1 Technical Field Manager; 2 mechanical operators; 13 deminers; 3 Community Liaison staff; 6 drivers; 2 medics and 2 cooks. Teams live in tents in communities, and remain highly mobile, moving between tasks as needed. The team uses a MineWolf 370 in the dry season and a Bozena 3 fitted with a ‘mulching head’ on cluster strikes during the wet season that speeds the removal of vegetation to improve clearance efficiency.
The Bozena machines increase Cluster clearance outputs to 7 Ha (70,000m2), while the Minewolf clears 10 Ha (100,000m2) of minefield per month in the dry season.
LOCATION:
Torit, Eastern Equatoria
Using HRP funding, MAG would move MTT1 & the MineWolf 370 in January 2019 to prioritised minefields in Torit. The MineWolf will support the team throughout the dry season. In the wet season the team will move to clear cluster strikes with the Bozena. 100% of spot tasks reported in Torit will be destroyed as part of the workplan.
Canal/Fangak, Jonglei
If agreed as a priority, in Sep 2019, MAG could send the MineWolf to Fangak and Canal, to resurvey & clear the minefields there. MTT1 would fly to Northern Jonglei as soon as the land is dry enough for operations.

Needs

A thirty year war of independence and subsequent protracted civil war in South Sudan has resulted in approximately two and half million people dead, four and half million displaced and millions more in a state of hunger and extreme poverty. South Sudan’s long struggle for independence from Sudan has also led to widespread contamination from landmines and other UXO. Over 50 women, girls, boys and men have been killed or injured so far in 2018 as a result of accidents with mines and other UXO. The true scale of human cost is likely much higher as many accidents go unreported. The scale of the contamination reflects the complexity, intensity and scope of the conflict. Through the success of QRT and EOD survey and removal of spot tasks in 2017 and 18, 2,891 spot tasks have been removed. 166 remain, with more found each day. UNMAS reports that 212 minefields and 156 cluster strikes leave over 39 million square metres of land contaminated. This needs dedicated cost effective clearance. The impact of this contamination on the humanitarian situation has been recognised internationally as well as at a national level. Both the 2018 South Sudan Humanitarian Response Plan and most recent National Development Plan cite contamination from landmines and ERW as a key challenge to the country’s reconstruction efforts. Contamination continues to pose a physical threat to local communities, refugees and IDPs, while also impeding the safe delivery of vital humanitarian aid. It also curtails freedom of movement, and significantly impedes the development of affected communities who cannot settle, farm or use natural resources on their land. The situation is likely to become more pressing as returnees increase the demands on available land. Since 2013, the humanitarian situation in South Sudan has consistently deteriorated, affecting nearly all of the country’s newly defined twenty-eight states. Thousands have died and hundreds of thousands have been uprooted. The July 2018 Humanitarian Bulletin, published by the UN Organization for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) in South Sudan, reported that an estimated 1.74 million South Sudanese have been internally displaced by the conflict and nearly 2.5 million have taken refuge in neighbouring countries. Economic hardship, and outbreaks of Cholera and Malaria continue to destabilize the country, where 6 million people – the highest number ever reported in the country – were suffering food insecurity as of June 2017. The return of land through mine and cluster strike clearance increases the resilience of at-risk communities and reduces the reliance on other forms of aid, as they become increasingly self-sufficient in terms of food and shelter. MAG plans to deploy teams to two Tier 1 areas: Torit in Eastern Equatoria and Fangak/Canal in Jonglei. Clearing this is slow but essential work. Of the known Hazardous Areas 37% are in Central Equatoria, 23% in Eastern Equatoria and 16% in Jonglei. Many of these minefields were laid to protect the towns, the cluster strikes dropped to empty them. This land now needs clearing to accommodate returnees, allow houses to be built and farms to be planted, building resilience and reducing the demands on the humanitarian response. Understanding that clearance takes time, life-saving MRE is still is desperate need to protect the most vulnerable women and girls, as well as targeted for boys (22% of those killed and 21% injured) and men (57% of those killed and 63% injured) who are usually harder to reach.

Activities

COMMUNITY ENGAGEMENTMAG’s CL teams lead on community engagement. Meetings with State, County and Community leaders, identifies both priorities and the need for a safe place to camp. MAG deploys and lives in the community as close as possible to the tasked HA. The CL team is mixed gender, so as to improve access to women, girls, boys and men. CL teams meet with community leaders explaining operations. Efficient mine and cluster munition clearance needs community agreement on phasing to follow harvesting and safety distances whenever MAG is working. The Community Liaison Team will hold repeated meetings with local communities on prioritisation of clearance activities and who needs to be prioritised for MRE Sessions. Focus Group Discussions and household visits will also enable to CL team to ensure the full community is represented and to improve feedback, information sharing and the understanding of planned use/prioritisation of land. CLEARANCE OPERATIONSMAG would allocate MTT1 to Torit for the 2019 dry and rainy season. A desk review of the known minefields, cluster strikes and other HAs would lead to a technical prioritisation of the order of clearance. Activities on the site would include non-technical survey of HAs to reduce where possible the size of minefield to be cleared, mechanically supported Technical Survey of the HA to identify high threat area and mechanical ground preparation to speed clearance. Should UNMAS agree to the deployment of capacity, MAG would undertake similar activities in Jonglei in September 2019. During the wet season, the Bozena will deploy in support of the team. Fitted with a mulching head, it can clear quick-growing, dense vegetation from the cluster strike and allow quick access for clearance teams. The support of both assets is proven to significantly increase outputs. Quality assurance, quality control hand over and completion processes will be carried out in line with National Technical Standards and Guidelines. The primary goal of this project is to clear large areas of minefields and cluster munition strikes that can be handed over and used productively by the community for settlement, cultivation, and grazing. The clearance of larger areas of minefields is particularly beneficial in assuring the safety of IDPs and returnees transiting through unknown areas. The target for land release is 1,420,000 square meters, MAG intends to survey and clear all of the 11 known minefields in Torit county, 2 cluster munition strikes in Torit county, and 8 (of the 18 known) minefields in Fangak and Canal. MAG will also survey and clear all 13 known UXO spot tasks in Torit, Canal, and Fangak. MINE RISK EDUCATIONThrough community mapping and engagement MAG CL teams agree with communities which families and schools need to be prioritised for Mine Risk Education and which households need visits before clearance starts as it will impact them directly. MAG will also ensure that NGO staff who work in the field in both locations are provided MRE on a needs/request basis. MRE beneficiaries include:NNGO staff: 131 men, 75 women; INGO staff: 207 men, 121 women; Community sessions: 600 men, 768 women, 2280 boys, 3552 girls. (7,734 total) CAPACITY BUILDINGThe CL team will train Community Focal Points (CFPs), such as community leaders, teachers and representatives of the communities. The CFPs can sustainably provide risk education after MAG’s teams have left the area, report items as spot tasks and help in the prioritization of clearance tasking. Target: 105 women and 70 men. MAG will also engage national NGOs that are in a position to provide MRE and build the NGO’s capacity to do so effectively. MAG expects the national NGOs to have better access to at-risk populations, and MAG is in a position to strengthen delivery, reporting, assessments and other elements of MRE delivery. Training will be needs based and participants will be given an ‘MRE pack’ of MAG produced and NMAA/UNMAS approved materials.

Does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

Yes

How does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

MAG meets with local authorities, community leaders and other members of the community before starting operations on any task to secure permission to carry out clearance activities. It also provides an opportunity for MAG to alert the community to the hazards of the contamination, the methods used to clear it, the importance of maintaining safe distances from operations. During these meetings and other community liaison visits, MAG also provides information on how to avoid, recognise and report risks, and explains that any reported dangerous items can be addressed by MAG’s roving teams.

Are messages context-specific or/and tailored?

Yes

Briefly describe how

MAG’s teams use risk education and community liaison methodologies that are tailored to the specific needs and behaviours of women, girls, boys and men. MAG is aware that mine and ERW contamination affects the groups differently. Traditional roles leaves men and women open the different threats, with men grazing cattle in areas they are unfamiliar with and women tilling ground that might be contain UXO. MAG addresses these differences with risk education, by providing tailored sessions (activity based education for children, for instance), deploying mixed gender teams to improve access to women, girls, boys and men, and by focussing on men and boys (the group most at risk of accidents) when they return after the grazing season. When training Community Focal Points of national NGO staff to provide MRE, MAG will ensure that a representative number of women are trained. Messaging is also tailored for context. Where MAG teams target IDPs for example, messaging may resemble emergency risk education, wherein essential safety messaging is delivered with a view to target as many people in as short a time as possible. In communities living with more long-term contamination, emphasis may instead be placed on encouraging the reporting of EOD spot tasks, supporting clearance activities, and recognizing markings for dangerous areas.

Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse

Yes

Briefly describe how

MAG considers protection from sexual exploitation and abuse in all its interventions adhering to MAG policies on safeguarding as well as addressing local contexts and elevated risks, such as when working with refugees or IDPs. MAG has a Safeguarding Framework in place that encompasses six key policies including Protection of Children and Vulnerable Adults, Policy on Personal Conduct, Equal Opportunity Policy, Dignity at Work Policy and MAG’s Modern Slavery Statement. The Framework draws together our commitment to ensuring the health, wellbeing and human rights of all staff, partners, and beneficiaries and to provide a safe environment for anyone who comes into contact with our work. It details roles and responsibilities at all levels, from MAG’s board of trustees to individual employees. The framework aims to: set the standards and expectations of MAG’s staff and partners; provide a clear system of how to report concerns as soon as they are identified or suspected; model how MAG will respond rapidly to any concerns and carry out investigations diligently; and provide staff and partners with appropriate training and support. MAG is also currently improving its approach to safeguarding and has a full time safeguarding consultant who is visiting South Sudan in November 2018. The review will assess current culture and practice, strengthen the framework and ensure the maintenance of a safeguarding environment of the highest standard possible. MAG’s Safeguarding Lead will also be visiting the South Sudan programme before the end of 2018 in order to carry out a review. MAG’s overarching aim is to build a culture adhering to the highest standards of awareness, behaviour and practice with respect to safeguarding. The policies apply to all MAG employees and partners. MAG will provide lessons on safeguarding – highlighting sexual exploitation, abuse issues and GBV – when providing capacity building to any national NGO.

Nature of the consultations

Yes

Briefly describe the nature of the consultations

Once deployed, MAG’s Community Liaison Teams will visit the areas where operations are planned to secure permission from local communities to undertake the operations, inform the community as to the activities that will take place, respond to needs and collect information on the nature of contamination in the area. The teams will maintain regular contact with the local communities to respond to changing needs and emerging issues. Community Liaison Teams also conduct focus group discussions with different groups – leaders, elders, women, youths etc. – to establish the plans for cleared land as well as the use of land following clearance. Similar assessments at the household level will also be undertaken. Post-clearance studies will assess the extent to which these plans have been realised. When deploying to areas where national NGOs are able to undertake risk education activities, MAG will offer enhanced training to improve the capacity of the national NGO. Initial consultations would be held to establish needs. Such training could then entail conducting needs assessments, baselines, improving materials, tailoring materials, planning, impact assessments, ensuring equal access within the community, training CFPs, gender awareness and safeguarding practices.

Feedback /complaint mechanisms?

Yes

Briefly describe the mechanisms

As above, during the ongoing consultations with beneficiaries, MAG responds to feedback and complaints. Previously, MAG has drilled boreholes to improve access to water when concerns were raised that teams might affect the community’s supplies. Likewise, MAG tailors the tempo of its operations in relation to feedback expressed by the community. This might include returning for risk education sessions after the grazing season in order to provide messaging to the community’s men and boys or, in an agricultural area clearing cluster strikes once the area nearby has been harvested. During Mine Risk Education sessions, recipients are also provided with phone numbers on which to report items of UXO. Participants are in a position to respond with feedback or complaints during or after the sessions.

Community Engagement?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Owing to the specialised technical nature of MAG’s activities, there is little scope for community monitoring and evaluation. On clearance tasks, UNMAS is responsible for performing quality assurance to evaluate activities on each task. MAG is currently training national staff from the National Mine Action Agency to enable them to perform this quality assurance in the future. MAG’s experienced international technical staff undertake regular quality control of the tasks undergoing clearance. However, MAG’s Community Liaison Teams undertake impact assessments more than six months following clearance in order to see how the communities have used the land, their feelings of safety and whether the clearance has improved their economic opportunities. In order to assess change, the community will have been involved in a pre-clearance study. A proportion of risk education recipients – women, girls, boys and men – are also tested after the session to see whether the messaging has been effective and how the session improved knowledge and safe practices.

Centrality of Protection in humanitarian action
Protection Risk Analysis?

Yes

Briefly describe how

The principles of neutrality, impartiality and independence are embedded in all activities implemented by MAG in South Sudan and globally and ensures that MAG can reach vulnerable people without any political conditions. MAG South Sudan is committed to a “do no harm” approach which guarantees that its action will not contribute to further the conflict. MAG does not take side in disputes or political positions and respects the culture and social structures of the communities where activities are carried out. The villages and communities where MAG works are visited beforehand and any demining activity or risk education session must be approved by relevant local authorities before implementation. Following the July 2016 outbreak of violence and the entrenching of the conflict along ethnic lines, MAG has paid increased attention to the ethnic make-up of its teams. While MAG prefers to deploy teams of mixed ethnicity, security of MAG’s staff dictates the composition of teams in certain areas. Ethnic violence is widespread in South Sudan and the UN has repeatedly warned of the danger of genocide in the country. Protecting our staff is a priority and the topic of teams’ ethnic make-up undergoes constant analysis. In order to prevent and avoid any tension developing between resident communities and MAG's national staff, a security assessment prior to deployment highlights any ethnic concerns and agreement is sought to deploy mixed teams with both the local authorities and communities.

Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

When providing mine risk education, MAG deploys mixed gender teams to improve access to women, girls, boys and men equally. Risk education messaging is tailored to ensure that all groups are engaged and to respond to specific threats posed by mines and other ERW to each group. MAG trains CFPs with a mixture of community leaders, teachers, women and other groups in the community to ensure that there are limited barriers to access the risk education messaging once MAG’s teams have left. MAG’s community liaison team will also undertake focus group discussions with different village communities (teachers, youths, women etc.), and household visits to ensure that all members of beneficiary communities are engaged and have an opportunity to feed into the clearance process and impact measurements. When MAG is aware of a national NGO able to provide risk education in an area of operations, we will offer enhanced training. The combination of MAG’s technical expertise with the national NGO’s better understanding of the area and local relationships will also reduce the barriers among local communities to access risk education sessions, especially in areas deemed insecure for MAG’s teams to operate.

Addresses inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

The clearance of land provides access for accommodation, agriculture and access to resources and services. In agricultural areas, MAG has found that land is put to use almost immediately after clearance to provide food for local communities and markets. Likewise, impact assessments have demonstrated that those displaced by conflict return following clearance, as they know the land to be safe. In this regard, the provision of safe land mitigates the vulnerability of communities – or individuals and families displaced through conflict – by providing a means to establish housing and cultivating/collecting food. As a consequence, many beneficiaries of clearance activities – both direct and indirect – are expected to become more resilient and less reliant on other forms of humanitarian aid.

Does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

MAG’s community liaison teams are flexible, self-sufficient and able to move between communities increasing access to life-saving risk education as much as security allows. In this regard, MAG’s approach itself improves access to beneficiaries. As per MAG’s standard operating procedures, MAG conducts a baseline assessment prior to operations that establishes how contamination has restricted the access of communities to services, infrastructure and resources. Through focus group discussions and household visits, community liaison teams establish the preferred use of land and then assess whether the land has been used as planned following clearance. A significant proportion of land is traditionally planned for access – both to natural resources such as wood or food, as well as to infrastructure (schools) or services.

Does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

MAG monitors feelings of safety pre- and post-clearance. Previous post-clearance impact assessments have demonstrated a clear increase in the feelings of safety. In Peri, for instance, 88% of respondents had reported feeling “very worried” about mines and ERW prior to clearance. In May 2018 during a post-clearance impact assessment, 88% responded feeling “not worried at all”. Likewise, the provision of mine risk education ensures that recipients are able to recognise explosive remnants of war, respond appropriately and report the item for professional clearance. MAG performs post-session tests to measure the effectiveness of risk education messaging, which demonstrates a retention rate in terms of knowledge, attitudes and behaviour that can exceed 90%.

Does the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

Yes

How the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

While the project will not measure the dignity of beneficiaries, the improved ability of local communities and returnees to settle safely and provide for themselves is expected to improve the dignity of beneficiaries. MAG’s staff are also trained to ensure that the dignity of beneficiaries is protected during the course of operations. As noted above, all staff are briefed on MAG’s safeguarding practices and NGO staff trained by MAG will also be expected to adhere to these standards.
