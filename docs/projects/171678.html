Project Summary

In 2021, Relief International(RI) will provide life-saving preventative and curative nutrition services to Pregnant and Lactating Women and Girls (PLWG) and children 6 – 59 months of age from marginalised and displaced communities in Banadir, Hiraan, Galguduud, Lower Shabelle, Mudug and Gedo Regions. With the proposed intervention, RI aims to address the needs of displaced and rural PLWG and children aged 6 – 59 months in need of immediate and sustained nutrition interventions. RI will deploy mobile health and nutrition teams linked to static facilities to serve underserved host and internally displaced communities outside the main urban hubs. These mobile teams will increase access to lifesaving nutrition services to vulnerable people who are unable to routinely access the static health facilities in the target locations. Mobile teams will screen PLW and children aged 6 – 59 months, disseminate key nutrition messages, treat uncomplicated malnutrition cases, refer malnourished children as necessary, counsel mothers on appropriate infant and young child feeding (IYCF/IYCF-E) practices, initiate Mother-to-Mother (M2M) support groups, train mothers on mid-arm circumference (MUAC) measurement, and mobilize target communities around emergency preparedness plans that promote resilience.
RI's nutrition service provision will reach an estimated 185,471 PLW and children, predominantly from IDP and underserved rural and remote households. RI’s nutrition intervention is closely aligned with the Nutrition cluster objectives.
RI’s proposed project will be critical in improving nutrition outcomes in the target locations. Across the country, communities currently face multiple major shocks that combined increase household’s vulnerability to malnutrition: 1) Recurring flooding and droughts that drive displacement, 2) a current locust infestation, 3) and ongoing economic struggles continue to undermine food security and nutrition outcomes across Somalia. According to FSNAU, the 2020 Gu harvest in Southern Somalia is 40% lower than the long-term average from 1995-2019. COVID-19 has exacerbated the situation with restrictions on work due to lockdowns and a reduction in diaspora remittances impacting families’ ability to provide well balanced and nutritious food, with pregnant and lactating women (PLW) and children most affected. According to Somalia’s Demographic Report 2020, 28% of children under-five in Somalia are stunted, while 23% are underweight for their age. An estimated 849,000 children age 6-59 months face acute malnutrition between September 2020 and August 2021, including 143,400 likely to be severely malnourished (Somalia Post Gu Assessment, September 2020).
In Bulo Burte, an area affected by flooding for three consecutive years and currently plagued with locusts, RI's findings were particularly grave with 23% of children screened suffering from Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) and a further 17% from Moderate Acute Malnutrition (MAM). While numbers were lower in Belet Hawa (7% SAM, 17% MAM) and Mataban (7% SAM, 19% MAM), they remain concerning. Outcomes are compounded by a lack of access to services particularly in hard to reach remote areas that are far from the current static health facilities. In Bulo Burte, 79% of respondents interviewed by RI reported they did not have access to nutrition services and while in Mataban, this figure rose to 100% of respondents. In Belet Hawa, 54% reported they did not have routine access. In the proposed areas, FSNAU projects a Critical GAM rate (15-30%).
RI appreciates that mothers living with disabilities as well as mothers of children with disabilities do face additional barriers in accessing nutrition support and services. RI will therefore ensure that these categories are consulted and address barriers to access for nutrition support and services including ensuring inclusion in activities, information sharing and adapting services and facilities to the needs of PWDs.

Mitigation Measures

1. Risk of violence: To mitigate this risk, RI will ensure that services are provided in sites that are safe by 1) Operating in daylight hours; 2) Servicing multiple different communities with mobile teams- especially those marginalised or on the fringes of towns/cities/districts/societies to avoid tensions; 3) Ensuring that beneficiaries are not required to access parts of a city or town they wouldn't otherwise normally access for fear of safety/protection; and 4) By engaging and encouraging husbands to accompany their wives and children when attending nutrition units. 2. Risk of SEA for women and girls: Women and children comprise the majority of patients seen at RI’s health facilities. Data collected from RI’s health centres reveal that in many cases, women and children in need of health and nutrition services travel long distances. In Hiraan for example, RI found, in July 2019, that 30% of patients travelled more than 10km to reach health facilities. These journeys invariably expose these vulnerable groups to protection risks, as they traverse through unfamiliar and insecure environments. To ensure the safety and protection of community members accessing RI’s nutrition services, RI will select safe and accessible sites for the mobile nutrition units after consultation with community structures, including those representing minorities, women, and youth. With the mobile nutrition intervention, RI aims to reduce patients’ long travel times and thus reduce their exposure to risks. Services will be provided during the day and with female staff members present to attend to the needs of women and girls. The sites will also be accessible to people with disabilities. Men, boys, and leaders will be engaged to support the mitigation of risks. 3. Risk of marginalized group being excluded: This project is directly seeking to reach women, girls and boys in need. As such, RI is aware of the fact that these groups attribute the majority of malnutrition cases. Therefore, RI will 1) consult past projects, local health staff, community leaders and religious leaders in determining where best and how best to include women, girls and boys in decision processes; 2) Actively seek to meet the needs and wishes and requirements of these groups with the use of their input into service provision; 3) Engage female community health workers to go out into communities and meet and speak with women and their children regarding project activities; and 4) Ensure project sites are accessible and in convenient and safe areas for people with disabilities as well as for women and children from informal settlements.
