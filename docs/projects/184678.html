Project Summary

Multiple hazards including, drought, floods, cyclones and conflict have contributed to weakening Somalia's health system for the past three decades, exacerbated further by the COVID-19 pandemic since Somalia’s first reported case on 16 March 2020. To date, WHO has supported the Federal MOH to respond to COVID-19 by: providing coordination/technical support; distributing Personal Protective Equipment (PPEs) to frontline health workers, medicines, and medical equipment; establishing level 2 biosafety laboratories ; and collecting and analyzing epidemiological data to facilitate evidence-based decision making. As of 02 November 2021, A total of 22,369 confirmed COVID-19 cases in Somalia, including 1,238 reported deaths, with the majority of cases reported in Banadir, Somaliland and Puntland affecting different population groups with varying levels of severity. Modelling studies conducted by WHO have demonstrated that COVID-19 has severely strained the provision of essential health services especially maternal and child health. Indeed, child mortality increased by 13%, while vaccination coverage for measles amongst children aged below five years reduced by 20% since the COVID-19 epidemic. The number of cases of epidemic prone diseases reported from districts affected by drought and floods in Early Warning Alert and Response Network (EWARN) attributed to climatic shocks have increased since June 2021: cholera cases by 20% from 2933 to 3551; acute diarrhoeal cases by 15% from 51,760 to 59,786; and malaria cases by 38% from 1837 to 2536. The number of cases of these diseases are expected to increase as a result of further floods and drought expected in 2022 that will negatively affect access to safe water and proper sanitation amongst displaced communities. Similarly, due to the reduction of the number of children vaccinated against measles, cases reported during the same period increased by 24% from 2090 to 2585. Moreover, there has been a 33% increase in the number of trauma cases from 5033 in September 2020 to 6693 in September 2021. The capacity to manage the trauma cases in Somalia is very limited. It is in this regard that improved community-based management of targeted diseases could bridge the critical gap in the availability of essential health services at the health facility level.
In addition to the above, there is an urgent need for improving trauma care, medical waste management, access to medical oxygen and access to mental health services. For trauma care, a primary assessment conducted by WHO in 2020 across 21 hospitals concluded that trauma imposes a heavy burden on the Somali health system, with approximately 50% of all civilian trauma cases resulting from conflict. WHO has thus identified the urgent need for developing a more robust trauma care system. In the absence of a national policy on medical waste management, handful of hospitals and health facilities in the major cities outsource the services through private contractors, and a little information is available on their current practices. Considering the significant negative environmental impact, a joint systemic assessment is under planning, after which a the national medical waste management policy will be developed, followed by the infrastructures development to ensure the appropriate management of the biohazard materials. Medical oxygen remains in critical shortage across Somalia. To urgently address this vital life-saving gap, WHO is seeking to leverage on cost-effective medical innovations such as the instalment of solar powered oxygen concentrators at the peripheral health facilities and hospitals, which provide an environmentally friendly and uninterrupted supply of medical oxygen for all types of patients. Finally, mental health remains a major burden across Somalia, with almost no services available and approximately one in three Somalis suffering from some form of mental health illness.

4.6 - Mitigation Measures

The environment under which health activities are implemented in Somalia is always insecure and unpredictable. Communities affected by conflict and people living in IDPs are exposed to a number of risk due to the vulnerabilities created by conflict and displacement. Women and girls are usually exposed to sexual exploitation and gender-based violence. People living with disabilities and the elderly are usually not included during the planning of different activities. There is always segregation of minorities during the provision of essential medical and humanitarian services by majority clans. In circumstances where health services are provided far away from camps, the people may be attached physically by armed groups leading to physical and psychological harm.Mitigation Measures. To mitigate the foreseeable risks, the following contingency measures to mitigate these risks will be undertaken;(i) Community engagement through local leaders will be undertaken prior to the implementation of activities (ii) regular coordination meetings with local community leaders to ensure dialogue and early detection of any challenges and provide feedback in timely manner (iii) Equal opportunity will be provided to both men; women and PLWD during trainings and provision of primary health services (iv) WHO is committed to protecting women against sexual exploitation as explained in the prevention and response to the prevention sexual exploitation and abuse manual, measures highlighted there in will be observed during the implementation of health activities (v) all operations are guided by the United Nations Security regulations which promotes a safe environment for the implementation of different activities.
