Project Summary

UNICEF will implement Myanmar formal education curriculum for kindergarten to grade 12 by working with implementing partners (IPs) on learners’ enrolment, attendance, transition of learners to the next grade, and teachers capacity development as well as community engagement. 4,393 learning facilities (LFs) will provide access and the necessary teaching and learning materials.
For inclusive education, UNICEF, in partnership with Humanity Inclusion, will ensure inclusive practices are mainstreamed by providing services directly to children, capacitating partners, teachers and community, Rohingya disability volunteers will be assigned per camp and use the WGQ for screening, teachers will receive pedagogy training for working with children with disabilities, model LCs will be established to serve as examples as well as a policy for children with moderate disabilities will be developed.
For equitable education, UNICEF will mainstream gender responsive education activities through capacity development of key stakeholders, teacher training on gender responsive pedagogy, and strategies for improving attendance of adolescent female learners (AFL). Access to education for AFL will be facilitated by providing gender segregated classes for which female Rohingya volunteers will be assigned; they will walk with the AFLs to and from the LCs and attend classes with them.
For quality education, teacher professional development (TPD) programs will be implemented for teachers and master trainers (MTs) for teaching teaching the Myanmar curriculum for KG to grade 12. For teachers from KG to grade 11, they will receive grade and subject specific TPD to improve pedagogical skills and content knowledge. Teachers will also be enrolled in language courses and receive PSEA, Child Safeguarding, DRR & asessment training. Mentor teachers and master trainers will be available to further guide and support teachers. For learning assessment, teachers will receive training to conduct formative assessment in the classroom and children in grade 3 and higher sit an end-of-year summative assessment. A record of learning will be provided for enrolled children upon completion of the school year. The use of technology for education (T4E) will support taking attendance and recording learning assessment data, TPD and support teachers with interactive audio lessons based on Myanmar curriculum.
Community engagement will continue with Rohingya caregivers and the community education support groups (CESGs), which are established per cluster and meet monthly. The Rohingya caregivers, the CEGSs, and implementing partners (IP) staff will receive training on Minimum Community Engagement quality standards, PSEA, Child Safeguarding, gender and girls’ education, and disability and inclusive education, community mobilisation as well as DRR. Capacity development of IP staff will also include training on data management to meet the Data Quality Assessment standards for reliability and integrity. Engaging with religious and community leaders to address harmful social norms that create barriers in girls' education and partnerships with men and boys as champions.
UNICEF will implement skill programmes for out-of-school (OOS) adolescents through cross-sectoral interventions in Multi-Purpose Centres; those aged 15 to 18 years will have access to Lifeskills as well as literacy and numeracy courses.
In the host communities of Teknaf and Ukhyia, UNICEF will support 3000 adolescents, including those with disabilities, to access vocational skills.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

As the Rohingya response enters the 8th year, with the military coup in Myanmar, the return of Rohingya can take longer and in the meantime the number of Rohingya children continues to grow with approximately 30,000 new-borns every year. With the growing number of Rohingya children and with their movement being restricted to the camps and without a refugee status, only the humanitarian response can meet their growing needs. In such a context, education continues to be a crucial need for the Rohingya community as access to quality education can reduce instances of exploitation, and support community resilience with positive coping mechanisms. With the education needs growing, the reduction in funding will pose a great risk to ensuring quality education is available for all children. There will be increased need for learning spaces as the change in curriculum requires more classrooms and the growing number of children who will transition from one grade to the next will also require more learning spaces to be available. Along with learning spaces, having sufficiently qualified teachers is a challenge. It will take time to train all teachers for improving their language and pedagogy skills as well as for them to develop a stronger grasp of the required content knowledge. It is currently very challenging to find adequate number of teachers to teach higher secondary, especially female teachers. Having insufficiently qualified teachers conducting lessons can further exasperate the perception of education that adolescents share where they do not see education as a priority (JMSNA, 2023). Along with providing access and qualified teachers, efforts need to be placed for changing perception of education held by adolescents. IRC report further highlighted the state of learning of Rohingya children by pointing to key education needs: gender responsive and inclusive education, teacher recruitment as there is a lacking number of qualified teachers for Myanmar curriculum and mitigating further perpetuating the marginalization of the Rohingya community. UNICEF with this project proposal is looking to take steps towards addressing these challenges by advocating for learning spaces, capacitating teachers, and IP staff, and continuing effective community engagement activities. As per ISNA data, 59% of the children aged 3 to 18 are enrolled and accessing education services from different learning facilities. While 41% of children of the same age group are not enrolled or do not receive the education services. Overall, the enrolment rate is higher comapred to girls ( boys 62% and girls 57%) across the differnt educatin facilities. According to the Education Sector approved age group, the analysis reveals that only 8% of girls aged 15 to 18 are enrolled in educaiton facilities, while 75% of girls aged 5 to 14 are reciving ecducation services. 81% of chlidren are enrolled in Learnng Centrers (LCs), 14% in Commuity-Based Learning Facilities (CBLFs), 4% in multi-purpose centers (MPCs) and 1% in mixed -use or shared spaces. The latest Year End assessment results from Grade 3 to Grade 10 showed that boys achieved higher scores than girls across all subjects. Among the four subjects, learners scored the highest in English, averaging 63.99%. Mathematics had the lowest average score, with learners achieving an average of 36.32% across all grades. The lowest percentage was in grade 3 with 32.9% while the highest mark was achieved in grade 5 with 48.6%. Learners found Mathematics and science most difficult with 30% and 31% needing support respectively. More support in teaching mathematics and science is required and the provision of practical science lessons.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

UNICEF engages with the Education Sector, Protection Sector, Child Protection (CP) Sub-Sector, and relevant stakeholders to ensure that accessible reporting and referral mechanisms are in place which support a survivor-informed response to incident notifications. For example, UNICEF works in-conjunction with our CP team to ensure protection measures and referrals across the learning centres and multi-purpose centers. Protection mainstreaming focuses on the four key elements:-Prioritising safety and dignity by providing safe and secure learning facilities where IP staff and teachers are trained in child safeguarding and PSEA – this reduces vulnerability to physical and psychosocial risks. Additionally, Rohingya volunteers are specifically available to ensure safety and dignity of children with disabilities and adolescent female learners, two vulnerable groups. Rohingya disability volunteers will be assigned for each camp and Rohingya female volunteers are assigned for AFL classes, they walk with the AFLs to and back from the learning centers and attend classes with them to ensure safety. -Ensuring meaningful access to education for all through focused efforts for improving and retaining children with disabilities and adolescent female learners through targeted strategies, capacity development, and community engagement. -Fostering accountability by ensuring feedback is regularly collected by implementing partners from Rohingya community, teachers, and learners as well as monitoring visits and spot checks conducted by UNICEF and third-party monitoring institution. -Strengthening participation and empowerment through community engagement activities and capacity development of community as well as IP staff. CESGs are an example of how UNICEF and implementing partners ensure that affected populations know their right to education, including where/how to access education services and are supported, involved, and consulted about education of Rohingya children.

2.3 - Age & Disability

UNICEF will provide access to and deliver quality inclusive early childhood education primary and secondary education, and skills development opportunities for refugee crisis affected children, and adolescents aged 3-18 years. Adults aged 18 or above will be employed as volunteers in the schools. For the JRP 2025, UNICEF will reach 90% of children aged 5-14, 35% of children aged 4, 25% of children aged 3 and 30% of children aged 15-18. The reach for children with disabilities across all age groups and programs is 2.36% UNICEF will focus on inclusion of Children with Disabilities in schools, aiming to reach 2.36% of all enrolled learners. Activities will include outreach in communities to identify children who may have a disability through Rohingya Disability Volunteers and then screening these children using the Child Functioning Module of the Washington Group Questions (WGQ). Following this, children who are identified as maybe having a disability will be referred to Humanity Inclusion (Handicap International) for further assessment and to identify if assistive devices or other reasonable accommodations are required to support that child. The Rohingya Disability Volunteers will advocate with parents to allow Children with Disabilities to access education and will work with the children and HI to put in place reasonable accommodations, including home learning if this is required. Disability and inclusion will be mainstreamed across interventions such as in teacher trainings and the use of collaborative and cooperative pedagogical techniques.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

UNICEF’s work in the context of the Rohingya response, as with all emergency contexts, is informed by the Core Commitments to Children in Humanitarian Action, Accountability to Affected Population (AAP) and Building Back Better principles. For accountability to affected population, UNICEF will continue and strengthen the feedback mechanisms deployed by implementing partners. These mechanisms include feedback from the monthly meetings of community education support groups, meetings with caregivers, teacher learning circles and the suggestions boxes that are placed at the learning centers, Information Feedback Centres and feedback from adolescents. Additionally, UNICEF staff conduct field monitoring visits, and a third-party firm is contracted to also conduct monitoring visits to learning centers. UNICEF officers and specialist follow up with partners to ensure appropriate actions are taken to address feedback from these mechanisms in a timely manner. Implementing partners are also held accountable by UNICEF and are assessed for responsiveness and timely actions that are taken to address feedback.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

A zero-tolerance policy and approach on exploitation and abuse of children and young people is in-place to ensure the protection of children, adolescents and adults from sexual exploitation and abuse and sexual harassment (including staff). Importantly, these policies also apply to partners who support the implementation of programmes in support of UNICEF and partners. This includes access to incident reporting mechanisms, affording protection to individuals under whistleblower policies and referral of survivors to appropriate assistance services in line with the United Nations Victim Assistance Protocol including MHPSS and health services. Further, UNICEF supports implementing partners with responding to safeguarding and PSEA, ensuring clear findings, actions and recommendations are in-place. All implementing partners must comply with the policies on the protection of sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA). Safeguarding requirements are also stipulated for contractors and suppliers and in PDs. Staff, volunteers and associates of the UNICEF and their implementing partners will be trained on IASC guidelines on GBV and community outreach on PSEA will be undertaken. For PSEA, training will continue to be provided to all volunteers and IP staff, reporting mechanism/channels will be strengthened, and PSEA focal points will receive regular capacity developing on investigations. Monitoring visits will also be conducted to spot check understanding of PSEA and the reporting mechanism by IP staff, teachers, and learners. Communication materials are disseminated to create awareness among the affected population, on SEA including prevention, reporting and victim/survivor support. All UNICEF partners have committed to adhering to the ‘Zero Tolerance SEA Policy’. All UNICEF partners have been assessed on PSEA and have tailored PSEA actions plans. Ongoing PSEA plans ensure all partners have a low-risk rating on PSEA.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Yes – education programmes incorporate climate action including DRR and anticipatory actions and climate change adaptation. Information on risks and vulnerability are circulated prior to events occurring through mixed modalities (such as via WhatsApp, public communication messages and focal points). There are pre-agreed technical and operational plans to deliver activities, including to protect children, teachers and infrastructure, and pre-defined resources for fast-release financing. UNICEF maintains a dynamic thinking about risks including the deteriorating security environment. Within LFs, teacher training includes DRR and PSS whilst CESGs are trained on DRR and community mobilisation. Learners learn about prevalent risks in the camps in the LFs. Each LF cluster develop culture and practices to enable them to proactively anticipate, plan for, and respond earlier to protect children and their right to education from predictable hazards. An intersectional approach is used to understand the needs, strengths, and vulnerabilities of different groups of boys, girls, and women and men involved in education, including children with disabilities, ensuring that risk analyses information from girls and boys themselves, including children and youth not currently accessing education opportunities. LCs are child-focused community structures that often serve as a highly localized hub for organizing community-led disaster risk reduction and response efforts and can be used as shelters in emergencies. In addition to responding to the external shocks, early warning and mobilisation, CESGs play a key role in transforming community behaviour and perceptions of climate action and in greening projects. Where possible in the LCs and solar panels are used for generating power which is environmentally friendly. New construction techniques will be used to reduce reliance on bamboo and to be more weather and hazard resistant.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

UNICEF and partners promote and ensure emergency preparedness for hazards, worsening camp security and for new arrivals through EPR measures including a business continuity plan to sustain education and critical EiE services. EPR measures are embedded in the programme at a variety of levels and are focused on protecting learners and volunteers from harm, minimising the disruption and ensuring the continuity of education for all children (including new arrivals) and developing and maintaining a culture of safety. EPR LF management, safer LF infrastructure and EPR education are three pillars of the emergency response SOPs. These involve communities, learners, parents and caregivers and teachers on emergency response measures and continuity plans, as well as IP staff. CESGs prepare individualized contingency plans for clusters of LFs, and IP staff coordinate in an emergency situation and provide technical support to the CESGs. These EPR plans are to reduce the impact of natural hazards such as cyclones, tropical storms, flooding (and drowning), fires and safety threats including harassment, kidnapping, violence, early marriage, forced recruitment which are increasingly common threats in the Rohingya camps. UNICEF has EPR measures embedded in the programme including community-based learning and instruction and distance learning supported by Ed-Tech (IAI). Continency funds are available for the rapid mobilistion of resources, staffing and emergency response activities and there are pre-positioned items in the Learning Facilities and in the warehouses. The security and safety of individuals is taken extremely seriously, with LFs closed if there are deteriorating risks. UNICEF works closely with other teams including Child Protection and Social Behaviour Change (SBC) on EPR. In readiness for extreme situations, UNICEF uses a damage tracker to quickly assess, and report damage related to LF infrastructure for the prioritisation of repairs. All LFs have a fire extinguisher.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

The project includes adolescents and youth aged 15-18 providing them with skills development through MC, literacy and numeracy classes, life skills, and vocational/trade training of their choice based on the availability of the trade training in the learning facility in six-month courses. Embedded within the programme are modules on leadership training, entrepreneurship and business development training as well as a number of internship/apprenticeship opportunities and other post-graduate support. Adolescents and youth are also engaged through adolescent action groups to exercise their rights and to actively participate in decision-making processes and to harness adolescent and youth skills.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

Yes - through the certified teacher training programmes, volunteer teachers are receiving high-quality teacher training, promoting the self-reliance of the community to maintain education initiatives and programmes for themselves with reducing support required from host community volunteers and partners. Teacher training content is reviewed, edited to and added to by the volunteer teachers, ensuring their experiences, knowledges and skills are reflected in the course and to ensure ownership their ownership over the programme. The mentor volunteer model is a best practice CPD approach where a more experienced volunteer teacher supports and guides new volunteer teachers. Capacity building of CESGs to strengthen the linkages between learners, teachers and parents contribute to the long-term sustainability of educational initiatives. Technical products such as Interactive Audio Instructions, the MC assessment framework are valuable also outside of the camp context.

4.2 Do project activities in the host community link to development initiatives, and/or align with development priorities in the Cox’s Bazar district? If yes, briefly describe.If yes, briefly describe.

YES - Skills development (vocational skills, lifeskills and foundation literacy) continues to empower out-of-school Bangladeshi adolescents and youth—particularly girls and young women—by promoting leadership roles in their homes and communities. Through vocational training, internships, literacy skills and livelihood opportunities graduates contribute to their families’ financial well-being and develop skills that foster self-reliance. By offering the opportunity to learn a trade, secure employment, or start a business, the programme reduces vulnerability to violence and supports financial independence. UNICEF's programme is implemented through the Bureau for Non-Formal Education (BNFE) under the Ministry of Primary and Mass Educations a national pilot in Cox's Bazar district for scale-up across Bangladesh. This is aligned with the TAPP. The programme delivers literacy focused skills training through three models.
