Project Summary

Somalia continued to be under crisis in 2021 with continued conflict, political instability, economic decline, increasing food insecurity, climatic shocks, disease outbreaks including COVID-19, and the worst desert locust infestation in years.. Some 5.9 million people are in need of humanitarian assistance across 74 districts in Somalia in 2021.
The 2022 Humanitarian Needs Overview states that the high humanitarian needs in Somalia are driven by seasonal floods, severe drought conditions, acute water diarrhea/ cholera outbreaks and protracted crises, exacerbated by the COVID-19 outbreak. Drought remains the main driver of WASH needs in Somalia leading to displacement and communal conflict over water points.
According to the WHO-UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) 2020, only 39 per cent of people in rural areas have access to water supply. 39 per cent of vulnerable people in Somalia do not have access to sustained, improved and safe water source for drinking and other domestic uses, while 84 per cent still walk for more than 15 minutes to the nearest water source. Due to these constraints, one in five Somalis do not have enough water per day to cover their basic needs for both drinking and domestic use. 92 per cent of the households don’t have water collection and storage containers further impacting their regular access to water. It is estimated that 1.3 million people in 24 drought hotspot locations hugely depend on water trucking during drought due to frequent breakdown or and dry-up of strategic water sources. This is quite unsustainable.
Access to improved sanitation facilities in Somalia is very low. JMCNA 2021 noted that 57 percent of households do not have access to improved sanitation facilities. Additionally, 23 per cent of people across Somalia still defecate in the open, 49 per cent of which are in rural areas. There are 1,507 IDP sites which still have less than 1 latrine per 50 individuals as per the recommended WASH Cluster minimum standards.
Over half of all IDPs reportedly still faced challenges in terms of access to soap and hand washing facilities according to JMCNA 2021. Only 53 per cent of households across Somalia practise proper hand washing with soap and water while (39 percent) reported that they wash their hands but without soap and only 45 per cent of households have access to Menstrual Hygiene Management (MHM) material at home or can access it through their local market.
According to the HNO 2022, the population in need of WASH services in 2022 includes approximately 1,619,415 IDPs and 4,818,944 vulnerable host communities. It's estimated that 6,058,603 people (38 per cent) in the 'Severe' category lack access to safe water and proper sanitation services and are highly vulnerable to AWD and cholera and around 379,757 people (2 percent) are in extreme condition as they access water directly from rivers and surface bonds, practice open defecation and lack access to soap and other essential hygiene materials and are susceptible to communicable diseases
Women and girls are exposed to protection risks including gender-based violence (GBV) when collecting water from too distant water sources or using unimproved overcrowded sanitation facilities which lack safety and protection features.
CPD is targeting 24,900 people and will train gender-balanced management committees in order to avoid over reliance on unsustainable means of accessing clean water by rehabilitating strategic reliable water points. Committees will also be established to maintain clean sanitation facilities, as well as scale up hygiene promotion initiatives to help minimize AWD/cholera outbreaks during the rainy seasons. Marginalized groups will be consulted in all project phases including design and implementation and ensuring that the location, distance and access routes to the water points, latrines and distribution sites are accessible to all members of the community, including the disabled.

1. - Projects activities

Emergency Water Services 1) Rehabilitation of 3 strategic existing boreholes, 12 strategic berkads and 12 strategic shallow wells. The boreholes are to be fitted with solar powered systems, water tank, animal trough, water kiosks and laying of pipeline to distribute the water. The berkads will be lined to prevent seepage and roofed to prevent evaporation. The shallow wells will be protected. Up to 6km of water piping from strategic boreholes to IDPs and vulnerable host communities will be done through cash for work. 10 water pans will also be rehabilitated using cash for work. It is expected that 24,900 women, girls, boys and men from (4,150 HH) will be involved in the design and implementation of these projects. 2) Establishment of 37 water committee to ensure proper maintenance of water servicesThe committees will incorporate women, youth and people living with disability. 3) Provision and distribution of 15 water bladders (each with a capacity of 10m³) to increase water access at strategic water points that lack adequate storage facilities. People with disability, women and girls will have easy access to water within reasonable distance. 4) A bulk chlorination treatment Programme will be put in place to disinfect water at points of use and water storage facilities. 5) Carryout monitoring on water quality at community level. Delivery of sanitation services7) Construction/rehabilitation of 500 lockable gender segregated and disability friendly communal and school latrines with hand washing facilities for IDPs and host communities in Galmudug, South West State, Hirshabelle and Banadir regions. This will include construction of 60 gender segregated twin latrines for 15 schools in the target areas. 8) Carry out 12 cleaning campaigns awareness sessions, focusing on solid waste removal and disposal focusing on behavior change and AWD/cholera prevention targeting 2,500 households for a period of 6 months. 9) Desludge 120 pits/septic tanks in every IDP camp in the targeted 20 camps. 10) Carry out environmental cleaning including spraying to control vectors in 20 target IDP camps and host communities. 11) Design and locate water and sanitation facilities that take into account people living with disabilities, women and youth to incorporate protection and accessibility measures while liaising with local community leaders and Camp Management committees. Designs to take care of the disabled needs will include rails in toilets and ramps rather than stairs in accessing wash facilities.Hygiene Promotion Activities 12) Carry out 5 days hygiene promotion training incorporating Covid-19 WHO guidelines for 120 hygiene volunteers (at least 40% being women) selected from the IDPs and host communities in the targeted areas. Thereafter, disseminate health and hygiene promotion messages including Covid-19 messaging to the target populations by the 300 trained hygiene volunteers using IEC messages, radio programmes, personal hygiene awareness, latrine use and food handling. 13) Carry out 12 Hygiene and sanitation awareness raising sessions targeting 5,440 households for a period of 6 months. 14) Distribute 4,000 hygiene kits (Each kits comprising of Washing Soaps, Aqua tabs, water collection, storage jerry cans, Menstrual Hygiene management kits for women and girls) to the most vulnerable IDPs and host communities in the target areas. CPD will develop a detailed work plan and implement by closely monitoring project activities on regular basis to ensure that all activities are undertaken in accordance with agreed outputs. CPD will also ensure that beneficiary feedback is taken into account and feeds into the project learning to improve implementation efficiency. High quality photos with GPS and time stamps depicting various activities planned will be provided as documentary evidence of implementation. 2.1.1 - What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

Empowerment: The availability of cash gives households a sense of restored power over their immediate situation. In addition, there is evidence that receiving cash can at least temporarily empower women within the household. Multiplier effects: By stimulating local markets, the cash transfers will benefit more than the direct recipients of cash. Local traders and service providers, transport workers and suppliers and transporters of goods may all benefit from households having money to spend, thereby having a positive effect in the whole local economy. Flexibility and choice: One size does not fit all! Using cash or vouchers recognizes that beneficiaries are best placed to decide what their households need, and gives them the flexibility to choose according to their own diverse needs and priorities. Dignity: In allowing beneficiaries to control what goods and services their households need, cash transfers are considered by many as more dignified than receiving in kind, and recognize beneficiaries as active participants in providing for the welfare of their families after a disaster. Supports or revitalizes local trade: Injections of cash or the use of commodity vouchers with local traders benefits the recovery of trade and the local economy. Cash-based support for livelihood rehabilitation can potentially also provide a link between relief and longer term economic recovery and may offer the opportunity for beneficiaries to increase income and create savings. Reduces negative coping strategies: In the modern world, most households depend on cash to some extent. When aid is given in kind, households are often forced to seek cash in ways that may be detrimental to longer term livelihoods, for example selling productive assets, or giving children to the care of relatives. Evidence shows that cash transfers reduce the negative coping strategies used by beneficiaries. Time and cost efficient for agencies and participants: When goods are available and accessible locally, cash or voucher-based interventions have the potential to be more rapid than bringing supply from out of country. When beneficiaries use local markets, agencies also eliminate logistical and import costs.

2.1.2 - Explain how the project design considered market functionality?

The project will use the Market Functionality Index (MFI) designed by WFP which is part of the new Business Process Model for cash-based transfers. It is made to support the ‘Market assessment and risk identification’ and 'Market situation monitoring' processes of cash-based assistance operations. Using the MFI, CPD has carried out market assessments that took the following into consideration;1) Assortment of essential goods,2) Availability3) Price4) Resilience of supply chains5) Market competition, 6) Infrastructure7) Services8) Access and protection. The project also considers use of the Somali shilling and its acceptance or not in some areas as well as the wide use of mobile money transfer by most of the population in Somalia.

4.6 - Mitigation Measures

Risk that minority clans/ethnic groups are not included in consultations and/or are excluded from humanitarian services/assistance.The project will promote equitable and impartial access for all by:• Consulting with every affected group before making any decision at every stage of the project.• Ensuring that assistance and services are reaching the most vulnerable by Identifying and prioritizing the most vulnerable groups in the community and prevent discrimination or exclusion of marginalized groups.• Applying a systematic outreach process in registering beneficiaries in order to reach all vulnerable groups.• Ensuring lack of documentation does not exclude individuals from accessing services• Ensuring that the location, distance and access routes to the water points, latrines and distribution sites are accessible to all members of the community, including the disabled and most vulnerable groups.• Separating male and female queuing areas and ensure female staff presence at distributions so that they are accessible to women and girls. Making infrastructure adaptions to WASH structures to make them accessible to persons with reduced mobility (eg. persons with physical disability, older persons). • Establishing mechanisms to give priority to those who cannot stand in line for long periods, such as older persons, persons with disability, unaccompanied children, pregnant and lactating women, so that they wait for less time. • Considering the gender balance in all project phases and in distribution teams and committees to ensure that there are enough women available as a contact point for women in the community. • Ensuring that beneficiaries know about WASH services, how to obtain hygiene kits, where and when the distributions will take place, what they will get, how long it should last and how to use the items. • Providing information through various communication means to reach the broader community and to account for different literacy levels eg. door-to-door, poster, radio, social media, use of pictograms. • Using this information to inform monitoring activities and identify any barriers to access, discrimination against particular groups or whether assistance is being diverted. Take this into consideration when determining locations for WASH interventions. Risk that beneficiaries are not accessing/using complaint and feedback mechanisms. This will be minimized by; • Being transparent with the affected populations by providing them with accessible and timely information on selection criteria for targeted assistance, organizational procedures and processes that affect them. • Setting up accessible, confidential and well understood feedback mechanisms for suggestions and complaints with a view to improve programming, understand community perceptions, promote beneficiary empowerment and assist in detecting misconduct. Eg. place feedback surveys in hygiene kits, feedback box at WASH facilities, phone lines • Setting up mechanisms for submitting feedback that do not require the beneficiary exposing themselves to project staff. • Considering a joint feedback mechanism with other sectors to minimize confusion. • WASH staff and committees should be representative of all groups within the community (e.g. gender, age, ethnicity, socioeconomic group, disability) and all staff and members should be given protection mainstreaming training. Risk of land disputes and tenure insecurity in targeted project area. Risk of dispute on land ownership/land tenure and/or contamination by explosive hazards in targeted project area.• Taking into account the land tenure arrangements of IDPs, including statutory/legislative and customary access rights to land and inheritance rights. Advocate with land and property owners and local authorities to avoid eviction or disproportional rising in rent. • Obtaining permission before using or building on any land or property, in writing, where possible.
