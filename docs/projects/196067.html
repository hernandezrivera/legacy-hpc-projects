Project Summary

Several assessment reports published by UNHCR and other partners revealed that there is a deficiency in the provision of basic services such as health, education, water and sanitation, food and nutrition relief, protection and livelihood opportunities due to reduced/lack of funding level in each of the settlement camps across South Sudan targeting asylum seekers and spontaneous refugees returns. This project is designed to address these deficiencies focusing more on improving opportunities for youth in refugee camps through livelihoods training, improving the nutrition status of children between 6 to 59 months through nutrition supplementary feeding, improving access to primary health care services for vulnerable groups such as the elderly, women, girls and those persons with disability as well as improving the provision of safe, clean, and reliable water in camp and surrounding community. The project sets out to the protection of asylum seekers in Juba and other major refugees camps in South Sudan, provide a multi-sectoral response to refugees, and monitor spontaneous refugee returns to Pochalla, Maiwut and Melut, Magwi and Kapoeta Counties in South Sudan, and as well as providing Protection and Prevention Services to the most vulnerable children (boys and girls). This project will ensure the most vulnerable South Sudanese children (boys and girls aged 12-17 years old) exposed to conflict-related violence are reached with protection, impact mitigation, and prevention services in Pochalla, Melut, Kapoeta East, Maiwut, and Magwi counties of South Sudan. It will also foster increased respect for international Humanitarian laws (IHL) and international Human rights laws (IHRL) and accountability for violations against children through documenting and monitoring violations to inform advocacy and increase accountability.
This project is in line and contributes to the Protection Cluster objective that aims to ensure vulnerable South Sudanese have access to protection services that mitigate the effects of occupation and conflict-related violence.
The main objective of this child protection project is for children in the target counties to be safe, protected, and secure from violence, exploitation, and grave violations while in schools, in their communities, churches, playing ground, and are less exposed to violence and danger. The project will:
• support initiatives to engage vulnerable and at-risk children positively in structured recreational, educational, awareness-raising, life skills, and resilience-building activities to improve their resilience and coping mechanisms. Child-Friendly Spaces will be established to address the psychosocial needs of children who have experienced distress, sexual abuse, children separated from their families, those who have been trafficked, exposed to physical violence, and those forced into armed groups.
• support protective services and awareness-raising for at-risk children and families to inform children and families about their rights and where to seek assistance in case of violence, arrest, and/or detention.
• support vulnerable children previously arrested and detained by warring forces with integration and rehabilitation services especially structured counseling and MHPSS support.
• support capacities of families, community members, and leaders to identify, prevent and respond to violence against children through families and community group discussions, counseling and awareness-raising sessions, and training.
• support incident monitoring, data analysis, and reporting on grave violation trends related to child protection concerns to provide evidence and inform advocacy. Data collection and monitoring will be enhanced through documentation of testimonies of children in detention centers. This project will support the ongoing UNHCR programs in the country through provision of lifesaving basic services, where possible, and will ensure that government and community capacity is built.

1 - Needs

The conflict has claimed innumerable lives and left millions forcibly displaced from their homes and unable to meet even their most basic needs. More than two million people have been turned into refugees and another two million internally displaced. In total, 7.74m (62.7%) people are in need of urgent humanitarian assistance. Owed to the previous COVID-19 pandemic that resulted to movement restrictions, school closures, job losses, and socio-economic pressures have exacerbated vulnerabilities in South Sudan with increased reported incidences of negative coping mechanisms including domestic violence, child labor, and child marriages. Many injured or previously injured children (formerly within combatant ranks) remain in need of continuing case management support, in some cases requiring previously closed cases to be re-opened. An urgent need has been identified for the scale-up of monitoring and documentation, to ensure data collection, verification and reporting, and timely analysis to inform the humanitarian response. This is clear in light of the limited capacities of protection partners to conduct monitoring activities to the extent required in the context of the current violence and previous COVID-19 crisis. In addition to this, there has been violence conflict between refugees and host communities in South Sudan particularly in Maban, Juba, Gorom, Magwi and many other locations. reduced capacity of the government, and lack of funding by partners had reduced protection mesh accorded to the refugees and asylum seekers in the counties, making it a major protection concern. As of March 2022, there is currently 2.02m IDPs in South Sudan ((344,043 IDPs in Central Equatoria State with majority in Juba, Yei, and Lainye, 109,739 in Eastern Equatoria with majority in Kajo-Keji, and Magwi, and 207,004 in Upper Nile with IDPs Maban and Melut (Q1 2022))- (UHNCR Reports). These counties are either in emergency IPC or crisis phase IPC3,4 and 5 as of July 2022. The risk of exclusion is significant in IDP settlements or returnees coming back home. There is a low level of understanding and acceptance of the rights of refugee returnees and IDPs and refugees and returnees both within the host community and with authorities, as well as prevailing misconceptions of respective roles and responsibilities. There is a lack of mutual trust, respect, and recognition for the need for social cohesion, inclusion, and integration between IDP, returnees, and host communities. In the absence of the government authorities providing IDPs and returnees with assistance and protection, informal managers have often become the primary go-to person and provider of support and, to a certain extent, protection. This dependency can lead to exclusion from services that are provided. Returnees are faced with similar risks of exclusion after an absence from the country to prolonged periods. CRADA’s protection activities aim to counter exclusion risks as part of the Centrality of Protection IDPs and returnees continue to face a myriad of protection risks, including lack of access to basic services, risk of exploitation, and abuse including at the hands of so-called ‘gatekeepers’ and social exclusions. GBV remains a pervasive issue, with domestic violence the main form of GBV in IDP sites, though sexual violence and harassment occur when girls and women leave settlements to collect firewood, feed livestock or on the way to and from school, and girls working as housemaids report sexual exploitation and abuse. Fears of reprisal lack of confidentiality and weak police and court system result in a majority of GBV cases going unreported. Young girls and single women are at the greatest risk of GBV, especially in temporary sites of the newly displaced population that lack adequate WASH services or community lighting.

2 - Project activities

This intervention aims for children (boys and girls aged 12-17 years old) to be safe and protected from violence, exploitation and grave violations. The project’s main goal is to reduce vulnerabilities of children and families and improve their resilience. It also aims to document Children affected by Armed Conflict (CAAC) incidents. This will be achieved through: 1) Protecting children at-risk of conflict through coordinated community level prevention programmes and protective services; 2) Responding to the needs of directly affected children and their families through provision of impact mitigation services and psychosocial support and 3) Documenting and reporting on CAAC incidents. To address the increasing needs resulting from conflicts and previous COVID-19, the intervention will prioritize children at risk of domestic violence, children with disabilities, children exposed to conflict related violence and children living under the poverty line. CRADA will ensure provision of both face-to-face and remote services. In order to prevent increasing child protection concerns, the intervention will scale up awareness-raising activities on child protection, the impact of stigma, social exclusion and bullying caused by conflict and previous COVID-19. CRADA will consult children to review existing feedback mechanisms including accessibility, and then process their feedback, and complaints in line with CRADA Operational Guidance on Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP). Acknowledging the different needs of boys and girls and thus the need to design interventions accordingly, gender will be mainstreamed throughout the programme cycle; during implementation and monitoring. The project will seek to achieve the following outputs and activities: Output 1: Support vulnerable children and their families to access prevention and protection services to improve their resilience and coping mechanisms in the worst affected areas. (Target: 1,050 children and 1,400 adults); Activity 1.1 Engage vulnerable and at-risk children positively in structured recreational, educational, awareness-raising, life skills and resilience building activities to improve their resilience and coping mechanisms. Activity 1.2 Raise public awareness and commitment of community members on children’s right to protection, the impact of stigma, social exclusion and bullying caused by previous COVID-19 through community outreach materials and competitions. Activity 1.3 Provide children previously in combatant ranks and their families with integration and rehabilitation services especially structured counselling and mental health and psychosocial support. Activity 1.4 Strengthen capacities of families, community members and leaders to identify, prevent and respond to violence against children through families and community groups discussions, counselling and awareness raising sessions, and trainings. Output 2: Support incident monitoring, data analysis, documentation, monitoring and reporting on grave violations trends related to child protection concerns to provide evidence and inform advocacy. (Target: 24 children and 48 adults); Activity 2.1 Build capacities to monitor and document of violations against children (including collection of testimonies of children), verification and legal redress of cases of children arrested, detained, indicted and sentenced. Activity 2.2 Support in house internal capacity for documenting, reporting and advocacy.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

CRADA considers the specific needs and vulnerabilities of boys and girls of varying ages, as well of men and women, recognizing their roles in the communities, while trying to empower those who tend to be left out of power. To ensure meaningful access and participation; all groups (including people with disabilities) will be identified through outreach (including home visits), awareness raising sessions in the community and engaged in all project set about feedback and complaints mechanism. Community-based complaints and feedback mechanisms will be established for all services, and participant satisfaction assessments using CRADA tools and through quarterly focusgroup discussions with women, men, girls and boys (including individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate) will regularly inform the project of what’s working and what needs improvement and to be further tailored.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

CRADA recognizes global and local complain mechanisms procedures and standards and as such a Feedback and complaints mechanism is in place as part of the M&E process, in the form of a “letter box” and email address. Response is provided regardless of whether corrective measures are feasible to be implemented. One awareness session every 6 months is to take place in order to ensure that beneficiaries understand the mechanism function. All information kits written in locally understood language and with illustrations will be provided to beneficiaries at the project inception and throughout the project implementation cycle to inform as well as solicit feedback. At CRADA,all project staff abide and sign a code of conduct document, and receive refreshment training once per year so to ensure principled assistance is always maintained.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

Project staff are required to attend PSEA training, Project has built-in clear process for receipt and referral of complaints of PSEA, in accordance with IASC guidance, Project staff will directly or indirectly engage in the HCT PSEA Network

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

CRADA through quarterly focus group discussions, home visits, satisfaction surveys with women, men, girls and boys (including individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate) will regularly inform the project of what’s working and what needs improvement and to be further tailored. CRADA team members will every month analyse and reflect upon feedback and complaints received to ensure regular reflection on the issues raised, solutions proposed and who reported them, and as such help CRADA to address these problems, and feed into strategic decisions and help to the program as needed. All groups of beneficiaries will be consulted to review existing feedback mechanisms, to express their views and provide suggestions on how to make those mechanisms more accessible. CRADA proposes to leverage suggestion boxes, satisfaction surveys, focus groups discussions and routine home visits as feedback mechanisms. Upon collection and receipt of complaints, responses and feedbacks CRADA will process the feedback, responses and complaints in line with CRADA Operational Guidance on Accountability to Affected populations (AAP).This engagement will include community groups discussions and awareness raising sessions, with the appropriate measures including personal protective equipment (PPE) put in place to mitigate the spread of COVID-19 and respect current restrictions. In all of these activities, CRADA will ensure a diverse participation of community members including girls and boy, women and men, adolescents, the elderly and people with disabilities. Data confidentiality and data protection will be ensured through all phases of collection, transfer, storage and communication.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

Preventing risks of exclusion based on the displacement, the selection and prioritization of support will be need-based, regardless of communities' status and identity. Project areas, for instance, will be prioritized base on the humanitarian needs overview. The people displaced by conflict and drought in IDP camps will be supported with the support UNMISS security. Decision making in local structures like water committees, CRADA will strike a 50% representation by the woman. Mitigating the impact of armed conflict and violence competing over access to essential commodities and services are often one of the significant causes of community conflict. To reduce conflict, the project will closely engage social classes within the target areas. Community engagement will be fostered throughout the project cycle. Examples of engagement will include involvement in siteselection, and making sure the accountability system is in place to allow them to voice their concerns and complaint. CRADA, throughout the project cycle, will ensure the safety of beneficiaries and staff during the program. This will ensure the timely provision of services to prevent late travels by the staff and beneficiaries, which might expose them to security risks. Throughout the project duration, CRADA will monitor the security situation and also collaborate with the local administration and the community to ensure that the beneficiaries and the staff are well aware. CRADA has the security team that will closely monitor the security situation within the target areas, hence able to advise and prevent staff and also beneficiaries from being affected. CRADA is engaging with communities to better understand protection challenges and to strengthen the community’s “protection networks” so that they can respond to protection problems and facilitate access to institutions and available services. Building trust with the local authorities, service providers and the international community will require continuous investment, both in areas of displacement and in return areas. The participation of the displaced population needs to be ensured through the establishment of committees coupled with the presence of community mobilisers, including for the provision of information on essential services, available assistance and returning process. Participation of women in community-based committees is rare and needs to be reinforced to guarantee a truly participatory approach and give voice to the whole community. CRADA will further strengthen community structures and engage with all persons of concern through age and gender sensitive approaches. Displaced women, men, girls and boys remain victims of various protection incidents and other human rights violations. Violence is occurring in places of refuge and in areas which are controlled by non-state actors and which are not accessible by humanitarian actors. Continuous displacement and lack of livelihoods has also reduced the capacities of these uprooted communities to access humanitarian and medical aid. CRADA will continue to monitor closely protection incidents occurring within the context of forced displacement. By advocating and putting in place response systems, protection incidents should get referral or direct assistance. Additionally, the information gathered through a thorough monitoring system feeds into the wider system to better analyze main protection incident in the country and better tailor programming to needs.
