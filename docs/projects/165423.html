Project Summary

The program’s overall objective is to strengthen social cohesion amongst conflict affected communities in North East Syria and contribute to long-term peace and stability of Syria. Mercy Corps emphasizes an integrated approach that builds households’ economic well-being as well as communities that are cohesive, inclusive and equitable.
Mercy Corps’ Making Markets Work in Crisis approach will drive interventions to increase economic opportunities. Through this approach, Mercy Corps will target market support to key value chains, market actors, producers and local institutions. The specific implementation approach will vary according to the different contexts in Syria, taking into account market robustness, and the constraints. While some of the activities can include simple financial support to key businesses, such as micro, small and medium enterprises (MSMEs) or smart subsidies using vouchers, other activities go deeper, analysing market systems and then providing support that enables both market actors and consumers to cope and recover through the local economy. These interventions may include better access to informal financial services and quality agricultural input supplies. For example, linking traders to bigger suppliers and providing them with better market information or short-run subsidies can reduce their operating risks. This improves traders’ ability to access key goods and sell these goods at affordable prices. Through this program Mercy Corps will address: 1) limited access to economic opportunities and financial services; and 2) the weak and insufficient, but slowly growing, body of private agricultural input suppliers in Syria.
Strengthening cohesive communities is vital to supporting the stability that is finally emerging in NES, and reinforcing post-conflict economic recovery. The combination of improving inter-communal relationships and reducing resource-based tensions will serve to prevent the escalation of some economic-based disputes into violence – for example between host and IDP communities over livelihood opportunities or social service provision – and reduce the ability of political actors to manipulate ethno-religious divisions and mobilise violence. To this end, and in close collaboration with local and neighborhood councils, Mercy Corps will implement community service projects to support local economic development and address resource-based tensions.
Mercy Corps social cohesion approach is grounded on a sound context, conflict and conflict sensitivity analysis that supports the identification of viable entry points for strengthening social cohesion within and between communities on different sides of the conflict divide. Mercy Corps supports community members with the knowledge, skills and confidence they need to analyze opportunities and impediments to strengthening social cohesion; and to formulate and implement options for strengthening social cohesion at the community level. Through participatory methodology including community mobilization, dialogue, interest based negotiation and the use of sports and creative arts; Mercy Corps will encourage positive interactions between and among community members from across the conflict divide. Mercy Corps will also support conflicting communities to address drivers of conflict through the joint implementation of projects that address resource based conflict drivers. Mercy Corps community mobilization methodology multi-pronged approach to social cohesion Conflict sensitivity is at the heart of all social cohesion activities. The project will develop a conflict sensitivity strategy, guided by various conflict sensitivity analysis tools including the do no harm matrix and stakeholder analysis matrix, building off the findings of context and conflict analysis in the areas of implementation. Mercy Corp’s social cohesion project will ensure the meaningful participation of all sections of the community with an emphasis on women and youth (male and female).

4 - Needs

Livelihoods: High rates of unemployment, conflict-related loss of livelihood assets, combined with soaring prices, a devaluation of the Syrian pound and disrupted markets, have led to millions of Syrians being unable to earn a minimum income. Two-thirds of Syrian households have lost one or more of their main income sources and two-thirds of households do not have secure access to food. The sector still accounts for an estimated 26% of Syria’s gross domestic product and represents a critical safety net for the 6.7 million Syrians – including those internally displaced - who still remain in rural areas. Due to the conflict, the value chain of many sectors has been interrupted or broken. Insecurity, multiple displacements, destroyed infrastructure, and dysfunctional markets have left women and men without access to land, quality agricultural inputs, and processing facilities, leading to overall reduced food production and loss of income generation opportunities. Even before the conflict, vulnerable people lacked access to financial services in Syria, and a culture of taking out loans from formal microfinance institutions. NES experiences frequent political violence involving the administration’s Syrian Democratic Forces (SDF), multiple Arab tribes, armed groups aligned with Damascus or Ankara, and ISIS remnants; as well as socio-economic conflicts involving individuals, communities and tribal/clan structures. The contextual changes also re-surfaced inter-group divisions particularly within and between the multiple ethnic and religious identity groups residing in NES, rooted in resource based conflicts; unresolved historical grievances; perceptions of exclusion, marginalization and discrimination; education curriculum and other inter-group grievances derived during the conflict. Hate speech and narratives promoting violent extremism are common place in local and social media outlets.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk of exploitation or abuse by staff members/service providers/partners: Mercy Corps monitoring and evaluation systems include accountability frameworks, and Mercy Corps has a beneficiary complaints mechanism. Mercy Corps is committed to the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse and Child Safeguarding through appropriate policies (Code of Conduct) which are enforced through on-boarding and training to all staff, suppliers and partners. Distribution are carefully planned to ensure they are done at times and in places that are safe and accessible to women and girls, as well as the elderly and people with disabilities, and that any barriers to access as a result of gender norms or physical ability are taken into account when planning for the distribution.Programming exacerbates community tensions due to perceived discrimination/favouritism: Mercy Corps upholds a conflict sensitive and DNH approach throughout programming. MC utilizes appropriate and transparent targeting techniques, prioritising the most vulnerable and those at risk, ensuring services will be delivered without discrimination, ensuring inclusion of minorities.Local authorities restrict activities of partners due to lack of support for protection interventions. Similarly, where programming is sensitive to authorities but a priority for local communities, Mercy Corps will endeavour to engage with local actors to increase opportunities for operating. Persons without documents refused assistance: As part of a protection mainstreamed intervention, Mercy Corps does not require beneficiaries of protection or non-protection projects to possess national ID documents. In line with protection cluster guidelines, information can be triangulated using local verification processes.

7 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Mercy Corps and partner teams are able to access target communities and safely implement programs. The recent change in context in North East Syria, occasioned by Turkish military invasion has limited access to some geographical areas for both national and international organizations. Additionally tribal leaders in some governorates such as Deir ez Zor wield significant power and influence and can act as gate keepers, effectively limiting access to communities even when local or international organizations have permission from the Self Administration.
