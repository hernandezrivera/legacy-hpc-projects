Project Summary

Recent data (HNO, 2022) indicate that 8.3 million people in South Sudan are at the risk of experiencing severe food insecurity at the peak of the 2022 May to July lean season. In the Greater Kapoeta Counties, specifically Kapoeta East, communities are already facing severe food insecurity following failed rains, poor agricultural practices, and over-reliance on pastoralist livelihoods. This has led to the displacement of about 4200 people to Kapoeta North where host communities are reporting that their food supplies are already running out given the sharing with IDPs from Kapoeta East. The planting season is a month or two away but the early harvests will be in July, which necessitates emergency food provision to Kapoeta East and Kapoeta North communities.in light of these needs, OPRD will provide emergency food assistance to these communities while strengthening through resilience to future shocks through capacity building in improved agronomic practices and in businesses. The project targets 3,500 beneficiaries through general food distribution, provision of agricultural inputs, training and capacity building, and 60 women groups through business training and seed capital interventions. The project targets the most vulnerable members of the community including women, children, people with disabilities, older persons, and women-headed households. The proposed activities align with and contribute to the 2022 FSL Cluster objectives (1, 2 and 3).

1 - Needs

Based on recent food security analysis – HNO 2022 – 8.3 million people in South Sudan are at the risk of experiencing severe food insecurity at the peak of the 2022 May to July lean season. Of the 8.3 million people, 50% are female, 54% are children and 15% are people living with disabilities. The level of severe food insecurity in the country has seen a 7% rise as compared to the number of people in need in 2021. The factors associated to this increase are conflict, economic shocks, covariate climate, and household level stressors such as GBV, death of breadwinner, lack of access to basic services, and mortality due to poor hygiene. The Greater Kapoeta counties are among the most severely affected counties given the three consecutive years of widespread flooding and localized dry spells. According to IRNA report (2022), about 4000 people of Kapoeta East County were displaced to Kapoeta North County due to severe lack of food in Kapoeta East, which was associated with failed rains, poor agricultural practices, and over-reliance on pastoralist livelihoods. Additionally, there is scarce access to water for household and livestock use in Kapoeta East: the only water dam in Jie is drying up and the water is too dirty for human consumption, which triggered the displacement of Kapoeta East communities. The report highlights the need for food assistance to communities in Kapoeta East as well as to those in Kapoeta North given the influx of IDPs in the county. The number of IDPs in Kapoeta North County are expected to continue rising as more people flee from Kapoeta East in search of food. At the same time, the food security situation in Kapoeta North is likely to continue deteriorating since the food supplies of the host communities are fast running out (IRNA, 2022). The elderly people, children, and people with disabilities in Kapoeta East are being abandoned as the energetic population flees to Kapoeta North and other locations seeking survival. The next rains and planting seasons are still months away (in April/May) and the earliest crop harvest will be in July 2022 leaving the Kapoeta East and Kapoeta North communities in severe and emergency need of food. According to the HNO Severity 2022 report, the Greater Kapoeta Counties are among the 21 counties classified as Counties of Very High Concern. The report highlights that the most affected people are children, people with disability, older persons, and women-headed households. According to the FSL Cluster’s IPC May to July projections, the Greater Kapoeta Counties are anticipated to be in IPC level 4 (extreme). According to REACH (2022), the high level of severe food insecurity in the Greater Kapoeta Counties is driven by flooding, population displacements, declined crop production, pests and diseases, economic crisis, the effects of COVID-19, limited access to basic services, and the cumulative effects of the prolonged years of asset depletion and loss of livelihoods. The continuing currency depreciation and associated impacts on commodity prices and incomes coupled with the effects of COVID-19 continue to erode the purchasing power of vulnerable households who rely on markets to purchase food and other basic needs. With the anticipated severity of food insecurity, about 302,078 children under five years are expected to suffer from acute malnutrition in 2022 with further deteriorated of nutrition projected to be at the peak of the lean season – May to July 2022. In light of the above food security needs in the Greater Kapoeta Counties, emergency food assistance and robust food security and livelihoods response by OPRD is needed in order to save lives and improve the living standards and well-being of the communities.

2 - Project activities

Organization for Peace, Relief, and Development (OPRD) in line with the FSL 2022 strategic objectives will conduct the following activities:• General food distribution to 3500 most vulnerable persons including 8000 IDPs, host communities, returnees, children-headed households, people with disabilities, elderly persons, and women-headed households. • Distribution of crop kits to 3500 farmers (1000 male and 2500 female)• Distribution of vegetable kits to 2500 farmers (all female)• Distribution of 500 malodas• Distribution of 1000 fishing kits (to 1000 male beneficiaries)• Capacity building of 60 community animal health workers (CAHWs) on animal health and livestock production• Provision of veterinary kits to the 60 trained CAHWs• Training of 2000 beneficiaries on improved animal production and health practices• Training and capacity building of 60 community based extension workers (CBEWs) on improved agronomics and climate smart agriculture• Training of 3500 beneficiaries on improved agronomic practices including kitchen gardening and climate smart agriculture, and post-harvest management • Support 60 women groups with income generating activities through trainings and business start-up capital• Train and support the formation of 15 Village Savings and Loan Associations (VSLAs) – 5 in each county

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

As a way of diversifying sources of income for the beneficiaries, the project will build the capacity of selected beneficiaries in business management and equip them with skills they need to successfully run businesses. Once the beneficiaries complete training and develop feasible business plans, OPRD will provide them business start-up capital in form of cash. The use of cash in funding business start-ups is based on flexibility: it will allow the business owners purchase goods with high demand in their areas allowing them the freedom of choice in the commodities they sell. In addition, OPRD will facilitate the establishment of 15 VSLAs and offer them capacity building for their sustainability. To operationalize these VSLAs, OPRD will provide cash assistance, which can be lent to business persons and entrepreneurs in the communities and returned back with some interest. The use of cash for the VSLAs intervention is based on the preference noted by VSLAs in the Greater Kapoeta during a participatory assessment conducted by OPRD: the VSLAs noted that their customers purely prefer cash as their businesses are very diverse thus through cash assistance they are able to serve the heterogeneous needs of the market. The VSLAs stated that if they received in-kind support; for example supplies, the market would be flooded with homogeneous goods, which would lower demand and in turn the entrepreneurs’ ability to service the loans.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

Despite the challenges in South Sudan, markets functions are able to supply goods and some limited services to persons in need at a fee. Regional infrastructures through roads which bring supplies into the county from Kenya, Uganda and Sudan mostly ensure goods are available for entrepreneurs and consumers. Nonetheless, not all consumers are able to buy goods directly from markets and some rely on resellers in the local markets and community shops to acquire goods. Although the target beneficiaries are majorly farmers, some items they may require are not produced at home but can be easily purchased in nearby marketplaces and shops. Recent report (HNO 2022) indicates that women and girls are avoiding marketplaces among other areas in fear of facing GBV. At the same time, the elderly and people with disabilities face mobility challenges, which underscore the need to bring markets and shops closer in the communities. In response to these needs, this project will fund (through cash) feasible business plans that will operate in the communities. Additionally, the project will support the formation and operationalization of 15 VSLAs where entrepreneurs can easily access funds to run their businesses.

7.4. Community Engagement Strategy

Yes

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Beneficiaries will be actively involved in the design and implementation of the project activities. Farmers groups will help shape and design the agricultural activities and determine the times and days that are most convenient. They will be consulted for the roll out of training sessions. Farming groups will also be consulted when mapping threats to the agricultural productivity of farmers within the community. Adults, in particular farmers groups, women groups and community leaders, will also be directly involved in the design of the program, particularly during the establishment VSLAs and in deciding the CAHWs and CBEWs to be provided with training and complimentary wares. OPRD will undertake FSL needs assessment with the help of community FSL stakeholders to identify bomas with the highest food assistance needs and determine the most workable solutions for food security in the Greater Kapoeta Counties. OPRD will lead FGDs and key informant interviews to understand who are the most vulnerable and adapt services to prioritize their specific needs

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

OPRD has developed some tools and mechanisms to capture feedback on key aspects of programming to enhance the quality of services provided and these will be adapted for this project. To capture feedback from all stakeholders and beneficiaries including the most vulnerable, OPRD will hold regular meetings with the project management committees, CAHWs, CBEWs, community organizations and farmers groups to assess the needs of the vulnerable populations and monitor the extent to which the implemented activities are responding to the livelihood concerns raised by the marginalized communities. OPRD will also conduct focus group discussions and key informant interviews among the vulnerable members of the communities to ascertain that the project is responding to their expressed needs. The focus group discussions will be broken out by age and gender to ensure that the participants are comfortable voicing their concerns. Other feedback mechanisms that OPRD will adopt for this project include suggestion boxes, help desk, and hotlines. Separate activities for marginalized segments of the population (adolescent girls, female headed households, widows, and elderly women, child-headed households will also be facilitated: the sessions will be delivered face-to-face to ensure an open feedback loop. The marginalized community members will be engaged using local dialect to enhance their comprehension of the message and increase their likelihood of providing feedback.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

OPRD’s programming is designed to adhere to and uphold the humanitarian principles of Do No Harm. OPRD has a Prevention of Sexual Exploitation, Abuse (PSEA) Policy which sets out the expectations, systems, and processes aimed at preventing sexual exploitation of other people we work with such as vulnerable adults and community members, as well as the actions that will ensue if incidents of sexual exploitation, abuse and harassment are identified. This will be shared and read out to beneficiaries at the onset of the project with periodic refresher sessions on what is expected of them. OPRD with the help of our protection department will ensure that our strategy will be inclusive and user-friendly for all targeted beneficiaries. OPRD will continue to communicate and or consult the community through FGDs and KIIs. The stakeholders i.e BHIs, RRC, Administrators, women leads, and chiefs are the bridge between OPRD and the beneficiaries. OPRD will work with community GBV and child protection stakeholders to develop confidential complaint mechanisms where beneficiaries can report attempts of SEA. OPRD will create awareness on these mechanisms and encourage the beneficiaries to use them.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

To promote accountability to beneficiaries, OPRD will be sure to communicate the aim and objectives of the project to beneficiaries and clarify on the assistance to be provided. To ensure the project activities effectively respond to the needs of the beneficiaries, OPRD will use two-way communication channels such that it is able to disseminate information and gather feedback from the beneficiaries. At project inception, OPRD will conduct consultative meetings with community stakeholders, local authorities and community representatives to introduce the project and the targeted beneficiaries. OPRD will encourage stakeholders’ participation in project design and implementation of activities that better respond to the protection needs of the communities. During beneficiary recruitment, OPRD will inform every selected beneficiary of the aim of the project and the assistance to be provided and welcome the beneficiary’s input on how the services should be provided, where they should be provided, and the time considered safe for individual beneficiaries. During implementation of the project activities, OPRD will establish different feedback/complaint mechanisms to collect beneficiaries’ view of the activities and the extent to which they respond to their needs. Among these feedback/complaint mechanisms will be help desks, suggestion boxes, hotline, beneficiary feedback forms, and beneficiary surveys among others. During trainings and other meetings, beneficiaries will be given opportunities to ask questions and seek clarifications. Key messages will be passed in local dialects to promote comprehension and they will be complemented by short dramas to promote retention. Messages will be majorly communicated verbally and in the written form through visibility materials such as T-shirts, brochures, and banners. OPRD will hold regular meetings with community stakeholders, leaders, and representatives to discuss arising issues and the raised concerns and determine how to adapt the project to meet these needs.
