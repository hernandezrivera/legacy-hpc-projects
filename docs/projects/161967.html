Project Summary

The Emergency Food Security and Vulnerable Livelihood (EFSVL) project focus to provide continuous support to the Rohingya refugees for the live savings interventions (e.g. complementary food supports; farmer market approach; enhancing DRR resilience linkages to the food security and self-reliance) to improve access to adequate diversified food access. In addition, EFSVL project concentrating on Homestead garden & Pit Composting; rural infrastructure through Cash for Work (CFW) approach; entrepreneurship and small trade business through Income Generating Activities (IGAs) and Vocational Training (VT) for the HC people. Considering the current needs of food security & livelihood option as well as Government policy stands for restriction on direct cash injection in the camps, Oxfam EFSVL sector prefers to do self-reliance activities for the Rohingya refugees and livelihood activities for the Rohingya refugee. These interventions will contribute to Rohingya refugees and host communities in Cox’s Bazar to live with good health, dignity, and better safety.

Needs

Emergency Food Security: Access to fresh and nutritious food continues to be an obstacle for the refugee population residing in Cox’s Bazar camps. Based on the findings of the August 2018 WFP REVA Technical Report, dietary diversity remains low; and whilst basic food needs are being met by the WFP general food basket, access to other types of food (dairy, meat, fruits, and vegetables) remains limited. According to REVA assessment, 65% of refugees reported not having access to minimum acceptable dietary diversity. This lack of dietary diversity and micronutrients results to increased cases of malnutrition, especially among children who are at risk of exposure to chronic malnutrition. Further, the REVA assessment also established among the refugee population, the economic vulnerability is high where 1 in every 2 households (HHs) is extremely poor, and 3 in every 4 households do not meet the Minimum Expenditure Basket (MEB) level.Voucher programs are essential to ensure minimum access to an extended menu of up to 20 items, including fresh produce, vegetables, and a few protein options. However, according to Reva assessment, approximately 83% of all sales constitutes rice, followed by 5% for oil, and 3% for eggs. Further, WFP VAM’s REVA II findings demonstrate that what is purchased in the retail outlets does not necessarily align with what is consumed. Beneficiaries consume more protein and spices than they purchase in the retail outlets. This indicates that although retail outlets offer increased choice, beneficiaries continue to re-sell the rice that they purchase from the retail outlets, and use obtained cash to purchase other preferred commodities from the market.Similarly, in May 2019, Oxfam conducted a study on the efficiency of aid (re-sell of in-kind food aid). The preliminary findings of the study show that few beneficiaries after receiving vouchers do not retrieve them from the shops and instead, sell the vouchers to middlemen or vendors to access additional money to purchase other items. On the other hand, currently, host communities, and specifically local smallholders gain little benefit from the increased demand that came with the refugees. Most of the food that is provided in the camps comes from wholesale providers which are sourced in areas that are away from the camps. From several social cohesion studies, Oxfam has undertaken around the relationship between the refugee population and host communities. It shows clearly that the restoration of livelihoods is an important demand expressed by the host communities.
