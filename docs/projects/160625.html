Project Summary

South Sudan has experienced above-emergency levels of food insecurity, and even famine at times, since civil conflict broke out in 2013. Protracted insecurity and economic crisis has driven more than 4 million away from their homes, with 2 million internally displaced and 2.2. million residing in neighboring countries as refugees. The August 2019 IPC analysis indicates 6.35 million facing severe food insecurity (IPC Phase 3+_. Despite favorable climatic and geophysical conditions for large-scale cultivation, more than 80% of the country is dependent on subsistence agriculture. Moreover, hyperinflation combined with limited livelihoods alternatives has led to extremely eroded household purchasing power. These conditions combined with combined with a lack of physical and water infrastructure as well as poor hygiene and sanitation practices has led to a high disease burden and emergency rates of malnutrition. Action Against Hunger’s food security and livelihoods strategy is centered on long-term reduction in the burden of undernutrition. Therefore, in direct response to the crisis in the Bahr el Ghazal region of South Sudan, the organization will increase access to food and dietary diversity levels by providing opportunities for beneficiaries to improve their agricultural knowledge/practices and food preparation practices. Additionally, access to sources of food will be improved through the improvement of community infrastructure, diversification of livelihood sources including access to credit/loan for increased supply of nutritious foods. To improve the efficacy of nutrition and health programs, our FSL intervention will be fully integrated with local nutrition and health programs, targeting households with children and pregnant and lactating women facing acute malnutrition. Moreover, the program will be linked with local WaSH interventions (e.g. hygiene promotion) to promote prevention of disease and, in turn, positive nutrition outcomes.

1 - Needs

Despite efforts of partners, the food and nutrition security situations remain critical in states such as Northern Bahr el Ghazal (NBeG) and Warrap. Local food production is hampered by periodic flooding, prolonged dry spell and pest infestation including army worms. Extremely poor infrastructure and hyperinflation limits market functionality. A severe lack of basic health and WaSH services drives a high disease burden, which contributes to persistently emergency level rates of acute malnutrition. According to IPC projection for January-April 2020, 47% (545,000) of the population of Northern Bahr el Ghazal will remain classified as in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or above. Similarly, in Warrap, 42% (520,000) of the population will remain in crisis or above. Action Against Hunger latest SMART surveys conducted in Aweil East and Gogrial West show GAM rates of 15.5% and 20.1%, respectively. This trend will be difficult to reverse in the coming years, hence requiring concerted efforts of humanitarian and development partners within HRP framework. The food insecurity in Northern Bahr el Ghazal is also due to limited income-earning opportunities, high morbidity and limited access to services. Limited access to farming inputs due to the lack of purchasing power has negatively impacted the capacity of the farming population to adequately produce staple food for household consumption. Vulnerable households in the regions resort to negative coping strategies due to the lack of social support systems including access to credit/loan at the community level. Therefore, in direct response to this reality, the organization will increase access to food and dietary diversity levels by providing opportunities for beneficiaries to improve their agricultural knowledge/practices and food preparation practices. Additionally, access to sources of food will be improved through the improvement of community infrastructure, diversification of livelihood sources including access to credit/loan for increased supply of nutritious foods.

2 - Activities

Nutrition-sensitive cultivation of arable land using cash:The cultivation of arable land will last for 3 months during which participating households are expected to work for 15 days in each month. In return, participants will be paid cash through a third-party financial service provider. Each participant will be issued a voucher/electronic card that will be used for the cash transfer. Action Against Hunger will advocate for the cultivation of nutritious food products to contribute to a sustainable reduction in the burden of undernutrition. Construction of dykes and access roads through cash:Participants of the program will work on a dyke, multipurpose pond, or an access road that will be identified by the community through a participatory process. Participants will be required to work for 15 days in each month Cash will be transferred to participants based on the number of days worked in each month. It is intended that the cash will be used to supplement participating households’ dietary needs for the period of the lean season. Construction of demonstration plots around nutrition centers: Demonstration plots for vegetables will be established around feeding centers to teach farmers and mothers at the feeding centers to teach mothers vegetable cultivation methods. Action Against Hunger staff from the nutrition department will conduct cooking demonstrations using vegetables produced from the demo plots. Demo plots for crops including sorghum and groundnuts will be established at lead farmer’s sites. The plots will be used for learning best agricultural practices but the ownership of farm will revert to the lead farmer providing the demo plot. Formation of Village Saving and Loan Associations:Members of the cultivation groups (farmers) will be organized into Village Saving and Loan Associations (VSLAs). Farmers who are already involved in farming and earning some income from the cash for work will be encouraged to join the associations, although other community members who are interested will also be encouraged to join the group as it will be a voluntary association. Capacity building for nutrition sensitivity in agriculture: Demonstration plots established around the feeding centers will serve as learning centers for both farmers and mothers coming to the center. Mother/farmers will be trained and supported to grow nutritious vegetables/crops and taught preparation skills. The project will support producers to have nutritious produce available all year round. Support for value chain addition:Action Against Hunger will support groundnut farmers’ groups with appropriate groundnut oil extraction machines. Groups around existing markets who produce surplus will be trained on marketing skills prior to receiving the equipment. Seed fairs (seeds and tools):Vulnerable households who qualify for cultivation support will be given seeds and tools through input trade fair or direct distribution. The seeds will be sourced locally from vendors in the project locations. In locations where appropriate tools are available, vendors will be supported to supply the needed quantities. Farmers will be issued vouchers to procure the needed tools. Action Against Hunger will also seek support for in-kind donation of tools and or seeds that will be distributed directly to farmers. Early market recovery:Action Against Hunger will use the existing market survey data collected in Warrap and Aweil East to provide support to vendors in terms of training on supply chain, storage and awarding of contracts (vouchers) for the provision of farming inputs including tools and seeds.

6.6 - Will beneficiaries' rights be legally recognised?

Entitlements will be legally recognized as far as humanitarian assistance is recognized under the laws of South Sudan.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

Where displaced affected communities are identified during the project identification, they will be incorporated. According to the latest IOM DTM, approximately 30% of the NBeG and Warrap populations are internally displaced persons. These persons will be involved into community action planning processes to incorporate their perspective and plans for the future.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

The project is linked to community systems as it involves initiatives undertaken by communities, including local level duty bearers, with the aim of enhancing their resilience. A focus on building communities' knowledge on agrocology and agronomy will promote long-term improvement in the prevention of undernutrition and food insecurity.

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

The project is aligned with the Food Security Cluster Objectives for 2020, as well as linked to the third pillar of the Partnership for Recovery and Resilience framework.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

The project protection environment is stable: markets are functioning and farmers can cultivate their fields. Northern Bahr el Ghazal and Warrap States have enjoyed relative security since 2016. Warrap in particular has been affected by inter-communal conflict in previous years, however has witnessed some improvement in 2019. Nevertheless, we will continue to monitor the security situation in both areas and work with local authorities to prevent and mitigate violence and criminality which may affect site operations.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

All of the initiatives of the project are led by the communities as a means of beginning the handover process from the project inception phase up to the end of the project. This process is driven by a focus on Community Project Management Committees.

8.18 - Please indicate how the risks selected above will be mitigated.

We will create awareness on the availability of protection referral services where they exist. Survivals will also be supported to pursue the appropriate means of support. The best interest of the victim/survivor will always take prominence. The organization liaisons with local protection actors (IRC, ARC, WVI) to identify available services and contact information. We will work with local authorities including Payam Administrators, Boma Administrators and other local government officials in ensuring that land selected for project activities are void of conflict including personal ownership. We have experience in negotiation land acquisition in Aweil East. Where applicable, we and the Project Management Committee of the area will negotiate the acquisition of land, in line with applicable laws. To mitigate the interference of external stakeholders in the needs-based beneficiary selection process, a beneficiary selection committee will be formed and trained on selection criteria and procedures. The selection process will involve local authorities and representatives of women’s, youth and minority groups, charged with the role of producing the provisional beneficiary listing. This process will culminate in a town-hall validation procedure. All beneficiaries registered will be validated in a public and transparent process involving beneficiaries and other stakeholders. We will also work with external stakeholders with the potential to negatively impact the process to understand the criteria for the selection of beneficiaries and the probable ramifications for their interference in a transparent process. We will train key stakeholders on the use of the feedback mechanism at each project location. Additionally, the organization will print complaint procedures in the local language and in simple English. The project team will regularly review the use of the feedback mechanism and information gathered from the review process will be used to make improvements.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

Three groups are prioritized for feedback solicitation: 1. First-line program implementers (including volunteers and field staff); 2. The local communities where our programs operate; and 3. The direct beneficiaries of our programs. First line implementers work directly with beneficiaries on a day to day basis: they can often provide the most useful readily available information regarding the challenges beneficiaries face. Five modalities have been identified as “minimum standards” for the site level: 1. Monthly staff meetings with first-line implementers, 2. Focus Group Discussions with Direct beneficiaries, 3. Individual interview form, 4. Comment Box at Field Office Gate, 5. Hotline Number. The identities of all feedback and complaints mechanism (FCM) complainants are kept strictly confidential by program management staff when they are provided. We avail a comment box and anonymous hotline for those who wish to keep their identity strictly confidential. We have standard, context appropriate FCM IEC materials (posters, stickers, flyers), which are displayed at all project sites and on all project structures (e.g. equipment used by beneficiaries). IEC materials include images and text in both English and the local dialect to ensure comprehension by a maximum number of individuals. During all community meetings, trainings, and sensitization sessions, a standard script is used by staff to inform beneficiaries on available FCM modalities. Depending on the urgency, feedback will be delivered to the first line program implementers on a monthly basis during the monthly meetings. Specific responses can also be discussed bi-monthly at the beginning of focus group discussions at the site level to inform beneficiaries of what changes are being made given previous feedback, or if any concerns are being addressed at the national level. Urgent issues are addressed immediately by the Field Coordinator and PM through appropriate channels (community meetings, Ministry).
