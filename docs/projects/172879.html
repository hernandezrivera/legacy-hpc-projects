Project Summary

The main drivers of the humanitarian health crisis in Sudan include, but not limited to, armed conflict, displacement, disease outbreaks, and floods. Three groups of epidemics dominated the disease outbreak in Sudan for the last decades: water and sanitation related, vector-borne and vaccine preventable. The humanitarian health response project focuses on addressing major risk factors and providing free-of-charge essential and life-saving health services to vulnerable populations affected by natural and manmade disasters at community, primary and secondary level of health care delivery points strengthening the locality and community level structures. In so doing, WHO’s preparedness and response to health emergencies will be consistent with the national health sector and health cluster strategies and adheres to national and international standards. The WHO in Sudan has well-established country office and 12 field offices located across Sudan. Technical and operational support will be provided by the WHO to strengthen the SMOHs coordination and emergency response teams up to community. The collection, centralization, analysis and dissemination of essential health data disaggregated by sex and age-groups will be maintained and strengthen by WHO field teams jointly with all partners. The WHO procure and distribute free of charge medicines, medical supplies and laboratory reagents to support the NGOs and SMOH in dealing with humanitarian case load at all levels. Training of health workers on topics directly related to prevailing humanitarian situations such as on surveillance and case management and Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) will be provided based on the needs assessed. The project supports small repair and rehabilitation of damaged health facilities and supporting operational costs for humanitarian health services as necessary. The project supports community mobilization to promote healthy behavior. Besides, the project adopts an integrated approach that facilitates access to health services to all age groups, male and female with their specific health needs adapted to the local cultural customs, including advocacy and synergic linkages with ongoing recovery-development interventions (GBV, FGM, Protection, FSL, WASH, Nutrition, NFI, and Education). Optimal capacity for early notification, investigation, confirmation and immediate response to public health threats (outbreaks) is another priority contributing to the health security of targeted groups and beyond. This is achieved through technical and operational support for EWARS, epidemic alert investigation and response operations by supporting the RRTs; supporting response vaccination campaigns and strengthening the routine immunization; providing laboratory reagents and medicines to fill in the gaps during eventual outbreaks. The project targeted 5.6 million people residing in 107 localities with severity ranking level of 4 and 5 for humanitarian health response. The activities related to outbreak control are not restricted only to the 107 targeted localities, the WHO will intervene wherever the needs arise under its mandate for health security and protection of health. COVID-19 community transmission is likely to continue in all states of Sudan in 2021 stressing the vulnerable populations and their access to health care. As of its mandate, WHO has integrated in this proposal interventions to contain the outbreak and to support adequate health services for the target population. This includes among others infection prevention and control, isolation center support, scale up of access to testing also in remote areas and outbreak alert investigations. These interventions are cost intensive and contribute to a large extend to the over-proportional increase of the requested budget.

1 - Needs

A total of 8.1 million (72% female) people identified in need of humanitarian health assistance in 2021. Children constituted 25% of the people in need. The health sector targets 5.6 million people residing in 107 localities with severity ranking level of 4 and 5 for humanitarian health response. The main drivers of the humanitarian health crisis in Sudan include, but not limited to, armed conflict, displacement, disease outbreaks, and floods. Three groups of epidemics dominate the disease outbreaks in Sudan: water and sanitation related (e.g. cholera), vector borne (e.g. malaria and viral hemorrhagic fevers (VHF)) and vaccine preventable diseases (e.g. measles, polio and diphtheria). Malaria has the highest number of cases among all vector borne diseases with more than 1.7 Million cases reported throughout the country in 2020. The VHFs cases reached 2,863 affecting mainly northern and eastern states, while 458 cases of chikungunya were reported from Darfur states. An outbreak of vaccine derived polio virus type 2 outbreak was declared after the positive laboratory confirmation of the circulating virus across 13 states by end of October 2020. The vaccination coverage of measles-containing-vaccine second dose (MCV2) of less than 74% (2019) does not provide sufficient herd immunity in the vulnerable age groups to prevent regular measles outbreaks. Access to improved drinking water and sanitation facilities remained very low at 55% and 24% (2014) respectively; and hence, the risk of cholera outbreaks remains high in the majority of the sates in Sudan. New species of mosquitos transmitting Malaria have been identified in Sudan ( An. stephansii) with a risk to increase Malaria transmission especially in urban settings and also the predominant mosquito vector responsible for transmitting most of VHFs is present in 16 out of 18 States. A total of more than 888,150 population were affected by the recent flood in all 18 States of Sudan including Abyie between the period June and October 2020. Consequently, vector-borne disease outbreaks have since been flourishing. The most affected States were North Darfur, Kassala and Khartoum. Episodes of armed conflict and inter-communal clashes are regularly occurring in Darfur, Blue Nile, South Kordofan, Kassala and Red Sea State. In areas of acute conflict and newly accessible areas present with additional need for health services to the vulnerable and marginalized communities. One of the main challenges in 2020, however, is the community transmission of Covid-19 pandemic affecting entire Sudan. As of 28 November 2020, a total of COVID-19 confirmed cases reached 17,810 including 1,249 associated deaths, CFR: 7.0%. However, the number of cases and death are highly underreported. The second wave of transmission started in week 43 and cases are rapidly increasing exceeding the capacities of the fragile health system and its public health response. The high case fatality ratio (CFR) reflected the low capacity of the health system on all levels and the lack of health care especially for vulnerable populations. There is little investment for health sector from external aid (3%) and government (12%) causing catastrophic health expenditure (76% out-of-pocket-expenditure) on the vulnerable communities. The HRP 2020 for Sudan was severely underfunded (<30%). As a result, the national population coverage with essential health services remained at 43%. The disease surveillance sentinel sites only cover 28% of the health facilities. Annually, the FMOH has been facing about 40% turnover of the Rapid Response Team (RRT) requiring continuous trainings. Availability of selected essential medicines in public health facilities remained very low at 49.3%. Anecdotal evidences had shown that importation of essential medicines by the government significantly dropping for the last two years at a rate of 50% and 25%.

2 - Activities/Outputs

To increase equitable access to health services for vulnerable communities, the project will implement the following activities. Activities 1:1 The project will support provision of primary and secondary health care services including trauma care, EmONC, and referral services through health partner and government counterparts as appropriate. The project support procurement and prepositioning of essential medicines, medical supplies, laboratory reagents, medical kits and equipment and provide training for health workers on diagnosis, case management and IPC to avoid stock out of medicines and increase access to quality of life-saving and essential health services for vulnerable communities. The project also support routine and mobile outreach immunization services and national campaigns to help boost immunization coverage to help prevent vaccine preventable disease outbreaks; support minor repairs of damaged health services to provide quality of care that respect confidentiality and privacy of clients; establish and strengthen referral pathways with special attention to vulnerable groups such as women, children, IDPs. The project mainstream COVID-19 response into existing and future emergency health responses. This includes among others infection prevention and control, isolation center support, scale up of access to testing also in remote areas and outbreak alert investigations. To strengthen the health sector capacity to prepare and deliver health services for vulnerable communities affected by man made and natural disasters such as armed conflict, floods, and disease outbreaks. Activities 2:1: Train and support RRTs at national, State and locality levels; strengthen and expand disease surveillance systems at health facility and community levels through training of health workers and community volunteers and provision of tools including cross-border and cross-line operations; strengthening surveillance at point of entries, support epidemic alert investigation and response through provision of operational costs; support sample shipment and confirmation in WHO certified labs; facilitate international notification as per IHR; support response to outbreaks vaccination campaigns; establish mobile clinics and outreach services to reach marginalized and displaced communities in remote and inaccessible locations; establish referral mechanisms to ensure continuity of care; provide life-saving medicines, medical supplies and kits including laboratory supplies, reagents and equipment; support rehabilitation of health facilities in conflict affected or flood damaged areas to restore interrupted service deliveries. Activity 2:2: As health cluster lead agency, the WHO, also supports coordination and leadership in health emergency preparedness and response efforts; foster interagency collaboration, support establishment of task forces, activation of Emergency Operation Centers (EOCs), co-lead health cluster coordination at national and sub-national level with FMOH/SMOH; support monitoring emergency health response through health cluster tools such as HeRAMS, 4 Ws matrix, State’s vulnerability, Clusterbulletin, updates regularly. To strengthen health systems capacity to enhance vulnerable groups and communities (including IDPs, refugees, and people living with disability) resilience to absorb and respond to shocks. Activity 3:1: The project support improving access to health services for vulnerable communities through mobile and outreach services when appropriate; strengthen mental health and psychosocial service delivery for communities affected by manmade and natural disasters; support coordination andcollaboration with relevant stakeholders with specific mandate for vulnerable groups suchas UNHCR for refugees and UNFPA for GBV related services. The project mainstreamgender and disability in planning and monitoring health responses to track specific needsof vulnerable groups; ensure gender segregated reporting.

5.2 - Impact on environment/natural resources

Climate change is deepening in most part of the globe and Sudan is no exception. The pattern and ecology of the world is shifting rapidly. Some areas with hot and dry weather are now becoming wet. The frequency and scale of natural disasters such as drought and floods is widespread due to shift in climate change. New vectors started to emerge transmitting diseases. Some vectors undertook genetic mutation and started to become resistant to drugs and insecticides/pesticides. Spread of disease patterns is rapidly shifting. Some locations, such as highlands, start reporting diseases such as malaria which was not the case before. Climate change is also aggravating and precipitating drought and subsequent food insecurity and high acute malnutrition levels. Climate change also influenced the pattern of the outbreaks of vector and water borne diseases that indeed increased over the past fewyears with re-emerging and expansion to new geographical areas of Sudan such as malaria, DengueFever, Chikungunya, Rift Valley Fever. About 30% of the project inputs are factored in for the prevention, early identification, confirmation and response to outbreaks of water and vector borne diseases as well as water related diseases. The project will implement strict infection prevention and control IPC at HFs and during the mobile and outreach health delivery points to do away with aggressive pathogens (microbes, Viruses, protozoa, fungus, etc) and medicines, sharp medical tools, disinfectants, lab reagents etc would contaminate/affect staff, other patients and environment outside the clinics creating severe risks for population living in the areas. The training program of WHO includes modules (initialtraining and re-fresher) on Universal Precautions that includes Infection Prevention, Injection safety, Solid and liquid medical waste safe disposal for all levels of care. The national guidelines are distributed to all participants to the training, and to the SMOH so all HFs in targeted area are covered. All WHO medical kits include barrier protection equipment, and disinfectants that are biodegradable. In addition, the WHO/MOH monitoring and supervision check list includes indicators for Universal Precaution, and the repairs of any HF takes into account the standards for environmental protection throughcorrect medical waste disposal (pits for sharps and placenta, incinerators, fence, septic tanks, etc. The disposal of any expired medicines is done according with WHO/National Guidelines by incineration in the presence of Health Authorities.
