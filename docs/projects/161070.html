Project Summary

The education core pipeline project will contribute to the Education Cluster objectives CO1 and CO2 by improving access to safe and protective education services for emergency/crisis affected boys and girls aged 3-18 years (40 per cent girls) as well as improving the provision of quality education services at all levels. The project is designed to address not only the access to education but also the quality of education service.
Since the outbreak of the second conflict in July 2016 , South Sudan has been confronted with many challenges. These include large population displacements, skyrocketing hyperinflation, protracted civil conflict and severe food insecurity that has made the country volatile. The humanitarian crisis, displacement, protracted conflict emergency, poverty, and livelihood insecurity are the main drivers leading to interruption of education (HRP 2020 Education Cluster Narrative). The already fragile education system drastically deteriorated at both the systemic and service delivery levels. It is characterized by high numbers of out-of-school children and adolescents, particularly those affected by conflict and other emergencies estimated to be 2.2 million (UNESCO 2018, Global Initiative on OOSC: South Sudan Country Study).
Through this project for core pipeline, essential education supplies will be procured, distributed and efficiently managed to ensure the right to equitable access to quality education for all children (boys and girls) in South Sudan, particularly the most vulnerable and those affected by conflict are safeguarded. Without education supplies at the school level, the teaching and learning process will not function smoothly. As most parents cannot afford to provide learning materials to their children, the need for education supplies will remain high in the education system.

1 - Needs

School enrollment rates in South Sudan remain well below the regional average with gross enrollment rates (GERs) of 10 per cent at pre-primary level and 72 per cent at primary level. The trend in girls’ enrollment is particularly disconcerting with the Gender Parity Index (GPI) standing at 0.92 in pre-primary to 0.68 in primary and, ultimately, 0.46 in secondary level (EMIS 2015). Overall, school life expectancy, or years of education completion for a South Sudanese child is five years; again, well below the regional average. Any modest gains in enrollment are immediately undercut by dropout rates of over 10 per cent. In South Sudan, continuity of access to education drops off significantly as children move through the system. While 99 per cent of schools provide Grade one education, only 27 per cent offer up to Grade eight education (South Sudan Sector Analysis, 2016). The protracted conflict has also led to the closure, damage or occupation of schools. Some families who have fled the conflict are residing in areas without access to protective learning spaces or in host communities where education resources are overstretched. Thus, the number of out-of-school children in South Sudan is likely to remain the highest in the world. The growing insecurity, coupled with hyperinflation and low and delayed payment of teachers' salaries has also led to higher rates of teacher absenteeism. The involvement of volunteer teachers has partially filled this gap, but only 30 per cent of South Sudan’s primary-level teachers are qualified. All these supply-side challenges are magnified in the nascent early childhood education system, responsible for preparing children to succeed in primary school. The quality of education remains a major concern, with the majority of primary school children (boys and girls) attending schools that are not achieving foundational literacy, numeracy and life-skills. Learning achievement among primary school-aged children remains low: at the end of Grade three, most learners cannot identify a single written word in their native languages or English. Without basic literacy skills, it is expected that South Sudanese learners will struggle to engage with other subjects on the curriculum and will be at higher risk of dropping out of school. With the school-aged population (aged 3-18 years) set to grow by 0.5 million to an estimated five million by 2021, and if there is no significant improvement in the political and economic environment by then, it is expected that these trends will continue and, perhaps, worsen. This will place a further burden on a public education system that at present lacks the resources and capacity to deliver quality education at scale. The scarcity of gender and age appropriate teaching and learning materials is particularly worrying given that so many teachers are untrained. Therefore, the project will procure essential education supplies (students kits, teachers kits, school in a box, recreational kits, ECD kits, Dignity kits and textbooks) and deliver them to the affected children in schools in order to resume their education. The provision of education supplies will support access and contribute to improving the quality of education service.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

The project will reach out with education in emergency services for vulnerable, out-of-school children (boys and girls) including those whose education has been affected by the conflict and other emergencies. Measures will be taken for education of children (boys and girls) from the approximately 40 per cent pastoralist population whose lifestyle, including insecurity caused by cattle raiding and inter-communal conflicts, restricts their educational opportunities.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

The project will be linked to long term interventions within the government/community systems rooted in the key components of the General Education Strategic Plan (2017 - 2021). The Ministry of General Education and Instruction (MoGEI) has set laudable goals for making progress toward access, equity, quality, and management but it lacks the human and financial resources to do so. Dormant or ineffective coordination networks will be revitalized and supported with the appropriate strategies and resources. Given the inherent weakness of government structures and the high risk of conflict, conditions for change towards long term interventions will rely on the actions of key education actors and civil society. The project will remain focused on ensuring children (boys and girls) in humanitarian situations have improved access to safe and protective quality education services. Continued engagement with and strengthening of education-related systems will provide medium- and long-term support to recovery and resilience. Donors, international agencies and NGOs are key partners in supporting these systems at the national and sub-national levels. The success of this support relies on a certain degree of coordination and coherence within the Education Cluster, particularly through transparent and evidence-based planning and decision making.

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

Provision of technical support to national and sub-national platforms and mechanisms will encourage the necessary dialogue, partnerships, and local solutions around which education stakeholders can coalesce and maintain conditions for sustainable education. Where these entities already exist, they will be strengthened, and where they do not they need to be created.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

The protection environment is characterized by seasonal accessibility challenges due to bad roads affected by heavy rains/flooding and often looting of education supplies during conflict.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

Long-term sustainability will require parents and communities to take on greater responsibility for initiating and sustaining their children’s education. Parents need to better understand that education is a better life choice for their children than child marriage or marginally skilled employment. They also need to understand that prioritizing education for their children requires their commitment to engage with their children’s learning and create a culture of education in their community. Education must be understood, at the family level, as a critical pathway to peace and in the best long-term interests of children, family, and community.
