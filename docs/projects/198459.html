Project Summary

The specific objective is to provide inclusive, safe, and dignified access to immediate food, WASH, and protection assistance and to strengthen community resilience in at least 20 communities in hard-to-reach areas of Kaga and Magumeri LGAs in Borno state. The intervention will focus on providing direct access to integrated multi-sectoral, context-adapted assistance, which will cover the most urgent humanitarian needs of the most vulnerable people and communities. The action will include humanitarian protection, food assistance through cash, access to water for domestic use, improved sanitation and hygiene, protection monitoring, community-based protection activities, as well as referrals. The villages to be covered have been selected in coordination with local authorities and humanitarian clusters to ensure reaching into areas with high needs that have largely been deprived of humanitarian assistance.

1 - Needs

The 20 villages targeted by this action are located in hard-to-reach areas in the northeast of Borno and have a total population of approximately 40,000 persons. They have not received previous humanitarian support or only limited assistance with regard to nutrition and WASH. IDPs form about 25% of all community members in these villages. In the focus group sessions with community members, both men and women primarily indicated food (96%) and water (67%) as their most immediate needs, followed by credit/cash support (45%), health support (40%), and education (35%). The three most critical months for households to meet their food needs are June, July, and August, followed by September. The overall food situation has been described as bad to very bad by 84% of participants in focus group discussions and interviews with community leaders. Households are mainly satisfying their food needs through their own agricultural production (40%), work for food (27%), markets (18%), and barter trade (12%). Around 45% of households skip meals due to a lack of resources. Around 30% of children in the communities are undernourished, but only 23% have received treatment against undernutrition during the 12 months prior to data collection. Over 60% of the population can be classified with a "poor" or “borderline” food consumption score (<35 points). Only 12 villages have water sources inside their communities, and only 30% of participants in focus group discussions have indicated to live within 500 meter from the nearest water source. Women (28%), girls (27%), and men (16%) were mentioned as responsible for fetching water in the households, transporting water in jerry cans (57%), open buckets (41%), and buckets with a lid (3%). The results indicate that only 32% of households are having access to at least 15 liters of water per day per person. 38% of households are accessing 7.5 liters of water per day per person. According to respondents, in 80% of cases animals are drinking from the same sources used for human consumption. The main water treatment methods used in the communities are letting water settle (42%), sieving/filtering (24%), followed by boiling (11%). The groups identified to not have equitable access to water sources were people with disabilities (8% of households include at least one person with disabilities), older people, and children. 61% of people in the communities are practicing open defecation at least once a week. In most communities (69%), there are no toilet/latrine facilities available. 82% of respondents reported that there were no separate toilets for women and men. In three (27%) villages, the defecation areas or toilet facilities were located outside the community. While this might reduce the risk of contamination or the spread of diseases, it also constitutes a security threat to villagers, especially to women and girls. Two (18%) villages made provisions for the discrete disposal or the drying and washing of hygiene materials. No village offered access to handwashing facilities. Respondents indicated that only 26% of households have access to at least 250g of soap; only 35% of women have sufficient access to menstrual hygiene materials. The overall access to health care services was considered very bad or bad by 83% of respondents. Groups identified as most vulnerable by the communities were child mothers (19%), persons with disabilities (15%), elderly (13%), and pregnant women or lactating mothers (13%). When asked about channels for victims or witnesses to report incidents of sexual and gender-based violence (GBV), 54% of respondents mentioned community leaders and 39% religious leaders. 87% are not aware of any organisations offering services for survivors of GBV or child protection services in their community, and 90% have not received any training concerning GBV or child protection in the last three months.
