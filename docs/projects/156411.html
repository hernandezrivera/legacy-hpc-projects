Project Summary

WASH services are critical to the survival of displaced people and returnees, fundamental to the recovery of their dignity as human beings and a basic point for their acceptance by host communities.
In 2019 AAH will prioritize activities that directly support life-saving WASH interventions as well as activities that focus on the maintenance and/or the light rehabilitations of existing WASH systems. Both aspects of the sector strategy are life-saving and life-sustaining and can help reduce recourse to harmful coping strategies that risk exacerbating existing needs.
In line with this sector strategic objectives, AAH offers a mix of delivery modalities ranging from water trucking, coupled with the installation of water tanks and the distribution of jerry cans, and more permanent options such as rehabilitating wells or boreholes or extension of the existing infrastructure.
Sanitation systems will be improved through the maintenance and rehabilitation of sewage. The connection of sewerage to the public network has been identified as a key intervention by beneficiaries (including women and people with a disability) to eliminate exposure to faecal contamination and to remove the physical danger of open pits. WASH team will design the system to minimize the risk of leaks and breaks. Once the sewerage systems will be repaired and functional, the local municipality will be responsible for continuing the operation and maintenance.
In terms of hygiene women, men, girls and boys will receive emergency gendered hygiene kits, potentially during the first days of displacement. These kits have an important role to play in preventing disease outbreaks and to carry out everyday requirements. Some items are lifesaving, such as soap for maintaining hygiene. Other items may simply contribute to people’s sense of dignity in difficult circumstances e.g. underwear, razors and toothbrushes. Distributions will be done mostly through SARC.
AAH will integrate protection and gender-sensitive approaches to ensure the infrastructure are safe for women and girls, boys and men at the point of delivery using artificial light, safe routes and segregation by sex. It will also take in consideration general public safety and environmental issues to ensure that there are no irreversible impacts in public health or ecological footprint.

Needs

Before the crisis, most of the population in Syria was properly served by well-managed water systems controlled by the Government. After several years into the crisis, piped water distribution has been jeopardized, finding dysfunctional or non-functional systems in some areas, operating at varying levels of efficiency, with urban centres generally better served and rural areas facing more challenging situations. Limitations in regular operations, maintenance and repair, have had a significant impact on the systems efficiency, leading to a dysfunctional management and a higher risk of contamination. During the past months, AAH field teams witnessed the progressive returns of population across Aleppo, Rural Damascus or Deraa, in communities such as Shabaa, Ein Terma, Kafr Batna, Deir Al Adas, Kafr Nassij, Seikh Miskine or Athsana, with public services still heavily affected by the conflict. In most of the locations visited by AAH teams, water supply infrastructures (wells, water towers, supply network, equipment) had been destroyed and/or looted during the crisis. The problem is aggravated by the absence of electricity and the high cost of fuel to run generators. Returnees are then compelled to use detrimental coping mechanisms to afford water trucking and use of unimproved water sources. In communities heavily burdened by IDPs, AAH teams performed technical surveys to find solutions to increase water productivity and meet the daily needs of the population. In absence of sufficient amount of water, people resort to buy water at excessive rates, also leading to a change in their hygiene practices to decrease water use. Despite some investment in water treatment systems and provision of water disinfectants the main WASH need across Syria remains access to sufficient quantities of safe water. Up to 55 per cent of surveyed households are relying on alternative and often unsafe water sources to meet or complement their water needs. Results of free chlorine (FRC) testing are even more concerning and indicates that countrywide only 40 per cent of water received from networks have a sufficient level of FRC at the point of use (below both national drinking water and emergency standards of min. 0.2 mg/l). The worst situation is observed in Dara’a and Idleb where only around 13 per cent of the population is served exclusively via piped water, with this figure only slightly higher in Aleppo (16 per cent) and Deir-ez-Zor (22 per cent). Results of FRC testing for water supplied by water tankers indicates that water safety is much lower than water from networks, with only 13 per cent of samples taken from across Syria indicating a sufficient level of chlorine at the point of use. The sanitation infrastructures in Syria have also suffered from the conflict, with airstrikes and IED’s causing leaks in the sewage network, provoking potential contamination of the groundwater and wells. Is consider that at least 70% of the sewage wastewater is untreated, and at least half of the sewage systems are not functional, exposing communities to significant health risks such as acute bloody diarrhoea, typhoid or jaundice. Moreover, sanitation needs are considerably higher among displaced people living in informal settlements and camps compared to the rest of the population, with over-crowding, cleanliness and protection concerns linked with substandard conditions of sanitation facilities frequently reported.

Implementation of activities

In line with this sector strategic objectives, AAH offers a mix of delivery modalities, ranging from emergency live-saving support WASH programmes, like water trucking (coupled with the installation of water tanks and the distribution of jerry cans) to more permanent options, such as the rehabilitation of boreholes or rehabilitation of the existing water or sewage network. The emergency water supply is defined with a flexible duration, depending on the actual needs and gaps identified in the field, in benefit of newly displaced, returnees, or severely affected host communities. To ensure the quality of the water delivered by water trucking, AAH conducts chemical and bacteriological tests for the identified water sources before starting the activity and trucks are also sterilized by chlorination. Water quality monitoring continue during the service provision, with AAH teams establishing a plan to collect random samples and perform FRC tests at every critical point of the water delivery chain: source, reservoir and water tanks. Other live saving WASH responses include the installation of latrines, solid waste management and vector control activities in collective shelters and/or IDP’s camps.Access to safe and sufficient water at community level is usually achieved through the rehabilitation and repair of boreholes, reservoirs and/or water network. The activity is usually implemented in collaboration with the Ministry of Water Resources, after AAH technical teams have assessed the situation and identified the potential needs. In other cases, the need is related to the water network itself, which has been damaged during the conflict and is in need of replacement or repair.But rehabilitation is only part of the works to be achieved, AAH also ensure the operation and maintenance of the systems, through the provision of power sources and building capacities of the technical staff from the Water Boards. AAH also ensures the treatment of water through the provision chlorination systems that allows water to be free from bacteriological contamination.Sanitation systems are usually improved through the maintenance and rehabilitation of sewage systems. The connection of sewage to the public network has been identified as a key intervention by beneficiaries (including women and people with a disability) to eliminate exposure to faecal contamination and to remove the physical danger of open pits. Typically, work will comprise repairs to stretches of pipes which have been rendered non-operational; repairing and installing new, high-quality pipes, including excavation works; installation of new manholes in the sewage system, facilitating their future maintenance and repair; repair/install of electrical pumps to unblock sewage pipes, and rehabilitation and maintenance of sewage treatment plants, when possible, in collaboration with local authorities. When a hygiene crisis is detected, AAH usually distribute through partners emergency hygiene kits to cope with the situation, potentially during the first days or weeks of displacement. These kits have an important role to play in preventing disease outbreaks and to carry out everyday requirements. Some items are lifesaving, such as soap for maintaining hygiene. Other items may simply contribute to people’s sense of dignity in difficult circumstances e.g. underwear, razors and toothbrushes. Also, in order to improve hygiene practices, AAH carries out hygiene promotion campaigns to promote a change in behavior and encourage good hygiene practices.

Protection Risk Analysis

Protection Risk: Inequality in distribution, timing and process of distribution put women and girls at higher risk of GBV. Likelihood: High, Impact: High. Mitigation measures: Promote the establishment of an adequate management plan and involve the communities during the distribution; monitor the activity closely with partner teams to ensure equality in distribution; follow closely the distribution to girls and women and ensuring to get their feedback on the kits.Protection Risk: Gender insensitive contents varieties of kits with inconsistent items. Likelihood: Medium, Impact: High. Mitigation measures: Harmonize and standardize gender friendly hygiene kits among the sector members and indicate quantity and instruction for use, verify the quality and content of the hygiene kits prior to the delivery and the quality of the kits while they are distributed. Protection Risk: Quality and/or quantity not adequate as per humanitarian standards. Likelihood: High, Impact: High. Mitigation measures: Ensure provision of safe water as per AAH quality standards and establish regular water quality monitoring including water source, water trucks and end-user samples; collect water from safe sources certified by water authorities or local councils and reproduce water test to ensure the quality of the water, establish a clear monitoring planning involving beneficiaries and field monitors and local authorities. Protection Risk: Water distribution not safe or accessible, especially for women, girls (exposure to GBV risk) and children, elderly and people with disabilities; inequality in distribution. Likelihood: High, Impact: High.Mitigation measures: Ensure national standards are followed and closely monitor the activity during implementation; consult separately girls, boys, women and men, older people and those with disabilities; make sure that distance of water collection points remains at minimum to ensure that this does not overburden women and adolescent girls with extra work load in particular; get feedback from the communities. Protection risk: Toilets represent GBV or health safety risk. Likelihood: Low, Impact: High. Mitigation measure: ensure SPHERE standards are applied during implementation and women and adolescent girls are consulted during design, implementation and monitoring phase of projects. Protection risk: Potential exposure to explosive hazard during assessment or rehabilitation of water supply infrastructure. Likelihood: Medium, Impact: High. Mitigation measures: work closely with local authorities and conduct assessments and/or rehabilitation works only in areas declared as cleared; monitor and check with mine action actors on hazard contamination and infrastructure clearance for the targeted locations. Protection Risk: Risk of theft of WASH rehabilitation/construction materials such as generators and construction materials by armed groups and used in conflict activities. Likelihood: Low, Impact: High. Mitigation measures: MoUs to be agreed with the Water Units to ensure ownership of materials and rehabilitated facilities; promote cost recovery systems for longer term maintenance and ownership of the infrastructure; Technical teams to do follow-up visits and post-completion monitoring. Protection Risk: Improper storage of hazardous supplies - Possibility of explosion, human casualty and asset destruction. Likelihood: Medium, Impact: High. Mitigation measures: Ensure proper storage of supplies with clear provision of care and safety consideration; Technical teams to do follow-up visits and post-completion monitoring to the locations. Protection risk: Health risk associated with construction works and exposure to raw sewage and gases. Likelihood: High, Impact: Medium. Mitigation measures: ensure AAH staff and appointed suppliers involved in the construction works wear appropriate Personal Protective Equipment and follow established safety regulations; Monitor safety of construction activities.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risk 1: The security situation in targeted areas is unstable and AAH has no access to implement the activities; there are additional unforeseen administrative procedures imposed, and restrictions on access to beneficiaries. Likelihood: medium. Impact: highMitigation: AAH works and coordinates with authorities and Ministries to maintain access to target areas and beneficiaries, and the capacity to implement activities. AAH will reach out to local authorities and line ministries with trust building activities to further develop relationships and capacity to avoid or mitigate restrictions in AAH capacity to operate in Northern and Southern Syria. Advocacy and coordination with the humanitarian community and humanitarian diplomacy activities on the local level are also considered to contribute to this mitigation measure. Risk 2: Substantive devaluation of the EURO or USD against the Syrian pound; International bank transfers to Syria are fluid and timely.Likelihood: medium. Impact: highMitigation: The eventuality of significant changes in the value of the Syrian currency will be closely monitored and, if materialized, timely communicated to donors and options discussed. Risk 3: Aid diversion, fraud, and corruptionLikelihood: medium. Impact: highMitigation: diversification of supplier, triangulation of information, standard monitoring procedures in place, security plan and risks assessments tables updated and followed. Risk 4: Information gathering activities hampered by suspicion or misunderstandingLikelihood: low. Impact: mediumMitigation: Transparency, trust-building, developing relationships with municipalities, district authorities and facilities managers
