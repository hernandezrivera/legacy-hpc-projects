Project Summary

The project aims to (1) improve the quality, accessibility, and inclusion of response services for GBV survivors, especially linkages between case management, MHPSS, medical care, self-reliance, educational, and legal services for the most vulnerable groups; (2) mitigate the risk of GBV and PSEA through multi-level and cross-sectoral strategies, including capacity building and community-led (especially by women and girls) initiatives and; (3) prevent GBV by motivating significant changes in social norms undergirding violence, systematic discrimination, and gender and age inequalities through structured and evidence-based programs. Highly vulnerable and underserved populations will be targeted, such as people with disabilities and gender-diverse populations. UNHCR and partners work based on community, right-based, and survivor-centered approaches, engaging refugees as active agents and emerging leaders within their communities and during individual case management. We will increase women and girls’ participation in decision-making and leadership by building their confidence and capacities with skills-building and vocational training sessions, engaging female refugees of all ages in our community-based protection work, and involving men and boys as allies in promoting gender equality. Key components of the 2024 GBV programming include improving the quality of safe spaces, delivering life, vocational skills, and continuous MHPSS, enhancing community volunteers’ capacities on how to work with people with disabilities and GDP, engaging men in accountable practices, encouraging women-led initiatives, and delivering women’s leadership training to make them empowered to make their own life decisions. Considering the recent trends and the exponential increase in GBV incidents due to the security situation in the camps and the impact of the cuts on food and NFIs delivered to refugees, UNHCR and IPs will continue to provide case management and specialized MHPSS to survivors with particular attention to strengthening the survivor-centered care practices within the multi-sectoral referral network and increasing accessibility for and capacity to serve target populations, gender, and age-wide. A more integrated approach to GBV response services is prioritized to streamline our services and improve free access. Capacity-building components include caseworkers’ minimum training package on case management and IASC Minimum standards. GBV risks will be mitigated through a three-tiered strategy: (i) working with the community, (ii) UNHCR multi-functional, and (iii) inter-agency levels. Community-level work will include GBV risk mapping and the generation of risk mitigation measures by refugee women/girls, with community GBV risk mitigation actions. UNHCR’s multi-functional work will develop GBV risk mitigation action plans across functional units, using safety audit tools to assess and measure GBV potential risks. Finally, the inter-agency work will be done in collaboration with other agencies to mainstream GBV risk mitigation across all eight sectors in the Rohingya humanitarian response and an initiative for protecting refugees from SEA. UNHCR and its partners will implement structured and evidence-based GBV prevention activities that take a whole-of-society approach (SASA! Together) and those that target specific groups (Girl Shine, EMAP, Male Role Models). Programs promote the centrality of protection and AGD approach. Despite UNHCR mainly focusing on the refugee camps, we are also present in 5 Host Community areas, Thaingkhali, Whykong, Leda, Ukhyia, and Cox's Bazar, implementing and assisting the most vulnerable groups at 5 GBV and health facilities dedicated to LGBTI individuals, sex workers, and extremely vulnerable adolescents (EVA) at risk, as per sector strategy. These facilities are managed by Bandhu as UNHCR IP and work based on an inclusive perspective, delivering unique services to refugees and members of the host community.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

Rohingya refugees who fled Myanmar in 2017 were escaping violence that included widespread gender-based violence and sexual exploitation and abuse against females and males, which inflicted lasting traumas on the population. These and more recent arrivals joined an existing Rohingya refugee community in Bangladesh, which shares social norms that maintain and reinforce gender inequalities and systematic discrimination that enable and perpetuate gender-based violence, especially against women and girls within their communities. According to the main findings of community consultations conducted in 2023, the risk of rape and sexual abuse was the top concern among girls 18 years old and under, followed by child marriage and human trafficking. Although the risk of rape and sexual abuse remains high across all age groups, statistics on intimate partner violence (IPV) increase as a concern with age. The threat of trafficking is also most significant for young women and adolescent girls. GBVIMS report shows that incidents in 2022 increased by 9% compared to 2021, and according to recent statistics and the protection scenario in the camps, the trend is to increase the number and severity of GBV cases significantly. Assessments over consecutive years (MSNAs, RGAs, FGDs, GBVIMS) have revealed a grim protection picture. Refugees feel unsafe both within the community and inside their shelters, particularly women and girls. During community consultations, women have reported violence in the home and violence in the community as their top two perceived risks. According to the GBVIMS Report in 2022, intimate partner violence is the most reported form of GBV, with 84% of the total. GBV cases are chronicle underreporting, meaning that reports can’t show an accurate and global reflection of reality. From the same report, we observed that 99% of survivors are female, and there is an essential lack of information related to the violence perpetrated against the LGBTI population. Social norms restricting females’ public movement create barriers to services and reduce the number of GBV reports. The camp design presents other risks, and access to functional latrines and general lighting remains a substantial GBV risk. The reduced number of trained humanitarian actors and the lack of coordination among GBV actors correlate with an increased violent crime within the camps, heightening fear and a sense of general insecurity. Parents fear for the safety of their adolescent daughters – including the learning centers and restrict their movements to prevent them from being sexually harassed. Facing closed schools and failing self-reliance activities, parents see marriage as a protective measure for girls under 18. Men in the camps are opportunistically practicing polygamy, while men in neighboring countries are arranging the smuggling of young brides despite the journey’s grave risks. Human smuggling and trafficking in the region have become ‘feminized,’ with more women and girls forced into dangerous journeys. Pre-existing risks have been further exacerbated for critical populations in host and refugee communities, including gender-diverse people and individuals engaged in sex work. These populations' discrimination, risks, and lack of services are identified. Male survivors of sexual violence are similarly underserved, but the GBVIMS has captured an increasing trend of male survivors seeking services. Despite protective legislation, access to justice for GBV survivors—both refugee and host—is limited by justice system inefficacy and distrust. Rohingya refugees are reluctant to seek remedies in the formal legal system. Most households reported that they would refer a friend who was sexually assaulted to a Majhi or a CiC. Informal, gender-biased resolution mechanisms further entrench unequal gender norms and disempower women. Success in women’s empowerment has also been threatened by backlashes targeting female leaders and volunteers.

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

UNHCR conducted an internal rationalization exercise to disengage two GBV Implementing Partners and integrate GBV services into CP and CBP facilities. For 2024, ActionAid will integrate GBV services into the Community Centers already working within the communities and manage our Safe Shelter. Besides rationalization, UNHCR works based on localization principles, keeping the partnership agreements with NGOs and assisting in raising their internal capacities. Considering facilities operating under UNHCR funds, comprehensive data on case management and awareness activities were requested. In addition, recent statistics were analyzed to understand better the impact of activities on the community's well-being, as well as potential gaps or overlapping interventions. By analyzing the case management data, it was possible to observe the specificities of each facility and how community members - especially girls and women, are engaged in the proposed activities. UNHCR closely monitored GBV facilities to follow-up if partners met basic requirements and if activities are aligned with global Policies and GBV Guidelines. Based on these results, UNHCR decided to close some facilities and merge services to adequate our programming to guarantee a more integrated approach. All UNHCR facilities are open to receive GBV survivors, including LGBTI clients, men survivors, and people with disabilities, following a mainstreamed protection strategy. UNHCR is making all efforts to respect the needed rationalization process without jeopardizing the quality of the service delivered to our survivors. Activities managed by UNHCR are inclusive, neutral, and transparent. Statistics are uploaded monthly on GVBIMS and shared with other stakeholders, members of different working groups, and task forces, including GBVSS, on time. Coordination can guarantee good communication among partners, greater coherence with the Government, and better continuity of services to affected refugees.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

UNHCR Implementing Partners, as well as UNHCR staff members, work based on the four principles of GBV programming: (i) Safety and Security, (ii) Confidentiality, (iii) Dignity and Respect, and (iv) Non-discrimination. UNHCR’s protection programs are built on analyzing community power structures and cultural norms and aim to strengthen existing community capacities while ensuring access to and inclusion of the most vulnerable and marginalized groups. UNHCR and partners provide access to services through community centers that provide integrated protection interventions in a non-stigmatizing manner and also ensure outreach activities reach those who cannot or do not feel comfortable attending centers. UNHCR conducts regular community consultations, risk assessments, and safety audits that engage men, women, boys, and girls, including persons with disabilities, persons of diverse sexual orientations and gender identities, and persons with specific needs to identify and mitigate risks, and to optimize safety, dignity, and equity. UNHCR works closely with HI to proactively identify people with disabilities living in all camps refer cases for further protection in third countries and deliver immediate assistance to mitigate protection/GBV risks. All IPs also conduct disability assessments to make facilities more welcoming and accessible to all individuals. UNHCR works from a protection, gender, and disability mainstreaming perspective. All activities implemented by partners and UNHCR aim to identify potential protection risks confidentially and make safe referrals to local service providers. Activities are led by the Do not Harm principle and survivor and rights-centered approaches, and all partnerships are signed based on a previous and transparent assessment of the organizational capabilities to prevent and respond to SEA/SH during the execution of their contract, in line with the zero-tolerance policy advocated by the UN system.

3.3 - Age & Disability

UNHCR protects children by responding to their specific needs and mitigating any risks they may face before, during, or after displacement. Considering the GBVIMS Annual Report from 2022, 93% of child marriage is reported by girls and 7% by boys, representing the main GBV risk highlighted by this population. The same report informs that underreporting of child marriage has been a constant challenge as this has always been interlinked with other types of GBV. From the GBV programming, significant efforts are dedicated to coordinating and creating awareness campaigns focused on mitigating the risks of child marriage to support child survivors. Girls are highly exposed to different forms of violence and exploitation, so UNHCR works to ensure immediate access to appropriate services and provides durable solutions based on the child's best interest. Since September 2021, UNHCR has implemented the Girl Shine program as an evidence-based curriculum. It offers an alternative life perspective to develop their skills and confront gender social norms. To keep enhancing community empowerment, UNHCR works based on an inclusive approach to promote gender equality and protect children, GDP, and people with disabilities, who are often at higher risk of GBV, exploitation, and abuse. Most vulnerable beneficiaries face barriers to accessing essential services and are often excluded from education and livelihood opportunities. UNHCR and partners engage outreach volunteers to reach out to and include refugees of all ages, focusing on adolescents, youth, adults, older persons, and persons with disabilities. UNHCR activities are focused on mitigating GBV risks and strengthening mechanisms to ensure AAP. UNHCR GBV facilities are child and disabled-friendly, with trained national staff, as well as volunteers to provide services to conduct safe identification and confidential referrals of GBV Cases, based on an integrated approach without discrimination and respecting confidentiality.

3.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

UNHCR programming is defined based on the 2020 Global GBV Policy, which determines three working pillars: (i) Prevention, (ii) Risk Mitigation, and (iii) Response. Under the Risk Mitigation pillar, UNHCR is working with all operational units to create an objective work plan that considers all potential GBV risks those operations may create or increase to mitigate them timely. Effective and accountable humanitarian responses require (i) continuous and meaningful engagement with persons that we serve, (ii) understanding their needs and protection risks, (iii) building on their capacities, and (iv) pursuing protection, assistance, and solutions that consider their perspectives and priorities. Our responses include continuous and meaningful engagement with the people we serve by assessing their needs through continuous community consultations and the FDG process and building their capacities with training and monitoring strategies. UNHCR has multiple accessible complaints and reporting mechanisms spread within the facilities. UNHCR is therefore supporting strengthening AAP, including notably protection from SEA, within the wider humanitarian community. All staff is trained on CoC and PSEA annually, and internal focal points are established in the operation, together with PSEA Network focal points. To ensure the implementation of a zero-tolerance policy on SEA, humanitarian actors will continue to show leadership and build capacity to prevent, mitigate, and respond to risks and incidents of SEA through a survivor-centered approach in coordination with the PSEA network. The UNHCR helpline is fully operational and reformed, aggregating a new, innovative, and inclusive online platform with accessible information oriented towards refugees of all ages, which makes the service more streamlined and active 24/7 to receive complains, deliver helpful information about service providers, and make confidential referrals.

3.5.1 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

Protection from sexual exploitation, abuse, and harassment is a crucial area for UNHCR. UNHCR is an active member of the PSEA Network, a coordination structure to prevent, mitigate, and harmonize the response against SEA/SH incidents in Bangladesh, having a dedicated group organized for the Rohingya Response and a second one based in Dhaka. All UNHCR Implementing Partners are also members of the Network and follow the Inter-Agency SoP review in 2023. Only in 2023 UNHCR assessed 13 Implementing Partners on their capacities to prevent and respond to SEA/SH, intending to update another five assessments in 2024 to guarantee excellent and safe deliverance of services. UNHCR staff is annually trained on CoC and PSEA/SH, as well as all personnel working for UNHCR Implementing Partners, including volunteers. UNHCR delivers an annual refresher to Focal Points from NGOs and INGOs and monitors their performance to cascade the training to the national staff, volunteers, community leaders, and members of all communities, focusing on the safe identification and confidential referral of cases. According to UNHCR Partnership Agreements, all Implementing Partners need to focus on communicating with communities – using non-verbal and accessible language, on the complaint and reporting systems, the importance of reporting, as well as the protection and reputational risks related to SEA incidents. An audio and poster awareness on SEA, created in the Rohingya language, was used to reach volunteers in a short period to convey the most critical information and we intend to keep using this material to enhance community empowerment and create a safer environment in the camps.

3.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

UNHCR will remain a central player within interagency disaster preparedness efforts for the monsoon and cyclone responses. UNHCR includes monsoon, cyclone, landslide, fire, and other disaster preparedness aspects in communicating with communities’ activities and community initiatives. UNHCR trains community members to mitigate risks and quickly respond to climate concerns. When disasters occur, UNHCR targets protection services to respond to the needs of those affected, focusing on women, children, and persons with specific needs. UNHCR-built facilities include consideration of environmental and climate concerns and DRR. Protection Emergency Response Units (PERU) consisting of 55 teams with 330 members (consisting of staff from agencies and refugee volunteers) including PERU members with specialization on CP and GBV, supported by the Protection Sector, will continue to engage with the community to disseminate key protection messages, identify urgent cases and referrals, and monitor the situation of Persons with Specific Needs. During emergencies, PERU teams ensure that protection mainstreaming and strengthening of community involvement in all Sectors is met, and the response on the ground is in line with Protection principles and sensitive to Age, Gender, Disability, and Diversity concerns. As part of the Protection Emergency Response Preparedness Plan (linked to the ISCG Multi-Hazard Response Plan), the Rapid Protection Assessment (RPA) approach was revised and reformed into 3 Rapid Protection Assessment teams (consisting of General Protection, CP, GBV, Inclusion, and Gender Diverse Population specialists) with a focus on protection risks after an emergency, response, advocacy, and mobilization.
