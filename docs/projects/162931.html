Project Summary

Relief International’s (RI) proposed intervention will provide health and nutrition services to refugees and vulnerable host communities, with a focus on widows, orphans and poor families in the East Darfur localities of Abu Jabra, El Ferdous, and Bahr El Arab (Al Matariq) and the North Darfur locality of Al Lait. The goal of the project is to reduce morbidity and mortality among vulnerable and conflict-affected communities. The project has two core objectives: (1) Improved access to quality primary health services; and (2) Improved access to quality preventative and curative nutrition services.

1 - Needs

Based on UNHCR’s August 2019 East and North Darfur Population Dashboards, there are 14,574 refugees in Abu Jabral, 8,122 in El Ferdous, and 7,093 in Al Matariq (Bahr El Arab) in East Darfur, and 4,119 in Al Lait in North Darfur, with numbers continuing to rise. As a result, the already strained resources shared between refugees and host communities are unable to meet current needs for primary health care and nutrition services. Within both North Darfur and East Darfur needs remain acute as it does across Sudan due to the macro-economic crisis and the fragility of the new transitional government. Lines of people waiting for bread and fuel remain long across the country, and access to basic health, nutrition and WASH services remain low with people having limited levels of household assets to meet immediate needs (e.g. purchasing power for food, education, medical expenses, etc.). Eighty percent of Sudan’s poor live in rural areas, with rural poverty more than twice that of urban. Average food and livestock prices are steadily increasing (+53% for sorghum and + 116% for goats compared to June 2018) (WFP Monthly Market Update, Jun 2019). The price of sorghum in North Darfur increased from SDG 5.09/kg to SDG 19.8/kg from January 2017 to June 2019, and the price of a goat from SDG 535 to SDG 2,782 in the same period. Localities targeted are classified as Stressed (IPC Phase 2) or Crisis (IPC Phase 3). In North Darfur, new and protracted displacement due to conflict, chronic poverty, underdevelopment, and recent economic crisis impede access to basic services and disrupt livelihoods, exposing vulnerable communities to high levels of food insecurity and malnutrition. The WHO Q1 2017 HeRAMS shows a health facility functionality rate of 67%, which suffers from the SMoH’s ability to fully cover the costs of staffing, facility management and maintenance, and supplies. Average monthly consultations at RI/OFDA supported health facilities have almost tripled from mid-2018 to early 2019 (11,146 to 32,936). Diarrhea, malaria and acute respiratory infection are the most common morbidities seen at RI-supported health facilities, which provide over 16,000 consultations per month. Outbreaks are common, and SMoHSD staff face significant capacity limitations to respond appropriately. East Darfur hosts 101,866 refugees; the rapid influx of refugees this year has further strained the capacity of host communities and social services to absorb the new arrivals and address their needs. There are critical gaps in the health sector, specifically a limited number of trained health staff, poor infrastructure, low rates of immunization coverage (<80%), and regular stock outs of key medical supplies. Refugee communities lack access to free health care services.

2 - Activities / Outputs

RI proposes an integrated suite of activities in Abu Jabra, El Ferdous, Al Matariq, and Al Lait based on needs and existing coverage. RI assumes 47% male and 53% female participation. The proposed activities are in line with the Cluster priorities, and will be integrated through an integrated health/nutrition approach. Protection and environmental mitigation will be mainstreamed across all sectors. It is anticipated that host communities will comprise approximately 20% of beneficiaries. Health: a) Provide a basic package of services through 10 PHCCs/BHUs to cover 32,152 consultations (assuming 75% coverage of targeted population, excluding Al Lait, which has no Health gaps) for communicable diseases, EPI, and maternal and reproductive health (including 342 pregnant women assisted by trained birth attendant); b) respond to conflict, natural emergencies, and disease outbreaks through facility staff and community leadership capacity building on early warning systems, and mobilization of an Emergency/Rapid Response Mechanism; and c) strengthen the health system through training of 100 SMoH seconded staff on provision of the basic package of care and health facility management in line with the SMoH/WHO guidelines, and facility rehabilitation; and c) response to outbreaks . Nutrition: a) Use the CMAM approach to continue providing timely and equitable lifesaving assistance to 1,751 CU5 with MAM, 272 CU5 with SAM (with and without out medical complications), and to 144 PLWs with MAM through 10 OTPs/TSFPs integrated with a health facility in East Darfur localities and a mobile OTP in Al Lait; and b) Provide IYCF counselling at the nutrition centers through 50 mother-to-mother support groups and 160 quarterly NIPP cycle groups to capture secondary caregivers, fathers, and other community leadership that can positively influence social norms around nutrition behaviors (4 NIPP groups per OTP/TSFP per quarter). Complicated SAM cases will be referred to El Dain Hospital.

6.1 - Explain how your project aims to ensure protection mainstreaming during its design and implementation

RI promotes and adheres to the Minimum Inter-Agency Standard for Protection Mainstreaming for Health and Nutrition. This tool is part of RI sector standards and provides specific lists and higher-level protection standards for each sector. Promoting equitable access to services will ensure that the project reaches the most vulnerable members of the targeted communities. All indicators are gender, age, and disability disaggregated (as applicable), and it is expected that over 50% of beneficiaries will be women. RI’s participatory approach also mainstreams protection within RI activities, ensuring that the most vulnerable households are selected for participation in activities; that the needs of men, women, children, the elderly, and people with disabilities are considered; and that activities occur at a time, location, and in a manner that ensures safety and dignity. Special attention will be paid to mothers and pregnant and lactating women (PLW) through reproductive health services, malnutrition treatment for PLW, and IYCF counseling for mothers. Through the new approach of integrating hygiene messaging into existing health and nutrition programming, RI will look for opportunities for gender mainstreaming, such as incorporating menstrual hygiene management as awareness session topics. Community influencers, such as men, grandmothers, and traditional healers will also be included in IYCF and other nutrition awareness sessions. RI will seek to prioritize people with disabilities and the elderly in the participant selection and provide assistance to reach services as needed. RI will also ensure that all clinics are accessible to the disabled and elderly. For eligible health and nutrition participants with disabilities and/or caretakers with a disability, CHWs and the CNVs will recommend and ensure the provision of home visits if the disability impedes travel to RI facilities. RI adheres to a Do No Harm (DNH) approach. All staff will be oriented and held accountable to RI’s Code of Conduct on professionalism, impartiality, and confidentiality.

7.2 - Please outline how the project or your agency will inform and interact with inter-agency approaches to accountability to affected people and feedback mechanisms, including SEA and abuse complaints?

Under RI’s global Accountability to Communities Framework and Incidents Management Policy, accountability is a key pillar of RI’s programming. RI is committed to mainstreaming principles of accountability throughout our program in accordance with the Humanitarian Accountability Partnership (HAP) standards and the Core Humanitarian Standards (CHS) and includes minimum standards for beneficiary participation, information sharing, complaints and feedback mechanisms and staff competencies. RI will promote accountability by ensuring that the target communities have access to information about RI and to the planned activities, and by creating opportunities for those communities to participate in programming decisions and to provide feedback on the project interventions. RI’s Feedback and Accountability Mechanisms include three forms: sex-disaggregated focus group discussions on a monthly basis led by a MEAL officer; feedback boxes situated at each project site opened bi-monthly by a committee comprised of an RI MEAL Officer, a Village Health Committee (VHC) member, and a clinic staff member; and a telephone hotline managed by an Accountability Officer for highly sensitive feedback.
