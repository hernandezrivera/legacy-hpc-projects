Project Summary

Syria is currently in its twelfth year of a protracted armed conflict that has devastated its health infrastructure and strained the health system due to the forced exodus of healthcare workers and the increasing humanitarian health needs of Syria's population. Syria continues to face an on-going crisis resulting in one of the world’s most complex emergencies with extensive humanitarian needs. Since its start in 2011, the country has seen significant loss of human lives and widespread destruction and damage of the physical infrastructure affecting the provision of basic services. The COVID-19 pandemic only compounded an already complex situation in the country. According to the World Bank, a cumulative total of US$226 billion in GDP was lost due to the conflict from 2011 to 2016. The Syrian annual inflation rate is one of the highest in the world. The uneven and dynamic nature of Syria's conflict is reflected in the multi-governance structures that have emerged in different geographical regions within the country as the conflict continues to affect different geographical regions to different extents. The political division has resulted in different governance and health systems with different characteristics and structures, which influence how health and other basic services are delivered to the Syrian population, who are caught in the middle of shifting power dynamics and territories. "Whole of Syria" approach was developed in 2014, seeking to ensure strategic and operational coherence in the delivery of humanitarian assistance among partners providing support inside Syria across the operational hubs (Syria, Turkey, NES). Every aspect of Syria's health system has experienced significant destruction alongside the population's changing health needs. The security situation remains highly dynamic and prone to escalation, particularly in areas of mixed or contested control (the northwest, northeast and south of the country).
Coordination and health information management are essential as the nature of humanitarian needs across the country has not changed in 2022 but rather increased. The years of 2022-2023 demonstrate a clear shift of the necessity of coordinated humanitarian response withing “severity scale” prioritization as number of sub-districts areas ranked as 4 (113 sub-districts) and 5 (21 sub-districts) have noticeably scaled up. WoS coordination will focus to identify solutions for access: to deliver health supplies; to expand and carry out direct health services; to carry out assessments and surveys; to monitor and evaluate needs and levels of assistance. COVID-19 pandemic and continuous 2022 outbreaks and major situational developments related to armed conflict (and its potential) has only revealed the systematic challenges existing in public health system and in the country as a whole. Coordinating health sector response in a country with divided governance remans a critical task. Issues of standardization, needs’ identification, proper planning, effective response, limited funding, prioritization based on the principles of neutrality and impartiality are very serious. WoS WHO 2023 approach will continue a “hybrid” response, with cautious and objective balance factoring in humanitarian and resilience related priorities.

3 - Needs

Under 2023 HRP, 15.3 million people are in need of health services. The fragile health system in Syria continues to face concurrent emergencies and chronic challenges which affect the availability and quality of health services across Syria as well as the physical and mental wellbeing of the population. Reactivation of the PHC and SHC services is essential to ensure primary care, emergency care, rehabilitative and palliative services, community care, hospital inpatient services, immunization, cancer services, care for older people. It is critical for health sector to accelerate provision of essential medicines and supplies. Political fragmentation negatively affects health standardization while lack of healthcare personnel is a chronic challenge. Quality of health information and data on current situation, disease burden and coverage by health services remain low. Major 2022 developments led to 6 major contingency preparedness actions, including plans for Hassakeh governorate, Jan 2022; north-west Syria (extension of XB resolution), Feb 2022; northern Syria (NWS and NES) (expansion of Turkiye), May – Jul 2022; southern Syria, May 2022; 2023 winterization plan; AWD/cholera response, Aug – Nov 2022. Key public health threats of 2022 (with spillover effect in 2023) have included: COVID-19, Jan 2022 – present; suspected meningitis outbreak in NES; malnutrition in NES, TB outbreak in NES, Leishmaniosis outbreaks in NES, and current AWD/cholera outbreak.

4 - Implementation of activities

The WoS health sector in Syria requires continuous support and strengthening within cluster coordination performance management parameters. In 2023, the health sector’s priorities will be guided by the severity of needs. The health sector will aim to further buildup operational capacity to adapt operations to meet emerging needs. Through improved dialogue with the health authorities at all levels (and while acknowledging their leading role), the health sector will work to secure faster, more flexible approvals to support the humanitarian response in areas where needs are greatest (e.g., patient referrals and evacuations, the delivery of medicines and supplies, vaccination campaigns and the continuum of care for NCD patients). Main activities will include the provision of a minimum and essential package of integrated health care services at primary and secondary levels.The sector will continue enhancement of mechanisms to eliminate duplication of service delivery/activities: updating health partner reporting tools (4Ws); provision and consolidation of bi-weekly inputs (health sector operational updates).2023 approach will continue a “hybrid” response (humanitarian and resilience): Maintaining humanitarian response: technical and operational support, as well as investments in new strategic areas in health: Health workforce; Services (packages & levels)); Financing; Pharmaceuticals, Infrastructure (reactivation); Information and Comprehensive response to NES and NWS.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

Protection, including violence against health facilities and healthcare providers: • There are continuous shifting lines of conflict. The situation (political, security) is fluid. There are de-factor four governance structures. There is a large degree of uncertainty. • Essential health services are largely not accessible to people. • Information on functioning and supported health services should be continuously updated and validated and verified. • Health care staff, patients, health facilities are subject of violence with a large degree of neglect of existing protection mechanisms in place. • Protection related activities should be continuously mainstreamed throughout health programming, coordination fora, training/workshops with health partners. • Hard-to-reach areas and areas of higher severity scale must be clearly prioritized and accessed through different modalities of response. • Advocacy should be in place to register and report on all incidents related to the protection of health. • • There are limitations related to the pro-active role of civil society, grass-root organizations, community-based approach in health sector planning and response. • Systematical adoption of a gender sensitive-transformative approach in the designs of programmes, and analysis of the identified needs & barriers will be addressed through specific gender-related consideration • WHO and partners will promote continuous engagement with all beneficiaries in order to adjust services


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

WHO has zero tolerance for SEA and inaction against SEA. As part of the UN system, for the purpose of preventing and addressing cases of sexual exploitation and sexual abuse, and taking into consideration EB148(4), WHO aligns itself in essence with the considerations and criteria set out in the UNSG Bulletin 2003/13. WHO has also adopted the UN protocol on assistance to victims of SEA, the UN protocol on implementing partners related to SEA and the IASC core principles and minimum operating standards. In this context, the term “sexual exploitation” means any actual or attempted abuse of a position of vulnerability, differential power, or trust, for sexual purposes, including, but not limited to, profiting monetarily, socially or politically from the sexual exploitation of another. Similarly, the term “sexual abuse” means the actual or threatened physical intrusion of a sexual nature, whether by force or under unequal or coercive conditions. Some specific standards which align with existing general obligations under the WHO Staff Regulations and Staff Rules, apply to all WHO personnel and partners.The duties of responsible officials are well defined. On referral to national authorities, if the review by IOS concludes that there is evidence to support allegations of sexual exploitation or sexual abuse, these cases may, upon consultation, be referred to national authorities for criminal prosecution by the WHO Office of the Legal Counsel (LEG).


9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

WoS WHO will equally address the defined risks: Quality of access/Extent of access; Sexual exploitation and abuse; Limitations on protection risks analysis; Attempts of influence; Fraud, corruption, waste and aid diversion; Stark differences of views; Limited availability and capacity of partners; Deteriorating social and economic conditions; Uninterrupted presence of UN and humanitarian actors; Association with human right offenders; Interventions creating harm; COVID-19 transmission; Fund transfer difficulties. At WoS level, WHO will focus on strategic opportunities: WoS structure - all modality approach; Contingency plans for XL and XB, updated regularly, continuous information exchange; “Building back” vs “Building back better” by the MoH, “sense of ownership: one country” by the authorities; “Access” is one of priorities. Advocacy at high levels; Ongoing dialogue and progress on resilience and recovery: HRP; UN SF, WHO CCS; UNICEF, UNFPA, UNDP, UNHCR; Engaged WHO as Cluster Lead Agency. Same for technical opportunities: Institutional capacity: national authorities and health partners; Recognized rolled out programmatic response; Major types of health intelligence information available; National governance remains over key public health programs. Maintained “command and control”; NES – strengthened footprint; strong work relations with XB NES-Forum; Highly responsive WoS health Cluster team in Amman. Syria is kept on radar. Same for operational opportunities.
