Project Summary

The overall objective of this project is to protect food production capacities and income
sources in locations that are most at risk due to the chronic electricity deficit in the Gaza
Strip. The rationale of this project is to target areas most impacted by the increasing
electricity deficit in order to protect current levels of food and agricultural production,
livelihoods and food security by providing vulnerable agricultural producers in the Gaza
Strip with a consistent, clean and renewable source of energy to guarantee sufficient
electricity for essential food production. The intervention pays particular attention to the impact of electricity deficit on female-headed households and the workload of women and youth. This intervention responds to the first need under
the Agri Livelihoods section in the Food Security Sector Response Plan, which indicates
the need for interventions that prioritize responses to the electricity shortage in the Gaza
Strip. The intervention falls under Strategic Objective 3 of the HRP 2018-2022 as it invests
in solar energy sources to ensure that targeted farmers have a consistent electricity supply
as and when required in order to cope with frequent energy cuts as part of the protracted
crisis. As an emergency intervention, this project aims to minimize damages and losses to
domestic crop production in the current season and to enable normal levels of cultivation
in subsequent seasons. In doing so, this project will protect agriculture based livelihoods,
food security and domestic food production that are at risk given the chronic electricity
deficit and will enable normal levels of cultivation if electricity supply to the Gaza Strip is
further reduced.

1 - Needs

Gaza’s farmers have been impacted considerably by the ongoing chronic electricity deficit, particularly in the southeast of the Gaza Strip where a significant proportion of local agricultural produce is sourced. Given the need for approximately 10 hours of electricity per day for the irrigation of crops, farmers had barely been managing to sustain agricultural production on eight hours of electricity per day. With electricity supply down, Gaza’s farmers are faced with the choice of either substantially reducing the scale of agricultural production or investing in costly diesel generators with which to pump the required amounts of irrigation water. Farmers in the Gaza Strip are increasingly less able to cope with sudden shocks, such as the spread of the COVID-19 pandemic, the spikes in conflict, trade restrictions and persistent electricity shortages in Gaza, natural and environmental hazards, such as winter storms, the effects of climate change, and inadequate water and sanitation services (OCHA, 2020b). The energy crisis coupled with already low levels of food security adds on to an already fragile context. As reported in the latest Socio-Economic and Food Security Survey (SefSec), food security conditions in 2018 have worsened in Gaza Strip relative to 2013 and 2014. In total, the Gaza Strip accommodates most of the food insecure households in Palestine carrying as much as 87 percent of the severely food insecure and a mere 16 percent of food secure households. These findings suggest that the low levels of food security have been driven by a combination of adverse factors further aggravated by energy shortages: more households have become poorer, less resilient, and more food deprived. As of August 2020, as a response to intermittent hostilities, the Israeli authorities halted the entry of most goods to Gaza, including fuel, leading to the shutdown of the Gaza Power Plant (GPP) and triggering power cuts for up to 20 hours a day. This is in line with similar trends related to energy shortages, where in the year prior, energy demand in the Gaza Strip has exceeded 500 MW while supply has stagnated at 200 MW. This imbalance between available supply and demand has resulted in acute energy shortages leaving residents with a range of 5 and 15 hours of energy access per day. This poses a particular challenge for women, youth, and female-headed households who perform tasks and roles that take care of the house members and children and need electricity to complete them for example cooking, cleaning, and other essential roles. At the end of 2019, energy was partially supplied by Israel on average of 120 MW, while domestic energy by the Gaza Power Plant (GPP) generates approximately 70 MW, supported by the ongoing donor-supported provision of fuel aid. According to Ministry of Agriculture (MoA) estimates, the cost of one cubic metre of irrigation water has increased by up to 40 percent since Gaza’s sole power plant ceased operations in April 2017, before resuming at very limited capacity, with fears that the cost of irrigation water will increase further should the supply of electricity decrease further. Moreover, according to the MoA, up to 60 percent of agricultural land across the Gaza Strip remains at risk of being set aside due to the lack of electricity with which to pump water for irrigation. The overall impact of the increasing electricity deficit, which currently remains at more than half the current need, in terms of the extent of damages and losses to Gaza’s agriculture sector, has the potential not only to reverse the gains made by reconstruction and recovery efforts since the end of the 2014 conflict but also to further jeopardize agriculture based livelihoods, to exhaust the coping mechanisms of farming households and, consequently, lead to a sizeable reduction in local agricultural production which will in turn have a negative impact on food security levels in the Gaza Strip. Preliminary findings from a participatory assessment of solar energy needs in agriculture by FAO indicate that the private sector maintains interest and capacities to investment in narrowing the energy deficit. The contribution leveraged from this proposal will be designed in a way to also reflect and strengthen a portion of investments by the private sector related to energy. Moreover, according to the findings of a field assessment conducted by FAO in 2018 in response to Gaza’s deepening electricity crisis, farming communities in the Gaza Strip reported that input costs for planting had increased significantly as a result of the chronic electricity deficit. Increased water scarcity has further impacted the quality of crops for the summer season and has negatively affected prices in local markets as well as the competitiveness of Gazan produce in the West Bank and Israel. Women farmers are most impacted as they bear ever increasing levels of responsibility both inside and outside the home. According to the Gender Alert Report (2015) by UN-Women and OCHA, women’s limited share of assets makes them increasingly vulnerable during difficult economic times. Overall, the work of rural women in the agricultural sector is under recognized and women farmers work are considered “helpers” with many being absent in formal cooperatives regardless of the level of responsibility they have for the household’s agricultural work. Data from PCBS indicate that 18.2% of economically active women work in agriculture and constitute 30% of agricultural employees in Gaza, thereby masking biases with regard to recognizing the contribution of women. Similar to youth who support their farming families but their work is merely seen as support and not recognized work.

2 - Activities

The proposed intervention will deliver the following outcome, outputs and activities: Outcome: At least 440 at risk, food producing households have their food production capacities, income sources and food security protected with a further 300 food producing households benefiting indirectly Output 1: Agricultural producers supported with solar energy systems to ensure sufficient irrigation of crops and protection of farmer’s livelihoods. Based on the horsepower of the water pump different beneficiaries will have different PV systems designed. Activity 1.1: 20 x irrigation wells equipped with solar powered pumping systems in: Rafah (x5) Khan Yunis (x6) Deir al Balah (x3) North Gaza (x3) Gaza (x3) Activity 1.2: Installation of solar energy units for 200 x water harvesting ponds in: Deir al Balah (x25) Khan Yunis: Al Sattar (x25); Al Abasan al Kabira (x25); Bani Suhela (x25) and Qizan Al Najar (x25) Rafah: Shoka (x25);Muraj (x25); Mawasi Rafah (x10) North Gaza: (x15) Note: For Activity 1.3, agreements will be signed by the groups of farmers who own the wells to provide irrigation water to neighboring farmers at a reduced price to be determined and monitored in cooperation with the MoA. 1.4, Awareness campaign and training for women and men on irrigation management, solar energy maintenance in coordination with MoA Output 2: Poultry and Dairy Farms supported with solar energy units to maintain normal levels of egg and milk production, and mitigating climate change damage. Activity 2.1: 190 x Poultry Farms across the Gaza Strip supported with solar energy systems with which to maintain essential lighting for egg production and operating water sprinkler network during heat waves in summer in: North Gaza (x40) Gaza (x40) Deir al Balah (x30) Khan Yunis (x50) Rafah (x30) Activity 2.2: 30 x Dairy Farms across the Gaza Strip support with solar energy systems for operation of portable milking machines in: North Gaza (x8) Gaza (x7) Deir al Balah (x5) Khan Yunis (x5) Rafah (x5)

3 - Indicators

Results will be measured by change in income level and survey of energy availability of beneficiaries. Each indicator will be gender disaggregated. Project activities and achievements will be monitored consistently to ensure continued alignment with the FSS response plan and the FAO Country Programme Framework 2018-2022. Sex dissertated data will be collected on all activities. Participation of women will be closely monitored and tailored adjustments will be made to ensure effective participation of women. Post distribution monitoring will be conducted by interviewing a sample of female and male beneficiaries in order to monitor use of the inputs distributed, prospects for sustainability of the intervention as well as overall satisfaction of beneficiaries as part of FAO’s accountability to beneficiaries. Project impact will be assessed by comparing the results of a baseline and end line survey. A Grievance Redressal Procedure on the basis of FAO guidelines and taking into account local best practices has been established and is currently functional. It includes clearly defined responsibilities and follow up channels, starting with the officer in charge of receiving the feedback/complaint, and the proceeding processing procedures, up until final decision and the subsequent follow up. The procedure includes several well-defined steps tied to specific time frames including: preliminary review for eligibility, verification process and resolution and will be publicized among targeted communities in the context of beneficiary selection processes, post distribution mounting as well as in the context of partnerships agreements if and when relevant.. All project activities and results will be coordinated with the Food Security Sector to avoid duplication of emergency support efforts. Relevant results, analysis and lessons learned shall be shared with all Food Security Sector stakeholders. In addition, FAO will maintain coordination with the protection cluster and the Humanitarian Country Team in order to ensure alignment with and support of advocacy initiatives.

9 - Protection mainstreaming
9.1 - Do No Harm

The intervention proposed by FAO aims to support marginalized and vulnerable farmers whose livelihoods are endangered. The selection of farmers is solely based on needs and the most food-insecure and does not consider any other difference or discriminatory factors. The interventions proposed under this project do not put beneficiaries in risk, however, FAO aims to ensure that all efforts will be made to prevent and minimize as much as possible unintended negative effects of the interventions. As part of the project inception phase, FAO will develop a risk log identifying all potential risks, their probability and ways of mitigation. The assessment and analysis of risks will be followed by a coordinated application of resources to monitor, minimize and control the probability and/or impact of risks. External risk factors will continuously be considered in order to ensure an adequate understanding of the context of FAO operations during the implementation of the project. FAO will also ensure the implementation of PSEA principles and will have a built-in process for reporting such incidents. The proposed intervention is meant to protect endangered livelihoods of vulnerable female and male farmers and herders in the West Bank and Gaza Strip through securing the availability of time critical indispensable inputs and services, hence the core of the intervention aims at protecting beneficiaries rather than doing any harm. FAO also abides by the principle of Do No Harm; and respects a low profile during implementation of activities to avoid any harm coming tobeneficiariesThrough FAO’s participation in the UN Country Team (UNCT) and Humanitarian Country Team (HCT), the development of the political situation in the WBGS will regularly be monitored and reviewed in line with project objectives. FAO contributes to policy-level initiatives to protect Palestinians from further reduction in access to land, sea and livelihood resources (e.g. water and agricultural inputs) owing to the political situation, through its participation in the UNCT Commendation Group and HCT Advocacy Working Group. All advocacy efforts will be conducted under the banner of the UN in the WBGS to ensure that FAO’s mandate is not compromised

9.2 - Equal and impartial access to aid

FAO’s approach provides a framework for addressing and integrating issues such as gender equality, protection, the inclusion of the aged and people living with disability, and the prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse by FAO and partner staff. Throughout the process of beneficiary selection, disability and age will be a factor taken into consideration. Beneficiaries with this vulnerability will be prioritized. The project also seeks to engage with targeted stakeholders and mainstream FAO’s commitments in each phase of the project cycle. FAO maintains its commitments through regular stakeholder engagement and participation, two-way communication, transparent disclosure and a grievance mechanism.
