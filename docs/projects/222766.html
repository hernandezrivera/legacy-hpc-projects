Project Summary

"The project is an ongoing effort of FIVDB and aims to provide gender-responsive, inclusive, safe, and high-quality education, alongside psycho-social support (PSS) and disaster risk reduction (DRR) strategies for children, adolescent girls and boys, and members of the LGBTQI+ community, with a particular focus on school dropouts. The interventions will be designed following international standards, such as the minimum standards set by the International Network on Education in Emergencies (INEE), as well as their contextualized version for Bangladesh.
FIVDB will implement Integrated Early Childhood Development (IECD) and the Myanmar Curriculum (MC) in a gender-transformative approach using primers tailored for the Rohingya community. A total of 2,404 Rohingya children will be enrolled in Community-Based Learning Facilities (CBLFs) across two targeted camps (Camp 14 and Camp 24). Specifically, 2,184 children will be enrolled in MC curriculum within the 89 CBLF (50 CBLF will be set up as new and the others are ongoing) and 01 Learning Center (ages 12-18) at Camp 14 and Camp 24, while 220 will be enrolled in 10 ECDs (ages 3-4) at Camp 24 in Teknaf. The project intends to achieve a 60% enrollment rate for girls, addressing the current low enrollment rates for girls in the camps. Additionally, 3% of the learners will include children with disabilities, in line with sector targets.
The project will ensure gender-segregated WASH facilities at all 100 centers, equipped with handwashing points and materials for menstrual hygiene management (MHM) for adolescent learners in both MC and LC programs. All female facilitators will be supported, and a contingency plan will be established for emergency preparedness and response. The project will recruit 60 female Rohingya teachers, making up 60% of the total 100 center-based teachers, from the respective communities. These teachers will benefit from a capacity-building approach that includes training in pedagogy, PSS, life skills, subject-based instruction, DRR, first aid maintenance, protection against sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA), gender sensitivity, and disability inclusion. All project staff will also receive training on waste disposal and environmental pollution.
The project will directly reach 4,970 parents, caregivers, and community members, providing sessions on child marriage, child labor, and trafficking through home visits and parent support group meetings. To address barriers to girls' education, including the onset of puberty, security concerns, and social prejudices, CESG groups, community meetings, and parental involvement will be prioritized. Data will be collected and managed according to SAAD criteria. Activities will be closely monitored, and lessons-learned workshops will be conducted. New CBLF locations will prioritize learner safety. 20 Community Education Support Groups (CESGs) will be formed, each comprising 4-5 CBLFs and 8-10 members, with at least 50% being female, following education sector guidelines. CESGs will monitor student enrolment, retention, attendance, and promote parental involvement.
The project will coordinate with Child Protection, WASH, Health, Food Security, and Nutrition sectors to mitigate child protection risks. This includes emphasizing parent and caregiver engagement, involving boys and men, establishing referral pathways, creating child-friendly feedback mechanisms, and collaborating with relevant stakeholders. The project will prioritize safeguarding, accountability, meaningful access, participation, and the empowerment and leadership of girls.
The project will prioritize protection, safeguarding, humanitarian principles, and a do-no-harm approach. Rights-based and effective aid will be implemented, focusing on the seven pillars of AAP. Disaster risk management committees will be established at all facilities, and staff will receive protection gear. FIVDB's gender, PSEA, and safeguarding policies will be enforced throughout the project.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

"The Rohingya humanitarian crisis in Bangladesh is entering its seventh year, and the prospects for improvement remain uncertain. As of October 2024, 969,096 refugees are living in 33 extremely crowded camps within the Ukhiya and Teknaf Upazilas of Cox’s Bazar district, Bangladesh, according to a UNHCR factsheet. Recently, there have been 50000 new arrivals of Rohingya from Myanmar, which has further exacerbated the already dire conditions and added extra burdens to the situation.The JMSNA 2023 identified critical needs across various sectors, including access to informal education, skills development, and disaster preparedness. As the population continues to grow, these needs will intensify, particularly for vulnerable groups such as children, women, the elderly, and persons with disabilities. According to the 2024 REVA-7 data, approximately 48% of Rohingya individuals in Cox’s Bazar currently lack access to education. Furthermore, there are significant challenges for children below 18 years, as many are compelled to drop out of school or engage in work to support their families. According to the JMSNA 2023, the primary reported reason for school dropouts among children aged 12 to 18 was the inability of households to afford education costs (60%). Additionally, 15% of girls dropped out due to marriage or pregnancy, while this issue was not reported among boys. In contrast, boys were more likely to drop out to help their families or work on the farm (17%), compared to 7% of girls. Only 22% of children aged 4 attended in any ECD program during the 2022-2023 school year. The situation is particularly challenging for girls, who have poor access to education. As a result, children from the refugee population are at a disadvantage regarding educational access, and substantial efforts are needed for them to catch up.As of the ISNA 2024 data, around 41% of children aged 3 to 18 are either not enrolled in school or do not receive education services. The enrollment gap in Rohingya camps shows that for children aged 3-4 years, 53% of girls and 56% of boys are not enrolled; for those aged 5-14 years, both girls and boys show non-enrollment percentage of 25%; and in the age group of 15-18 years, 92% of females and 70% of males are not enrolled. Overall, the enrollment rate is higher for boys (62%) than for girls (57%) across various educational facilities.In camps 14 and 24, Caregivers and teachers report shortages of materials, teacher absenteeism, and inappropriate behaviour. Overcrowded classrooms, especially affecting girls, particularly girls who require gender-segregated classrooms and latrines as they grow older. Among children aged 11 to 14, girls are less likely than boys to attend educational facilities, and this gender disparity increases with age (Assessment of the Education Sector Response to the Rohingya Crisis, January 2023). This trend results in the highest proportion of out-of-school children being Rohingya adolescent girls. Additional social and religious barriers further restrict girls' mobility in education once they reach puberty. These factors contribute to social isolation, low levels of foundational literacy, psychological distress, and an increased risk of exploitation and abuse.Adolescents and young adults are increasingly engaging in harmful employment due to a lack of skills and educational opportunities. Despite alarming rates of child labor, there is a concerning gender disparity (as reported in the CPIMS+ from January to September 2024): 7.58% of working children are reported, with only 0.34% being girls while 7.24% are boys. Access to education remains a significant challenge for Rohingya children, with nearly half out of school. Barriers include a lack of awareness about education's importance, particularly affecting girls and children with disabilities. Safety concerns, including physical threats and harassment, deter children from attending school. "

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

"FIVDB ensures that protection is integrated throughout its response efforts. This approach is built on four key protection principles: prioritizing safety and dignity, avoiding harm, ensuring meaningful access, and promoting accountability, participation, and empowerment. Special attention will be given to mechanisms designed to protect refugees from sexual abuse and exploitation by humanitarian workers. FIVDB will tailor its assistance to vulnerable groups, including girls, LGBTQI+ individuals, and children with disabilities through several focused strategies:- Inclusive Training: All staff will receive training on preventing sexual abuse and exploitation (PSEA), gender responsiveness, and safeguarding to create a safe environment for participants.- Accessible Feedback Mechanisms: A transparent Complaint and Feedback Response Mechanism (CFRM) will be established, allowing beneficiaries to voice their concerns, along with regular surveys to assess satisfaction.- Targeted Outreach: Specific initiatives will promote girls' education through community awareness and door-to-door outreach, while safe spaces will be created to support LGBTQI+ youth.- Support for Disabilities: Accessibility audits will be conducted to ensure facilities meet diverse needs. Additionally, individual education plans and home-based support will be provided for children with disabilities.- Community Participation: The formation of community groups, particularly those led by women and individuals with disabilities, will be encouraged to enhance their leadership roles.- Ongoing Monitoring: Regular consultations will be held to gather feedback, allowing for adaptive management of the project based on community needs.FIVDB will ensure that the principles of beneficiary accountability, as outlined in the Core Humanitarian Standards (CHS) framework, are integrated into its projects. "

2.3 - Age & Disability

"The project aims to support 3% of children with disabilities aged 3 to 18. All staff and volunteers will receive training in inclusive programming to ensure they are equipped to serve these children effectively. FIVDB will conduct an accessibility audit of all project facilities and service points to guarantee meaningful access for individuals with disabilities. Throughout the project, FIVDB will utilize social behavior change communication materials that are accessible to everyone. For women and girls with disabilities, the project will offer door-to-door services. Advocacy efforts will focus on ensuring the participation of all women, including those with disabilities, in forming community groups and leadership development initiatives.Capacity-building approaches will incorporate life skills training for all participants, following sector standards. The project will also provide support and knowledge to help families access existing services. Individual education planning sessions will be conducted for learners with disabilities, and we will procure or develop accessible teaching and learning materials.Additionally, the project will implement home-based education support tailored to each child's needs. Inclusive sports and recreational activities will be organized for children with and without disabilities. Learners and their parents will receive awareness sessions on individual preparedness, safe routes, and evacuation points or centers tailored for persons with special needs, including the elderly and those with disabilities."

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

"The project will implement a coordinated accountability mechanism to ensure responsiveness to the affected population. This AAP mechanism will be organized based on the needs identified through the Inter Sector Needs Assessment 2024 and the findings from joint assessments.The project aims to reduce vulnerability by building the capacities of the most disadvantaged groups, including children, adolescents, men and women, LGBTQI+ individuals, and persons with disabilities. Community consultations will be integral to beneficiary selection, project planning, implementation, and monitoring. Periodically, lessons learned workshops will be conducted with community involvement, and the findings will inform the implementation of future projects.Evaluations will be carried out based on established criteria, and community engagement will be prioritized during evaluations and post-distribution monitoring. The results will be shared with all relevant stakeholders. The joint response plan developed by the inter-sector coordination group will be translated, and key messages will be communicated to the affected individuals and their communities through regular focus group discussions, community meetings, and other sessions.All facilities and project offices will be equipped with materials on Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) and safeguarding, including awareness posters about free humanitarian assistance, FIVDB’s complaint hotline number, and a complaint and feedback box to ensure timely responses and follow-ups, fostering community engagement.To maintain transparency, the project will facilitate accessible channels for sharing information regarding services, assistance, and other project-related matters. A biannual meeting will be organized with all relevant stakeholders to review the assistance provided to affected individuals, discuss upcoming initiatives, and share best practices concerning the Complaint and Feedback Response Mechanism (CFRM)."

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

"FIVDB is an active member of the PSEA network and maintains regular coordination with it. The organization’s PSEA policy is developed by incorporating core principles adapted from the IASC on PSEA, as well as the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW). In 2023, FIVDB participated in a PSEA assessment conducted by UNICEF and was recognized as having high/full PSEA capacity. During the recruitment process, FIVDB conducts PSEA reference checks for all staff and includes knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) questions related to PSEA in job interviews and written tests. All staff members are required to undergo mandatory training on the prevention of sexual exploitation, abuse, and harassment. This training also covers gender sensitivity and responsiveness, safeguarding, gender-based violence (GBV), referral pathways, and mechanisms within intervention areas. Additionally, staff receive orientation on all FIVDB policies.All project facilities will feature PSEA and safeguarding informational, educational, and communication (IEC) materials, including awareness posters that inform beneficiaries of their rights and provide FIVDB’s complaint hotline number. Visible and accessible complaint and feedback boxes will be installed in all facilities. Beneficiaries of the project will also receive regular information sessions on PSEA, GBV, gender sensitivity, available services, and other protection issues."

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

"Yes, the project aims to reduce the impacts of potential future disasters and climate-related harm through community-led management and awareness initiatives. We will establish a Climate Action Committee (CAC) at each center or facility, ensuring representation from various stakeholder groups, including vulnerable populations such as women, adolescents, and people with disabilities.The project will focus on building the knowledge and capacity of CAC members to prepare for and respond to disasters. This will be achieved through comprehensive and tailored training programs that will strengthen early warning systems. After receiving training on how to develop a Climate Action Plan (CAP), the committees will create plans for their respective centers and facilities, addressing issues related to disasters, landslide protection, and environmental protection challenges.As part of our plans, the committees will initiate community awareness campaigns, DRR education, and early warning sessions through courtyard meetings, emphasizing the importance of a climate-resilient environment. Acknowledging the diverse impacts of emergencies and climate change, as well as the potential for social stigma that often affects individuals with disabilities and exacerbates stress, the program will include awareness-raising sessions that cover both DRR and climate change-related topics."

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

"Yes. An emergency preparedness and response plan called the “Climate Action Plan (CAP)” will be adapted for the project, aligning with the ISCG’s multi-hazard response plan for 2022 and the national disaster management plan (2021-2025). The project will maintain minimum contingency stocks and support overall response efforts for both host and Rohingya communities through relevant sectors and the ISCG. FIVDB will ensure effective coordination among relevant government entities (DRRO, RRRC, CPP), ISCG’s operational partners, and sectors, along with the Energy & Environment Network (EEN). The project will refrain from any construction that could harm the environment and will discourage the use of plastic. All project staff will receive orientation on proper waste disposal and waste management practices.Community-based CAC (Climate Action Committee) will implement a CAP plan, which includes a list of vulnerabilities and capabilities for identified risks associated with every hazard. The hazard mapping list will be developed with the participation of children. CBLFs will be equipped with disaster risk management (DRM) kits, accompanied by a trained facilitator who understands the kit and its contents. All CBLFs will establish a disaster risk management committee trained in disaster risk management and referral pathways. Additionally, all CBLFs (Community-Based Learning Facilities) will have an emergency communication tree in place. Each CBLF will also develop a Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) 2019 for all identified natural and man-made hazards. This SOP will cover preparedness, response, recovery, mitigation, prevention, and impact assessment, and will include remarks regarding responsible persons and specific timelines."

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

"Yes, the project specifically targets refugee youth aged 15-18. It provides access to both formal and non-formal education programs, which include literacy, numeracy, and vocational skills training, as well as child protection mechanisms. By equipping these young individuals with essential skills, the project aims to enhance their employability and future opportunities.The project actively engages youth in program implementation and monitoring, ensuring their voices are heard throughout the process. To reflect youth participation in the program cycle, we will create referral pathways where possible. Additionally, the project will develop age-appropriate youth engagement strategies in line with the IASC Youth Guidelines.We will incorporate gender-sensitive training practices to ensure equitable access to opportunities for both young men and women, addressing the specific challenges faced by young women in the region. Continuous monitoring and mentoring will be provided to ensure that youth participants are making progress and receiving the support they need to succeed in life. Through these strategies, the project aims to equip youth with the skills and resources necessary to improve their livelihoods and contribute positively to their communities, ultimately enhancing their resilience in the face of economic and environmental challenges. Furthermore, the project will ensure that staff and volunteers who work directly with adolescents and youth receive specialized training on how to communicate effectively and appropriately with this age group."

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

Yes, our project promotes self-reliance and resilience for refugees through various sessions and training for learners' parents, caregivers, and community members. These programs will empower participants by providing them with soft skills training that covers leadership, communication, social cohesion, disability inclusion awareness, and gender-based violence. Additionally, we will advocate on their behalf with other agencies and related organizations to engage them in portable skills capacity training and income-generating activities (IGAs) in areas such as agriculture, livestock, small businesses, handicrafts, and tailoring. The project aims to connect participants with the Government of Bangladesh, private sector entities, and other financial organizations to mobilize essential services and resources.
