Project Summary

The economic collapse and the significant reduction in Government’s social spending have led to the complete breakdown of critical national social protection programmes, including the pre-conflict programme for people with disabilities. This has left many families unable to provide for the basic needs of their children, in particular families caring for children with severe disabilities.
Children living with severe mental and/or physical disabilities, are today the most socially and economically vulnerable group of children in the country. They are more likely to be poor than others and have more difficulties in accessing services. UNICEF’s monitoring surveys point to multiple deprivations and the hardship that children with severe disabilities and their families face. For example, over 70 % of children with severe forms of disabilities are out of school (UNICEF PDM 2021). They are often discriminated against and face greater risks of abuse, harassment and violence. Families with children with severe disabilities have even less opportunities to provide for their livelihoods. – mainly because of the full-time care requirements, the disruption of social care services and social stigma.
To respond to this situation, UNICEF is implementing the Integrated Social Protection Programme for Children with Disabilities. It is the largest social protection interventions for children with severe disabilities in Syria. The intervention promotes an integrated social protection model with links to public services, with a view to prepare the ground for a future transition from an emergency response to a nationally owned social protection scheme. Since the programme began in 2016, over 30,000 CWD in eight governorates have been reached.
The programme enhances the resilience of the most vulnerable families and strengthens the continuum of humanitarian and early recovery intervention through an integrated response, which combines regular and un-conditional cash transfers with case management services to facilitate the referral of CWD to available social services and increase their social inclusion. For a 24-month period, families of CWD receive a regular quarterly transfer of US$ 40 per month or US$ 120 per quarter. Each child is followed throughout the period by a dedicated and trained case manager. While the cash help families meet the basic needs of children and the households, the case management component facilitate the referral of CWD to available social services, increase their social inclusion and respond to their specific protection concerns. Therefore, the case management is a vital component that seeks to empower families to better respond to the needs of their children, beyond the life of the cash transfer intervention.
In 2021, three baseline monitoring surveys for the CWD programme were conducted in Homs, Al Hassakeh and Hama governorates. The results of the surveys conducted have indicated the positive impact of the programme on targeted beneficiaries. Key highlights of the PDM survey in Homs indicates high level of satisfaction with the payment modality (86 % satisfied/very satisfied) and increased investment in children after receipt of the first quarterly transfer with a 57 % increase in spending on education for the child and a 48 % increase in the spending on healthcare of the child. It also demonstrated high level of satisfaction with the services provided by case managers (82 % satisfied/very satisfied) in addition to increased access to disability cards (9 % of children without disability card were issued a card).
In 2022, the programme aims to reach 13,000 CWD and their families with regular cash transfer and case management services in seven governorates Dier-ez-Zor; Hassakeh; Aleppo; Hama; Homs; Rural Damascus and as Sweida. Similar targets are planned for 2023.
Accountability to beneficiaries’ is ensure through two main channels: UNICEF centralized redress and grievance mechanisms; and regular monitoring survey

4 - Needs

Children living with severe mental and/or physical disabilities, are the most socially and economically vulnerable group of children in the country. UNICEF’s monitoring surveys point to multiple deprivations and the hardship that children with severe disabilities and their families face. For example, over 70 per cent of children with severe forms of disabilities are out of school (UNICEF PDM 2021). In addition, they are often discriminated against and face greater risks of abuse, harassment and violence. In Hassakeh Governorate, UNICEF survey (September 2021) indicated that the average monthly expenditures of families enrolled was SYP 380,000 and that the average size of families was 6.6 people. This is well below the MEB, which was estimated in September 2021 at SYP 103,469 SYP per capita or over SYP 680,000 for families with an average size of 6.6 members. This is particularly concerning since families of children with severe disabilities require increase resources to meet their specific health, education and protection needs. The programme specifically targets children with 12 forms of severe disabilities as per the “Syrian National Classification of Disabilities”. Because there is no accurate estimate of the number of Syrian children falling under the programme disability criteria, UNICEF identifies eligible children through a vast network of national NGOs, community platforms such as child friendly spaces, school clubs, health clinics and outreach activities.

5 - Implementation of activities

The programme is implemented by national NGOs specialized and accredited to work with CWD. IPs are responsible for the identification, registration, and verification of beneficiaries and providing case management services. There is a division of roles whereby UNICEF uses the services of a third-party FSP to transfer the cash grants to families, while it uses a national IP to implement the case management (CM) component. For a duration of two years, families receive cash transfers and case management. The main activities are: -Training of case managers and orientation of IPs on the programme objectives and criteria. All case managers receive professional training based on a comprehensive package that has been introduced by UNICEF. -Identification and registration of beneficiaries through IP registries, community platforms like child friendly spaces, school clubs, health clinics, and outreach activities/field assessments. -Verification of the family composition, children specific characteristics and needs conducting regular monitoring visits by case managers. - Provision of 8 quarterly payment- US$ 120 per quarter; using the services of two FSPs. The cash is handed over the counter. - Provision of CM services to children and their families. Each child is assigned to a dedicated case manager to facilitate their referral to the available social services. - Monitoring activities, including PDM, randomized verification, and centralized grievance and redress mechanisms.

9 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk 1: Risk of exacerbating real or perceived inequalities through unequal distribution of cash assistance, which further erodes social cohesion and generates intercommunity grievances.likelihood (High) Mitigation measures: (i) clear communication strategy to engage with beneficiaries with knowledge and skills about programme eligibility criteria using community dialogues, print materials, such as brochures during the screening, identification, and registration phases; (ii) strict verification mechanisms; (iii) continuous on-site monitoring including post monitoring surveys and beneficiaries’ satisfaction monitoring; (iv) complaint and response mechanism through a dedicated service line managed by a UNICEF Accountability Assistant; and (vi) dedicated case manager that follow-up on the child situation throughout the period. Risk 2: Risk of excluding vulnerable communities from access to assistance by failing to factor in dynamics inhibiting their access due to lack of access to affordable transport means, particularly in remote rural areas.likelihood (High) Mitigation measures: Provide transportation cost as top-up to the cash assistance amount provided to facilitate access of beneficiaries to collect their cash in remote rural areas.


10 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

UNICEF has its PSEA policy in place and ensures establishing mechanisms to prevent and respond to sexual exploitation and abuse. UNICEF as co-lead agency for the inter-agency network (ICN) on PSEA, will continue to support and work closely with other agencies aiming at enhancing the portfolio pf protection from sexual exploitation and abuse. The main goal of the ICN is to enhance the networking for the benefit of people at risk, and ensure access to complaint mechanisms and specialized services for victims. Therefore, continuous capacity building for humanitarian workers on zero tolerance for SEA and awareness raising for the community on rights to humanitarian aid free of any exchange in dignified way is integrated in project activities. Consequently, as per-condition for contracting implementing partners, PSEA assurance and capacity building plan are guaranteed.


11 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

The overall assumption is that the socio-economic situation will continue to deteriorate and that the need of children with disabilities will aggravate further.Risk 1: Effectively deliver cash to beneficiaries in a timely manner due to the limited absorption capacity of the FSPs.Likelihood (high)Mitigation measures: Identification of additional FSP to be able to respond to the increasing demands from humanitarian agencies.Risk 2: Cultural factors impede inclusion of girls in the programme.Likelihood: (high)Mitigation measures: strengthen the gender component of the case managers/social worker training manual; pro-actively identify girls through dedicated case managers and identify the specific overall support needs for the protection of girls as well the specific differences in needs between boys and girls for children with disabilities through assistive technologies.Risk 3: High level of inflation which limited the effectiveness of the cash component.Likelihood: (high)Mitigation measures: negotiate with the Central Bank a preferential exchange rate; adjust transfer values on inflation.Risk 4: Delays in obtaining official approvals leading to delays in implementation of the programme as planned.Likelihood (medium)Mitigation measures: development of standardized agreements with the IPs across targeted governorates to ensure speedy review; establish good communication channels with MoSAL officials to ensure all challenges are shared and addressed in a timely manner
