Project Summary

After 12 years of hostilities and persistent economic crisis, people in Syria saw their situation worsen when a series of earthquakes hit northern Syria and Türkiye on 6 and 20 February 2023. The earthquakes uprooted hundreds of thousands of families, resulted in almost 5,900 deaths and more than 12,800 people injured in Syria, and severely damaged already weakened infrastructures. Many families lost their main breadwinner due to death or injury, at a time when the economic situation was already dire, increasing the vulnerability of millions of people previously unable to meet their basic needs.
Currently, over 6.7 million Syrians are internally displaced (IDPs), with 2.8 million in the northwest region alone, living in dire conditions. Access to essentials like safe water, food, medicine, healthcare, and livelihoods is limited and the humanitarian situation is expected to deteriorate further in 2024, with threats to access and a stalled political resolution.
According to REACH Needs Assessment 2023, food and livelihood were reported as the top unmet needs by and resident/host community members, with 43% of households lacking income and work opportunities. Food insecure households are highly likely to adopt food-related coping strategies, including reducing the number of meals and the size of portions. Additionally, the ever-increasing prices of food and other necessities further compounds the vulnerability of households. The NWS Cash Working Group (CWG) has reported that the price of the food component of the Survival Minimum Expenditure Basket (SMEB) had increased by 40% in NWS from October 2022 to October 2023 in Turkish lira terms. (NWS CWG REACH market monitoring, Feb 2024).
In this context, IOM aims at contributing to the improvement of the food security status and reduce the negative coping mechanisms of food insecure people in NWS through the provision of monthly food assistance. More in details, following FSL Cluster and CWG’s current recommendation, IOM, in collaboration with an implementing partner (IPs) and a financial service provider (FSP) will provide Monthly Cash for Food ($65 per household) for vulnerable displaced persons and host community members in need through cash in hand modality (8 rounds of cash distribution: one per month).
The selection of the locations will be based on the updated needs and gaps in food severity ranking and the FSL Cluster’s gaps analysis. IOM will make sure to establish coordination with FSL Cluster before mobilizing cash for new emergencies, guaranteeing that the response is coordinated with other organizations and prevent any duplication of assistance. Before implementing the cash assistance, IOM will conduct a market functionality assessment to ensure local suppliers are able to absorb the extra demand for goods and services. IOM will also monitor local prices to mitigate risks of inflation resulting from the injection of cash in the community and reassess the replicability of cash distribution month by month in targeted locations. Before the end of the project, IOM will establish coordination with other organizations and refer target locations to other actors on the ground, so ensuring the continuity of the assistance.
PSEAH strategy
Risks:
1: Abuse or exploitation of beneficiaries by IP staff during beneficiary selection and distribution processes
2: Lack of staff awareness and code of conduct on PSEAH
3: Lack of availability of information on reporting mechanisms for beneficiaries
4: Lack of female IP staff in the field
Mitigation measures:
1: Code of conduct includes PSEAH language
2: All IP staff are trained in PSEAH
3: Internal SEAH reporting mechanism for staff and beneficiaries and investigative capacity exist and are known within the organization; PSEAH awareness raising activities and materials for beneficiaries and local communities are conducted and shared
4: All field activities are supervised by humanitarian staff, including female staff members

0 - Project Summary (extended)

Protection Risk Analysis Risks: 1. Receiving food or cash can make beneficiaries more vulnerable to violence, abduction, theft, or other kinds of abuse at distribution sites or after leaving the distribution sites. 2. Cash can be used for anti-social purposes, for example, the purchase of small arms or alcohol, tobacco or drugs that can be abused that may result in an increase in domestic violence. 3. Cash may increase risks of corrupt diversion or seizure by armed groups and may expose beneficiaries to security risks. 4. Pressure on the IPs from the local authorities on who should or should not receive the assistance. 5. The selection of beneficiaries can lead to tensions among the communities. Mitigation measures: 1. Set the point of delivery close to a beneficiary’s homes or provide transport to ensure safety. 2. Financial service providers are vetted prior to each project and never have access to the beneficiary list so as to mitigate any interference from local actors. 3. Thorough needs assessments and targeting ensure that the beneficiaries are likely to use the cash assistance primarily to cover their food needs and to not sell their in-kind assistance. 4. Raise awareness with IPs about the importance of principled humanitarian assistance and reporting any incidents of interference. 5. Explain the selection and vulnerability criteria clearly to the communities via the IPs and IOM AAP. Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures. Assumptions: -The security situation in Aleppo and Idleb allows to implement activities-Local authorities cooperate with IOM-Consent-based approach for access in NWS is renewed again in July 2024 -Functioning markets exist in target areas Risks: 1: Security situation in Aleppo and/or in Idleb does not allow for viable IOM response activities to be undertaken. 2: Local actors influence IOM response activities or beneficiary selection. 3: Sanctioned groups benefitting from assistance through procurement of vendors, cash distribution Mitigation measures: 1: Establish a security and accessibility plan for targeted areas, and a process that allows to consult communities on how to proceed with proposed response in a deteriorating security environment; develop a ‘duty of care’ strategy for local partners. 2: Wider and transparent call for beneficiary registration by IPs. Conduct beneficiary verification by IOM Third-Party Monitors. Beneficiary selection based on cluster-recommended beneficiary scoring system. 3: IOM’s sub-partners in the Cross-Border response – Turkish vendors, Syrian NGOs and INGOs – as well as their sub-contractors are vetted using a system that searches publicly-available databases, including USG databases, to ensure that neither the company/organization nor its key personnel are affiliated with any sanctioned group prior to engaging the sub-partner for the first time.
