Needs

According to the latest DTM from IOM the most recent count conducted in August 2018 the total No. of IDP stands at 1,926,748 Million. They are in 110 LGAs and 804 wards. Including areas of priority for the protection cluster highlighted for 2019 funding. DRC conducted a broad scope of assessments that provide evidence of the nuances of inter-linked need across multiple sectors. This action seeks to address some of these critical gaps through a holistic and protection-led approach. Throughout 2017, DRC identified new trends in displacement linked to the evolution of the conflict, military operations, as well as the possibility to travel more easily in military convoys for the civilian population. In Western and Southern Borno State, as well as in Adamawa State, three "corridors of displacement" with a trend in mobility of population, based on previous commercial main axes. All corridors are based on pre-existing populations staying as close to their area of origin as possible. The first corridor starts in Jere, along the Muna Corridor, and moves toward Mafa, Dikwa and ultimately Ngala. The second corridor encompasses Konduga, Bama and Banki, as well as Pulka and Gwoza. The third corridor follows Gwoza down to Madagali, Michika and Mubi. DRC's analysis shows that movement of displaced persons - whether returning to their areas of origin, trying to find a different level of humanitarian assistance or access to livelihoods - will increase in 2019, making mobility of assistance and consistency of presence and services along the corridors one of the main added value of the DRC programme. The protection cluster echoed by security advisories and DRC’s own internal safety and security forecasts tensions rising in 2019 during elections and possibly increasing displacement and mobility. Resounding the cluster priorities, DRC presence in majority of the displacement corridors has identified the key protection priorities for displaced communities revolve around challenges accessing basic needs; limited access to sufficient food and livelihoods exacerbated by lack of access to farm lands as a result of the continual combative threats between the AOGs and other armed forces. A recent INSO report showed that over 800 civilians have died as a result of AOG and Armed forces activities in 2018 so far. The camps are overstretched; Bama was initially supposed to take up only 200 HH it now has 28,00 HH. The camps with education facilities within them, barely accommodate 40% of school going age children. Majority of the boys of 9- 15 years then engage in hazardous work to support their family’s income. They leave the camps to in search of odd jobs and to hawk. DRC has recorded cases of boys and girls abducted, beaten and sometimes sexually violated as a result of these risk. Female headed households make up over 50% of the population, DRC internal reporting recorded cases of adolescent girls engaged in risky coping mechanisms in attempts to survive. Majority of these girls are child mothers and consequently head a child headed household. Women increasingly face severe difficulties in accessing income generating opportunities, DRC has reported over 120 women who have been physically assaulted by armed personnel as they try to access the exits of the camps to farm. Women in many of the IDP camps are highly restricted to leave the camp, those who leave have to compromise either by paying cash or sometimes be exploited as a way of negotiating their exit. Reports of harassment, beatings and torture upon arrest are common in most of the camps. Adolescent girls and young women are constantly faced with the options of negative coping as a result of this imbalance between men and women’s access to livelihood. IDP Communities have reported that there is a lack a proper structure of reporting such issues or a system that holds these armed personnel responsible for their wrong doing.
