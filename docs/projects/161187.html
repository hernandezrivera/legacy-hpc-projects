Project Summary

After 5 years of civil war in South Sudan, the 2018-signed peace agreement provides hope. Yet, the country’s humanitarian crisis goes on: 3.71 million people are displaced, including 1.47 million internally displaced persons (IDPs). Among this population, 61% are children. Children and adolescents (particularly those between 10–19 years) are especially vulnerable to the violent crisis in Unity State, specifically in Rubkona County, due to the lack of protective structures and systems, psychosocial distress, being associated with armed forces or groups, as well as Gender-Based-Violence (GBV) and gender inequalities that have increased with the prolonged violent conflict.
Within this context, Mercy Corps (MC) proposes a project that contributes to achieving South Sudan’s HRP objectives and the objectives outlined by the Protection Cluster and Child Protection Sub-Cluster (CPSC) for South Sudan for 2020 by providing critical protection-related assistance and specialized services to the most vulnerable; preventing and mitigating protection risks through enhanced preparedness and resilience; and enabling durable and sustainable solutions.
More specifically, MC proposes a 10-months project with the objective to provide critical Child Protection (CP) services to empower and strengthen the resilience of children and adolescents affected by the crisis. This will include the provision of socio-economic reintegration support to children associated with armed forces and groups (CAAFAG) and other children and adolescents in need in key hot spot areas in Rubkona County.
In particular, the intervention will address factors associated with voluntary and forced participation of children and adolescents in armed forces and armed groups, especially the social alienation of this age group, and the lack of livelihood and educational opportunities. The ultimate aim is that children and adolescents will safely transition to adulthood, become a full member of the society and have lasting positive multiplier effects on their families, communities, and social and economic structures.
The project activities will be based on four components specifically aiming to:
1. Provide quality age and gender-appropriate focused and non-focused psychosocial support (PSS), as well as comprehensive case management services to children and adolescents in need in safe spaces;
2. Strengthen the skills of adolescents to be able to seek healthy livelihoods and income-generating opportunities;
3. Strengthen community-based mechanisms to prevent and respond to violence against children and adolescents and help build a protective, enabling, integrating and peaceful environment for them;
4. Empower and enhance the safety and well-being of specifically vulnerable girls through GBV risk mitigation and prevention activities
Through these activities, the project aims to target a total of 22,619 people (6,440 boys, 5,690 girls, 5,484 men, 5,005 women,) who will benefit from different types of activities.
By focusing on the most vulnerable and at-risk children and adolescents in key hot spots in Rubkona County, the proposed project will fill an important gap in providing critical child protection services to the most vulnerable and affected children and adolescents in need of protection assistance.

1 - Needs

After 5 years of civil war in South Sudan, the 2018-signed peace agreement gives hope. Yet, the country’s humanitarian crisis goes on: 3.71 million people are displaced, including 1.47 million IDPs (OCHA Oct‘19). About 61% are children (HNO'19). Unity State has been severely affected by the war. Bentiu is the largest PoC in country, hosting 103,424 IDPs (IOM-DTM Headcount Bentiu Jul‘19). With the conflict, the population’s access to basic services was seriously impacted, resulting in severe hardship. Yet, with the peace agreement, the situation is becoming more stable. People started returning to locations outside the PoC (incl. Rubkona County), counting a decrease of 12,101 people since Oct’18 (ibid). A growing number of returnees were also registered: from 15 Apr to 5 Sep‘19, IOM registered 27,102 returnees arriving from Khartoum in Rubkona bus station, out of which 38% intended to return to their habitual residence in Rubkona County, and 48.1% to relocate their habitual residence to this county (IOM-DTM Bentiu&Rubkona: Arrivals from Khartoum, Oct‘19). Despite these positive developments, it can be seen that children and adolescents (especially those between 10-19 y) are still suffering from the impacts of the prolonged crisis in Rubkona County: • Family and community structures, institutions and systems that normally protect children and adolescents, disappeared with the conflict. Until today, the majority of these protective structures were not yet re-established. Often, children and adolescents have to take on greater roles and responsibilities. Many have become marginalized and economically isolated. In such context, they are at an especially high risk of violence, exploitation, abuse and neglect.• Psychosocial distress, incl. the impacts of traumatic events on psychological development, learning abilities, and use of violence, is affecting children and adolescents. Difficulties in accessing food, water and other services further contribute to their stress. In particular, adolescents are going through a pivotal and difficult time in life with the onset of puberty, putting them at an even higher risk of psychosocial distress. About 1 million children are estimated to need Psychosocial Support (PSS) in South Sudan; a devastating reality for children and adolescents in Unity State (CPSC Strategy 2019-21). • Over 19,000 children are estimated to be associated with armed forces and groups (ibid). Assessments and discussions with community members in Rubkona County in 2019 have further shown that forced recruitment, notably of adolescent boys, remains a reality (MC discussions, DRC Assessments Apr/May’19). Children and adolescents are either forced or voluntarily join in exchange for support (e.g. food). Those who are returning home often face problems of reintegration and may be alienated from daily life. Employment and training opportunities to generate an income are rare, which already led some going back to armed groups/forces in the past. Yet, recent developments in Unity State show signs of increasing positive developments: MC was able to trigger discussions between UNMISS and SPLA-IO in May/Jun’19 with the view to release children in Dingding (Rubkona). • Girls, especially adolescent girls, are particularly affected due to their gender and age. Rape and sexual assault are just a few GBV forms they face. Economic desperation may be the most common reason for families to force their daughters to marry. Accessing education is another drawback: In 2018, only 45% were girls in schools in Unity State (Education Cluster, Oct’18).• Specifically, adolescents are often grouped with children, youth (18+) or adults, but their needs are different. Yet, humanitarian actions addressing their particular needs are rare. Thus, MC proposes a project to empower and strengthen the resilience of both children and adolescents through a response based on their particular and specific needs in key hot spots in Rubkona County.

2 - Activities

1. Provide quality age-and gender-appropriate focused and non-focused PSS activities, as well as comprehensive case management services to children and adolescents in safe spaces: • 4 Adolescent & Youth Centres already established in Rubkona (PoC, Bentiu, Rubkona, Dingding) will be maintained, being a safe space where children and adolescents can meet and participate in structured PSS activities. • 14 PSS Facilitators and 17 youth mentors, providing a role model to children and adolescents, will be trained on relevant topics e.g. CPiE, PFA, PSS, management of the Adolescents & Youth Centres, etc. to help ensure the daily functioning of the Centres. • Structured, gender- and age-specific recreational and creative activities will be organized in all Centers to help children and adolescents build and strengthen social relationships with peers, reduce stress and explore new skills. • Life skills trainings will be organized for 400 children and adolescents over several months. Topics will focus on teamwork, peace and conflict, responsibility etc. Sessions will be highly interactive to help enhance learning• Child and adolescent volunteer groups will organize awareness-raising activities on topics relevant to them (e.g. CP, peace building) for their peers. This will help build their leadership skills, self-confidence, and help reach those unable to come to the Centers. • Recognizing that children and particularly adolescents are undergoing a difficult period in their life in a crisis context, and that a smaller number might face problems requiring more focused support, a help desk will be operated in the Centers (except PoC), offering comprehensive case management services. 2. Strengthen adolescents’ skills to seek healthy livelihoods and income-generating opportunities • Vocational and Entrepreneurship trainings will be offered to adolescents. • After the training, a small business start-up kit and grant will be provided to a selected number of (older) adolescents to support them to start their own small business 3. Strengthen community-based mechanisms to prevent and respond to violence against children and adolescents and help build a protective, enabling, integrating and peaceful environment for them: • Parents/caregivers, gatekeepers, community leaders will be engaged in workshops to help build an integrating enabling, peaceful and protective environment for children and adolescents. Topics will include parents’ roles and responsibilities, PSS, PFA, CPiE, advice sessions, meaningfully engaging children and adolescents as alternative to violence and joining armed groups,etc. • Community awareness-raising activities will be organized on topics related child rights, the importance of protecting children and adolescents in conflict, the prevention and reintegration of CAAFAG, etc. 4. Enhance girls’ safety and wellbeing through GBV risk mitigation and prevention • Community awareness-raising activities on GBV risk mitigation, prevention and response will be organized to help reduce harmful practices against girls and facilitate a change in attitudes and behaviours. Overall,MC will coordinate and collaborate with all relevant actors, e.g. those working in CP, GBV, FSL etc to avoid potential overlapping and find solutions how interventions can complement each other (e.g. allocate areas, referrals). MC will also participate in coordination meetings (e.g. CPSC, community meetings) at local & national level to strengthen coordination.

6.6 - Will beneficiaries' rights be legally recognised?

Mercy Corps is taking a community-based and human rights approach in its proposed intervention, with the ultimate aim to empower and strengthen childrens' and adolescent’s resilience, including CAAFAG, in Rubkona County, while coordinating and collaborating with other relevant stakeholders. As such, the services provided by Mercy Corps may not be legally recognized, given the close collaboration with targeted program participants on the ground. Nevertheless, Mercy Corps will closely collaborate with other actors working in the legal field, such as the UN and NGOs providing legal services, and /or providing other services that are legally recognized (e.g. those working in Education) and refer program participants to them, according to their particular needs.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

The perspectives and challenges of displacement affected communities are incorporated into the program design. Overall, the project is implemented in a displacement setting, with people slowly starting to return to their home locations or identified new places (see section on “Needs” for more details). Within this context, Mercy Corps has closely involved the community – that is not only displaced people but also host communities and returnees – in the project design to ensure that their particular perspectives and challenges are incorporated (see also sections above for more details), such as through consultations and its ongoing projects implemented in the target locations. During the project implementation, monitoring and evaluation, they will continue playing an important role and able to express their particular perspectives and challenges, so as to see how they can be included in the ongoing project and possible future interventions. Taking into account that displacement affected communities are present in all targeted locations, they will be able to access and benefit from the project. In addition to this, Mercy Corps will ensure that: • The offered services are provided at an adequate time when displaced communities can realistically access them• The offered services can be accessed by displaced adolescent girls, • The offered services can be accessed by displaced persons with reduced mobility and non-mobility-related disabilities• The offered services are free of charge to them• The location of facilities and the routes to them are distant from threats of violence; especially the risk or threat of GBV and attacks from armed groups• Infrastructure and facilities are adapted so that all displaced individuals and groups (boys, girls, adolescents with disabilities etc.) can access and use the facilities in safety

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

The project is linked to longterm interventions realised by MC and other humanitarian actors, by taking a community-based approach and coordinating with governmental and other relevant actors. MC opened its Country Office in 1995 focusing on children and youth, among others. Since 2005, MC has operated in Unity State. Since 2017, MC has realized a CP program in Rubkona County, aiming to empower and enhance youth and adolescents’ resilience.Focusing on CAAFAG, activities involve PSS, life skills, case management, livelihoods, community engagement, GBV risk mitigation, prevention. It follows an integrated approach with MC’s Education, WASH and FSL programs having a vital focus on children, adolescents and youth in the same zones. Through this, MC is able to share resources, benefit from each other’s expertise, and realize complementary humanitarian interventions. As such, MC has become a recognized CP actor. In this context, MC proposes to continue its CP Program in Rubkona County in 2020 to respond to the needs of the most vulnerable children&adolescents, incl. CAAFAG, in a durable and sustainable way.The project will thus fill vital gaps to target the unmet socio-economic needs of children and adolescents most affected, and complement and be complemented by MC’s other programs that are planned to continue in those zones. MC will link the project to interventions implemented by other government and non-government actors (e.g.CP, GBV actors) by collaborating and coordinating with them to avoid overlapping, ensure complementarity and enable sustainable and longterm interventions. Responding to specific needs of children & adolescents, the project will complement other programs often targeting children or youth generally. MC will also participate in coordination meetings (e.g.CPSC, community meetings) at local and national level to strengthen coordination and complementarity of interventions. Through all this, the scope and impact of the CP project will increase.

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

The proposed project is line with national objectives for the Child Protection Sector. Overall, it is in line with the Child Protection Sub-Cluster Strategy (2019 – 2021) and, as such, in line with: • Child Act, 2008• South Sudan Development Plan, 2011• Ministry of Gender, Child and Social Welfare Strategic Plan, 2013 – 2018• National Gender Policy, 2012• National Disability and Inclusion Policy, 2013• Child Responsive Budget in South Sudan: A guide for policy makers and child focused organisations, 2014• National Action Plan on the United Nations Security Council Resolution 1325 on Women, Peace and Security and Related Resolutions, 2015-2020 • Agreement on Cessation of Hostilities, Protection of Civilians and Humanitarian Access of, 2017 • End Child Marriage in South Sudan: Strategic National Action Plan (SNAP) 2017-2030 Furthermore, it is in line with the national Child Protection Sub-Cluster’s rollout of the Case management SOPs, PSS manual, and the Practical guide for working with girls formerly associated with armed forces and groups. These are certainly only a few of the many national objectives that the proposed project is aligned with.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

MC recognizes that the peace is young in Rubkona County and that the situation in more remote areas remains volatile. Nevertheless, MC considers that the environment is sufficiently safe and stable to enable durable solutions, which has further been shown through the signing of the peace agreement in 2018. Increasingly, people started returning to locations outside the PoC. A growing number of returnees were also registered in Unity State (see section on “needs” for more details). Furthermore, MC has been continuously operating in Unity State since 2005, showing that the environment is sufficiently safe and stable to operate and enable solutions. Despite those positive trends, MC nevertheless keeps monitoring the security situation closely and being present in all humanitarian security groups where the latest information is constantly shared. Its staff also follows the security situation to complement the latest security updates. The analyses from the latest security information importantly inform the implementation of activities. If deemed necessary, the needed actions and changes are immediately taken to ensure the safety and security of MC staff. In particular, the Head of Office Bentiu is in charge of following the local security as well as MC’s Country Security Manager, who conducts recurrent field visits to update the Security Management Plan and Risk Matrix.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

The project includes a handover component, in line with MC’s global guidelines, entailing: (1) Administrative close-out – ensuring that all reporting and donor-close out requirements are met, documents are organized and filed, all expenses are recorded, etc.(2) Programmatic transition - transitioning program deliverables to external actors (e.g. beneficiaries, community), preparing communication to external actors; finalizing M&E processes; sharing lessons learned, etc. About the project’s sustainability and impacts, MC believes that these will be positive due to the its design and approach, such as: • Community involvement: Involving the community is key to have long-term impacts and ensure the sustainability of any project. The proposed project will involve the community at all stages, giving them ownership of the project. Thus, the chances are high that activities – e.g. Youth Centres - will continue after the project end. As the entire process will be participatory, the skills and knowledge gained by the targeted group of people will be transferable. • Enhancing capacities: Enhanced capacities of program participants are important to have long-term and wide-ranging impacts. The proposed project focuses on enhancing their capacities with a long-term view. For ex., by enhancing capacities of its target participants through vocational trainings, coupled with the provision of business kits/grants, MC gives important start-up skills and support so that they can start own small business which, if successful, will have wide-ranging positive impacts not only on themselves, but also their families and communities. • Building on existing structures: An important element for MC is to build activities on existing structures to increase the sustainability of the project. The proposed project is not a new project and its components have been there for several years. In fact, the Adolescents & Youth Centers have already become integral parts of the community.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

MC uses the Community Accountability Response Mechanism (CARM) feedback mechanisms, used across MC globally, to ensure that the needs of the most vulnerable are met and their risks are minimized. CARM involves the community in problem solving and ensures their feedback is taken into account in the response. Besides this, MC will:• Continuously inform community members (in written/oral language, pictures etc) how to provide feedback on problems and achievements with the services rendered and support them to monitor them jointly.• Install a secure box in various, accessible locations where feedback can be lodged• Hold informal gatherings alongside activities where community members can confidentially provide feedback• Reach out to communities and administrate a questionnaire to capture feedback• People can call, SMS or visit the MC Office to provide feedback. About PSEA, MC reinforced its global Integrity Hotline and response to complaints.MC has a dedicated independent Ethics Team staffed by trained, professional investigators to monitor the hotline and oversee investigations. An in-house team works to identify options for stronger processes and procedures for vetting new team members such as background-checking mechanisms, safeguarding-screening questions. Another team works to find legal ways to ensure anyone terminated for sexual misconduct at MC cannot find work in the NGO sector again. On the ground, MC will ensure that its field staff is trained on PSEA; messages on PSEA are posted in offices and public spaces, as well as discussed during meetings with program participants communities to ensure they receive the information. Overall, MC will monitor the various feedback mechanisms to ensure that they can be accessed by the most vulnerable (incl those who are illiterate, live with disabilities, women,girls etc) and, if not, propose recommendations to adapt mechanisms accordingly. MC will evaluate and analyze the gathered feedback and take the needed action
