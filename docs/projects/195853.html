Project Summary

Bangladesh now hosts almost 1 million refugees in Cox's Bazar with limited health service coverage in 33 congested camps. The proposed programme will support the continuation of the existing 5 health facilities (3 Primary Healthcare Centres (PHC) in 1E, 8E and 13 and 2 Health Posts (HP) in 9 and 11) and either the establishment of one new PHC in the camp or the taking over of a new PHC from another organization. These health facilities will provide primary healthcare services according to the Minimum Package of Essential Health Services (MPEHS) in the refugee camps in Cox's Bazar including Sexual and Reproductive Health and Rights (SRHR), Maternal, Neonatal, and Child Health (MNCH); and Adolescent Sexual and Reproductive Health (ASRH) with a special focus on disabled people and women and girls. In addition, Mental Health and Psycho Social Support (MHPSS) will be provided to the Rohingya refugees through the existing all HPs and PHCs.
The health facilities will provide outpatient medical consultation, immunization for children and women, ANC, NVD, PNC, family planning services, adolescent counselling, prevention of non-communicable diseases and curative services, physiotherapy, referral services, and routine laboratory investigations and medicine dispensation. In addition, there will be 24/7 ambulance services to facilitate the referral of the pregnant mother and other emergency cases to the secondary and tertiary health facilities.
BRAC health facilities will offer consultation with patients, need-based physiotherapy, counselling and referral to persons with disabilities, and providing assistive devices as per their needs. Special focus will be given to providing services and making health facilities inclusive for persons with disabilities. In addition, BRAC will focus on adolescent reproductive health and engage men in SRHR services.
The programme will also focus on improving the health-seeking behaviour and health practices of the Rohingya and the host community. The project will train and engage community health workers to conduct awareness sessions with women, men, and adolescent girls and boys in both Rohingya and host communities, including basic antenatal and postnatal cares and family planning services. BRAC will engage community health workers to conduct regular household visits and refer eligible children and women for immunization. They will also refer pregnant women to avail facility-based delivery to reduce widely practised home delivery by local midwives and community women.
SRHR among the Rohingya refugees will be strengthened by promoting gender equality and gender mainstreaming activities. The awareness-raising efforts will target key actors to be sensitive on gender rights to influence the decision-making process of SRHR to strengthen such efforts. BRAC will ensure an inclusive response to reduce the gap induced by stigma and oppression. BRAC will provide survivor-centred health management training and support to its health workers and practitioners to ensure optimal clinical management of Sexual and Gender-Based Violence (SGBV) survivors, ensuring confidentiality and privacy for referral services and screening.
BRAC will aim to strengthen MHPSS services and increase access of the refugees as well as provide individual level PSS service and group sessions to the Rohingya community integrating with the Health services. As the facility for psychosocial support among the refugees is still low, BRAC will provide these services ensuring inclusion of children, adolescents, and caregivers under the Health sector.
BRAC has an updated and simulated contingency plan to respond to any health-related emergencies to ensure uninterrupted service and increase further capacity during disaster and disease outbreaks. In addition, BRAC will ensure optimum infection prevention and control embedded in the programme.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

Women and girls comprise 52% of the Rohingya population, while children under 18 years are 52% of the total population in the camps. Yet, the patriarchal and misogynistic culture of the refugee communities has resulted in a high rate of GBV, SGBV, and child abuse cases which needs regular medical attention. Over the last five years, the health needs of the Rohingya refugees have changed from emergency to regular and long-term needs. Multiple efforts have been made to alter health-seeking behaviour and utilisation of Primary Health Care facilities. Yet, women and adolescent girls still have limited access to these services due to gender inequalities prevailing in harmful cultural practices. The other factor is their disenfranchisement and little participation in socio-economic activities, thus limiting women and other genders' exercise of their sexual and reproductive health rights and the ability to make key decisions regarding important issues that directly affect their lives. It is estimated that 2.4 % of the Rohingya population are pregnant and lactating women. As per SRH working group bulletin, January to March 2022, a total of 99,541 ANC visits (Rohingya: 74.4%, Host: 25.6%) were reported during the mentioned period. 5.7% of the total visits were from adolescent girls aged below 18 years. On the other hand, there were 22,203 PNC visits (Rohingya: 77.3%, Host: 22.7%) of which more than 5% were from adolescent girls. Adolescent girls (<18 years) accounted for 4.4% of the deliveries. Early pregnancy poses significant risks to the health of the adolescents girls in the camps. According to ISCG Health sector, maternal, stillbirths, and neonatal deaths are still existent in the community with a high rate (23% of 1,474 deaths reported in Jan-Sept 2022). According to the midterm review of the Joint Response Plan (JRP) 2020, only 53.2% of deliveries are conducted in health facilities. Many pregnant women are not accessing adequate Antenatal Care services and delivering their babies at their shelters, increasing the risk of maternal mortality and morbidity. The major reasons behind the home deliveries include cultural norms, the influence of traditional birth attendants, lack of caregivers/attendants to take care of children when the mother is admitted to the clinic for delivery and poor access to transport in the camp, especially at night. Critical gaps have been found regarding family planning services in the Rohingya camps. Long-acting reversible contraceptives are neither widely available nor an accepted practice in the centres. Intrauterine Devices (IUDs) are offered in only 43% of the health facilities inside the centres. In addition, comprehensive medical care for sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV) survivors is only offered in less than 50% of the primary healthcare centres in the camps. The above gaps show that a lack of gender equality and poor male engagement are closely linked to poor access to SRHR services among the Rohingya community. There is a need to continue community mobilisation at the camp level to share health information, education and information sharing support health-related preventative measures. Preventive measures and response to any outbreak or health hazard need careful planning and coordination, which is vital for stabilising the health situation. For example, the rate of Malaria and Dengue is high in Cox's Bazar, and that affects the health of refugees.The host community, which is very much culturally aligned with the neighbouring Rohingya communities, has poor health-seeking behaviour.

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

BRAC wants to ensure that all Rohingya refugees can have equitable access to all basic health services in a predictable, efficient, and timely manner and that the humanitarian community is more transparent and accountable. Rationalisation ensures that common strategies, systems, protocols, and tools are used across all the camps leading towards a standardised and cost-effective approach to providing equitable services to refugees and a minimum standard of service delivery by different partners through jointly coordinated approaches between agencies and key stakeholders. The health sector of BRAC emphasises integrated health service delivery to refugees and host communities by adopting inter-sectoral camp-based coordination and strengthening professional relations with the government. BRAC supports the integration strategy of 'one implementing partner per sector per camp' in the possible areas. BRAC has the capacity to provide education, child protection, WASH, health care services and other services in an integrated manner. So, BRAC can provide these services in the same camp by applying an integrated approach among these sectors. Multi-year funding is essential to provide improved health services with long-term engagement. Hence, coordination with government agencies, UN organisations, refugees, and other implementing partners in the camps have become stronger over time. The health sector considers all of its experience (gathered through internal coordination with different sectors) in the humanitarian context as an asset to continue improved-health services for the Rohingya community in a better and cost-effective way. Simultaneously, BRAC as an organisation possesses the capacity to adapt with one implementing partner per sector per camp, where possible.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection

BRAC involves all genders to ensure collaborative participation in capacity building and maintain age & sex disaggregated data for reporting. Our health facilities provide a comprehensive support for SGBV survivors. The initial primary medical checkup is conducted, and referrals are made to the Secondary Health Centres, and law enforcement agencies for necessary support. BRAC will establish GBV corners at the heath facilities to provide specialised services and counselling to the GBV survivors. BRAC also works closely with the protection sector in a synergic way where the health sector provides primary health care to the affected population, and the protection sector provides them with safety and security advocacy support. All health facilities work closely with the protection sector for referral and prevention. In addition, BRAC's health facilities are designed in a persons with disabilities friendly way to ensure their easy accessibility.

3.2 - Gender

Ensuring gender equity is the core focus of BRAC's strategy. BRAC will strive to address the barriers faced by vulnerable genders, mainly women and girls, older people, LGBTQ and persons with disabilities by sensitising the male members of the communities through community male leaders and volunteers. BRAC will consider women, girls, and other marginalised people at the centre of the health services. BRAC will ensure that health centres emphasise an inclusive approach to reach women, girls, the elderly, persons with disabilities and gender-diversified individuals.

3.3 - Age including child safeguarding

BRAC is a member of the Child Safeguarding and PSEA Network and has stringent safeguarding policies for adults and children. In addition, a dedicated PSEA committee is formed in BRAC, having a focal person to deal with all cases, including child safeguarding issues. During the project designing phase, as well as the monitoring of the project implementation phase, BRAC ensures incorporations of gender, age, and disability elements. Furthermore, our health staff, including community workers and volunteers, are trained and aware of the child safeguarding policy. If any such claim is found in our health programme, the PSEA committee investigates, mitigates and takes disciplinary action(s) according to the procedure.

3.4 - Diversity

BRAC promotes equity and equality at every stage of project design and provides services to all according to the needs of the people irrespective of gender, age, disability, religion and ethnicity and insists on upholding the rights and dignity of the affected people. BRAC’s Health sector acknowledges diversity through disability inclusion while selecting volunteers and community workers. BRAC prioritises the refugee women and other genders and supports the group to enhance their capacity and engagement with the communities. Our physiotherapists assess the types of disability in camp areas using the Washington Group Questionnaire on Disability and afterwards provide appropriate support. The health sector has a Gender Response Team which will be engaged to include gender-diverse people in the Rohingya community. BRAC's gender equality and diversity practices ensure that the services are provided to all the affected people and their differences are welcomed.

3.5 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

While designing humanitarian projects, BRAC considers UNHCR’s AAP framework- i) participation and inclusion; ii) communication and transparency; iii) feedback and response; and iv) organisational learning and adaptation. In the designing phase, BRAC will ensure community participation through consultations. Community health workers will conduct home visits to share information regarding services and the feedback mechanism using Rohingya dialect. Complaint Feedback and Response Mechanism (CFRM) related information in the facilities will also be displayed in Burmese language. The Health sector collects and analyses gender, age, and disability-disaggregated data to understand various needs related to health for the affected population. BRAC regularly analyses the feedback/trends/unresolved issues and links with respective organisations/sectors for feedback referral to ensure AAP. Based on the community needs, interventions, planning and priority of the programme can be modified.

3.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

BRAC HCMP has an EOC (Emergency Operation Centre) and ERT (Emergency Response Team) structure with representatives from all sectors to respond in an integrated way in an emergency. During environmental emergencies and man-made or natural hazards, the Health and DRR sectors jointly work in the affected areas by conducting primary assessments and forming temporary primary health posts; providing emergency medical support; and through referral support, both sectors ensure their other basic supports. As a regular activity, health message dissemination through volunteers on natural disasters, personal hygiene, fire extinguisher, medicine, and advocating for handwashing or use of hand sanitiser are conducted in the camp areas during a disaster. DRR staff will provide basic Disaster Risk Management and Emergency Response training to our health staff, including community volunteers, to respond effectively to an emergency crisis.
