Project Summary

CARE in partnership with two local organizations, the Women and Media Development (TAM) and the Women's Center for Legal Aid and Counselling (WCLAC), will directly respond to the previously established objectives outlined in the HRP 2023 (Cluster 2 Protection Mainstreaming).
The proposed set of interventions seeks to attend to the Protection needs of the most vulnerable community members in the West Bank , with a focus on women, children, female youth and people with disabilities (PWDs). The specific target group is in need of reporting and S/GBV and PSEA referral, as well as legal aid /representation in cases of reported violations, GBV emergency health services that include both MPHSS and SRH services, as well as protection cash assistance for GBV survivors, of whom the selected number will also receive income generating support through a holistic package of activities that will support their business ideation and development processes. In complement to direct service provision at the community level, CARE will work with Stars of Hope society (as contracted partner) and the two partners to first increase their capacity in gender and PWD inclusion, specifically with respect to emergency situations, and then to support and enhance their, as well as GBV survivors’, self-care and stress management skills.
CARE/partners have a long-standing experience and expertise in implementing said activities with the selected target groups, and here join together to address the existing needs and priorities synergistically. WCLAC, which already plays a prominent role in addressing GBV in Palestinian society in both the public and private spheres, under this intervention, will provide legal aid, social counselling, protection and referral services to 100 GBV survivors, and will conduct capacity-building activities for 125 participants, WLC members, humanitarian staff and activists (i.e. journalists, etc.) to support their inclusion of gender and PWD-focused services, and with TAM will jointly provide MHPSS services (900 total, including 200 girls, and 50 WWD/GWD). Also with TAM, we will work with 100 service providers who are front-liners working in response to GBV, MHPSS and CP to enhance their self-care and stress management capacities. TAM, who works to promote women’s rights and gender equality at the individual and community levels , will provide, in coordination with health providers like MoH and others SRH services to further strengthen the response to GBV survivors.
CARE has global expertise in all modalities of assistance, as well as technical knowledge of GBV integration, , codes of conduct, protocols/training materials on Safeguarding and PSEA with regards to GBV referrals, so is able to monitor risks and service delivery in terms of ensuring safe access and delivery mechanisms, Post-delivery Monitoring tool (PDM) and feedback mechanisms. To further participants’ economic enablement, CARE will also bring in its well-established programming in women’s economic empowerment to provide a package of income-generating services, including coaching, business plan development, and in-kind tools, as necessary, to 30 participants, focusing on GBV survivors. Finally, CARE will also provide dignity kits to 1000 GBV survivors/at risk.
The proposed intervention will be targeting 4,350 vulnerable community members in 5 governates in West Bank.

1 - Needs

The design of this project has been guided by the needs assessment and gender analyses of target communities, including the protection cluster needs analysis/HNO 2022, the recently published MSNA by OCHA and REACH (September 2022), CARE’s discussions with partners and stakeholders working in protection against GBV who have articulated the existing gaps vis-à-vis protection mainstreaming that is both gender and PWD-inclusive, and the shortage of support for the most vulnerable community members who experience violence, and/or are survivors of GBV. CARE also has recently conducted a rapid needs analysis that included several meetings and discussions with targeted community members, as well as MoSD. The assessments considered all new needs and implications of the most recent conflict in Gaza on the protection-focused services provided in the identiﬁed locations/communities and/or the capacity of service providers to provide life-saving protection to women, girls, men and boys in response to their experiences of GBV. OCHA estimated that in 2022 there would be 1 million people in need of GBV prevention and response services, with the highest number in Gaza. Women and girls in Gaza, as well as in Area C and East Jerusalem, women and girls with disabilities (WWD/GWD), Bedouin women, internally displaced women and girls, and those residing in refugee camps, face greater risks of GBV, as they lack access to services, basic needs and livelihood opportunities, which keeps many in poverty and trapped in the cycle of dependency and abuse. As per the Global Protection Cluster (September 2022), 38% of ever-married women in Gaza experienced some form of violence (psychological, physical, sexual, social or economic) by their husbands at least once. 64% of ever-married women experienced psychological violence, 11% of ever-married women experienced sexual violence, and 26% of ever-married women experienced physical violence by their husbands at least once. 31% of households in the West Bank and 19% in Gaza are concerned about the safety and security of girls, while 24% and 17% in the two areas respectively are concerned about the safety and security of women. The COVID-19 pandemic has increased the fragility and shortages suffered by the Palestinian health system and the accessibility to health services. Certain services that are preventative and life-saving, including the emergency SRH and MHPSS are not easily accessible to those in immediate need. The OCHA/REACH MSNA notes, 12% of households reported that women of reproductive age (15-49 years) had access barriers to specialized reproductive health barriers. Services in Primary Health Care centers and district offices are inaccessible after working hours, and many lack the ability to pay for follow-up treatments, forensic examinations and medicolegal reports. Weak clinical management of rape services is an obstacle to assisting and supporting survivors of sexual violence. The survivor-centred approach is often breached through lack of privacy, survivor-blaming practised by service providers and mandatory reporting, all of which are likely to affect the willingness of survivors to access health care services after instances of sexual violence. The scarcity of competent professionals, technical and financial resources and fragile referral services, continue to undermine MHPSS services to survivors of GBV provided by the MoH. In the absence of adequate training, GBV and mental health cases can go undetected by primary health workers who are often the main point of contact at health facilities. Those working directly and indirectly with the affected populations of conflict and violence, and with survivors of S/GBV are likely to be secondarily exposed to traumatic events and to experience symptoms of secondary trauma. Therefore, it is necessary to provide them with skills and knowledge that support others and advance their own internal coping mechanisms. The Protection Cluster identifies several key priorities, some of which include mainstreaming GBV as a crosscutting priority in the oPt humanitarian response, strengthening legal protection for women and girls and destigmatizing women and girls’ access to RH services, including strengthening clinical management of rape to efficiently assist GBV survivors. The ongoing exposure to recurring violence across the oPt has also had considerable repercussions on the mental health of community members. In September 2022, OCHA reported that one-third of Gazans need psychological and social support. MDM France reported that 60% of the research participants in 10 West Bank communities have never received MHPSS while those who did receive it, did so directly from CSOs and I/NGOs (2022). Hence an immediate need for MHPSS services that respond to distress, including psychological first aid (PFA), suicide prevention, specialized case management, and clinical mental health services.Livelihood opportunities for Palestinians are highly affected by the occupation ad blockade, escalations in hostilities, as well as COVID-19. Women’s access to livelihoods is constrained by traditional gender roles and limited economic opportunities. Meeting basic needs such as food, water and sanitation expose women to further risks of GBV. In oPt, 48% of households report difficulties in obtaining enough money to cover basic needs. In Gaza, this was reported by 81% of households and in the West Bank by 31% of households. 10% of households reported spending more than 75% of their total household expenditure on meeting basic needs (14% in Gaza and 8% in the West Bank). While 50.1% of household expenditures was reportedly spent on food, almost 30% of households reported worries about not having enough food to eat. It is clear that assistance to community members in oPt needs to be more holistic, linking humanitarian aid with employment and income-generating opportunities.

5.2 - Please briefly explain why the specific modality/ies for this response were chosen.

Service delivery and Cash Voucher Assistance (CVA) are the most suitable modalities to address the identified protection needs in the targeted communities. Direct provision of said services cover/ address most of the access barriers that were identified in the different assessments including MSNA 2022, HNO-2022 and CARE’s assessments. All identified barriers, including physical access for most of the targeted communities due to either physical barriers (whether imposed by the existing geo-political separation and checkpoints across oPt, and/or potential risk of conflict and violence, or lack of infrastructure that would allow PWDs to access said services), or due to the lack of available services or cultural stigma vis-à-vis mental health needs, are addressed through the direct provision of protection services. CARE has recently piloted the CVA for Protection intervention in Gaza, and the participants, according to the PDM results, reported an enhanced sense of dignity and self-worth (89.3%) and increased positive coping mechanisms (78.7%) as the most notable impacts of the assistance. Most participants identified Health (74.7%) and Safety /Protection (61.3%) as the most prioritized services they were able to access, and the majority of them spent their cash on Food (86.7%) and to pay of their debts (77.3%). The CVA modality targets GBV survivors to help cover their urgent needs. As they may be vulnerable groups with additional risks or more marginalized (i.e. PWDs), and lacking access to livelihood opportunities, it provides economic support, as part of GBV prevention and response, that enables access to life-saving, as well as livelihood services for those at risk. CARE has vast experience as well as technical expertise in GBV integration and well developed CVA standards operating procedures, codes of conduct, protocols/trainings on PSEA with regards to GBV referrals and is able to monitor risks and service delivery to ensure safe access and delivery.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

CARE’s ‘Do No Harm’ principles are embedded in all approaches and interventions by CARE, from the ideation process of a project, throughout its duration and after the project is finished. CARE has identified risks, their potential impact and mitigation strategies, and all activities proposed are designed based on numerous discussions with community members, partners and other stakeholders, as well as on needs analyses (i.e. rapid / gender needs assessment) that are sensitive to the complexities of the local context to ensure that people are not exposed to additional risks through our action. CARE is committed to protecting and ensuring the safety and security of participants, dignity and accessibility of services; CARE’s activities are inclusive and designed jointly with the community members’ feedback, and the selection criteria for activities are transparent; the data collection processes are sensitive to the context, power dynamics in communities, and also the management of sensitive information is conducted with caution and care.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

PWDs are considered as one of the main targeted vulnerable groups in the proposed project, as they were identiﬁed by HNC as one of the vulnerable groups who needs special consideration, for that the project team will map out, with the community representatives, partners and other relevant actors, the PWDs in need of services and assistance proposed in this project. The referral will be also an option for those who need to be referred to advanced or specialized services, which will be coordinated with other actors as needed. Those PWDs who are not able to access services will be visited at home. Similarly, social workers specialized in working with children will be part of the MPHSS team, will work with them on the psychological aspects and will follow up with them.
