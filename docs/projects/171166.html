Project Summary

The program seeks to respond to the prolonged humanitarian crisis affecting the Palestinian population under occupation, currently exacerbated by the COVID-19 crisis, by contributing to the protection of their rights and dignity in accordance with IHL and IHRL, and with a cross-cutting focus on gender and community participation. The program is to be implemented by the Action Group for Organised Local Response (Action Group) and other protection cluster partners: a group of civil society organisations that seeks to promote the localisation of humanitarian response and to respond to the multiple needs of Palestinian communities in an organised manner, by building on the respective sectoral and geographical strengths of each of its members. The program as a whole is multi-sectoral and includes actions linked to protection, livelihoods, WASH, education, mental health and advocacy, as well as to reduce the impact of COVID-19 in areas throughout the West Bank. While the program is multi-sectoral, it has been divided in sectoral projects to facilitate the management of such a comprehensive program. In this sense, the present project includes only the protection-cluster-related activities even though the whole program is in line with the strategic objectives of the Protection Cluster, including i) Increased respect for International Humanitarian Law IHL and IHRL and accountability for violations as well as ii) Protection mechanisms are put in place –such as Community Protection Committees or the provision of protection services - to prevent and mitigate the effects of the occupation and conflict- related violence, such as settler violence, property damage and confiscation, blockade and restrictions and violations against children. The population targeted under the program is highly vulnerable to IHL/IHRL violations due to the presence of their communities in Area C and East Jerusalem and to their proximity to settlements, military bases and Israeli military checkpoints. The program pays special attention to the different vulnerabilities, needs and capacities of women, men, girls and boys. Specific target groups include children affected by the conflict, women affected by GBV and others vulnerable to conflict-related violence, such as agricultural workers and youth.

1 - Needs

In addition to the HNO needs assessment, the current program is based on different assessments done by the Action Group and other protection cluster partners: three conducted in 15 communities of the Governorates of Qalqilya and Salfit in 2017, 2018 and 2019-20, one in 6 communities of the Governorate of Jerusalem in 2019 (3 in East Jerusalem and 3 out of the boundaries of the wall) and also an assessment done recently to address the needs produced by the COVID-19 crisis, especially focusing on the Hebron, Bethlehem, Qalqilya and Salfit governorates. Together, these assessments cover a population of 195,152 people, including 93,977 women and girls, 101,175 men and boys; 7,044 people aged over 65 years, and approximately 4,098 people with intellectual and/or physical disabilities (1,974 women and girls, 2,125 men and boys).Through focus group discussions, more than 600 people from the communities participated in the assessments; half of them were women. Also specific focus groups were conducted to incorporate a gender and child protection perspective, with 161 women and 149 children participating respectively. Notice that the geographical scope of the project responds to (1) the capacity of the Action Group to deploy the already built and ongoing mechanism they constitute in the West Bank (2) to address the most urgent humanitarian needs. These human rights-based, integrated and participatory assessments found that the protection of the rights of the target population in accordance with IHRL and IHL is compromised by several factors, currently worsening because of the consequences of the COVID-19 crisis: The main protection problems identified are: (1) Confiscation and isolation of land and harassment of settlers and the Israeli military: The assessment found that agricultural land and resources are being abandoned progressively in the target communities, and with this, the appropriation of the land by settlers and Israeli authorities and the risk of confiscation is increasing. In addition, families are losing the ability to continue with their traditional agricultural and livestock activities. The western communities are more affected by the Wall and the Seam Zone, leaving lands isolated on the other side and difficult to access. In the target communities 26% of the lands have been already confiscated or are at risk of confiscation because of the construction of the Separation Wall (2) Lack of accountability, isolation from HR offenders and lack of legal support: Incidents related to settler harassment and violence, which has increased during the COVID-19 crisis, cause the population to have to take long and costly legal actions to protect their property and themselves. Lacking resources and support, the main response strategy of families is to monitor these violations , often being insufficient and unable to provide necessary evidence for legal defence procedures; (3) Lack of protection and support services for GBV survivors: There are some service providers but physical, economic and cultural problems to access them, such as stigma, lack of time for women, lack of resources or lack of transportation. Through the assessments conducted, together with the PCBS 2019 “Violence Survey in Palestinian Society”, 19,000 women and girls are estimated to be suffering violence in the target communities. Moreover, GBV against women has also worsened since lockdown measures were put in place. Women’s organizations are reporting an increase in the incidence of GBV and a 10% rise in calls to hot lines (UN Women, 2019). The lockdown also forced 1 out of 6 households’ main income earner to stop working during this period (PCBS, 2020). Other protection-related problems that fall into other sectors are: - Cultural norms, frustration and stress exacerbated by occupation (Gender): GBV has its root causes in unequal power relations between women and men. The occupation contributes to aggravating GBV by increasing the frustration of some male family members, who end up turning their frustration into violence against their families. (4) Movement restriction imposed by Israel: Mobility is limited by the multiple controls, barriers, checkpoints, settlements, etc., which prevent the population from accessing their livelihoods, and basic services such as education or health; the restrictions imposed by COVID-19, added to the end to PA coordination with Israel in response to its proposed annexation of the West Bank, has led to a further deterioration of the situation.(5) Limited access to mental health services and psycho-social support (MHPSS): The main barriers to accessing MHPSS are stigma, lack of available services, economic access, lack of qualified personnel and the impossibility of leading a normal life without a psychological balance. Teachers are generally unable to address these problems due to a lack of capacities and motivation, so they are in need to improve their knowledge and awareness in psychosocial distress and GBV that can be applied with students. It is calculated that around 55% of children in the West Bank are affected by some kind of physical and psychological violence, affecting 55,331 children in the target communities. (6) Social stigma regarding mental illness: The main barrier to accessing support services is social stigma. Likewise, there are many members of the community who do not recognize that there is violence against women, boys and girls.

2 - Activities

Part of the program relates to the provision of services for GBV survivors and vulnerable groups, in a sustainable way (responsive and remedial) involving the community and legal support and targeting needs exacerbated by COVID-19: *Psychosocial support for 584 women survivors of GBV and/or other women showing symptoms of psychosocial distress. *Psychosocial support for 52 men ex detainees and/or other men showing symptoms of psychosocial distress.*Legal assistance (representation in courts and consultation on HLP and family law) for 284 women, with a special focus on survivors of GBV. *Awareness sessions with 410 teachers and 415 parents on addressing the specific psychosocial situation and needs of children (girls and boys) showing signs of psychosocial distress (PSCCW coordinates these actions with the Directorate of Education and schools in the target communities). *Distribution of dignity kits, whose contents will take into account the needs of the different family members –pregnant women, people with disabilities, elderly people, children- (120 families-282 women, 294 men) *Unconditional Cash Transfers to 120 women heads of households (576 people: 282 women, 294 men) for them to satisfy their basic needs with dignity. Another pillar of the program involves the promotion of the community-based protection approach in the target communities, in the form of: *The participatory design of Community Protection Response Plans (CPRP) and related tools, based on the conducting of Participatory workshops with key stakeholder groups (women, children, youth, men and people with disabilities) in the CPRP; *Central workshops, composed by different groups of the community to collectively identify and prioritise risks and responses to be included in the CPRP. *Strengthening of Community Protection Committees in 11 communities, through trainings on the role of women in conflict resolution and peace-making as per UN Resolution 1325; sexual and gender-based violence; the rights of the people with disabilities and other special vulnerabilities; Women's Land, Housing and Property Rights; and child protection, among other topics. Through this standardized process, 220 engaged members (50% women) of the communities increase their knowledge and capacities to denounce IHRL/IHL violations and access legal assistance for vulnerable and risk-threatened groups. PSCCW (engaged with the Protection cluster and the GBV working group) is responsible for the implementation of all the activities mentioned and will apply the security measures against COVID-19 to ensure the safety of the target population and the service providers (no more than 10 people per group, masks, gel, open spaces if possible, distance between participants). Contingency plan in case face-to-face activities cannot be conducted: as PSCCW already did during the first lockdown in early 2020, their PSS services will be provided remotely, through electronic devices, ensuring the best timing for participants in order to have the safest possible environment/Trainings will be adapted online/Dignity kits and Cash Assistance will be distributed minimalizing the number of people engaged with the delivery. They have offices in Salfit and Bethlehem for the implementation and monitoring of the program. Notice that ACPP and PSCCW are partners and have fully coordinated in order not to duplicate the geographical locations targeted in our corresponding HRP projects. The reinforcement of the Community Protection Committees (CPC) are the core protective mechanism for the communities. There is long experience in the creation, expansion and strengthening of these groups which are formed by members of the community such as leaders, members of the local councils, teachers, etc. and always with an equal participation of women. Their role in the protection program is related to the engagement with the distribution of dignity kits (which will be coordinated with UNRWA and the GBV sub-cluster) and an advocacy campaign in West Bank, consisting of the documentation and dissemination of cases of IHL/IHRL violations by the CPC. Another pillar of the advocacy strategy embraces the monitoring of IHL/IHRL violations and the legal aid for the victims of HR violations. The associated activities are the following: Yesh Din (participant at Legal Task Force): *Accompaniment of victims to file complaints with the Israeli police and coordinate access to police stations and make complaints to the IDF (legal assistance to individuals at court).*Registration of testimonies of victims affected by crimes against property, violent crimes, land confiscation or environmental damage committed by Israeli settlers/military. *Registration of the cases through GIS by type of incident and geographic location, and with data disaggregated by gender.*Use of data from registered cases for advocacy. BIMKOM:*Legal and technical assistance to Palestinian communities threatened by demolition orders, confiscations, and so on. issued by the ICA through the creation of a report to present an objection to the ICA in coordination with the municipalities of the affected communities. *Dissemination through social media and advocacy actions based on the creation of a report that analyses the consequences of Annexation for Palestinian communities.B´Tselem (engaged with the protection cluster):*Monitoring, documentation and dissemination of violations related to IHL/IHRL violations committed by Israel against the Palestinian population under occupation, targeting local and international stakeholders; Advocacy campaign on settlements directed at responsibility holders.*Monitoring and updating of data on Israeli settlements and outposts and their links to IHL/IHRL violations. *Presentations, workshops and field visits in East Jerusalem and WB.

8.6 - Feedback and complaints

Yes

8.6.b - If 'Other', please specify :

A Local Committee is established in each community with members of the Municipality/Popular Committees of the refugee camps, of the community and implementing partners. This committee is responsible for the adequate implementation of the project, risk management and communication with the people targeted. The first action they undertake is to announce the project in the community, ensuring the participation of the people targeted and relevant members of the community. They inform about the activities planned and the selection process, as well as complaint mechanisms. In case that any target person or member of the community has any feedback or complaint, they address the committee so all engaged organizations in the project are aware, including the Municipality/Popular Committees of the refugee camps and the implementing protection partners, who have to respond and take adequate measures. Each organization can respond to complaints and risks in a different manner: the Municipalities/ Popular Committees of the refugee camps can support their communities according to their responsibilities, the implementing partners could adapt or improve the activities and ACPP should inform donors, note the lessons learnt and improve the design of the project. The local committee regularly conduct meetings with beneficiaries and community members, fostering women participation, to monitor and assess the implementation of the project, the difficulties faced and receive feedback.

8.6.c - How will feedback be used?

As mentioned above, the feedback received by the Local Committee translates into a different response depending on the tasks of each organization. Other times, the implementing partners need to coordinate to develop more adequate tools, surveys, questionnaires, templates, timelines, etc. Some challenges are difficult to deal with such as those related to cultural and traditional issues that need time, sensitivity and patience to address and see the impact, so in these cases they increase the follow up of the challenge being mindful and proceeding with a clear road map. In all cases, feedback and complaints are noted, filed and used to improve the design of the project.

9 - Protection mainstreaming
9.1 - Do No Harm

The project has been designed in a way that responds to the needs of the target communities, and taking into account existing and emerging risks that may occur during the implementation. In order to meet the "do not harm" principle, the project remains sensitive to the cultural reality of the targeted communities and puts the focus on local capacities. The first measure is to rely on the implementing partners, and their deep understanding of the communities and their reality and have reached a trust environment after many years as reference organizations in this context. This approach allows the activities to be sensitive and take into account all those issues that may exacerbate the conflict. Examples for this is the relevance of engaging the Municipalities with the strengthened community protection groups or the attention that must be placed when workshops are planned with participation of both women and men, in some communities.Activities such as mental health support requires special discretion to be effective and avoid harming the participants as stigma and social pressure remains present, mainly in conservative communities. Visibility and communication are also an essential part to ensure compliance with the "do not harm" principle as far as the political context of the conflict compels us to be very careful in terms of the level of visibility we give to our work. This is particularly relevant when implementing advocacy activities, legal assistance and denouncing rights violations. As mentioned in section 8.6, feedback and complaints are gathered and not only answered and addressed but discussed among the implementing partners in order to assess the context, potential social tensions and over-expectations that may prejudice the population. In this sense, the participation of the target population and the communities stands out as a key element not only for the relevance and ownership, but also as a way to minimize potential risks.

9.2 - Equal and impartial access to aid

The target population is chosen through a precise selection process based on humanitarian needs and vulnerability criteria. Assistance is delivered to those most in need and greatest situation of vulnerability, over everything else. Responsive activities that provide services directly to those who are most in need start with a careful planning to make sure that they will be the ones that will receive assistance in an equal manner. To that end, each respective implementing partner makes sure the coordination with reference organizations such as women’s community-based organizations, local institutions (municipalities, sectorial ministries...) and any other sector-related actor in the scope of intervention. Each specific case should be treated according only to their specificities, thus avoiding any favorable treatment. Here again the expertise of the implementing partners ensure professionalism and the use of adequate and standardized criteria. This project in particular addresses women affected by GBV, regardless of age, as well as men, mainly youth affected by detention and/or imprisonment. Legal assistance and support is provided to those people identified by the partners that meet specific vulnerability criteria. The fact that the target areas include Area C, East Jerusalem and H2 (though cases from other areas may be addressed if they meet the same vulnerability criteria), is indicative of the aim of addressing the most in-need cases. Awareness raising and advocacy do not provide services directly but put the focus on the most vulnerable, seeking to improve their situation and holding duty bearers to account for ensuring the rights and well-being of those under their responsibility.

10.1.b If 'Other', please specify :
If not checked please explain why (narrative text)

In terms of PSEA, ACPP has a written Code of Conduct containing provisions promoting ethical behaviour, applicable to Board, management, staff, partner organisations and sub-contracted organisations. The CoC clearly identifies (a) the types of prohibited conduct and clearly states that staff must refrain from any acts of misconduct; (b) states the consequences of engaging in conduct that breaches this policy; and (c) includes a statement of receipt and an understanding section that staff are requested to sign and date. This CoC is annexed to all agreements signed with implementing partners and sub-contracted organisations.Annexes to the CoC include protocols on moral and sexual harassment; and on prevention and action in the event of possible situations of lack of protection and abuse, harassment and sexual abuse of children and adolescents, that are in line with best practices (IASC’s Six Core Principles Relating to Sexual Exploitation and Abuse), and that reflect zero tolerance for sexual exploitation, abuse and harassment as well as any type of unethical behaviour. ACPP regularly assesses safeguarding risks in its work, including identifying key staff involved in sensitive posts with risk related to safeguarding and child protection
