Project Summary

Across northern Syria, some governorates, including Raqqa, Hassakeh, and Deir Ez Zor, have the some of the highest ranking of severity of Nutrition needs, according to the WoS Nutrition sector (Humanitarian Needs Overview, 2018). SC offers increased access to quality Health and Nutrition services and knowledge to children and their families. A detailed analysis of the Nutrition situation inside Syria remains largely unconfirmed, due to the long-term insecurity; inaccessibility; and lack of Nutrition responders, who have the capacity to undergo timely and quality representative Nutrition surveys to provide a clear picture of the needs on the ground. With the exception of Idlib, the Ministry of Health has approved a series of SMART surveys to be rolled out across Syria in 2019, including areas within Northeast Syria (NES), to collect representative data on acute malnutrition. Despite the lack of adequate data, one recent SMART survey conducted in Tel Abyad District in Raqqa Governorate (June, 2018), and reinforced by monthly data from the Nutrition sector surveillance system (collected at health facility level) shows a Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) rate of 2.1% - categorized as “low” according to WHO. The rate reflects similar GAM rates in other areas of Syria over recent years. The stunting rate is alarmingly high (32%), reflecting chronic malnutrition over a sustained period of time. The Raqqa SMART survey recommended to scale up Infant and Young Child Feeding (IYCF) programmes. In September 2018, the Nutrition surveillance in Raqqa found that out of 4,102 children screened (aged 6-59 months), 281 children were identified with Severe Acute Malnutrition and 98 children were identified with Moderate Acute Malnutrition. Apart from IYCF messages delivered by humanitarian actors, SC is currently providing the only IYCF services that go beyond messages with 1-1 counselling and follow up. Continuing and scaling up infant and young child feeding support is fundamental to the overall nutrition response in Syria. Agencies and Health practitioners in NES in particular lack appropriate skills and knowledge to support caregivers with appropriate infant feeding interventions. Capacity strengthening of all responders in IYCF is essential to the Nutrition response inside Syria. Moreover, the need to build capacity and integrate PSS skilled support into feeding counselling is especially needed to provide additional support during feeding counselling, as well as refer caregivers for specialized services if needed. Data concerning IYCF is also lacking inside Syria, and currently relies on anecdotal evidence, observations, key informant interviews, and data in other areas of Syria. In 2018, SC completed an IYCF baseline assessment in South Syria to help better inform the IYCF programme that was supported by a previous OFDA grant. The survey revealed very low exclusive breastfeeding rates (only 3% and 6% in two areas) and poor breastfeeding patterns in relation to frequency. Artificial feeding was high (93% and 76%), with the majority of mothers mixed feeding. Bottle feeding was common and most caregivers cited ‘breastfeeding difficulties’ as the reason for the cessation of breastfeeding. Overall, the dietary diversity was poor, and although there was high consumption of certain individual foods groups (dairy; eggs; tubers; grains), it did not equate to an adequate diverse diet. In Northern Syria, it is expected that IYCF practices are just as poor. The numerous requests by caregivers seeking infant formula for their infants indicates that assessment and urgent protection of the non-breastfed child needs attention, as they are even more vulnerable in emergencies. Barriers to appropriate infant feeding need to be investigated further during IYCF counselling and to provide guidance and support on achieving appropriate dietary diversity as well as improve exclusive breastfeeding, as both are life-saving feeding practices.

Needs

The Child Protection (CP) risks faced by girls and boys inside Syria, combined with the challenges families and caregivers experience in addressing the Nutrition needs of families and specifically of PLW and infant and young children has contributed to an overall decline in the physical and mental health of children. According to data from current SC psychosocial support (PSS) programming, children and their caregivers are having difficulty coping with the traumatic effects of ongoing insecurity. SC recognizes that the mental state of a caregiver has a direct impact on the care practices of infants and children. Immense stress caused by the conflict such as trauma, lack of time, lack of privacy and misconceptions are some of the most frequent the disincentives to safe nutrition practices in emergencies, namely breastfeeding. Maternal stress can lead to worsening care practices, such as neglect or less frequent feeding times, or introduction to infant formula feeding, compromising a child’s immune system and leading to malnutrition. The prevalence of infant and young child feeding practices are low and suboptimal. In the 2015/2016 SMART survey, rates of exclusive breastfeeding were 30 percent and early initiation of breastfeeding were 35.9 percent. The results of a Knowledge, Attitudes and Practice (KAP) survey on IYCF from March 2017 conducted by cross-border Nutrition partners in parts of Aleppo, Idleb and Hama governorates indicated prevalence rates of exclusive breastfeeding were at 30.9 per cent (25.3 percent - 36.8 percent, 95 percent CI), prevalence rates for early initiation of breastfeeding were 37.8 per cent (34.6 per cent - 41.0 percent, 95 percent CI) and the minimum acceptable diet was achieved for 57.3 per cent (53.4 percent - 61.1 percent, 95 percent CI) of those surveyed.Livelihoods depletion, limited financial and physical access to food, continued high levels of displacement and unexploded hazard contamination/insecurity relating to the conflict have exacerbated vulnerability. The limited access to quality water as well as significantly reduced purchasing power that inhibits access to hygiene items, may contribute to a high prevalence of childhood diseases. These factors can negatively influence the nutrition situation, especially in the context of worsening health-care and service coverage that characterizes the situation faced by many people living in UN-declared besieged and hard-to-reach areas as well as people living in overburdened communities or among populations “on the move”.These combined factors have the potential to further jeopardize the health status of girls and boys under five and PLW, increasing the overall risk of undernutrition as well as the short- and long- term consequences impacting their Nutritional and Health status, including stunting and growth faltering, morbidity and mortality. 4.6 million girls and boys under five years of age and pregnant and lactating women are at risk of under-nutrition and in need of preventive and curative nutrition services in 2018. 84,200 girls and boys aged 6-59 months are acutely under-nourished, of which around 18,700 are in the severe category and are eleven times more likely to die than well-nourished children. 865,295 girls and boys under five years of age suffer from micronutrient deficiencies. 3.05 million girls and boys under 5 years of age require optimal feeding for adequate nutrition status while 1.55 million pregnant and lactating women require preventive nutrition services (HNO 2018).

Implementation of activities

To improve children feeding practices to contribute to the reduction of morbidity and mortality among infant and young children by ensuring access to safe, adequate and appropriate nutrition services. Building on and contextualizing integrated nutrition and protection programming model in southern Syria, the Infant and Young Child Feeding in Emergencies component of the project will implement integrated IYCF-E and PSS activities in the camps of Ain Issa, Al Hol, and Mabrouka and Suluk town. Currently, there are 10 mother-baby areas (MBAs) in NW and NE in total providing safe and quiet space for mothers and infants to receive feeding support and 1-1 counseling on maternal nutrition and child feeding. The MBA's are spaces for mothers to meet, bond and discuss facing similar issues to infant feeding. In addition, the centers will outreach and deliver IYCF messages, conduct mass MUAC screenings, and asses, refer, transport and follow-up CMAM cases. The IYCF-E staff will be trained on PSS skills to support mothers who are in distress or unable to engage with their infants, and to recognize when a mother needs further specialized mental health support. SC will assess mother-child pairs to determine the type of support needed. Inside MBAs, newly enrolled mothers will be provided with a breastfeeding shawl to encourage breastfeeding and support privacy. Group education sessions will take place on appropriate maternal and child nutrition. On a daily basis, caregivers will be provided with short and detailed sessions that address common feeding challenges, misconceptions, foster mother-baby bonding and to promote how IYCF-E practices are life-saving. As complementary feeding is a topic that is often overlooked yet much needed, education sessions will also include demonstrations on how to improve complementary feeding practices in emergencies. The intensive counseling and education will be supported by information, education, and communication (IEC) materials that have been developed by or in line with the Nutrition and FSL clusters. Nutrition educators will also conduct community outreach activities to deliver nutrition messages or orientations at key points, as well as screening acute malnutrition using Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) where treatment services are available. IYCF-E training for health practitioners will also be held. Two Mobile Health Clinics will have IYCF corners where a partitioned area will be designated to conduct 1-1 counseling. MUAC screening is conducted as routine and any maternal and child feeding difficulties will be identified by health staff and referred for immediate skilled nutrition counselling. Outside the clinic, education sessions will take place for caregivers and extended families in the community. The Nutrition teams consisting of counselors, officers, and educators, will have a combined training on the WHO/UNICEF 40-hour course on IYCF and SC’s IYCF-E curriculum which will be led by SC’s internal expertise. In addition, the staff will be trained in PSS by the MHPSS Regional Technical Advisor and provided with regular refreshers by the Nutrition Program Coordinators. In addition, monthly meetings will take place to discuss trends in feeding difficulties and counseling/education techniques. Follow-up monitoring, coaching and mentoring will continue to ensure that the IYCF program is implemented according to best practices. CP teams will also be trained and supported to ensure that children and families requiring IYCF support are identified and referred. Moreover, trainings will be held with health practitioners on IYCF-E technical skills with emphasis on the importance of breastfeeding. Orientations will also be held with health workers and stakeholders at key forums such as the NES INGO forum to advocate for the adherence to the BMS SOP, highlight the dangers of uncontrolled infant formula, and underscore the life-saving practices of breastfeeding and complementary feeding.

Protection Risk Analysis

Risk: Security accidents or fires happening inside the learning spacesMitigating measure: All staff, guards, cleaners and facilitators must be trained on safety and security standards and fire fighting.Risk: Community tension due to service delivery locations or types of services offered if one community is served and a neighbouring community not served/cannot access or if information offered is not consistent between communities. Mitigating Measures: Continuously ensure service mapping, consultation with community on program interventions, regular communication with different communities on availability of services including host communities and returnee populations. Expansion of services to non-camp locations where needs are present, to avoid concentration of services in camps. Monitoring Mechanism: Regular consultations with communities, FGDs, feedback boxes Risk: Resistance from local authorities to accessing and implementing programming. Mitigating measures: SC has established relationships with relevant local authorities and stakeholders, such as local council, education directorate, ministries, community gatekeepers and religious leaders. The stakeholder will be, involved throughout program design and implementation. Monitoring Mechanism: Regular meetings with relevant authorities. Risk: Barriers to access for marginalized groups Mitigating measures: Specific measures are taken to ensure input is received from marginalized groups such as women and girls, children with disabilities and the elderly. Feedback is used to inform planning, implementation, monitoring and evaluating activities. Mitigation measures are brainstormed with the community to ensure that girls and boys (incl. children with disabilities) have equal access to SC activities. Additionally, as needed specific measures are taken to ensure activities are age and gender sensitive. Monitoring Mechanism: FGDs, feedback mechanism Risk: SCI or partner staff violating SCI CSG rules harms children; damages the reputation of the organisation; and can lead to loss of funding and prosecution. Mitigating measures: All SC staff, volunteers and representatives sign the Child Safeguarding Policy and Code of Conduct, child safeguarding risk assessments are conducted for all project sites and mitigating actions and put in place, all staff are trained on the Child Safeguarding Policy and Code of Conduct. Partners have received CSG training via Hurras. Partners have their own reporting mechanism as well as CSG focal points in the field and main CSG focal point in Turkey. Risk: Affected populations and SC staff and volunteers, are placed at risk of sexual exploitation and abuse, including harassment. Mitigating measures: Training will be provided to SC staff and volunteers on SC’s Anti-Harassment Policy and Code of Conduct. SC will also continue to provide trainings on the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) through SC’s PSEA focal points. Risk: Gender-Based ViolenceMitigating Measures: The MBAs operate through developing strong interaction between mothers, PLWs, caregivers and the nutrition teams. This ensures a continuous feedback channel on gender-related issues that will feed into the program design to strengthen the inclusion of gender-specific service delivery. As the primary target group includes mothers, PLW's, caregivers and children under 2 years, the scope of the Nutrition program is to significantly contribute to gender equality and increase awareness across target age groups. Overall Monitoring: SC upholds the importance of setting up various channels of communication and complaint to mitigate the risks or solve the issues in a rapid and most culturally sensitive way. SC and partners will be promoting various and confidential communication channels to collect feedback and complaints such as complaint boxes, phone lines, email and one on one meetings.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Safety & Security (S&S) All staff movements must be pre-approved and refresher security trainings are regularly conducted. The Hostile Environment Awareness Training is mandatory for NES deployments; local staff have been trained on Advanced Personal S&S. SC’s partners engage with relevant stakeholders and receive timely updates. SC works on a common response to unacceptable conditions. Airstrikes SC supports partners to immediately report security incidents to be analysed and to develop and implement comprehensive safety plans. Loss of access SC and partners conduct thorough security assessments and maintain updated contingency plans. If necessary, SC will suspend programs and work to resume activities. If this is not viable, SC would assess possibilities for relocation. Only in a worst-case scenario would project termination be considered. Work permits Measures are in place to mitigate the Gov. of Turkey (GoT) legislative environment hindering SC’s ability to operate, I.e. using a third-party HR company to overcome work permit challenges. Diversion of resources to terrorist/ proscribed groups In 2018, interviews were conducted to identify areas for increased risk mitigation; an action plan was developed. SC launched an initiative with OCHA to provide external training on international humanitarian law and negotiation with armed actors. Partners have their own systems of anti-terrorism vetting checks for all contracted suppliers and SC’s Syria office vets against the third-party database for procurements $750+. Shelling Whilst the most frequent attacks of shelling take place in southern rural Idlib and northern rural Hama, no area is immune. Escalation of military activity at Turkey/Syria border SC program locations and contingency plans are under constant review and the Hibernation, Relocation and Evacuation plan is shared with staff. Offices and guesthouses are located away from military bases and have the necessary S&S infrastructure. Attacks by armed groups Security assessments are conducted on potential project sites. Proximity to military locations is monitored and a back-up vehicle is used in high-risk areas. Staff transit must be pre-approved and take place during daylight. Improvised Explosive Devices SC coordinates closely with Tetra Tech, new project locations are cleared by the Mines Advisory Group and follow up trainings in Mine Risk Education are planned for staff. Road accidents SC drivers are tested; daily vehicle checks; all vehicles are equipped with tracking devices and satellite phones; mandatory use of seatbelts. Disruption to cash transfer operations In NES, SC is conducting a review of Hawala delivery to identify mitigation measures/additional suppliers. In NWS, NGOs have seen temporary suspension of Bab Al Hawa and have faced challenges in money transfer due to GoT restrictions.
