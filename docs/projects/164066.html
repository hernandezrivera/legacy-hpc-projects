Project Summary

NRC’s project will contribute to Food Security (FS) sector HRP objectives 1, 2 and 3 . NRC will implement its FS activities through a combination of direct and partner-led modalities, depending on context and access. The NRC Food Security project seeks to build resilience by providing a pathway from acute food needs to greater self-reliance and livelihood recovery, with a focus on the restoration of agricultural livelihoods related to food production and commercialization.
Geographic areas will be targeted based on the severity and scale of needs, inter-agency coordination at hub-level (including the Cash Working Group), and access.
NRC will provide Food Security assistance to vulnerable conflict and displacement-affected populations, including IDPs, refugees, members of host communities, IDP and refugee returnees, and non-displaced households suffering the effects of the conflict.
1. Emergency Response: Vulnerable households can meet basic needs.
To ensure the protection productive assets of displacement affected households and to ensure that households can meet basic needs and reduce negative coping strategies.
Cash Based Assistance: Unrestricted Cash Based Assistance as a primary response modality to empower households to define and attain their own specific needs whenever appropriate.
Information and Behaviour Change: Cash based assistance will be accompanied by information about the importance of dietary diversity and nutritious dietary choices to increase the impact of cash in promoting food security outcomes
2. Economic Recovery: Vulnerable households achieve self-reliance through own-food production and/or income generation.
To facilitate the transition of displacement affected populations from humanitarian assistance to self-reliance. NRC will use market-based approaches to facilitate access to demand driven income generation activities.
Home-Based Food Production: targeted grants (cash or in-kind) to support home-based food production and training in food production techniques.
Income Generation: Access to income-generation activities through access to temporary emergency income and grants (cash / in-kind) to support income generation, and basic business skills training.
3. Inclusive Markets: Households achieve resilience through sustainable livelihoods and inclusive markets.
To promote the development and access to inclusive markets that enable displacement affected populations fully restore their sustainable livelihoods.
Sectoral Market Based Support: Sectoral Market Based Support interventions to focus on the development of agricultural livelihoods. Targeted grants (or kits) supported with training, mentoring, and market access support to maximize impact.
Market Access Support: NRC will focus on the following core areas to promote inclusive markets for Conflict Affected Syrians: Employment Facilitation, Entrepreneurship, Local Capacity Strengthening and Infrastructure Rehabilitation

4 - Needs

Across Syria, 11.1 million people are in need of humanitarian assistance, with a total of 7.9 million people who are thought to be food insecure, with an additional 1.9 million at risk of food insecurity as well as physical and mental wellbeing. Food insecurity in Syria remains affected by the continued conflict causing protracted displacement and loss/lack of livelihoods opportunities, which is contributing to high financial vulnerability levels, hampering people’s accessibility to food. Approximately, 2.5 million people are displaced across Northwest and Northeast Syria, with 2 million people relying on monthly food assistance in the former. In Northeast Syria, in the 17 sub-districts that are impacted by the “Peace Spring” operation, an estimated 376,775 people are thought to be food insecure. Approximately, 69 per cent of Syrians are believed to be living on less than USD 1.9 a day, more than double since 2011. Unemployment rates are believed to be at 50 per cent, leading households to struggle to meet their daily food needs. Depreciation of the Syrian pound, hyperinflation, high food prices, and decreased livelihoods opportunities have left many Syrian households unable to cover their needs with approximately 70 per cent of Syrians spending more than 65 per cent of their expenditure on food, suggesting high economic vulnerability. Pockets of acute food insecurity are prevalent in the hard-to-reach areas of Idleb and Raqqa. Highly vulnerable categories include: female-headed households, the elderly, and PwDs. Large-scale displacement and urbanisation mean some livelihood skills are not in demand locally. Unattended field, looted equipment, destroyed infrastructure, power cuts and a lack of fuel have prevented farmers from planting and harvesting. Explosive remnants of war reduce access to land.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

Protection Risk (1): Risk of corruption at the level of beneficiary selection, which might include exclusion of specific people or communities. Likelihood: High Impact: HighMitigation Measures: 1) Complaints/Feedback mechanisms are put in place and are accessible to beneficiaries. 2) Various outreach modalities are utilised and own criteria is used to select beneficiaries. 3) Post-delivery monitoring includes questions about exchange of aid for services or money, 4) NRC compliance standards and procedures, 5) staff awareness on anti-corruption policies and reporting lines. Protection Risk (2): Centralised, large-scale distributions might create security risks for beneficiaries and staff/partners. Also, they may not be physically accessible for people affected by impaired mobility or those unable to leave the house. Likelihood: High Impact: HighMitigation Measures: 1) Prioritisation of household-level distributions where feasible and appropriate. 2) Where centralised distribution sites are required, selection will include risk assessment and alternative distribution methods for those unable to access centralised distribution sites. 3) Complaints and Feedback mechanisms are put in place for beneficiaries to voice their recommendations, suggestions or concernsProtection Risk (3): Diversion of aid/assetsLikelihood: High Impact: HighMitigation Measures: 1) Staff are trained on targeting and community mobilisation. 2) Meetings with community leaders/gate keepers/local councils on targeting criteria are conducted. 3) Complaints and Feedback Mechanisms are put in place. Protection Risk (4): Cash not being shared within receiving households, creating tensions among household members. Likelihood: High Impact: HighMitigation Measures: 1) Post-distribution monitoring includes questions on tensions. 2) Complaints and Feedback Mechanisms are put in place.

7 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

-Threats to health, safety and security of staff, partners and beneficiaries linked to conflict or criminal activities: NRC has dedicated Health, Safety and Security staff, both at coordination and filed level, to ensure a close context follow-up and the correct implementation of NRC Security policy and standards. NRC produce regular risk assessments and updated security and contingency plans for all operational areas. All NRC staff receive proper security and safety training.-Interference from local authorities (Government or oppositions) preventing NRC to access or work in specific areas and to select independently beneficiary or staff: networking with peer organisations through different fora to advocate for unified policy positions, regular meeting with local key stakeholders to reinforce humanitarian principles, transparent recruitment policy and criteria for selection of beneficiaries, clear red lines identified while negotiating with local authorities, careful liaison work.-Non-renewal of UNSC resolution 2165 after 10 July 2020: Continue negotiations with GoS to expand access from Damascus. Keep strengthening LHPs’ capacity to ensure continued operational quality. Scale up internal procurement to guarantee access to supplies. -Neighbouring states push for large-scale return of refugees to Syria (including coerced return): Collaboration and coordination with other NRC country programmes in the region. Development of specific contingency plans. Adaptation of programmes. Advocacy efforts.-Fraud/Corruption/Bribery: Enforcing NRC’s policy of zero tolerance of corruption through the continuous strengthening of procurement and operating procedures, promote the “whistle-blowing” policy, corruption risk mapping, ensure partners implement anti-corruption measures.-Changes in territorial control: good understanding of context, contextual analysis, Security Plans and procedures, contingency planning, community acceptance and good relationship with local authorities
