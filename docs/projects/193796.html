Project Summary

This intervention will support the most vulnerable households (HHs) across the 5 governorates of the Gaza strip whose food security was impacted by prolonged economic deterioration caused by more than 15 years of siege. It also aims at supporting farmers whose livelihoods are impacted by reduced capacity to acquire basic inputs and whose productive assets are affected by natural and man-made hazards. Through the proposed action, the productive capacities and food security of small-scale farmers will be improved through a mix of cash assistance and in-kind modalities, thus contributing to increased resilience for themselves and their wider communities. The project is informed by assessments ACTED conducted between September and October 2021, the 2022 Multi-Sectorial Needs Assessments (MSNA), extensive consultations with key stakeholders, and recommendations from the Ministry of Agriculture (MoA), Food Security Sector (FSS) and Cash Working Group (CWG) in Gaza. Specifically, ACTED and its implementing partners Arab Center for Agricultural Development (ACAD) and the Palestinian Environmental Friends (PEF) will implement a multi-pronged approach to protect livelihoods and strengthen food security through the following means:
•Provision of food assistance to the most vulnerable HHs through the distribution of e-vouchers. 350 selected HHs will receive 4 e-vouchers of 342 ILS over a period of 4 months.
•Facilitating the full and/or partial rehabilitation of land and agricultural assets using a self-rehabilitation approach through the provision of cash-for-assets directly to selected farmers. ACTED will provide 90 farmers with cash assistance to facilitate the rehabilitation of 180 donums of land (2 donums per farmer);
•Improving water storage to increase agricultural productivity through the rehabilitation and installation of PV systems for agricultural water ponds and wells, using a vendor approach. The target is 200 farmers, for the rehabilitation of 15 agricultural water ponds including PV systems, and the installation of PV system and urgent/needed maintenance works of 4 communal wells nominated by MoA. 15 groups (average 3 farmers per group) will benefit from the rehabilitation of 15 water ponds.
•Facilitating the rehabilitation of greenhouses to increase the growing season through the provision of cash-for-assets directly to 45 selected small-scale farmers.
•Facilitating the rehabilitation of 45 poultry farms and the installation of solar panels through the provision of cash-for-assets directly to 45 selected farmers;
• Providing Cash-for-work (CfW) opportunities to 210 unskilled workers who will support the implementation of the aforementioned activities;
• Strengthening existing Community-based Protection Committees (CBPCs) through the conduct of refresher trainings to ensure protection principles are mainstreamed within the target communities as well as community engagement and meaningful access to aid.
Indirectly the combined population of targeted localities across all the Gaza Strip will indirectly benefit from the land, water ponds, greenhouses, water wells rehabilitation, e-vouchers as well as the strengthening of CBPCs, through the injection of cash into the local economy, the increase of fresh products within local markets, and the enhanced protection delivered by the CBPCs. More specifically, the intervention will directly benefit 940 beneficiaries and their household – which accounts for a total of 5,264 direct beneficiaries.
The project will be supported by the mainstreaming of an Integrated Protection Approach (IPA), with specific focus on the most vulnerable groups, including women, girls, boys, PwD and the older people. Gender principles will be mainstreamed throughout the entire action and CBPCs will be trained on referral mechanisms of protection cases such as GBV with a particular focus given to women and children. Finally, Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) will be core to the proposed Action.

1 - Needs

The Gaza strip continues to be affected by the prolonged economic deterioration caused by more than 15 years of siege and sustained undermining of potential economic rehabilitation, with an unemployment rate reaching 44.1% for the second quarter of 2022. It must also be noted that according to the Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics (PCBS), unemployment in Gaza is still much higher amongst women than men, reaching 65.1% in the second quarter of 2022, compared to 65.6% in the previous quarter and 64% in the second quarter of 2021. Regarding Gaza’s younger population (ages 15-29), the unemployment rate stands at 59.1%, compared to 62.5% in the previous quarter and 61.9% in the same quarter last year. Additionally, the recent escalation in Gaza along with the global commodities prices’ volatility due to the Ukraine crisis, as well as the disruption of supply chains, and the shortage of supply compared to demand in the post-COVID era, have had a strong impact on the access to basic commodities in the Gaza Strip. Since the beginning of the Ukraine crisis, wheat flour prices in Palestine have increased by 32%, vegetable oil prices by 15%, and fuel prices by 10%. Based on the MSNA conducted by REACH and OCHA in July 2022, increased food prices and shortages have led to poor food-consumption levels among most vulnerable HHs, and negative coping strategies including buying food on credit (incurring debt) (75% of HH), borrowing money to cover food needs due to lack of food or money to buy it (54% of HH), reducing food consumption to cope with a lack of food or money to buy it (81% of HH); and reducing or ceasing payments on utilities (45.7% of HH). The widespread reported use of negative coping strategies is thus an indicator of difficulties faced by households in meeting their most basic needs, which this proposed action attempts to address. In addition, according to the results of an assessment conducted by ACTED in September 2022, surveyed HHs had borderline food consumption score (FCS) averaging 30.6; where 31% had a poor FCS averaging 15.4. This shows that a high percentage of HHs suffer from food insecurity and do not consume adequate nutritive food. 70% of surveyed households are counting on social aid or financial support from friends or family to be able to provide for their basic needs. About 94% of HHs interviewed had to buy food on credit or by borrowing cash the past month as a coping mechanism; this left 94% of HHs with at least one adult member with debt to repay. Due to the accumulated debts from providing basic needs, paying rent, and some medical expenses, 92% of household prefer receiving cash assistance over other types of assistance. These findings support observations noted in the FSS HNO 2023, which highlights that 75% of the population in the Gaza Strip suffer from moderate to severe food insecurity and that 1.3 million out of 2.1 million Palestinians in Gaza (62%) are in need of food assistance. In terms of protection, this analysis should be understood keeping in mind that the inability/difficulty of caregivers in Gaza to meet basic needs of the family, may lead to GBV, including intimate partner violence and violence against children (Tdh 2021) – which emphasizes the need to ensure the food security of most vulnerable HHs and their access to income-generating activities. Looking at the agricultural sector more specifically, according to the MoA in the Gaza Strip, the last hostilities in August 2022 hit tens of agricultural lands, greenhouses, livestock, and poultry farms, causing damages and losses estimated at $1m. For the HRP 2023, the FSS has as such highlighted the following priorities for the Gaza Strip based on its recent assessments: 1,550 Dunum of land and 970 greenhouses need to be rehabilitated, and 1,080 poultry farms, 1,750 livestock farms, 95 water wells, 300m3 of water ponds, among others. Some of these elements, like the water well rehabilitation – including the provision of renewable energy – or poultry farms rehabilitation were noted as high priority for the whole of the Gaza strip. Overall, there is an urgent need to enhance the capacities of farmers in the Gaza strip to ensure the continuity of food production activities, and that food supply remains stable and covers consumers’ nutrition needs. In addition, needs in the agricultural sector have to be understood through a gender-lens : women’s employment in the agricultural sector in Palestine is in fact mainly only through their unpaid work on family farms. Although 84% of the adult family agricultural workers are women in Palestine, they are usually deprived of any financial returns for their work, despite that work having helped to increase profits for the family (FAO, 2022). Given that women’s role in agriculture is in addition to their roles in housekeeping, caregiving and community involvement, often with negative health consequences, there is an urgent need to support women in agriculture, including opportunities to reduce their manual labour burden, increase their wages and enhance their livelihoods and the livelihoods for their families (FAO, 2021). Moreover, during the last PCBS census in 2010, there were only 6.20% of female agricultural holdings in the Gaza Strip (FAO,2021). In addition, the inequity in water distribution should be highlighted as it takes place on two levels in Palestine. Firstly, between poor water resourced small-scale family plots and large productive farms with wells, and secondly, the gender discrimination by water suppliers in terms of the time slots (FAO,2022). As such, this proposed action will attempt to support small-scale female farmers, which are often discriminated in the current system.This project will thus support the implementation of the SO3 and the FSS objectives 1 and 2.

2 - Activities

A1. BNF Selection: 940 BNF and their HH will be selected based on MoA and MoSD coordination, their lists of affected/vulnerable HH, and through calls for application, advertised through multiple platforms and information sessions, in order to introduce eligibility criteria and the overall application process. To ensure the application process accessibility for PwD, older people and women, the project team will conduct HH-level information sessions. 350 BNF will be identified for A2, 90 for A3, 200 for A4, 45 for A5, 45 for A6, and 210 for A7. All applicants will be made aware of ACTED’s CRM and PSEA leaflets will be disseminated. ACTED will conduct assessments and verifications of HH confirmed as eligible by MoA and MoSD, and a panel will score the assessed farmers to select the most vulnerable BNF. ACTED will apply the UVAT as recommended by CWG. For each activity, special focus will be dedicated to ensure inclusion of PwD, older people and women, both prioritizing their HH or targeting them as direct BNFs. For agricultural activities, ACTED’s AMEU will conduct a verification exercise to ensure fair selection. ACTED will share final lists with MoA and key actors to avoid duplication. ACAD agronomists will produce tailored BOQs and workplans, and will follow-up and monitor the agricultural rehabilitation works. Agricultural activities will use a BBB approach through self-rehabilitation with support from ACAD agronomists to increase farmers resilience, the works durability, and ensure works could be replicated if needed. A2.Food assistance to most vulnerable HH: The 350 selected HH will receive 4 e-vouchers (342 ILS over 4 months; 1,368 ILS in total, distributed in ILS). As per Gaza CWG guidance, the transfer value for e-voucher assistance is 60% of the eMEB, which equates to 342 ILS/voucher. E-vouchers will be provided to BNF through Palpay, a MTA with which ACTED has already signed a framework agreement. Relying on Palpay ensures timely and accessible transfers for BNF and allows ACTED to monitor and track e-vouchers reception. Palpay has the capacity to automate several steps of transfers to ensure timeliness and provide technical support to ACTED and vendors. Palpay has a wide vendor network in Gaza, which will ensure BNF proximity to redeeming points.A3. Cash assistance to 90 farmers, for land rehabilitation of 180 donums (2 donums/farmer): ACTED will use a self-rehabilitation approach by providing cash-for-assets (3,000 ILS) directly to selected farmers to purchase agricultural inputs needed for rehabilitation works such as small irrigation network components, fertilizers, seeds (only vegetables and not trees) etc.. ACTED will disburse cash-for-assets in 3 instalments (40%, 40%, 20%) via Palpay, following the signature of an agreement including tailored BoQs developed following the technical assessment. ACTED/PEF/ACAD will monitor workplan implementation through HH-visits, to review progress and decide whether to trigger, postpone or cancel subsequent instalments. A4.This activity will target 200 farmers, with the rehabilitation of 15 agricultural water ponds including PV systems, and needed maintenance works of 4 communal wells nominated by MoA. 15 groups of 3 farmers on average will benefit from water ponds rehabilitation. A vendor approach will be used, with an average cost of 13,600 ILS per water pond and 85,000 ILS per well. This activity will be based on strong coordination with MoA and FSS. PEF and ACAD will monitor workplan implementation through visits, to provide corrective actions if needed. A competitive tender will be launched to select contractor(s) for well rehabilitation and PV installation for water wells and ponds. A5. A self-repair approach will be used through the provision of cash-for assets (3,000 ILS based on MoA guidelines) to 45 small-scale farmers (1 donum/farmer) to allow the rehabilitation of their respective greenhouses. ACTED will lead this activity. A6.ACTED will provide 45 farmers in the Khan Younis, Rafah and Middle Area governorates with cash assistance to facilitate the rehabilitation of 45 poultry farms and installation of PV, through the distribution of cash directly to farmers to cover essential rehabilitation works. Farmers will receive 6,800 ILS to conduct the rehabilitation work and install solar panels on their farm, relying on a vendor approach. Where possible, female-headed HH and women farmers will be prioritised in the selection of BNFs. Female staff will be included to conduct the assessment in order to understand specific needs/protection concerns, and and for women involved in the project to refer to if needed throughout implementation. A7. 210 unskilled agricultural workers will support other vulnerable small-scale farmers in targeted localities and will benefit from 40 working days with a daily wage of 60 ILS. CfW will ensure other farmers not supported under other activities can benefit from enough labour force to enhance their production capacity and ultimately support food security in Gaza. CfW will enable unskilled agricultural workers to access short term employment for income generation and promote further capacity building through work experience. Given the difficulty of the work, men will be mostly selected for this, according to vulnerability criteria (affected by the war, unemployed, HH with children with disabilities, HH size>6, etc).A8. Strengthening 5 CBPC: 1 CBPC/ governorate (6 members each; 40% female) will be strengthened through refresher trainings, including PSEA. CBPC will ensure community engagement throughout the project and refer any identified protection issues or outstanding needs, acting as an exit strategy for the project. ACAD will administer trainings on food security and best agricultural practices. CBPC will be tasked with conducting awareness raising sessions to disseminate information to ensure any assets rehabilitated or installed are efficiently maintained to maximize impact.

3 - Indicators

Project implementation will be supported by a comprehensive monitoring system to gather all relevant data to steer the action, maximize its impact, measure its effects, anticipate and mitigate any challenge. Specifically, ACTED’s Appraisal, Monitoring and Evaluation Unit (AMEU) is independent from the Program Department and will be in charge of reinforcing the projects’ accountability throughout the project cycle management by regularly assessing project implementation. AMEU is an essential part of ACTED’s project management structure, providing a mechanism to crosscheck and give recommendations on the work of the project team and partners. AMEU leads assessment of baseline situations of beneficiaries through data collection by disaggregation based on age, gender and disability, monitors ongoing implementation through regular field visits and consultations with beneficiaries, and evaluates the outcome of the activities. This methodology ensures the AMEU uses an outcome monitoring approach to analyze the effect of the action on target groups. ACTED will measure the following indicators: # of farmers, herders, breeders, fishers receiving inputs and services (# of individuals not HHs), target: 1,008; # of dunums made productive or improved production capacity, target: 180;# of greenhouses rehabilitated, target: 45;# of water wells rehabilitated, target: 4;# of water ponds rehabilitated, target: 15;# of livestock and poultry farms/sheds rehabilitated, target: 45;#m3 of water storage rehabilitated or provided, target: 2,850;# of beneficiaries receiving food vouchers (E- vouchers), target: 1,960; # of individuals benefiting from Cash for Work, target: 1,176; # of HHs received adequate awareness information about PSEA, target: 940;# of HHs that had received adequate information about the complaints and response mechanism (CRM), target: 940;% of females, old people, and people with disabilities reporting that the assistance is delivered in a safe, accessible, and accountable manner, target: 80%;% of female members are participating actively in the community based committees, target: 40%;# of Community-based Protection Committees (CBPCs) reinforced throughout a refreshing training, target: 5;% of CBPCs' members who demonstrate improvement in their knowledge after attending the refreshing training, target: 80%;% of HHs reporting that the humanitarian assistance delivered is appropriate and satisfying, target: 80%;ACTED’s project management team will use the Project Management Framework (PMF) tool to plan and monitor implementation to assess progress and highlight blocking points. The action will promote the use of best practices in accountability and transparency, in line with Core Humanitarian Standard (CHS) principles, by using ACTED’s Complaints Response Mechanism (CRM). The CRM will enable beneficiaries and other stakeholders to provide feedback and suggestions directly to ACTED’s staff through a phone line, post, email, and complaint boxes in relevant locations, as well as directly to partner’s staff members. The CRM should ensure that beneficiaries can easily and without fear of repercussion communicate cases of abuse of power by financial institutions, community leaders and/or other key actors involved in the cash-transfer program and/or aspects of program and/or transfer modality that inadvertently have negative implications. ACTED’s AMEU is in charge of handling this mechanism to provide timely response or adequate action to address any concerns.Financial monitoring will be undertaken by all project partners and then consolidated by ACTED, with external audits of the action taking place on an annual basis. ACTED staff will request from ACAD monthly reporting on activities on top of contractual obligations to ensure all indicators are on track and register beneficiaries’ satisfaction and any difficulties encountered. This monthly reporting will minimise the risk of delays in the activities’ implementation, that any change in the implementation of the project is notified and finally that all indicators are on track and if needed corrective measures are taken to reach the target.

5.2 - Please briefly explain why the specific modality/ies for this response were chosen.

As noted in the 2022 MSNA findings, cash assistance is beneficiaries’ preferred method (70% of HHs), especially for women and PwD, as it provides them with more choice and flexibility, while safeguarding their dignity. In addition to being a cost-efficient method for humanitarian actors, the multiplier effect associated with injecting cash within the local economy will contribute towards restoring the Palestinian economy. Furthermore, cash assistance ensures multisectoral coverage, while keeping beneficiaries involved all along the activities, as they can choose the more adapted items to cover their multiple needs. Service provider solution is chosen for those activities, such as rehabilitation work or trainings, which require specific technical skills. In this way, ACTED will ensure the quality and effectiveness of the results and ensure a no harm approach for beneficiary, especially for the rehabilitations that require technical capacities. For this methodology, ACTED will identify experienced and professional contractors who will be selected through a transparent procurement process thereby ensuring the completion of activities on time whilst fulfilling all technical requirements.

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

The project will be underpinned by ACTED’s programmatic approach of meaningful community engagement and participation of the population, in line with the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) Five Commitments on Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP), namely (1) leadership/governance; (2) transparency; (3) feedback and complaints; (4) participation; (5) design, monitoring and evaluation. More specifically, AAP will be ensured throughout the intervention by taking account of, giving account to, and being held to account by the affected population as follows. ACTED will be held to account by the beneficiaries in line with Core Humanitarian Standard (CHS) principles, by using ACTED’s Complaints Response Mechanism (CRM). The CRM is overseen by ACTED’s AME Unit, which is independent from the delivery of activities. Beneficiaries can access ACTED’s CRM through a dedicated phone line, WhatsApp, email address and complaints boxes as well as directly to ACTED staff during field visits. Through this CRM, beneficiaries can raise concerns, make complaints and give feedback on ACTED and co- applicants’ work in a secure and confidential way, and receive a response in a timely manner. ACTED’s AMEU is in charge of handling this mechanism to provide timely response or adequate action to address any concerns. Having a well-designed and well-managed mechanism for handling beneficiary complaints will improve the quality of the action, enhance the trust and confidence of beneficiaries and identify areas of the project which need to be improved, and ensure that ACTED teams learn from the feedback provided through this process.

9.1d - Explain how you have ensured that mechanism are accessible to all population groups?

Beneficiaries can access ACTED’s CRM through a dedicated phone line, WhatsApp, email address, complaints boxes and directly through ACTED staff during field visits. This plurality of means aims to ensure CRM accessibility to all, including vulnerable groups with specific needs. Trainings on PSEA aim to ensure that all staff understand the Code of Conduct and the PSEA policy, their obligation to report through ACTED's grievance mechanism, any allegation or suspicion and the sanction measures taken for any breach of the Code of Conduct. 10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

In all programming, ACTED ensures respect and promotion of the rights of all individuals and communities involved, in accordance with the relevant bodies of law (international human rights law and international humanitarian law). To take account of affected populations, the action will consider the needs and interests of beneficiary communities, particularly in regard to designing activities with their safety and dignity in mind. As part of protection mainstreaming, ACTED analyzes protection risks; anticipates possible consequences that may arise; and ensures that issues can be reported/referrals can be made to appropriate agencies/host government structures if required. In particular, ACTED, PEF and ACAD will undertake regular visits to the communities and beneficiary HHs to ensure that protection concerns are incorporated throughout the intervention, thus enhancing safety, security and privacy for women, children and other vulnerable groups. ACTED is firmly committed to the humanitarian principles of humanity, impartiality, independence and neutrality, and strives to ensure the ‘Do-No-Harm’ approach by making sure that its activities do not inadvertently worsen tensions among different groups due to its mere presence and/or provision of assistance. To this end, all staff will be made aware of and in adherence to the Do-No-Harm principle as outlined in ACTED’S Code of Conduct and related policies including Child Protection; Anti-Fraud, Bribery & Corruption; Anti-Terrorism; and Policy Against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse. Under this framework, ACTED has developed mechanisms that can contribute to prevent sexual exploitation and abuse such as: signing of the code of conduct by all staff in order to clarify unacceptable behaviour and in particular sexual exploitation and abuse; information to the beneficiaries of existing remedies (Complaints and Response Mechanisms); implementation of training and awareness sessions in order to train and empower the teams and identify risk factors on an ongoing basis.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

In order to maximize the reach of the intervention, the project will adopt a deeply integrated protection approach (IPA) throughout all stages of the project cycle. The action fully acknowledges that different groups and individuals have specific needs and assistance will therefore be adapted accordingly. To this end, ACTED will ensure that information is provided through accessible channels, formats, languages and family and community support networks to ensure that the beneficiary community are kept fully informed of the assistance available under the project.ACTED’s AMEU will also ensure equal and impartial access to aid by overseeing the CRM, as well as by conducting various verification exercises. For example, ACTED’s AMEU will conduct a verification exercise to authenticate the fairness of the selection process and all applicants will be notified of the final decision and provided with the means to raise concerns regarding the selection process through ACTED’s CRM. Similarly, to ensure the agency of beneficiaries and further local ownership of project outcomes, the affected population will be actively consulted during the assessments to ensure BoQs and workplans are responsive to their self-identified most pressing needs. Where applicable, at least one male and one female per HH will be consulted to ensure gender-specific needs and concerns are considered, as well as those of boys, girls, PwDs, older people and other marginalized persons to ensure assistance reflects their unique vulnerabilities. In addition, CBPC members and ACTED staff will be present to ensure the integration of HHs concerns into BoQ and workplan design. In the event beneficiaries are dissatisfied with their respective BoQ and/or workplan an appeal mechanism will be offered through the CBPCs and ACTED’s CRM. If the CBPCs or ACTED/ACAD/PEF staff consider the request justified, a new BoQ and/or workplan will be produced by a different agronomist. To mitigate this, all staff involved in the assessment process will undergo extensive refresher training in protection standards prior to the assessments to ensure equal and impartial access to assistance. ACTED will put particular emphasis on informing beneficiaries on the functioning and use of the CRM in the Gaza strip, since the last MSNA conducted by REACH and OCHA in June 2022, revealed that only 21% of HHs which benefitted from humanitarian aid, reported awareness of how to access a complaint or reporting mechanism. Finally, the project will deliberately target the most vulnerable groups for inclusion into the program. For instance, women involved in farming activities in the targeted areas will be approached and highly encouraged to participate in the CBPCs to ensure that membership remains at least 30% female at a minimum. Women in fact represent a significant but underserved segment of the agricultural workforce in the target areas. Similar efforts will be made to ensure that vulnerabilities do not act as a barrier to accessing aid. For instance, CBPCs will conduct HHs visits to assist in the completion and collection of applications for the vulnerable (including older people, people with disabilities) that might be unable to reach the registration site.
