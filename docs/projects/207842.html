Project Summary

"The IRC developed the project in line with its global vision, the Strategic action plan for Bangladesh 2022-24, and JRP 2024 objectives. The prioritization of activities and targeted beneficiaries is based on the priorities expressed by the Joint Multi-sectoral Need Assessment (J-MSNA), a market assessment conducted in April 2023, client feedback, insights from IRC field teams, and gap analysis which confirms the livelihoods growth potential, and vulnerability of the populations, as well as their willingness and interest in skill and income generation. In response to increased vulnerability, IRC with its partners Shushilan and Prottayshi, will provide skills development and livelihood support to the target groups in camps and host communities. The project will be implemented in the Refugee camps 1E, 1W, 22, and 25 and the host community. The activities aim to increase future readiness for income-generating opportunities by developing market-relevant skills that open employment pathways to reduce protection risk and increase women's empowerment.
Following the guidelines of LSDS and the Refugee Skills Development Framework, the project will provide a competency-based training package to 160 Refugees consisting of 80 females and 80 males in line with a formally recognized 360 hours of intensive training curriculum including 80 percent demo, 4 hours in class, and one hour home learning per day in the selective trades. The trades will be Small Engine Mechanic, Aide to Midwifery and Nursing, and community health worker and plumbing trades and 40 trainees will be enrolled in each trade. The graduates will be facilitated and referred as skill and semi-skill volunteers to the health sector including IRC’s health service center and other sectors for creating employment opportunities. Alternatively, 160 Refugees who are more interested in acquiring non-formal technical skills will receive an intensive training package for 120 hours in the trades of Weaving, knitting, repairing household items, repairing gas stoves, Carpenters, and Tailoring, and also will receive equipment to practice at home and in applying their newly acquired skills for self-employment. The project will provide follow-up support to the refugees for developing diversified livelihood activities. The trainees of formal and non-formal training courses will receive a stipend for attendance in the training following LSDS guidelines. Upon completion of the training, participants will receive certification indicating that they have the necessary knowledge and qualifications to pursue their chosen field. IRC will capture learning during implementation and share it with into the LSDS sector for review planning and development.
IRC will provide diversified vocational skill development training to 240 host community youth and women in market-driven occupations and trades delivered by private and government training institutions following National Technical and Vocational Qualification (NTVQ) and the participants will be supported with toolkits and employment connectivity. 1600 youth and caregivers will be enrolled in soft skills training following the IRC’s signature business skill training module Learn to Earn (L2E) to support them in generating feasible business ideas and ensure women remain safe while generating income. Participants who successfully graduate from L2E by developing a business plan will receive technical training, start-up business inputs support i.e. tools, materials, and equipment, and tailored business coaching for off-farm trades. In addition, Village Savings and Loans Associations (VSLAs) will be established to secure greater financial inclusion for vulnerable women. The project will connect the graduated entrepreneurs with government and market actors to improve input and service delivery systems, access to capital, and market linkages for business profitability."

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

With 965,467 Rohingya refugees currently stateless in Cox’s Bazar, the Rohingya Refugee Crisis is a protracted crisis. With no solution in sight, crucial gaps still exist in provision of and access to GBV programming in the camps and host communities; 95% of all Rohingya households are moderately to highly vulnerable and remain entirely dependent on humanitarian assistance. Livelihoods needs within camps and host communities are blatant and exacerbate cross sectorial humanitarian needs. Joint Multi-Sector Needs Assessment (JMSNA) ,2023 by the Inter-Sector Coordination Group (ISCG) highlights, 42% host community and 18% Rohingya community have access to income-generating activities or employment. And 36% of host and 71% Refugee community households reporting barriers, 53% refugee community and 29% reported no income/livelihoods opportunity nearby. Moreover 27% Refugee need to wait long time for getting scope of volunteer engagement in the camp or cash for work. The fifth round of the Rohingya Emergency Vulnerability Assessment (REVA 5) published in June 2022, revealed that 95% of all Rohingya households are moderately to highly vulnerable and remain entirely dependent on humanitarian assistance. Forty five percent of Rohingya households have inadequate food consumption and 51% are unable to afford the minimum expenditure basket (MEB). This is further exacerbated by the recent reduction of food rations in the camps, straining already small household incomes: 29% of Rohingya refugees sell their humanitarian assistance items and 68% borrow money to buy food for the family; 55% of debts are associated with health care and 57% with food. Economic insecurity, such as food scarcity, triggered tensions, and violence at home. (Gender Analysis, IRC September 2023). In April 2023, IRC Consortium Market Analysis by BBC MA examined the livelihoods and skills development opportunities available to Rohingya and host communities to inform this project. The study revealed that Rohingya women believe they are insufficiently educated and skilled to obtain voluntary work in the camps and recommended that foundational trainings, such as life skills and financial literacy, are essential before women can start to meaningfully engage in skills building and self-employment. Rohingya men engage in daily labor in agriculture or construction and can benefit from skills trainings to start or continue engaging in work that is safe for them to practice. Host community residents face similar obstacles in their lack of access to learning, skills, and capital to start or grow their business. Value-added activities, such as packaging and processing, were also recommended as additional revenue sources. Recommendations for long-term sustainability of income-generating activities involved anchoring them in the needs of the local private sector, in close collaboration with government and communities. The influx of refugees has heavily impacted Cox’s Bazar host community. The strain on local services, systems, livelihoods, and the natural environment, combined with camp overcrowding and restrictions on Rohingya movement, have exacerbated tensions between the two groups. This is leading to a breakdown in trust and multiple protection issues (e.g. increased crime, human trafficking and child marriage), creating a need for social cohesion activities to build peaceful co-existence. Additionally, there is a need to address social barriers to increase service seeking behavior, reinforce prevention approaches, increase community engagement, and strengthen health, nutrition, food security, protection, and livelihood initiatives.

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

The IRC follows a client-centered approach in its delivery of services. IRC's livelihoods programs adopt a bundled approach model, allowing women referred from WPE or Protection from the camps or from the host communities to receive the services' information they need to allow them to make a better-informed decision about the service they are more comfortable to enroll in.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

IRC will select the clients from the protection program and integrate interventions to address protection mainstreaming. The planned camps will be covered by child protection and women protection activities. IRC will prioritize the safety and dignity through assessment and to respond the needs of the community. Child protection will address case management and positive parenting by ensuring meaningful access at the Safe Healing and Learning Shelter (SHLS) as a hub for the targeted clients. Simultaneously, the women protection program will focus on the Women and Girl Safe Space (WGSS), addressing adolescent girls (10-19 years) and women up to 60. It will work on awareness of gender-based violence and disaster risk reduction through an outreach approach. From the center, WGSS will promote women leadership in building networks for sustainability. The community based child protection mechanism will run the host and the camp to uphold the accountability of the community and ensure referral by them. The case management of girls and women is another focus of the Women Protection and Empowerment (WPE) program. Underage girls will be referred to the CP or other agencies. Another focused activity is women rise, which particularly emphasizes women leadership. LSDS will connect with the clients (beneficiaries) of Child Protection who have graduated and from the Women Protection and Empowerment (WPE) program. The IRC’s signature module Learn to Lead, Learn to Earn and EASE will roll out among the clients to ensure aware, empowering and participation on the leadership, civic engagement, conflict resolution, financial literacy, business development, and market linkage with other topics. The IRC's livelihoods program is needs-based and client-centered and as such the IRC will enroll clients who are referred from Child Protection or from WPE into the trainings of their interest and choice.

3.3 - Age & Disability

The IRC and partners will target clients aged 19-35 to enroll them in its vocational training and life skills programs, and the women and men also be engaged in the program as caregiver of the youth and adolescent. The IRC and partners will also proactively outreach to people with disabilities to ensure inclusive access to the project's services and activities. The IRC estimates around 12% reach to PWDs however this depends on the interest and engagement of PWDs. The Washington Group Questionnaire will use to screen and identify the desired clients. Program specific training will be provided to the staffs and volunteers. All clients would not be attended by the IRC program, but they will be referred to other service centers through coordinating and networking with other stakeholders. A service mapping will be in place to address these issues. The project will allow caregiver in different program activities to recover challenges of PWD clients, and IRC will allocate budget to target more clients and supporting to meet the needs.

3.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

IRC and all implementing partners have robust Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) Frameworks in place. The project's AAP approach aligns with the IASC’s commitments on AAP, emphasizing the integration of AAP principles and practices into all aspects of humanitarian programming. Consult with the women, men and youth participants, design feedback and complaints mechanisms, and ensure AAP-related questions are incorporated into templates. IRC's primary goal is to comprehensively understand the level of satisfaction with services, identify community needs, concerns, hopes, and preferences, and incorporate these insights into program design to enhance intervention quality. The participation of affected populations, including marginalized groups, is an essential and ongoing component of the program, involving in-depth participatory research and frequent community consultations. An effective and accessible Client Feedback and Response Mechanism (CFRM) is already established, allowing communities to provide feedback, raise complaints, improve referral mechanisms, and facilitate two-way communication between IRC/implementing partners and the communities. Prioritize strengthening the CFRM for protection from sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA). Maintain PSEA Network membership and provide PSEA training to all staff, volunteers, and contractors. The project will regularly collect community voices to support advocacy, allocate budgets for AAP activities, assess SEA risks, and ensure affected groups can register complaints and provide feedback during implementation. Information on accessing services will be widely disseminated, referral pathway will be developed and shared within PSEA networks and program teams and conduct orientation on AAP-related actions and principles, including PSEA for all.

3.5.1 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

Protection of our beneficiaries is of paramount importance to the IRC particularly as it may be perpetrated by humanitarian workers, including IRC employees, volunteers, interns, Board Members, incentive workers, partners, and all those that work on behalf of our mission. IRC is guided in its preventive efforts by the UN Secretary-General’s Bulletin on “Special Measures for Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse. All relevant staffs and volunteers will cover through PSEA training. Clients will know the reporting mechanisms at the community level and organizational level. Message on PSEA will collect from the sub sector and fix the appropriate places so that clients can have easy access to and use the different channels to provide their opinion. IRC have a strict confidentiality policy to protect the rights of the clients. PSEA resource person, Safeguarding point person, and Gender Equality champion will provide timely support to the project implementation team to ensure all relevant PSEA issues.IRC will recognize the unequal power dynamic and the resulting potential for exploitation inherent in our work with local beneficiaries. IRC maintains and upholds the present policy which explicitly prohibits all forms of exploitation and abuse by IRC staff, including but not limited to sexual exploitation and abuse as a recurrent and especially abhorrent form of beneficiary exploitation and abuse. IRC expects its staff to uphold the highest ethical standard of integrity, accountability, and transparency in the delivery of goods and services while executing the responsibilities of their position. IRC also have an ongoing activity to aware staffs, partners staffs, vendors, clients, and volunteers about to maintain the PSEA policy in practice. This policy and procedures will also be applicable with the activities, and it will together work towards mainstreaming by exchanging views and practicing among the beneficiaries, different groups and other stakeholders.

3.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

The project will integrate disaster risk management and the environmental aspect of climate change by empowering the community through implementing community-based disaster risk management approaches by building their knowledge and skills for multi-hazard preparedness and response to engage them in risk identification, mitigation, and preparedness to reduce their vulnerabilities and enhance their capacities. It will emphasize strengthening preparedness, anticipatory action, and response activities for pre- and post-disaster management, including forecast-based early action. IRC will train the youth and community groups in the communities on disaster risk management and handover knowledge to them. In addition to that, the landslide and flood-prone vulnerable household members, including women and youth, will be capacitated on context-specific disaster preparedness so that they can lead their community to build resilience through increasing awareness of disaster management, agricultural hazards and natural resources about climate change. In the host community, the climate information will be disseminated to the smallholder farmers by establishing the information-sharing mechanism and promoting climate-smart agriculture practices through technical training. Moreover, the overall local disaster risk management system will be strengthened throughout the project.
