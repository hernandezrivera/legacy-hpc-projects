Project Summary

WV's overall Goal is to improve Food Security and Nutrition Status of 34,112 Rohingya Refugees
and 5,229 vulnerable Host Communities HHs in Cox's Bazar District, Bangladesh by 2020. This will be achieved through increased consumption of diverse and quality foods that meet the nutritional requirements of vulnerable households in Refugee Camps and Host Communities. and enhancing capacity of Refugees and Host Communities to withstand future shocks.

Needs

The Influx of Rohingya Refugees in Cox's Bazar has negatively impacted Food Security and Nutrition of both Rohingya Refugees and Host Communities. Even though WFP has transitioned 318,847 refugees as of April 2019 from in-kind General Food Distributions to a Voucher system enabling access to both staple and fresh foods, dietary diversity remains an unmet need for the majority of refugee HHs, particularly women and children. Currently, NGOs (WV, Oxfam, RIC and GUK) are reaching an additional 104,936 individuals with complementary vouchers for fresh food items; WV serves 21,000 of these individuals in two camps with private funding and coordinates closely within the Food Security Sector at the National and response level. Expanding complementary voucher interventions is a priority for the Food Security Sector, as poor dietary diversity is one key reason for malnutrition among refugees (Ref: ISCG situation report April 2019, JRP 2019 and Global Report on Food Crises). Refugee women face additional hurdles in enabling food security and nutrition outcomes for themselves and their children. Cultural pressure to remain in the home means limited mobility and access to information; access to community or women’s groups are limited (only 14% of women as of August 2018 participating in Women Groups); males in the HH are traditionally prioritized for food intake; and the general stress of living in an extremely congested and high-risk environment for Gender Based Violence (GBV) all detract from child nutrition outcomes (OXFAM Gender Analysis report 2019). Due to restrictions on movement and on IGAs, most income is earned illegally via working in host community fields or unskilled labor opportunities. Due to the risks of leaving camps, such labor is done primarily by men. Fifty-nine percent of refugees said the person earning money does not share the income with their family, highlighting the lack of power-sharing in HH expenditure and the need to increase women’s access to income-generation and skills training. Female refugees also face the burden of 72 hours per week of caregiving as a main activity, requiring new means of support from neighbors and male HH members, as well as access to home-based livelihoods options (OXFAM, Rapid Care Analysis in an Onset Emergency. June 2018). The impact of Government policies relating to livelihoods and self-reliance on refugee HH food security and nutrition is expected to worsen, as refugees are increasingly turning to debt as a coping strategy. Currently, only paid volunteer work with aid organizations—in which better educated men and women are preferred—and cash for work (CFW) schemes are permitted for cash income; CFW opportunities are benefiting only 21% of HHs in any given month. While continued advocacy is needed for Rohingya to realize the right to work, training in select skills, recently approved by the Government under the banner of “self-reliance,” now enables limited but critically needed activities like micro-gardening, sewing and tailoring, and other vocational skills development. Prior to August 2018, Cox’s Bazar District was classified as Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) 3 (Reintegrated Food Security Phase Classification. Bangladesh: Acute Food Insecurity Situation for June-November 2016 and Projection for January-April 2017). The rapid refugee influx disproportionally impacted Ukhia and Teknaf, where the majority of new arrivals settled. The population of these two Upazillas nearly tripled with refugees now outnumbering Bangladeshi’s 3:1. Activities or outputs

1.1 Provide Rohingya households (34,112) in the camps with complementary vouchers to purchase a variety of nutritious, high-quality foods from selected vendor shops in Rohingya camps.1.2 Provide evidence-based support (cash for work, agricultural production, non-farm economic activities, small businesses, etc) to most vulnerable households (5,229) in host communities to meet food security and livelihoods recovery needs.1.3 Build linkages of farmers (4,183) in host communities to Rohingya camps (vendor shops) through bulk selling/collection centers.2.1 Provide invaluable life skills and food literacy to women (3,750) in Rohingya camps through community-based cooking and learning initiatives.2.2 Provide portable skills training for men and women (4,320) in Rohingya camps and host communities to help them improve their prospects and livelihoods either through self- or formal employment.3.1 Promote adoption of climate resilient agricultural technology (crops, breeds, species suitable to agro-ecology) and practices (use of organic manures, bio-pesticides, soil and water conservation, etc) by smallholder farmers (4,183) in host communities.3.2 Support appropriate initiatives (tree plantation, turfing, mulching, etc) to cover exposed soils, establish vegetation, minimize risk of soil erosion and maintain soil fertility and productivity. AAP: WV will ensure that all vendors have signed the Child and Adult Safeguarding Behavior Protocols as part of the MoU and have observed all health and safety protocols. To protect refugee beneficiaries, traders for voucher distribution will be procured and assigned for each refugee camp location to reduce distances covered by beneficiaries to the traders’ locations. Traders will be trained and tested on an efficient voucher redemption process to ensure beneficiaries do not spend too much time at the distribution point, thus increasing opportunities for SEA against women beneficiaries or cases of neglect, abuse or exploitation of children due to absence of care givers. Vendors will ensure beneficiaries with vulnerabilities such as elders, PLW and disabled people are served first. Likewise, for host community beneficiaries, vendors at the seed and tool fairs will sign on and adhere to WV’s Safeguarding Policy. Monitoring systems including post distribution surveys and feedback and complaints and response mechanisms in both camps and host communities will watch for any incidents of beneficiary intimidation by third parties, requests for favors by project staff or contractors in order to be registered or receive their entitlement, or any evidence that the beneficiary selection processes were not fair enough to allow equal access to assistance based on need. Special focus will be given to inclusion or exclusion errors in relation to marginalized groups such as people with disabilities. In addition to vendors and other third parties, WV ensures safeguards for beneficiaries by screening, training and monitoring all staff, volunteer facilitators, and contractors according to the organization’s detailed Safeguarding Policy. Given the project’s efforts to increase participation and inclusion of women in food security and nutrition-related activities outside the home, intentional sensitization of men, boys, and religious & community leaders will be done at the beginning and throughout the project. In host communities, MenCare groups will be established to enable male HH members to support female participation and improved food security and nutrition outcomes for the women, girls and boys in their HHs.
