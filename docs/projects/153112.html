Project Summary

Increased access to integrated emergency essential lifesaving healthcare services focusing on the major causes of mortality for conflict-affected and vulnerable IDP, host community and returnee households in Eastern Equatoria and Unity State . The interventions will include:
1. A community based iCCM programme that will engage 383 community health workers to not only provide treatment for malaria, pneumonia and diarrhoea, but also health promotion and education
2. 3 mobile response teams that will provide an intergrated package of health, nutrition and GBV services in locations with displaced populations with no access to static health facilities
3. Support to static Health facilities in Pairing with medical, particularly on capacity builing in line with the strategies and priorities of the health cluster (Malaria Treatment, Management of children with SAM with medical complications, Clinical Management of Rape and Mental Health Conditions.

Needs

Character limit already exists. The humanitarian situation in South Sudan remains volatile and unpredictable since the beginning of the crisis in 2013. Food insecurity, crumbling health services, hyperinflation are the hallmarks of this conflict with massive humanitarian needs unfolding. A ray of hope has been gleaned by recent peace agreement signed in Addis Ababa on 12th September 2018, which is hoped to end the devastating conflict that has ravaged the young nation since 2013. According to the September 2018 IPC analysis, 6.1 million people (59% of the total population) faced Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity at the peak of the lean season between July and August 2018. Of the food insecure population, an estimated 21% are children under 5 years, 7% are the elderly and 7% are pregnant and lactating women (PLWs). Pariang, Mayom and Abiemnon, Ikotos and Lafon were classified as Crisis (IPC Phase 3), while Rubkona was classified as Emergency (IPC Phase 4) and Torit is considered as Stressed (IPC Phase 2). A protracted conflict, with subsequent rise in internally displaced persons has had a negative impact on the food security of the populations and poor nutrition outcomes. More than 1.1 million children under the age of 5 were forecasted to be acutely malnourished in 2018 and in need of life-saving nutrition services though a concerted effort from the humanitarian actors has shown a steady improvement in the general nutrition situation. It is worth noting that the survival of these children is becoming locked in a cycle of recurring illnesses, and faltering growth, with irreversible damage to their development and cognitive abilities. South Sudan has one of the world’s highest maternal mortality rates (789 per 100,000 live births) and only 17% are fully immunized. Infectious There is a high burden of infectious diseases such as malaria, acute respiratory infections, compounded by the presence of Ebola in neighbouring DRC of which WHO recently elevated the risk level for South Sudan from “high” to “very high”. Continued insecurity leading to destruction of health facilities and attack of health workers in Unity and Eastern Equitoria,, coupled with frequent disease outbreaks have exposed the fragile health system existent not just in these two locations but also in other parts of South Sudan. Macharia et al (2017) estimated that only 27.5% of the South Sudan population live within 5km of a health facility. Shortages of drugs and other medical supplies, coupled with inadequacy skilled health workers continues to significantly reduce the capacity of the Ministry of Health to provide high quality health services, including the prevention of disease outbreaks. In Ruweng State , challenges abound of retention of staff, with access to health facilities, especially in the rainy season, reducing significantly, and therefore morbidity increasing. At the Yida Refugee Settlement, services have significantly been scaled down by UNHCR due to the downgrading of the refugee camp into a settlement. Still a population of close to 50,000 refugees and host community members are in dire need of health services in Yida. The HPF partners in Mayom and Abiemnom are significantly strained due to shoe-string budgets and thus unable to provide optimum coverage of services. The situation is similar in Eastern Equatoria (Torit, Ikotos and Lopa Lafon) of which still exist significant gaps in service. CARE has been responding to both chronic and acute health needs of the vulnerable communities in in Eastern Equatoria (Torit, Ikotos and Lopa Lafon), Unity (Pariang, Mayom and Abyemnom) and Jonglei (Pibor) through an integrated strategy, where health and nutrition are implemented in synergy to yield high impact on the affected communities.

Activities

CARE will support 1 Hospital, (Pariang Hospital), 4 PHCC and 3 PHCU in Pariang County, Ruweng State. The hospital will act as the main referral health facility, not just in Ruweng, but also for the neighbouring states. The Hospital provides a whole range of primary and secondary health services including Comprehensive Emergency Obstetric and Neonatal Care 24 hours a day, in addition to surgical care. the hospital has an inpatient department composed of a stabilisation center, paediatric ward, and adult wards. In addition, the hospital provides CMR and mental health services. the other 6 Health facilities refer patients to the hospital, in addition to 3 PHCCs within the refugee camps. It is worth noting that the Hospital, together with the 4 PHCCs will provide key services in the following areas:- Clinical Management of Rape (CMR)- Management of Malnourishes Children with medical complications (Stabilisation Centre)- Management of Patients with Mental Health In 2019, CARE will also deploy 4 integrated mobile teams (1 in Unity and 1 in Jonglei and 2 in Eastern Equatoria to deliver health services. CARE will work closely with the Health Cluster . The mobile teams will be deployed to the areas with the highest need or new emergencies. In addition to the provision of emergency services to pre-identified locations, CARE will provide support in new emergencies within Unity and Jonglei as and when needed. In order to respond to the new emergencies, CARE will put aside a certain amount of money as Crisis Modifier to be able to respond quickly to the emergencies. The teams will be deployed to the various sites within the states depending on need and pre-determined gaps, and in consultation with the Health Cluster at the National and State levels. Each team will be stationed in each site for between 1-2 weeks, before moving to the next site. CARE will support a total of 283 Community Health Workers to carry out community based health and activities including Integrated Community Case Management (iCCM). The CHWs will be involved in the treatment of three diseases (malaria, pneumonia and diarrhea) and screening and referral of malnourished children with medical complications to stabilization centers where they can be treated. The CHWs will also be trained to provide health and hygiene education and promotion in the community. Topics such as screening for health needs including key diseases and how to utilize referral pathways will form part of the training package. The Community Health Workers will work closely with the Community to provide health messages in the community (hand washing, food preparation, use of latrines and sleeping under treated nets), identify vulnerable groups in need of health care and assist them to access mobile health services. CARE will deploy female maternal and child health workers (MCHW) as members of the mobile health teams who will be instrumental in reaching out to pregnant mothers and young girls in the community with reproductive health messages (exclusive breastfeeding, menstrual hygiene, child spacing). They will also be involved in providing antenatal care, conducting clean delivery in the community, and making home visits for post-natal mothers. The MCHW will identify vulnerable mothers and young girls who need more comprehensive care and refer them to either the mobile clinics or static health facilities, where these are available.

Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse

Yes

Briefly describe how

CARE International in South Sudan has zero tolerance on sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA), therefore prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA) policy framework guides CARE employees tasked with direct engagement with beneficiaries. CARE South Sudan has put in place a mechanism of reporting any act or suspicion of acts committed by CARE staff or its affiliate deemed as a sexual exploitation and abuse for prompt action by the Country office’ response team..

Nature of the consultations

Yes

Briefly describe the nature of the consultations

CARE engages the crisis affected communities directly in getting their views for planning, implementation and monitoring of projects. This is done through project committees formed to ensure that the beneficiaries have a way of communicating their view as community leader may sometimes be biased. In order to design this project, CARE consulted community leaders, women’s groups, and youth groups through existing projects.

Feedback /complaint mechanisms?

Yes

Briefly describe the mechanisms

CARE, builds feedback, complaints and response mechanisms (FCRM) into all its interventions across all regions, sectors and projects. This is mainly to promptly get positive and negative feedbacks from the beneficiaries that CARE seeks to serve. Therefore, CARE instructs all the staff to respond or refer all complaints to the relevant focal persons promptly so that the issues are addressed timely. A number of context based systems have been in place, including suggestion boxes, and management committees to accommodate those who are illiterate. While the positive feedbacks are documented and reported for possible replication in future, complaints are categorized, acted upon and feedback on CARE would have addressed them provided to the community. This system will be implemented in this project.

Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

CARE considers the following factors that might bar access;• Inclusion and exclusion errors leading to tensions in the communities• Shift of power dynamics within the households• Increasing exposure to exploitation by employers, clients, suppliers• Inciting backlash from family or community members when women start earning money• Increasing vulnerabilities of theft and violence due to greater assets and wealth• Reinforcing inequalities by continuing to limit choices to more vulnerable groups• Causing false expectations and dependence on short term surge of cash or assets• Increasing costs and drawing on resources, such as providing small livestock without fodder or veterinary care• Exacerbating tensions between host community and affected community

Addresses inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

CARE tackles inequality and incorporates protection threats throughout all stages of its response cycle. As a SAG member of the Protection - GBV sub - cluster nationally, CARE is ideally placed upon alert, to raise, consult and resolve any issues with the protection cluster to ensure an intervention will not cause harm. Regular protection assessments are conducted for all of CARE’s projects in close coordination with other partners and stakeholders and enforced by protection staff involvement during project implementation where possible. In the first stage of assessment, partners review the context for conflict sensitive and HLP issues, and referral pathways, in order to improve understanding of the context dynamics and therefore decrease harm on the community. CARE’s field assessments begin with safety audits to understand risks of every gender. Both the assessment and response stages of CARE projects incorporate a high level of community engagement, capacity building, and feedback mechanisms in order to ensure all people, especially marginalized groups, have an active role in the way CARE projects are carried out. With evidence about particular vulnerabilities, CARE takes special measures to support vulnerable beneficiaries. Where possible, integrated thematic outreach and protection messaging is carried out.

Project activities address inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

As the proposed project will be implemented in an emergency context, CARE mainstreams protection principles into project activities. The Do no Harm principle will be maintained to ensure that project activities do not expose communities and the most vulnerable groups such as women and girls to harm and they are provided with protection when receiving vegetable and seeds kits. Information provided by survivors or beneficiaries are safeguarded and kept confidential so as not to be used to expose them to harm. Our actions addresses both basic needs including dignity kits that improve protection of those at risk of violations. We also pay special attention to people living with disabilities, children, child-headed and female-headed households and communities to further harm.

Does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

In all its operational areas, CARE is guided by the principles of fairness, high quality of work, respect, focus on community engagement and interaction, and integrity that have acknowledged by the communities and beneficiaries it serves. Strong community acceptance and buy in is secured during initial project design consultations, project inception during and after project implementation. Aside from the sporadic insecurity incidents of which community members and beneficiaries / CARE vice versa regularly warn of the threats and risks access is generally not a problem for CARE and beneficiaries since they are regularly consulted on appropriate site selection, developing beneficiary targeting criteria & selection and good open lines of communication have been established which ensure that only vulnerable beneficiaries are targeted and benefit. From previous experience, in new communities CARE is more than often invited to work with them, as opposed to the need to negotiate access into them. Regular joint supervision field visits are purposed and conducted in consultation with stakeholders and partners to monitor access and quality of services being proffered to beneficiaries.

Does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

CARE maintains a security team at country office whose responsibility is to conduct security risk assessments before project roll out, during implementation and after in all its operational sites. CARE works to obtain required security clearance before traveling to the proposed project locations in light of the context. In case of any impending or actualized insecurity incidents CARE endeavours to keep our beneficiaries, cluster members and other stakeholders duly informed of any associated changes. Before, during and after project implementation, CARE undertakes significant safety and security briefing for beneficiaries on the relevant context specific safety and security risks that face them. From experience, it is noted that communities and beneficiaries are very aware of the different risks and appropriate safety and security measure they can take. In some cases, as a result of the close collaboration, they do have prior information of such incidences and inform our staffs who then implement precautionary/ remedial measures. Continuous conducted safety and security trainings have ensured that staff have a growing security awareness.

Does the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

Yes

How the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

CARE bases its humanitarian response on impartial assessment of needs, vulnerabilities and capacities to identify the most appropriate response. These assessments provide CARE with an understanding of the humanitarian situation and analysis of threats to life, dignity, health and livelihoods, as well as people’s ability to help themselves. And further help project teams determine, in consultation with the relevant authorities, organisations and the affected population, what CARE's response should be and whom it should target. Considerable emphasis is placed on beneficiary targeting criteria and selection based on disaggregated data by sex and age to ensure that women, girls, boys and men are targeted appropriately. Some groups will be at greater risk because of their age, gender, disability, social status, ethnicity or religion. CARE through these assessments seeks to understand these distinctions and tailor its response accordingly. Our action endeavours to reflect impartial assessment, and the right of those affected by an emergency to the minimum conditions they need to live in dignity. It aims to reinforce local capacities for self-help by vulnerable and at risk beneficiaries, communities and the response of partners and authorities. Ultimately, CARE endeavours by building resilience to minimize communities’ future risk of disaster.
