Project Summary

The overall goal of the intervention is to save and protect lives, alleviate suffering and to initiate recovery through building resilience to and preventing future crises by breaking the cycle between crises and vulnerability. DRC food security and livelihood (FSL) activities are heavily inter-linked with Protection and sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV) response. FSL activities have been designed to ameliorate negative impact on the environment, and to foster local-level, sustainable solutions that build resilience, food security and livelihood development. DRC will encourage and train people on the use of sustainable and climate-relevant agricultural techniques, will encourage resilience through income generation activities (IGA) trainings and will inspire target communities to create businesses to build up local markets. All projects have been linked with information campaigns and trainings, and key topics across all areas will include environmental, protection and gender mainstreaming.

Needs

As the conflict in South Sudan entered its fifth year in 2018, the country continues to experience unprecedented displacement and extremely high need among affected households. According to the Humanitarian Needs Overview for 2018, over 4 million people have been displaced, including 1.9 internally displaced persons (IDPs), and a staggering 7 million are in need of immediate assistance. Conflict-affected and displaced households are facing high food consumption gaps, asset depletion and acute malnutrition rates, and of the 7 million in need of assistance, 5.7 million people are in need of emergency food security and livelihoods support. While localized famine ceased in 2017, IPC analysis for 2018 showed record numbers of people in Crisis, Emergency and Catastrophe conditions (IPC Phase 3, 4, and 5), and the number of severely food insecure people has steadily increased. According to findings from the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification for September 2018 – March 2019, an anticipated earlier than normal start of the lean season in early 2019 will result in an estimated 5.2 million (49% of the total population) people in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity, with 36,000 in Catastrophe (IPC Phase 5). Areas of greatest concern include counties where harvests are likely to be poor, access to humanitarian assistance may be limited for sections of the population, possible returns from within and outside of the country may place additional pressure on already scarce resources, and insecurity, lack of freedom of movement and extremely depletion of livelihoods is ongoing, including in counties across Unity State. Food production in and access to areas across Unity State have steadily worsened since the outbreak of civil war in December 2013. As a result of the conflict, communities are facing protracted and secondary displacement, loss of assets, ongoing insecurity and inability to access farmlands, as well as general poverty, resulting in chronic food insecurity, the inability of many households to meet their minimum food requirements, and an increased reliance on negative coping mechanisms. Households in displacement are largely dependent on food assistance from WFP and partners through general food distributions, which are able to reach accessible populations in Bentiu PoC, Bentiu town and Rubkona, as well as surrounding areas of Kuach and Dingding. That said, ongoing clashes and constricted humanitarian access to areas of Unity State has reduced the communities reached with assistance and increased need. Between April and June 2018, SPLA forces and affiliated armed groups attacked SPLA-IO1 controlled areas in Rubkona, Koch, Mayendit and Leer counties. Recent clashes caused the displacement of approximately 30,000 civilians, mostly in Mayendit and Leer counties. Following the signing of the Peace Agreement between the fractured warring parties, while there were no official reports regarding clashes between SPLA and IO-1 forces in the area, several small clashes took place around Bentiu, Buaw and Leer areas. Due to the absence of a clear understanding of the emerging power sharing dynamics and ways forward for the implementation of the Peace Agreement, the likelihood of armed clashes between the warring parties remains high. As a result, access to areas with food insecurity remains essential and the need for food assistance across the state remains critical.

Activities

The following key FSL activities will be implemented in close integration with DRC Protection and SGBV teams:1. Provision of food assistance through FFA: FFA households will receive food/cash assistance for 15 days a month. Monthly food rations will be distributed after completion of planned works, and DRC will work closely with community and farm leaders to monitor and validate the works completed before the food rations are distributed.2. Women’s income generating activities (IGA), literacy and numeracy trainings for basic business skills: DRC will form women’s livelihood cooperatives and provide IGA workshops with the aim of skills diversification for improved livelihood outcomes. Cooperative members will be trained through a 6-month programme on tailoring, and be complemented with numeracy and literacy skills trainings.3. Establishment of Community Sustainability Centres (CDCs) and Vocational Training Centres (VTCs): The CDC/VTCs will function as joint women’s community livelihood training centres and business hubs that will provide static bases for IGA and FSL activities. On-site FSL activities will include the creation of demonstration farms/plots where climate-relevant techniques, such as energy-efficient stove construction and use, can be tested and showcased, the hosting of workshops on themes for agriculture extension support and on the use of sustainable materials through upcycling for alternative IGA. 4. Establishment and support to communal grinding mills: The CDC/VTCs will host community grinding mills to facilitate income diversification and household economic resiliency.5. Distribution of agricultural inputs: Farming groups will be established and assisted with setting up crop and vegetable gardens with the objective of producing nutritious crops which will improve the food consumption and household dietary diversity scores of the vulnerable target populations. Crops kits will be provided to each HH. 6. Agricultural trainings: Farmer training and extension support services including basic record keeping for business will be completed to improve agriculture productivity among identified beneficiaries. The project will improve farming skills of the project beneficiaries through participatory approaches including the use of demonstration plots and basic Farmer Field School Practices such as IPM and basic record keeping for farm management.7. Provision of start-up SME grants and establishment of community businesses: Following completion of IGA, literacy and numeracy trainings, women’s group members will be linked with SME support for in-kind and cash start-up grants to enable the establishment of community businesses.8. Market assessments: Support to farmers’ groups will include a market systems approach; based on the completion of local market assessments, DRC will link producers to markets thereby improving income levels of the target population.9. Awareness raising campaigns on FSL and environmental themes: DRC will roll out integrated SGBV/FSL information campaigns disseminating key messaging on SGBV and environmental themes. Messaging will be done house-to-house and through community-based information sessions, and will be organized to ensure messaging is consistent, effective and community-led.

Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse

Yes

Briefly describe how

DRC has put in place global organisational policies and procedures to safeguard against harmful behaviour; DRC is in full agreement with the UN zero tolerance policy relating to sexual exploitation and abuse. Violations constitute acts of serious misconduct and are grounds for disciplinary measures up to and including dismissal, as described in sections 3.7 and 3.9 of DRC’s Code of Conduct (CoC). DRC implements awareness raising in project sites on PSEA, provides training to staff on PSEA and CoC, and has complaints and feedback mechanisms in place in all operational areas. Moreover, DRC also commits to organisational diversity and gender equity through adherence to the DRC Age, Gender and Diversity (AGD) Vision and Standards policy.

Nature of the consultations

Yes

Briefly describe the nature of the consultations

Yes, DRC has established Project Monitoring Committees (PMC). DRC has developed very close relationships with the farmers and has supported the establishment of farmers groups leaderships panels comprised of farm leaders, secretaries and positions of the choice of the specific farm group, which provide DRC feedback on specific activities. Consultations with these groups are regular and ongoing. Furthermore, DRC coordinates and consults relevant clusters, OCHA, Internews, IOM, WFP and DRC CPC, Protection and SGBV departments for wider coordination.

Feedback /complaint mechanisms?

Yes

Briefly describe the mechanisms

In all project sites, DRC operates complaints desks to enable residents to flag concerns or complaints in person or anonymously, improving participation and accountability for all affected populations. DRC also has a feedback hotline number that beneficiaries can call to register complaints or requests.

Community Engagement?

Yes

Protection Risk Analysis?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Yes, the project is based on protection risk analyses, which continuously inform DRC’s interventions in each location. Prior to providing assistance, DRC FSL team works closely with the DRC protection department for identification and verification of vulnerable people within the community, particularly when assisting them with cash transfers and in-kind distributions. DRC focuses on providing support to vulnerable individuals such as single female-headed households, amongst others.

Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Yes the project identifies and addresses barriers to access on a regular basis, through FGDs, observation, KIIs, assessments and advocacy with relevant agencies. Different population groups may face restricted access to specific services due to their vulnerabilities, DRC identifies these groups and individuals and works with them closely to address these barriers. A common barrier to access can be distance to distribution points, timing of distribution, or access to land. Throughout project implementation, DRC FSL teams liaises with relevant actors to break these barriers, such as through liaising with local authorities on behalf of beneficiaries to ensure access to land.

Does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes, the project measures safety of beneficiaries by ensuring safety questions are mainstreamed into FGDs and PDMs. DRC also ensure safety of beneficiaries by monitoring the protection environment, having regular discussions and meetings with the community based protection networks, talking to women, to children, to men, and to community leaders. When safety concerns are identified, DRC raises these issues with specific agencies who can respond adequately (safety providers or authorities).
