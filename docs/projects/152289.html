Needs

Voluntary returns are on-going despite the conflict, violence, insecurity, absence of functioning and effective institutions, impunity, and lack of infrastructure in Somalia. While there is increased effort to promote sustainable return and re-integration, a substantial number of returnees have limited access to basic services, precarious or no physical security and are constantly exposed to myriad protection risks. Possible and increasing military operations by security forces, inter-clan clashes or natural disasters may further worsen the protection environment of the return areas. Lack of proper legal, psychosocial and physical support also remain a challenge in most parts of the preferred return areas. Many returnees choose not to return to their areas of origin, preferring to settle in urban areas. Many of these already face pressures on basic services, further compounded by recurrent influxes of IDPs. The protection crisis is also characterized by entrenched but fluid societal divisions, aggravated by competition for resources, and compounded by chronic poverty, underdeveloped legal and policy frameworks, and hampered governance – all impacting on humanitarian assistance delivery and the right to freedom of movement of civilians and humanitarians, resulting in the risk of exclusion from assistance. Other risks include lack of access to basic services, such as education and health, exclusion from services provided by government, and harassment and attacks from police and host community. Sexual violence also remains a critical protection concern for returnees.

Activities

UNHCR supports returnees with access to basic needs and essential services through cash assistance. UNHCR provides multipurpose cash grants to returnees to re-establish their livelihoods and accelerate their re-integration. Each returnee receives a one-time re-installation grant during the return travel to Somalia (this cash grant is covered of the refugee response as it is part of the assistance provided at the way stations). In addition, each returnee household receives subsistence allowance in six installments with first installment released one month after their arrival. The subsistence allowance includes a percentage of rental subsidies. The subsistence allowance will be up to 6 months and will be based on a vulnerability assessment.This allowance is regarded multi sectorial support for non-refugee as the returnees are no longer refugees and their assistance is provided in form of an unconditional multi-purpose grant. UNHCR will continue to foster the achievement of protection outcomes through cash assistance, based on the evidence that cash inherently reduces protection risks.

How is the project linked to durable solutions/resilience?

Whilst the protection space in Somalia remains fragile, Somali refugees continue to return and require support to reintegrate. From the country of origin perspective, returns as a process is not considered as a durable solution, but sustained return and reintegration is the solution. UNHCR will work with government and other partners to create bridges to bring displacement on to the development agenda and find permanent solutions through local integration. Market surveys will be conducted to determine the sustainability and viability of livelihood opportunities. In the mid-term and longer term UNHCR aims to focus less on individual assistance and increase community based assistance. In the shorter term, individual support is required to ensure returnees can pay their expenses, until they find opportunities, in order for the return to be sustainable. UNHCR will continue to advocate for returnees to be part of any future government-led social protection initiatives and other recovery to development programmes being implemented in areas of return.

Protection risk analysis & Mitigating measures
Preventing risks of exclusion based on displacement

Voluntary returns are on-going despite the conflict, violence, insecurity, absence of functioning and effective institutions, impunity, and lack of infrastructure in Somalia. While there is increased effort to promote sustainable return and re-integration, a substantial number of returnees have limited access to basic services and are constantly exposed to protection risks. Lack of proper legal, psycho-social and physical support also remain a challenge in most parts of the favorable return areas. Many returnees choose not to return to their areas of origin, preferring to settle in urban areas. Many of these already face pressures on basic services, further compounded by recurrent influxes of IDPs. In terms of targeting UNHCR supports all the returnees and hence there are no exclusion issues. Support to returnees is required to reintegrate in the local community and therefor counters exclusion. UNHCR recognizes that returnees reintegrate in communities that have vulnerable host communities and IDPs and as such supports these communities with peace-building and community-based projects which ensures inclusion of these groups. UNHCR’s protection activities aim to counter exclusion risks as part of the Centrality of Protection. Where feasible UNHCR plans to promote inclusion of returnees in community development and other government-led processes by including some of the person in need such as IDPs and host communities with the support provided to returnees.

Preventing risks of exclusion based on gender and age

Returnees continue to face a myriad of protection risks, including lack of access to basic services, risk of exploitation and social exclusions. GBV remains a pervasive issue, with domestic violence the main form of GBV, though sexual violence and harassment occur when girls and women leave settlements to collect firewood, feed live-stock or on the way to and from school, and girls working as house maids report sexual exploitation and abuse. Fears of reprisal lack of confidentiality and a weak police and court system results in a majority of GBV cases going unreported. Young girls and single women are at the greatest risk of GBV, especially in temporary sites of newly displaced population that lack adequate WASH services or community lighting. UNHCR is reaching out to better understand protection challenges and to strengthen the community’s “protection networks” so that they can respond to protection problems and facilitate access to institutions and available services. Building trust with the local authorities, service providers and the international community will require continuous investment, both in areas of displacement and in return areas. The participation of the displaced population needs to be ensured through the establishment of committees coupled with the presence of community mobilizers, including for the provision of information on essential services, available assistance and returning process. Participation of women in community-based committees is rare and needs to be reinforced to guarantee a truly participatory approach and give voice to the whole community. In some areas progress has been made, and UNHCR will further strengthen community structures and engage with all persons of concern through age and gender sensitive approaches. Although it is not mandatory, UNHCR will promote that assistance recipients be female heads of households, or at least be involved in the decision making of how assistance is utilized at household level

Mitigating the impact of armed conflict and violence

Returnees and IDPs are affected in line with the general population by the ongoing insecurity and fragile political environment in Somalia. The insecurity results in restrictions in humanitarian access and restraints for persons of concern to move freely and in safety, further exacerbated by inter-clan conflicts. Threats targeted at beneficiary communities often force humanitarian organizations to postpone the implementation of affected projects until they receive security assurances for their staff and the beneficiary community. Due to practical considerations related to humanitarian access, concrete reintegration support and assistance can only be provided in areas of the country with relative stability and where UNHCR and partners are currently present and/or can establish operations. • UNHCR will continue to monitor closely all protection incidents occurred within the context of forced displacement in Somalia. By advocating and putting in place response systems, every protection incident should get a referral or direct assistance. Additionally, the information gathered through a thorough monitoring system feeds into the wider system to better analyze main protection incidents in the country and better tailor programming to needs. • Influenced by the HCT’s Centrality of Protection strategy, UNHCR will pilot monitoring in areas which are occupied by armed non-state actors. Especially in South West State and Jubbaland State, individuals will be identified with strong connections in areas which are currently not included in humanitarian programming and assessments. Expected is that these Independent Monitors will feed protection related information to UNHCR and the wider humanitarian community on specific protection needs among these communities.
