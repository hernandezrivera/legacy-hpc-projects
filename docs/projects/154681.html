Project Summary

QANDIL’s protection support for IDPs and returnees will cover the governorates of Ninewa and Basra in 2019. QANDIL will expand its protection interventions to wider geographical areas covering out of camps in 6 districts in Ninewa and 2 districts in Basra. QANDIL will focus on protection monitoring with the aim of reducing protection risks, referrals of identified cases to targeted service providers, legal counselling, cash assistance for highly at risk cases, assistance in reissuance of civil documentation, advocacy for harmonized returns to places of origin, and community cohesion and peacebuilding among communities through communication with communities (CwC) activities interventions, and supporting active communication with persons of concern including feedback and complaints mechanisms to further adhere to the targeted individuals’ needs and priorities.

Needs

The internationally supported military operations for outing ISIL elements from the wide geographical areas it was occupying for more than 3 years left millions of people to be displaced and infrastructure of the retaken areas to be severely damaged. Military victory did not follow a clear national dialoged paving the way for peacefully resolving the national disputes and achieving cohesion among the major components of Iraq. Hence, the political causes that were the primary reasons for the armed conflict are still on which may spark conflicts in years to come.In 2019, it is expected that IDPs and returnees will continue to face risks such as human rights violations, restrictions on freedom of movement, forced return, denial of return to areas of origin, property disputes, mass arrests, torture, forced eviction in some areas, identification cards confiscation, child abuse, gender based violence, safety and dignity of citizens including elderly, children, women and men. Although several IDPs are holding various documentations that were issued by ISIL such as personal identification cards as well as birth, marriage and death certificates, the government of Iraq does not recognize these documentations and several IDPs are left without proper legal documentations resulting in facing various protection risks as they will not be able to receive public food rations, affects their freedom of movement, children will not be able to attend schools, children will not be able to receive immunizations, becoming subject to arrest at checkpoints, and/or not being able to receive health services. Even within the relative safety of KRI, IDP families face unique protection risks; fractured community structures, separation of families, insecure shelter arrangements and the loss of income heighten the vulnerability of IDPs, and place them at increased risk of SGBV, child labor, early marriage and other protection concerns. Many IDP families are still living in make-shift shelters, including religious buildings, unfinished structures and informal settlements. As financial resources deplete, an increasing number of IDP families are at risk of eviction. Furthermore, the increasingly volatile political situation, lack of basic services, lack of livelihood opportunities, safety and security at the places of origin of IDPS, spurred secondary displacement for those who returned to their homes. For the above-mentioned reasons, ongoing protection monitoring is critical to follow the ever-changing protection trends and population movements, identify protection gaps; to link the vulnerable individuals with specialized services; and ensure that IDPs are able to access their rights. Continuation of the IDPs who are staying out of camps may spark tension with the local host communities as offering the basic services remains a challenge due to the ongoing economic crises in Iraq and reduction of national revenues that is depending on oil revenues. At the places of origins were fair percentage of IDPs have returned, tension among the communities do exist in a way that fatal incidents are reported against individuals, families and tribes that were known to be ISIL affiliates. Therefore, community cohesion and peacebuilding intervention alongside with Quick Impact Projects (OIPs) are needed to cover basic needs and strengthen community cohesion and dialog among the returning communities.Basra governorate is a sample of the southern governorates that hosts IDP families and did not attract the attention of the humanitarian aid and development agencies. IDPs at Basra are facing various protection risks, same as other places of the country and require immediate support.

Activities

Under the 2019 HRP protection program in Iraq, QANDIL will undertake the following activities:1. Protection MonitoringProtection monitoring is conducted by multifunctional teams allowing to deliver a package of services. Data collection tools will be utilized to gather specific information on the protection risks and identify persons with specific needs and link them to a broad range of services including governmental services, the services of other NGOs and humanitarian actors. Cases can be directly referred to QANDIL’s lawyers via the tablet to provide legal counseling and direct legal assistance as needed. QANDIL will target 30,000 individual IDPs and returnees across Ninewa and Basra through this activity.2. Legal AssistanceLegal counseling and assistance will form a key element of QANDIL’s protection activities. Legal outreach and awareness raising will be conducted through a number of channels including the protection monitoring teams, the Protection Assistance and Reintegration Center (PARC) helpline and awareness raising events. QANDIL will have teams of lawyers in the various geographical areas including Ninewa and Basra with Legal Team Leaders who are specialized in IDP protection, who will provide legal awareness on basic rights, legal counseling on issues of concern and direct representation where necessary; including representation in court and ongoing advocacy with local authorities. QANDIL will target 6,000 individual IDPs across Ninewa and Basra through this activity.3. Issuance of legal documentationsIssuance, replacement and renewal of missing civil status documentation for IDPs and returnees, including birth certificates, nationality certificates and civil identity cards. Ongoing advocacy will be conducted for IDPs who face difficulties with local authorities. The project will target 8,000 beneficiaries to have various legal documents.4. Protection Assistance and Reintegration Center (PARC) and Community CentersPARC and Community Centers in Ninewa and Basra will employ protection monitoring teams and lawyers, both in camps and outside of camps. Protection Team Leaders will oversee and manage the work of these teams. Legal Team Leaders will supervise the work of the lawyers. Stable teams will cover a number of camps on a daily basis. The project will target 8,000 beneficiaries. 5. Communication with communitiesQANDIL will ensure that a two-way communication with communities is established and feedback mechanisms with the community are established and functioning. QANDIL will coordinate its assistance deliveries proactively with the community, including local authorities and partners through the cluster and other regular bi-and multilateral meetings. The project will target 6,000 beneficiaries to have access to the various services of the project though its outreach mobile teams.6. Emergency Protection Cash assistance (EPCA)Through Protection Monitoring Teams, field visits and referrals from other partners, Qandil will identify IDPs with specific needs in urgent need of cash assistance to mitigate or prevent a specific protection risk. The intervention through EPCA aims at responding to specific needs of persons of concern who do not receive adequate form of assistance and protection from existing services and community mechanisms. Interventions should primarily target individuals, where the specific need stems from an individual protection risk/need for an intervention (e.g. single woman with no community support; survivor of GBV; case in need of legal assistance), while recognizing that specific needs of an individual may negatively impact the coping mechanism of the entire family. The project will target 500 beneficiaries.7. QIPsIn order to support durable solutions QANDIL is involved in the development of mechanisms promoting confidence-building and peaceful co-existence among local communities. Through QIPS, local communities at Ninewa will provide support to persons of concern through the project’s direct or indirect investment in projects aiming at assisting returning communities in a way that a positive impact could be made within a certain limited budget to the lives of persons of concern within the targeted communities. Same projects will enhance community cohesion among the returning communities. The project will implement 6 QIPs with costs of $20K-$40K, each serving an average of 2,000 individuals from IDPs, returnees and host communities. QANDIL will maintain and continuously update an electronic database, listing all beneficiaries of its assistances, as per its own and cluster guidance. Main aim of the database management will be to adequately track assistance delivery to individuals. QANDIL will make staffing resources available to permit the implementation of this project in a timely manner.

Protection Risk Analysis

1- Safety and Security: Ninawa governorate is generally considered as unstable from a security point of view. Due to the on-going conflict in the area security incidents recently increased. Moreover, the ISIS elements are repeatedly attacking different locations especially in the West side of Mosul city and the Qayiara Sub-District that is located at the West of Ninawa governorate. 2- Access: Lack of freedom of movement due to having several checkpoints on the roads who are stopping every car and checking ID cards. Camp settings are located in remote areas and IDPs cannot arrange transportation mostly due to the high cost that they cannot afford. Also, many IDPs do not have legal documents which makes it impossible for them to cross any checkpoint. Another example is SGBV victims cannot access to centers that are offering them support. Furthermore, persons with specific needs cannot move easily from one area to another due to lack of specialized transportation means for them. 3- Data protection and beneficiary privacy: Sharing personal data of returnees, IDPs or other affected individuals or households with third parties, potentially putting them at risk of violence and detainment discrimination.4- Community volunteers lack experience in community works and volunteering will be a new experience in their life.5- Cultural rejection on GBV protection issues as most of the communities the project will serve in Ninewa and Basra are conservatives.

Mitigation Measures

1- Safety and Security: QANDIL has very strict safety and security procedure putting safety of staff members and beneficiaries at first priority. QANDIL has full time security officer that is authorizing all transportations to unstable areas on daily basis and tracking movements of all mobile teams. QANDIL staff are trained to deal with difficult cases and follow clear SOPs for each type of risk at persons and properties. 2- Access: QANDIL hires local staff members from the areas of work checking their background at the beginning of their work with the organization. This is the most effective way to mitigate the risks of access as the locals know about their areas. QANDIL is coordinating access of its beneficiaries with the local authorities to make sure that they will have access of movement whenever is needed. QANDIL is also connected to the network of INSO and is momentarily updated about any access issues. 3- Data protection and beneficiary privacy: QANDIL’s code of conduct provides data safety of beneficiaries’ information. Staff members are trained on the code of conduct regularly and each staff member is required to commit to maintaining confidentiality and comply with all other articles of the standards of code of conduct that is to the international level of INGOs.4- Community volunteers will be empowered through training sessions to undertake community works and volunteer to serving their communities without receiving incentives.5- GBV issues for conservative communities: QANDIL understands the cultural differences and act carefully to respect the norms and culture of the host communities. Only women social workers, monitors and lawyers will approach women and the places for women gatherings. The chance will be taken as an opportunity to better understand the GBV issues in order to further study localizing the concept of GBV to the local communities’ needs within the context of Iraq.

How is your project accountable to affected population

QANDIL is very well connected to the affected population as it is operating in the country since 1991. It has deep knowledge about the context and is continuously communicating with the affected population, understand their needs and involve them in finding solutions. At the beginning of the project, focus group discussions will be held to further identify the gaps in services and involve the affected population to finding solutions that satisfies their needs. As mentioned earlier, most of the hired manpower will be from the affected areas who are also very well connected to their communities which makes it even easier to communicate with the community. QANDIL has a very practical feedback and complaint mechanism. Through the QANDIL Helpline, complaints are already received on a regular basis. Also, having a formal Complaints and Feedback Mechanism enhanced the accountability through a system that allows affected populations to report concerns in a safe, effective and culturally-appropriate manner. The Complaints and Feedback Mechanism and the Helpline are combined in the QANDIL Office Accountability Unit under the direct supervision of the Protection Coordinator to ensure protection remains at the core of the programs is mainstreamed throughout the process. A Complaints and Feedback Mechanism is a pivotal tool to be accountable to affected populations, it gives our beneficiaries access to safe means to voice their complaints, provide feedback and request information. A CFM improves transparency and accountability to beneficiaries and allows us to involve the people of concern in our programming by taking into account their inputs and priorities. A functional Complaint and Feedback Mechanism discourages and detects corruption, theft and abuse which result in building trust with the community. In the case of QANDIL, the Accountability Unit is a key department to enhance protection as the CFM facilitates identifying protection concerns at early stages.
