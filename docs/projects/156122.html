Project Summary

The project provides food security assistance through the distribution of food baskets to households affected by conflict in Atareb countryside which is related to Aleppo, Syria.
The food baskets will secure food security for the internally displaced households for 6 months, beneficiaries will be selected as per the vulnerability criteria set by Watan, the selection criteria include:
1- House headed by a woman, child or elderly.
2- HH with Disabilities “one of the householder members”.
3- Host communities and IDPs living under poor shelter conditions (i.e. destroyed houses or no shelter), and/or living in collective centers “vulnerable groups”.
4- HHs with more than five members.
5- HH has at least 1 Pregnant/lactating woman.
6- HH has three or more kids under 10 years.
Following are key principles for designing the project,
“Do no harm” approach - No conflict shall be created between or among groups engaged in the project activities
Relevance – The project should be relevant to the context and available market and infrastructure systems in target project locations.
Timeliness - The project should be able to meet basic Food Security of the target population.
Fairness and Equity - Families should be selected based on the vulnerability and prioritized based on the availability of resources of the project.
Inclusiveness - Involve relevant parties in planning, implementing and monitoring of the program. This includes local administration, relief committees, community leaders and other humanitarian agencies; ensuring women’s participation as well as people with special needs.
Gender: Ensure that the different needs of women, girls, boys, and men are taken into consideration and that they all benefit fairly.
Transparency - The program shall ensure transparency throughout the implementation process of the project (especially, in a beneficiary selection, vendor selection, distribution, monitoring, and payments). It should also include an adequate system in place to receive feedback and complaints from beneficiaries, non-beneficiaries, and vendors.

Needs

According to HNO 2019, an estimated 6.5 million Syrians are food insecure and an additional 2.5 million people are at risk of food insecurity. From the assessed households, an estimated 40 percent of Syrians spend more than 65 percent of their expenditure on food, 57.2 percent of food insecure households reported to have outstanding debt and 65 percent of food insecure people are adopting crisis or emergency negative coping strategies. Eight years into the crisis, the main drivers to food insecurity are prolonged displacements, loss and lack of livelihoods and reduced production capacity which in turn resulted in limited financial access to food, high prices and inflation contributing to reduced purchasing power and continuous depletion of livelihoods assets of the most vulnerable populations. Almost 40 percent of the surveyed households reported to be an IDP or returnee of whom 74.5 percent reported to be displaced for more than 12 months. Among them 478,283 displaced people living in last resort IDP sites/camps are of particular concern as they have limited access to a diversified diet or income opportunities. With an 83 - 89 percent people living below poverty lines , and with cumulative GDP loss of $226 billion between 2011 – 2016 out of which $16 billion in losses and damages attributable to agriculture sector alone, the relationship between poverty and food security is becoming inseparable. A monthly food ration with staple items costs at least 80 percent of an unskilled laborer’s monthly salary and 50-80 percent of a public service employee’s monthly salary thus demonstrating the existence of “working poor” in Syria. The worst drought in 30 years affected cereal production in 2017/2018 winter season in Syria. This has come as an additional burden on the prolonged crisis with a shortfall of 1.2 million metric tonnes of wheat to meet domestic requirements (food use) of 3.4 million metric tonnes. Unprecedented livestock asset losses estimated to about half of the pre-crisis situation are affecting livelihoods leading to irreversible coping strategies. The humanitarian needs in Syria are expected to remain high in 2019 and multiple actions are needed to ensure that all four pillars of food security related to access, availability, utilization and stability of food are adequately addressed through lifesaving, life sustaining, appropriate resilience, and early recovery interventions. Emergency response at the early onset of any sudden population movement as well as targeted food assistance and lifesaving emergency agricultural assistance to meet the nutritional and kilocalorie deficits of the most vulnerable population as per food security indicators is crucial across Syria. Agriculture sector is a major contributor to GDP with more than 25/30 percent of Syria’s GDP reliant on this sector, and it is also producing food for more than half of the population. Therefore, saving, restoring and protecting agricultural and related livelihoods and value chains will be critical to increase the self-reliance and strengthen resilience of Syrians.

Protection Risk Analysis

Although all Syrian population has been heavily affected by the current crisis, due to traditional local norms, women are exposed to additional protection related risks. All types of violence are cited as occurring in opposition areas of Syria, with domestic violence, sexual exploitation and child marriage as top three. While sexual exploitation is somehow new, child marriage and domestic violence are often normalized and ingrained in the culture. Women and girls are exposed to structural and societal risks as the lack of safe spaces and public transportation, limited freedom of movement, poverty, unemployment, displacement, stress associated to war, gender role change, lack of documents. All these poses enormous pressure, men feeling they failed in protecting and securing a good life for their families. With men perceiving women as a threat to their control due to gender roles change, domestic violence has increased in Syria. Whereas men actions are explained as a result of stress, women are often blamed for provoking the violence or allowing it to happen. Parental violence against children has also been reported as increasing. Displacement and living in camps are noted as being particularly unsafe environments for women/girls due to the lack of privacy (in tents or in latrines), overcrowding and mixing of people, poverty, financial desperation, and lawlessness.GBV Program will ensure women and girls receive help when they experience harm by providing GBV response and prevention services and by increasing the self protection capacity and mitigate safety risks. The lack of quality services and referral, lack of trust in available services and also the fear from stigmatization within communities’ lead to fewer disclosures. To address these barriers, WATAN will provide qualified and trained staff that have long experience in dealing with vulnerable women and girls and GBV survivors that will provide support and engage women and girls on sensitive and important protection issues, such as family planning, early marriage, stress management and GBV related topics. In addition, GBV case management services would ensure confidentiality principles to be in line with global standards and the GBV SOPs, ensure data protection protocols and private physical space are in place. WATAN will make sure to have the Community engagement from onset of project, from assessment phase and throughout all project cycle. It includes meetings with religious and community leaders and ensure to make trust relationships through them. Also, ensure the support of male family members by engaging them in awareness-raising activities and adaptation of GBV messaging to ensure sensitivity to the context and culture.
