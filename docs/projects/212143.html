Project Summary

In 2024, 16.7 million people in Syria are expected to require assistance, the largest number since 2011. Approximately 7.2 million Syrians remain displaced, many residing in overcrowded camps. The presence of unexploded ordnance poses significant risks to livelihoods and mobility, hindering long-term solutions for those in need. Additionally, the loss of civil documentation and property documents further compounds the challenges faced by hundreds of thousands of IDPs, exacerbating physical and psychological hardships and increasing the risk of statelessness. October’s JMMI factsheet noted a 4% increase in the SYP-USD exchange rate, leading to price spikes, especially for water, bread, eggs, and vegetables. Despite economic challenges, support for livelihoods and SMEs is limited, with insufficient mechanisms for loans or credit. Syrians are getting poorer as the economy continues its freefall. Since Feb 2023, the Syrian pound (SYP) has lost about half of its value against the US dollar, averaging, in Nov 2023, around SYP14,200/US$1 in the parallel foreign exchange market. From February to Sept 2023, inflation, as proxied by the WFP minimum food basket price index, rose by 88 per cent, driven by reduced access to goods, disrupted supply chains, and heightened logistics costs, all of which exerted significant upward pressure on prices.
The MPCT program includes 3 rounds to 3,488 HHs and 6 rounds to 1,048 HHs of cash distributions with a transfer value of $120 according to the CWG guidance, prioritizing vulnerable groups like PWDs, blast wound survivors, and GBV survivors. Beneficiaries receive financial literacy training, covering needs vs. wants, living costs, and start-up business skills. Cash distribution facilitated through Hawalas, chosen for environmental friendliness. In 2024, IRC will support 4,536 beneficiaries (3 rounds to 20,377 individuals and 6 rounds to 6,151 individuals), totaling 26,528 and including 18,835 IDPs and 3,183 PWDs. IRC employs WGQs to identify PWD. Barriers faced by PWDs and individuals with serious health issues are addressed through adaptive and inclusive measures. This includes incorporating ramps at IRC distribution sites and implementing door-to-door distributions as necessary. Criteria cover income, housing, family size, disabilities, and food security, with referrals from IRC’s Protection and Health Departments. IRC prevents duplication by coordinating with all partners and similar service providers in NES. This involves sharing monthly 4Ws with CWG, detailing planned locations and the number of targeted households. Furthermore, IRC utilizes a unique beneficiary identification platform to minimize any potential duplication.
IRC ensures marginalized groups review selection criteria, conducts FGDs, and engages authorities for clarity. Pre-project security assessments address conflict risks. Protection Risk Analysis identifies tension, mitigated through robust selection criteria and ongoing community engagement. Medium likelihood risks are addressed through collaborative plans, project plan amendments, and awareness of complaint mechanisms among clients. IRC's cash team, with IP team support, promotes "Do No Harm" principles and PSEA. Awareness is raised among stakeholders, and clients are informed about entitlements and support procedures. Stakeholders and staff are sensitized to uphold humanitarian principles. Confidential reporting mechanisms are available, and program activities are integrated with the protection sector to aid survivors and ensure referral pathways during beneficiary selection, data sharing, and follow-up. Community tension poses risks for project staff, mitigated through area-based coordination, robust selection criteria, harmonized targeting, engagement with leaders, adherence to standards, accountability, and staff PSEA training. Medium likelihood and high-impact risks are addressed through collaborative plans, coordination, and beneficiary awareness of complaint mechanisms
