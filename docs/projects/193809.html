Project Summary

Humanity & Inclusion (HI) proposes a response to contribute to supporting the ability of vulnerable Palestinians to cope with the protracted crisis (overall goal). The project aims at sustaining the ability of 300 vulnerable households in Gaza, including those living with disabilities, to restore their livelihoods and productive capacity and to meet their basic food needs (outcome). This will be achieved through a combination of two outputs, corresponding to the two main pillars of the project, provision of support for income generations activities (IGAs) start-up/business recovery, and provision of food assistance. Output 1 will enable 125 vulnerable households, including those living with disabilities, to engage in recovery and income generation activities (IGA) through the following package of measures: socio-economic assessment, participatory definition of livelihood recovery pathway or income generation pathways, participatory development of personalized support plans, business development, and management coaching, one-time conditional cash transfer for productive items, and market counseling. The value of the one-time cash transfer will be determined based on the productive assets’ gaps in the beneficiary’s business plan with an average of 2,000 USD. Post Distribution Monitoring (PDM) will verify that the cash transferred has been used for its intended purpose. Output 2 – will enable 175 food-insecure households to meet their basic needs food through external and internal referrals, provision of restricted food vouchers, and in-kind provision of seeds and basic tools, to target households with the potential to engage in home vegetable gardens for self-consumption and provision of adapted tools. The provision of food assistance will last for 6 months, based on needs. Tailored activities will be implemented to ensure that women and men with disabilities - as members of the target households – will be able to engage in the project meaningfully. The project upholds Community engagement (CE). Target households will participate in the development of their recovery/income generation pathway, will be encouraged to provide feedback and complaints through an existing Feedback and Complaints Mechanism (FCM) encompassing a variety of methods, and will be able to receive and provide information. The targeting will be according to established vulnerability criteria in line with Food Security Sector. The project targets vulnerable households, including households living with disabilities. These households will be supported with tailored activities to overcome the barriers they face. A sound Monitoring & Evaluation (M&E) system will accompany the implementation through Post Intervention Monitoring (PIM), Post Distribution Monitoring (PDM), Baselines / end-lines, and satisfaction surveys.
In upholding localization, the project will be implemented together with the Gaza Culture and Development Group (GCDG) alongside the five governorates of the Gaza Strip. HI will act as the primary implementer, playing a primary role in output 1; while GCDG will implement the provision of food assistance via an existing system. Both partners will jointly identify, select, and support the target households; at the time of accompaniment of the households, GCDG will be sensitized on disability mainstreaming.
The proposed intervention fully aligns with the Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP) 2023, specifically objective 3 and the Food Security Sector (FSS) objectives 1 - The resilience of vulnerable households are protected, and their livelihoods, food security and productive capacity restored/enhanced; and 2 - Households suffering from limited access to food and to income opportunities are able to meet their basic food need. In terms of activities, output one aligns with the FSS’ activity ‘Support to non-agricultural livelihoods, and Income Generating Activities (IGA); while output 2 falls under the activity ‘Providing direct access to food'.

1 - Needs

The context in Gaza remains a protracted protection crisis. Whereas the causes are multidimensional, the I) 15 years of blockade imposed by Israel; II) internal Palestinian political divide; III) lack of respect for international humanitarian and human rights law; IV) recurrent escalations of conflict between Israel and Palestinian armed groups are the main drivers of the crisis. Particularly, the long-standing restrictions on the movement of people and goods to and from Gaza - due to the blockade - have degraded the living conditions for thousands of individuals. Out of the 2 million residents in Gaza, 84% - 1.54 million people - are in need of humanitarian assistance [HNO, 2023]. Access through the main crossing for goods remains restricted and unpredictable, and the enduring import restrictions, as well as the lack of agreement and coordination between the Palestinian Authority (PA) and Israel, continue to trigger food insecurity and impede economic recovery.According to the Multi Sectoral Needs Assessment (MSNA) [REACH, 2022], in Gaza there is a prevalence moderate to severe food insecurity (FIES), showed by 62.9% of the interviewed households; with 75.5% of interviewees also reporting being worried about not having enough food to eat. Coping strategies are widespread; with 71.1% of the households (HHs) buying food/non-food on credit as the primary livelihood coping strategy. Among families reporting a reduced consumption strategy index (RCSI), there is a predominance in relying on less preferred/less expensive food, reported by 80.7% of the interviewees. 75% of the HHs show difficulties in meeting essential needs because they could not afford them, in addition to having difficulties in meeting health needs and utilities, respectively 65% and 54.8%. A similar pattern is assessed by the Gaza Protection Consortium (GPC), of which HI is a member. The baseline assessment 2022 reported that 88.3% of the targeted HHs are fully or mostly unable to meet their basic needs, and 97.7% among them are fully or mostly unable to cover their non-food items. Concerning livelihoods and employment, the MSNA shows that 60% of the HHs are unemployed and looking for a job. However, opportunities are scarce. The main obstacles in finding work are the increased competition/not enough jobs (65%) and the lack of livelihood/employment opportunities for women (29.9%). However, reflecting on gender, additional considerations are to be made. A gendered division of labor and traditional social norms in the occupied Palestinian territories (oPt) also contribute to the low employment rate among women [Global Protection Cluster, March 2022]. In the Palestinian society, men are considered breadwinners, whose roles lie on the public sphere, whereas women are relegated to the position of caretakers, whose roles rile in the private sphere. While there are examples of men and women who crosscut these gender norms, on the whole, this construct is very much adhered to in Palestinian society. The MSNA 2022 also suggests that in the Strip, 21% of households (788 HHs) responded that they have at least one member with a disability, and 9.3% of the HHs said they have at least one child with a disability (5-17). Persons with disabilities remain among the most likely to be left out of humanitarian assistance as they encounter multiple environmental, institutional, and attitudinal barriers [HI, 2021]. Observations from the field and consultations with humanitarian actors reveal the lack the know-how to turn guidance into practice and provide adapted responses [HI, consultations 2022]. In point of facts, the MSNA reports that households assessed to have a member with a disability were more likely to report having employed certain coping strategies. In Gaza, 48% of households with a member with a disability reported reducing the number of meals eaten in a day -compared to 35% of households with no member with a disability. There is, thus, an emerging need for tailored assistance to those vulnerable households living with disabilities.

2 - Activities

The overall project goal is to contribute to supporting the ability of vulnerable Palestinians to cope with the protracted crisis. It aims at sustaining the ability of 300 vulnerable households in Gaza, including those living with disabilities, to restore their livelihoods and productive capacity and to meet their basic food needs (project outcome). This will be achieved through a combination of two outputs, corresponding to the two main pillars of the project, provision of support for income generations activities (IGAs) start-up/business recovery and provision of food assistance through restricted food vouchers.Output 1 will enable 125 vulnerable households, including those living with disabilities, to engage in recovery and IGA activities. The project will begin with identifying vulnerable households, including agriculture value chain actors, whose food security and livelihoods have been deteriorating because of the effects of the protracted crisis and/or loss of income due to recent escalations (A 1.1). The identification will be conducted through a triangulation of sources, including recent assessment surveys, the Ministry of Social Development (MoSD) database, and the waiting lists of other actors. Most vulnerable households will be prioritized, and disability will be considered among the vulnerability criteria. Upon identification, households will be grouped into two categories: those in need of only support to IGAs and those in need of food assistance (who do not have potential to restore livelihood through IGAs); support to IGA is meant to restore livelihoods only. Each household in need of support to IGAs will be provided with the following package of measures: socio-economic assessment using the Sustainable Livelihood Framework, participatory definition of livelihood recovery pathway or income generation pathways and participatory development of personalized support plans (A 1.2). When possible, IGA that contribute to agriculture value chain will be prioritized. Each targeted households will further accompanied with business development and management coaching, one-time conditional cash transfer for productive items, and market counselling (A1.3). The value of the one-time cash transfer will be determined based on the productive assets’ gaps in the beneficiary’s business plan with an average of 2,000 USD. Post Distribution Monitoring (PDM) will verify that the cash transferred has been used for its intended purpose. Finally, the project will support adaptations of agriculture and other productive tools to ensure women and men with disabilities – among the targeted households - can meaningfully engage in IGA (A1.4). Output 1 will support 125 households. Output 2 – will enable 175 food insecure households to meet their basic food needs. Within the households of output 1, the project will identify and select highly vulnerable families in need of food assistance (A 2.1). In coordination with the Food Security Sector, these highly vulnerable households will be externally referred to other humanitarian actors with ongoing cash programming (in line with the referral activity defined by the Cash Working Group – CWG); those households who cannot be immediately taken in charge by other programs will be referred internally to the project for the provision of restricted food vouchers (A2.2). The provision of food vouchers will use PalPay as the delivery mechanism and will last for 6 months, based on needs. The amount of vouchers corresponds to 364 ILS per month / per households and represents 60% of the Minimum Expenditure Basket (MEB). Furthermore, as part of the adapted livelihood strategies supported in A1.2, the project will also provide support, including in-kind provision of seeds and basic tools, to target households with potentials to engage in home vegetables gardens for self-consumption (A2.4). This will be combined with the provision of adapted tools to enable women and men with disabilities to engage in home vegetables gardens (A2.5). Output 2 will support 175 households. The project will target vulnerable households who are food insecure and whose livelihoods have been affected by the protracted crisis and/or loss of income due to recent escalations. Vulnerability will be assessed through a mix of socio-economic indicators, which will include the main food security indicators (FCS and CSI). The project will ensure that households with persons with specific needs, such as persons with disabilities, chronic diseases and elderly people, will be prioritized. Households will be sorted into the two intervention approaches (livelihood restoration or food assistance) based on their level of food insecurity and their potential (or lack of) of restoring livelihood through IGA recovery or start up. For households with members with disabilities, the project will focus on assessing the constraints posed by disability on the household’s capacity to generate income and provide adapted measures.The project will be implemented together with GCDG alongside the five governorates of the Gaza Strip. HI will be the primary implementer, playing a primary role in output 1; while GCDG, will implement the provision of food assistance through vouchers. This will rely on an existing system. The adaptations to ensure women and men with disabilities can meaningfully engage income generation and home vegetable gardening are tailored activities to address specific barriers faced by persons with disabilities. Community engagement is embedded in the design; HHs will participate in design/implementation of activities, will be able to share feedback and complaints (see Section 3), and will receive information of the project and targeting. The project contemplates the four protection principles: do no harm – via an analysis of potential negative effects and mitigation measures, meaningful access via tailored activities (A1.4/A2.5), participation via specific activities (A1.2/A1.3), and accountability.

3 - Indicators

HI adopts a monitoring system that will ensure proper tracking of the progress and captured any challenges in the implementation in order to deliver the requested support on time. The assigned Project Manager will be in charge of the M&E system with close support from the Monitoring, Evaluation, Accountability, and Learning (MEAL) unit. This system is composed of: I) a monitoring tool (PMBox) developed at the project’s inception phase and regularly monitored during the project’s implementation period. This tool tracks the progress of different activities, achievements and indicators; it also allows for identifying the challenges and delays within the project activities and identifying potential solutions. II) A project review meeting is scheduled and conducted every four months according to HI standards. During this meeting, all the departments and the project team discuss the progress of the project’s activities and the achievement of results. Additionally, recommendations for improvements are identified and followed to ensure the quality and timely implementation of activities. III) Technical monitoring can be provided by the Regional MEAL and Technical Units based in Jordan, as required. IV) Field support visits conducted by HI project team to partners and beneficiaries, on a regular basis, to monitor and support the activities’ implementation and outcome achievement. The project monitoring will use and apply the following tools: Post Intervention Monitoring (PIM), Post Distribution Monitoring (PDM), Baselines / end-lines, and satisfaction surveys. The M&E of the project is designed in accordance with HI Planning, Monitoring and Evaluation Policy, which is derived from HI institutional policies on Child Protection (CP), Protection Against Sexual Exploitation Abuse and Harassment (PSEAH), Prevention of and Fight Against Bribery (PFAB), Code of Conduct (CoC), and Safety and Security (SaS). The M&E system complies with IASC guidelines on the inclusion of Persons with Disabilities in Humanitarian Action through the application of Common Humanitarian Standards (CHS) Commitments 4 and 5, SPHERE Standards, and Humanitarian Accountability Partnership (HAP). Concerning indicators, the project will be monitored against the following set: # of HHs supported (Output 1); # of beneficiaries receiving food vouchers (Output 2); # of highly vulnerable households/individuals in oPt have access to referrals pathways for sectoral needs, social protection, including Nexus and development programming (Output 2); % of beneficiaries (disaggregated by sex, age, and disability ( reporting that humanitarian assistance is delivered in a safe, accessible, accountable and participatory manner (Outcome). All monitoring data will be fully disaggregated by age, sex, disability status and governorate; standard PSEA indicators will be reported against; policies and practices are in place.A Feedback and Complaint Mechanism (FCM) exists and will allow different stakeholders to raise their concerns through different available modes of reception such as complaining via: a phone line, email, boxes, HI staff, and data collection exercises. Data confidentiality protocols are in place. All HI Palestine staff have received training on the HI safeguarding approach by the Safeguarding focal point. HI also has internal (via intranet, email) and external (website, email, post) complaints and whistle-blowing mechanisms with the clear investigation and disciplinary procedures. A PSEAH toolbox is available on HI’s intranet to implement the existing policy. Beneficiaries with and without disabilities are able to fill complaints if needed.

5.2 - Please briefly explain why the specific modality/ies for this response were chosen.

The project assumes a multiple response modality: 1) Service delivery, in the form of personalized technical advisory and accompaniment by project team to target beneficiaries, has been chosen for business development and management coaching and for the definition of the needed adaptations of productive processes and tools to enable meaningful engagement of persons with disabilities; the modality is chosen because it enables the highest level of participation of the final beneficiaries. 2) Cash modality, in the form of one-time conditional cash transfer, is chosen to address the gaps in productive assets in the beneficiary’s business plan (output 1) and to enable beneficiaries to purchase seeds and basic tools for vegetables gardens (output 2); this modality is chosen to ensure beneficiaries’ ownership in the implementation of her/his business plan, connection to suppliers, rapid and cost-effective execution. 3) Voucher modality is chosen for the provision of food assistance; the modality is chosen to restrict assistance to food (Output 2); 4) In-kind modality is selected for the provision of adapted tools for beneficiaries with disabilities (output 1 and 2); the modality in chosen because adapted tools are not directly available from the markets.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

In delivering services, HI applies the “do no harm” principle, taking into consideration the characteristics of each group, the risks they face, and the specific needs of women, men, boys, and girls, and persons with disabilities. This analysis was conducted in the framework on HI programming in the food security sector, in which potential negative effects were mapped alongside the intended mitigation measures. This process is embedded in the HI PME Policy and will be supported by: regular context monitoring – HI produces a monthly Sitrep in which the evolution of the context is monitored and discussed at a monthly operations review, project quarterly review and beneficiaries’ feedback

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

The project also enables equal and impartial access to assistance and services by assuming a defined targeting – detailed in the activity description. The proposed vulnerability criteria align with those agreed upon at the Food Security Sector and Cash Working Group. In addition, the project targets highly vulnerable households, some of whom living with disabilities. Persons with disabilities are considered in the HNO 2023 among the vulnerable groups most affected by the protection crisis. These households will be supported with tailored activities to accommodate the specific needs of women and men with disabilities, overcome the barrier they may face, and ensure their meaningful participation in the project (Activity 1.4 and Activity 2.5). Moreover, the project upholds the principle of participation by applying a participatory approach in the delivery of different activities. (Activities 1.2 and 1.3)
