Project Summary

This CMD’s Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP) for 2022 seeks to provide inclusive, protective and effective education services to accelerate learning recovery and make schools more equitable, efficient and resilient for 7, 339 (3, 302 girls) school-aged children, 250 (50 female) teachers, 275 (82 female) PTA members and 60 (10 female) local education officials in 6 Counties of Jonglei & Upper Nile States by building on investments made during the Pandemic and previous years of EiE programming in South Sudan.
The expected results are equalised participation and improved learning outcomes across gender and disability for 7, 339 (45% female) children and youth through provision of essential school facilities and supplies, psychosocial support, gender-sensitive delivery of curriculum, classroom management, teacher conduct and schoolyard dynamics in 25 target schools (5 ECD centers, 15 primary schools, and 5 secondary schools) in Ayod, Uror & Duk Counties of Jonglei State and Ulang, Nasir and Longechuk Counties of Upper Nile State respectively. These 7, 339 children (5-17) and 250 teachers will be provided with teaching and learning materials in the form of 10 ECD learner kits, 250 learner kits for primary and 10 learner kits for secondary schools. 250 recreational kits will be provided to enhance learning and play and psychosocial wellbeing during schooling.
For their protective and safe school environment, 30 temporary learning spaces will be established in line with Inter-INEE's Minimum Standards for Education in Emergencies and Standards in Education Cluster Strategy 2019; and 30 gender segregated latrines and 30 water points in line with INEE and sphere standards. (unit of measurement: 1:60 for boys, 1:30 for girls). CMD will distribute 95 (500-litre) plastic water tanks with taps, 95 (50-litre) hand-washing tanks with taps and on their metal stands; 285 (1-litre) liquid sanitisers, 190 cartons of toilet soap, 95 bags (20 kgs) of detergent/powder soap, 95, 000 face masks, and 375 pairs of gumboots to provide sanitation and PPE for students and teachers. 2, 000 adolescent girls will receive dignity kits and undergo MHM sessions to better enhance their self-esteem and school attendance.
The plan targets 2, 339 (935 girls & 1, 404 boys) children in Ayod; 1, 000 (400 girls & 600 boys) in Uror; 1, 000 (400 girls & 600 boys) in Duk; 1, 000(400 girls & 600 boys) in Ulang; 1, 000 (400 girls & 600 boys) in Nasir; and 1, 000 (400 girls & 600 boys) in Longechuk. 50 (10 female) teachers in Ayod; 40 (10 female) in Uror; 40 (10 female) in Duk; 40 (10 female) in Ulang; 40 (5 female) in Nasir; and 40 (5 female) in Longechuk will benefit from active EiE pedagogy that is centered on children and adolescents and their full development, and that responds to school needs and favours the equitable achievement of learning goals. These teachers will Improve the quality of gender and disability-inclusive education services and teachers' capacity to protect the wellbeing of all children and prevent the negative impact of the emergencies on learning environments.
Equally important, 75 (27 female) PTA members in Ayod; 40 (11 female) in Uror; 40 (11 female) in Duk; 40(11female) in Ulang; 40(11 female) in Nasir; and 40 (11 female) in Longechuk and 10 (1 female) education officials in Ayod, 10 (1) in Uror; 10 (1) in Duk; 10(1) in Ulang; 10(1) in Nassir; and 10 (1 female) in Longechuk will be trained in School Management, Psychosocial Support (PSS) and Disaster Risk Reduction in Education (DRR).
CMD will also conduct 12 Back to Learning (BTL) campaigns through public information campaigns intended for the entire affected population and/or awareness raising activities aimed at specific segments of the out-of-schoolchildren (OOSC) as well as current learners identified as most at-risk.

1 - Needs

According to the Education Cluster in South Sudan (2022), 836, 000 Children in Need (CiN) may now fall behind without provision of equitable access to safe and protective education services. Ayod, Uror and Duk Counties of Jonglei State and Ulang, Nasir and Longechuk Counties of Upper Nile collectively account for 39.7% (332, 535) of the CiN. In May 2021 CMD led an Inter-agency Rapid Needs Assessment Team in Ayod County consisting of CARE, PASS, UNCDR and EDA. 5 Payams comprising of 89 villages have been mostly affected by heavy floods with about 31,380 people reportedly displaced from their villages in Padek, Bajack, Guadit, Gorwai, Buot, Mhaar Weriath Nyanepal Kharmun, Jiech and others. Few schools have reopened following resumption of learning on 3rd May 2021; and there was a sharp drop of over 85% in school enrolment due to flood waters engulfing school facilities. In Duk County, CMD conducted 3 surveys in December 2021 on EGRA & EGMA, Child Marriage & Child Labour and Social & Emotional Learning (SEL), targeting 13 schools in Poktap. The survey on EGRA & EGMA has provided a definitive statement about the low levels of student achievement in basic literacy and numeracy skills and identification of which skills need reinforcement. The key finding of the survey on child marriage and child labour is that not enough has been done to counter the challenges of access to education by girls. The survey has recommended a course of action for creating alternatives for children other than child marriage and child labour anchored in the value of the child and benefits of education that can lessen the economic burden of adolescents on the family and enable young people to establish an independent life. The findings of the survey on SEL have established that there is need to develop and accept a set of standards or guidelines describing the social and emotional competencies students should know and be able to demonstrate at different grades. So as to acquire lifesaving and essential skills such as managing negative emotions, being calm and focused, following directions, and navigating relationships with peers and adults. A partner agency in Uror (RAO) carried out a rapid needs assessment in August 2021 and established that more than 96% of the population of Uror County are illiterate although 75% of the population is made up of young people (15-35 years of age). Inter-communal violence, floods and scarcity of food as reported January 2021 IPC have diminished the prospects for inclusive, protective and effective education services. According to the assessment, the total enrolment in Uror County stood at 21,921 out of which 8,980 (4,400 boys and 4,580 girls) have now dropped out. CMD took part in an IRNA for Ulang County in May 2021 that brought together Nonviolent Peace force, UNKEA, RI, FM & Malaria Consortium. There was an estimated number of 2,450 households affected (an average estimated number of 5 persons per family). Some schools across the County are not functional because they have been damaged by the flood waters. In July 2021 CMD, FH, RI, CFH, ADRA and W.H.O carried out an IRNA for Longechuk. Again persistent lack of clean drinking water, lack of teachers among returnees, lack of school supplies and teacher incentives have been consistently reported. Protection-wise, the main threats to women and girls are rape and intimate partner violence (IPV).

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Girls struggle more than boys for access to education and are also more exposed to hunger and malnutrition, more vulnerable to forced marriage, early pregnancy, violence and even human trafficking. Considering the large gender gap in education in the project locations (girls are at a serious disadvantage), all project activities will include clearly defined strategies to respond to girl children’s needs particularly in the physical (WASH and MHM) components, social (sensitisation and behaviour change communication) components; and the technical (training of schoolteachers and other functionaries) components. CMD has adequately designed project activities such as mobilization of communities through Back to Learning (BTL) campaigns and outreach visits to promote school enrolment and narrow the gender gap for 3, 302 girl children (45% of total project target of Children in Need), thereby helping break the vicious cycle of discrimination against girls. Equally important, the project will support 2, 000 adolescent girls in upper primary grades and secondary schools to access dignity kits and menstrual hygiene management sessions to increase attendance rates and improve their social status, reproductive health, and cognitive development. With regards to WASH programming, CMD will provide access to 30 gender-sensitive WASH facilities in line with South Sudan Education Cluster’s standards (WASH in school – establishment of latrines with handwashing platform: 1:60 boys; 1:30 girls; and WASH in school – Rehabilitation of 30 water points: 1 functional water point within 500 meters). This will include a robust sensitisation activity on school-related gender-based violence (SRGBV) by raising awareness among 7, 339 (3, 302 girls) students, 250 (50) teachers, 275 (82 female) PTA members and 60 (10 female) local officials about sexual & physical violence and implementing prevention strategies. There is also a concern around illiteracy as most girl children drop out before they complete grade 5 and are, therefore, not likely to have mastered basic literacy and numeracy skills. CMD’s project activities on teacher training and support will focus on mode and pace of instructional delivery of both the primary school curriculum as well as early grade intensive literacy and numeracy that will allow girl children (and boys as well) to achieve learning benchmarks. In addition to the obvious changes that girl children experience in relation to such things as school size, the number of female teachers and the range of new subjects, the move from primary to secondary school also involves a transition between two radically different cultures of schooling. On the one hand, the primary school culture emphasizes care and nurturance of learners and offers a sense of belonging to a human-sized group. On the other, the culture of the secondary school is oriented towards teaching academic subjects, it emphasises differentiation of learners according to achievement and produces experiences of fragmentation and isolation rather than cohesion and bonding. The effects of changes such as these for girls can be anxiety, confusion, lack of stability and subsequently alienation and disengagement. CMD intends to support 9 secondary schools in all its project locations that will maintain these adolescent girls’ wellbeing and learning as they transition from primary to secondary schools. CMD will work with school heads, teachers and CEDs to set up Zero-Tolerance Clubs (ZTCs) in 25 schools to support schoolchildren and young girls and boys who have already married, providing the chance to complete or continue their education, information on their human rights and GBV, and access to sexual and reproductive health. This project, thus, uniquely introduces an innovation in humanitarian protection programming by using the Zero Tolerance Clubs (ZTCs) in pre-primary, primary & secondary school settings to help mitigate school-related gender-based violence (SRGBV) among crisis

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

This HRP calls for the establishment of a robust, equitable social service system and partnership that is sustainable and accountable. It will do that by providing equitable access to quality pre-primary, primary, & secondary education to over 7, 339 children and youth and thus enhance education leadership at the lower tiers of government (State, County & Payam) by capacitating 275 PTA members and 60 education officials as school functionaries and local managers with a view to restoring service delivery as South Sudan recovers from the conflict. It also offers a multi-sectoral approach to CMD's response through training 250 teachers on basic psychosocial skills (including PFA), self-care & stress management and MHPSS-based pedagogical techniques in delivery of instruction and classroom management. This strong element of protection will enrich the provision of education services. The project will also create referral pathways to facilities and partners offering protection services such that acute cases of protection concerns, GBV clinical management and survivor support could be availed to 25 schools, 7, 339 children and 250 teachers. A plan will be devised to conduct awareness sessions on children's nutritional health and link these sessions with nutrition centres such that regular assessments can be done and the most vulnerable children can be referred for attention in an appropriate manner. The health component will create awareness about prevention of cholera, malaria, and other infectious diseases, including HIV/AIDs and adolescent girls' reproductive health. The goal is to present the school as a model for community living and engagement.

As a procedure to enhance Do No Harm principles, CMD will provided new measures for prevention and handling staff misconduct in its HR Policy with all staff signing a code of conduct to prevent abuse, exploitation, harassment of beneficiaries etc. Additionally, CMD will carry out renewed staff inductions to acquaint staff with AAP standards. The Project will mainstream effective female inclusion in all stages of project design and implementation. Particular attention will be given to tailoring activities that respect cultural norms and traditions for female beneficiaries. The Project will involve women, girls, and other at-risk groups in all aspects of programming while being duly cautious where their inclusion poses a potential security risk or increases their risk of GBV. Whenever feasible, data will be disaggregated by gender to track project progress and impact. CMD’s Staff ToRs will include gender mainstreaming and GBV mitigation responsibilities. A special focus of the project will be on the rights of the children to be protected from the worst forms of physical and sexual violence, child abuse and child labour

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

CMD will use the following Information sharing mechanisms: monthly focus group discussions and community meetings to share information; weekly or bi-monthly announcements through notice boards; quarterly (where feasible) printing and distribution of leaflets and posters; and monthly child participatory activities to share information and solicit feedback from children about the support they receive or the spaces they attend (using simple child participatory tools, such as the H-assessment, to generate useful information for quick decision-making). The Project will ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members, through: - Surveys and FGDs with specific age groups, women, men, students, youth out of school and potentially vulnerable or marginalized youth (e.g. with disabilities) will be conducted on a regular basis. Quarterly focus group discussions will be conducted with children and communities to assess child protection knowledge, attitudes and practices; including focused FGDs with girls and young women with participatory assessment. Bi-annual public meetings will be organized to gain beneficiaries’ feedback in a structured format, enabling different stakeholders to discuss and exchange views on specific topics, often adopting a face-to-face format which allows for brainstorming and testing ideas. In addition, in-depth interviews (face-to-face or virtual interviews) with individual stakeholders will be used to get a sense of stakeholders’ perspectives; and key informants/stakeholder assessments will used occasionally to explore an issue in depth and permit related issues to be introduced into the discussion. Interviews with key informants possessing particular knowledge of an issue can be especially useful. CMD will use its internal tools of checklists (pre-prepared lists of items to assess numerous activities rapidly, suitable for self-assessments, classroom observations and audit purposes); activity logs ( records kept by teachers/administrators of specific activities such as instructional practices, learner-teacher interactions, learner behavior and assessment of classroom layouts; and infrastructure audits ( assessments of the levels of education infrastructure, access and availability) to keep track of its information-sharing strategy. For complaint and feedback mechanisms, CMD will use focus group discussions/community meetings; help desks; suggestion boxes (including information on how the boxes will be handled); and child-friendly feedback mechanism (use of H-assessment, body mapping, etc.). CMD’s Complaints and Feedback Mechanism (CFM) will provide continuous information and feedback on the quality of services provided, gaps and ways forward to strengthen the response. It will also be part of monitoring exercises in schools/TLSs and lead consultation meetings at school-level. During these visits women and girls shall be given priority to air out their views in complete confidentiality. CMD will maintain and raise awareness on its Complaints and Feedback Mechanism. This mechanism will follow CMD guidelines, ensuring multiple methods for submitting complaints and receiving responses, including CFM boxes in schools and safe spaces, as well as through static help desks.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

This HRP provides for construction of 30 blocks of TLS (60 classrooms in total) and 30 two-stand pit-latrines to create safe learning spaces for 30 schools, benefiting 20,317 (9, 302girls) learners. During a crisis, such as armed conflict or natural disaster, institutions and systems for physical and social protection may be weakened or destroyed. Police, legal, health, education, and social services are often disrupted; many people flee, and those who remain may not have the capacity or the specialised support to access mental health care and psychosocial support. Families and communities are often separated, which results in a further breakdown of community support systems and protection mechanisms.CMD intends to provide a semblance of MHPSS aimed to reduce and prevent harm, strengthen resilience to recover from adversity, and improve the care conditions that enable out-of-school children (OOSC) and families to survive and thrive in school. The approach here will be focused, specialised support by trained and supervised teachers to schoolchildren and their families. 50 teachers and 5 schools will be trained on how to deliver MHPSS to prevent school dropout for OOSC. The training will essentially cover Psychological First Aid (PFA), which includes assessing needs and concerns; helping OOSC address basic needs; listening to and comforting OOSC and helping them feel calm; helping connect to information, services and social support; and protecting OOSC from further harm. Psychosocial First Aid (PFA) is a form of basic psychosocial support provided to acutely distressed children or adults soon after exposure to a stressful event. It is a humane, supportive and practical assistance offered in ways that promote the recipient’s safety, dignity and rights. PFA involves practical care and support that is not intrusive; assessment of needs and concerns; helping to address basic needs; sympathetic listening without pleasure to talk; comforting children/adults and helping them to feel calm; helping children/adults connect to information, loved ones and services; and protecting them from further harm.

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

Across all CMD’s 6 target Counties (Ayod, Uror & Duk in Jonglei and Ulang, Nasir & Longechuk in Upper Nile) for the 2022 Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP), gender gaps persist in education at girls’ expense due to the sheer number of hurdles that stand in a girl’s way. Traditional attitudes restrict women’s roles and underestimate girls’ learning potential. Physical disincentives, such as long distances, to the nearest school and lack of safe transport. Cost constraints, like buying school uniforms, paying exams fees and access to even basic technology needed for distance learning. Girls who are poor, disabled or live on the margins contend with the most. There are 6 girls to every 10 boys in South Sudan, and lowest in Greater Upper Nile. Child abduction, child recruitment into armed forces, child marriage, food insecurity, lack of gender-sensitive WASH facilities especially for girl children to manage their menstrual cycle, corporal punishment, lack of adequate and qualified female teachers, poor learning quality and entrenched negative community norms and practices have driven Greater Upper Nile (Jonglei & Upper Nile included) to the very precipice of poor school attendance and completion (Education Cluster, 2019). This HRP intends to provide safe access to education opportunities that allow 20,317 (9 vulnerable girl children and youth to achieve learning benchmarks as a result of effective and inclusive teaching-learning processes that are learner-centered and aimed at helping children to fully develop their personality, talents and capacities. This will drastically reduce their experiences of fragmentation and isolation, lack of stability and subsequently alienation and disengagement from education during crisis. In addition, the older among them will receive 2, 000 dignity kits and menstrual hygiene management training to ensure their school continuity and transition to the next level of education. Teachers require training and support to help them recognise and cope creatively with the psychosocial needs of their students and themselves. Evidence shows that, in times of crisis, many teachers as well as students may be suffering from the psychological effects of trauma, loss of or separation from family members, and other stressful circumstances. Participative training activities that relate to intrapersonal and interpersonal communication, to emotional awareness and coping, empathy, avoiding stereotyping and so on, can help with healing as well as providing the foundation for skills such as conflict resolution that are part of education for citizenship and peace. Thus, trained MHPSS teachers can be role models with regard to behavior change and promotion of help-seeking behavior for 20,317 children, including 9, 302 girls.5 days of training of teachers will be as follows: Day 1: Introduction to Psychosocial Well-Being and the Impact of Children’s Experiences in Emergencies; Day 2: Coping with Emergencies: Towards Recovery; Day 3: The Role of Teachers in Providing Psychosocial Support and Building Resilience in Children in Emergencies; Day 4: Effective Communication and an Introduction to Psychosocial Activities and Interventions; & Day 5: Psychosocial Activities and Interventions: Developing Skills and Strategies. In this manner, CMD will capacitate teachers to identify learners at risk of dropping out or those in need of specialised services and to be able to respond accordingly. There is immense empowerment in teachers who can define children’s wellbeing, encourage community participation and create safe, protective learning environments, practise constructive classroom management methods that explain why corporal punishment should not be used and that provide concrete alternatives to the use of such a punishment, know how to constructively deal with learners’ issues such as anger, fear and grief; and can conduct structured group activities such as art, cultural activities, sports, games and skills building

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

Considering the current level of fragility in South Sudan, CMD’s HRP seeks to promote resilience-focused approaches to strengthen the ability of families and communities to send 20,317 (9, 302girls) to school and keep them in 30 schools across 6 Counties in Jonglei & Upper Nile States. This is an important step towards establishing or maintaining local education networks and Payam service delivery plans which will reduce the risk to fail in the long- term and contribute to the overall national capacity development. These could be potential entry points for integration of locally embedded early recovery and resilience mechanisms into education programming in post-conflict South Sudan. In sum, it is crucial to promote quality educational activities during emergencies or early recovery, with specific mention of life skills issues and the prevention of sexual violence. Schools can and should provide a protective environment for girls and boys. The normality and routine provided by daily schooling is a stabilizing and crucial factor for children’s development. Schools are places not only for the teaching of traditional academic subjects, but also for the dissemination of life-saving and life-sustaining messages such as Covid-19 prevention, hygiene promotion, landmines, human rights, tolerance, and non-violent conflict resolution. Children who go to school are also less likely to join the military and armed groups. One key assumption made by this Project is that community attitudes can be changed within the timeframe of the plan. In particular, CMD sees that when community members, including men and boys, parents and community leaders better understand the returns possible from children’s education (including girls) and engage around this, they will support boys and girls to attend and stay in school.
