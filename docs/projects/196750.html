Project Summary

With the specific purpose of reducing the burden of leishmaniasis and other vector-borne diseases, strengthening community resilience, and building capacity within the healthcare system in Northern Syria, MENTOR proposes to implement the following activities:
MENTOR will support and equip 145 (NWS: 65, NES: 80) health facilities (HFs) in North Syria with leishmaniasis diagnostic and medical commodities. The organization also proposes to strengthen the technical capacity of healthcare workers (HCWs) by providing trainings on leishmaniasis case management and other infectious diseases as well as data collection and general disease surveillance processes by coordinating comprehensive data collection across HFs and mobile clinics (MCs) to ensure robust epidemiological data reporting. MENTOR will operate 12 MCs (6 MCs in NES and 6 MC in NWS) in several areas across Northers Syria that lack access to HFs and exhibit a high incidence of Cutaneous Leishmaniasis (CL) and/or Visceral Leishmaniasis (VL). Mentor proposes to implement Community-Based Surveillance (CBS) projects among displaced populations in NWS to strengthen surveillance networks for infectious diseases and enhance outbreak detection capabilities, reinforcing alertness and allowing rapid responses to stop disease outbreaks. For this purpose, MENTOR proposes to recruit, train and deploy 50 Community Health Workers (CHWs), which will be deployed in targeted communities, covering a population of more than 50,000 people.
Each HF that is supported by MENTOR receives leishmaniasis medications (such as glucantime and Pentostam), medical supplies and consumables, as well as diagnostic tools for both Cutaneous Leishmaniasis (CL) and Visceral Leishmaniasis (VL). MENTOR also conducts 2-day trainings that include all the required protocols and guidance for HCWs to detect and treat CL and VL based on the updated World Health Organization (WHO) guidance. MENTOR follows up with the trained HCWs to discuss complicated cases and provide on-the-job training. MENTOR’s MCs aim to increase the accessibility to leishmaniasis services, in particular that of vulnerable groups, such as persons with disabilities and those who cannot afford transportation costs to the nearest HF that provides leishmaniasis service or are out of reach of appropriate healthcare infrastructure.
MENTOR also strengthens leishmaniasis surveillance, and regularly shares the data with WHO, EWARN, the Health & WASH clusters, the Health Working Group (HWG), the DHIS system and other stakeholders to improve coordination and synergize humanitarian efforts against leishmaniasis.
During the year of 2023, MENTOR plans to support 145 HFs and 12 MCs with a total catchment population of 4.8 million people (NWS: 3,113,538; NES: 1,699,200). MENTOR will also train 390 HWCs (NWS: 225, NES: 165) and expects to conduct 610,000 leishmaniasis consultations (NWS: 410,000; NES: 200,000).

3 - Needs

People in Northern Syria remain highly vulnerable to infectious diseases. In 2023, an estimated 15.3 million people are in need of humanitarian assistance and close to 90% also live below the poverty line, resulting in the unaffordability of basic services such as healthcare. In addition, 6.7 million people are also still displaced across Syria, often living in displacement sites with a critical level of population density, which markedly increases the risk of communicable disease transmission. Leishmaniasis and other infectious diseases have taken advantage of those vulnerabilities and continue to proliferate. In 2021, MENTOR diagnosed and treated over 79,000 cases of cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) across northern Syria, 85% of all CL cases that were diagnosed in the northern parts of Syria in 2021. In 2022, up to October, MENTOR has already diagnosed and treated 51,080 cases of CL in northern Syria, and conducted 512,342 treatment consultations. MENTOR also diagnosed 69 cases of visceral leishmaniasis (VL) in 2022 (29 in Harim), and 87 cases were diagnosed in 2021, which represents an alarming increase when compared with historical data of this often-fatal disease. MENTOR bases all needs assessments on a network of MENTOR-supported HFs and MCs across NWS and NES. All locations and programs are based on the most recent incidence and population movement numbers, taking into consideration the needs laid out in the 2022 Syria HRP and the presence of partner organizations.

4 - Implementation of activities

MENTOR’s HF support is guided by CL incidence and the needs of affected communities and health partners. Employing regular coordination with health authorities and beneficiaries, HF support is started by providing a 2-day leishmaniasis training for medical staff to strengthen local capacity. HFs are supplied with medications, medical commodities and laboratory diagnosis tools on a needs basis. MENTOR ensures technical support to HFs via regular visits to monitor stock, treatment outcomes and service quality, enhancing the quality of long-term health services. Mobile clinics provide leishmaniasis diagnosis and treatment in areas that lack HFs. Each MC visits up to 10 treatment points twice weekly. The pathways of the MCs are chosen based on CL incidence, prioritizing IDP camps and people with disabilities. In addition to leishmaniasis, MCs are also able to diagnose treat other skin conditions and minor infections, referring patients to nearby HFs if treatment cannot be provided. To enhance disease awareness, self-protection capacity and healthcare seeking behaviour, MENTOR also proposes to conduct Information, Education and Communication activities within communities. Moreover, MENTOR proposes to implement Community-Based Surveillance projects in displaced populations in NWS, strengthening surveillance networks for infectious diseases, nutrition, pregnancy and mortality metrics, feeding into the EWARN and DHIS systems to provide data that can guide humanitarian programming.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

The key protection risks are that patients, in particular women and children, are being taken advantage of by health facility or mobile clinic staff, and potentially be exposed to violence or sexual exploitation. To prevent this, all staff sign the Child Protection Policy and MENTOR also provides them with guidance on ways to minimize risks to children and the standards of behaviour required when they are in contact with children. MENTOR’s PSEA policy and trainings describe standards of behaviour for our staff and representatives, specifically forbidding sexual exploitation and abuse. Communities are informed about the organization, the principles it adheres to, how it expects its staff to behave, the programmes it is implementing and what they intend to deliver as well how to report concerns and complaints through a complaint response mechanism that is widely advertised. All beneficiaries are also made aware that the diagnosis and treatment are free of charge and that both HFs and MCs are not allowed to charge for the services. MENTOR addresses any GBV-related risks and reports potential cases to partners working in GBV. All trainings for health staff have a component on how to deal with GBV cases, how to communicate with persons with diverse disabilities to and offer support. Referral pathways are regularly updated and staff are obliged to follow up with referred cases.


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

The possible risks for sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) are:1. SEA as an exchange for treatment2. SEA as an exchange for promises of hiring to work at the NGO3. SEA as an exchange for money All MENTOR staff sign a Code of Conduct, Safeguarding, Sexual Harassment and Bullying, Whistleblowing and Child Protection policy. They outline the principles under which MENTOR operates, and incorporates the Inter-Agency Standing Committee principles on Prevention of Sexual Abuse and Exploitation (PSEA). MENTOR enrols all staff on PSEA training and emphasizes refreshing the PSEA training regularly. Through the Complaint Response Mechanism, MENTOR makes available channels to facilitate feedback and complaints via a WhatsApp hotline that is advertised on IEC/BCC materials, complaint boxes, and a feedback tool employed during monitoring activities. MENTOR notifies all communities in which we work that our staff operate under a strict CoC, the standard of behaviour that should be expected from our staff, that all treatment is free of charge and the confidential reporting mechanisms available to communities and beneficiaries. MENTOR staff are trained to identify sexual violence and report these immediately through their line management to the Country Director for (CD) for further action. In case of any PSEA reporting, MENTOR applies a strict follow-up method to address and solve the issue. Recruitment is made in coordination with local health authorities.


9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Volatile security: MENTOR regularly conducts security risk assessment to avoid any risks to beneficiaries or staff. If the security situation interferes with MENTOR access, MENTOR switches to a remote management strategy. Necessary approvals for services not secured: MENTOR maintains high coordination levels with the authorities to obtain/renew the permission of implementing the activities. MENTOR signed various MoUs with local authorities. Fraud, graft and corruption: MENTOR asks all staff to sign policies and refer to manuals (Logistics Manual, HR Manual, Financial Manual, Field Operational Manual) where fraud and corruption are explained in detail. MENTOR regularly checks the financial, HR and logistic files from the field to HQ level. The IDP and host communities do not support the projects: MENTOR ensures accountability to beneficiaries through participation in design and implementation of activities, communication on project activities and objectives, transparency efforts, feedback and community response mechanisms.Procurement and supply chains interrupted: In this case MENTOR seek donations from partners such as WHO and draws on stocks from other locations and explore alternative transfer mechanisms. All supplies are always procured well in advance of project start. Lack of qualified staff: In case of a lack of qualified staff, MENTOR has the capacity to conduct training through the organisation, drawing on extensive training experience and qualified staff.
