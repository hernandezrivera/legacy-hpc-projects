Needs

As of March 2018, reports indicate that there are over 2.4 million IDPs, and 3.6 million IDP returnees across the country, with the most significant return movements beginning in the last part of 2017. Return figures do not accurately reflect the situation, however, as many people who have sought to return have been secondarily displaced or compelled to go back to IDP camps or settlements, because return has not been possible or sustainable. Displaced persons in Iraq face severe risks, including exposure to violence, human rights violations, including restrictions on access to safety and freedom of movement, forced encampment and forced/premature return, and denial of return to liberated areas. Most IDPs live in sub-standard conditions, often lacking access to basic services of a minimum standard, and facing the risk of eviction, often induced as a means to pressure them to return. Many live in unfinished buildings of informal settlements, with further 462,719 people living in camps. There is a constant need to maintain a solid camp management structure and strengthen the capacity of authorities on camp management tools, identification of protection risks and appropriate responses or interventions, especially in the context of gradual downsizing of camps, planning for consolidation, handover to government and eventual phase-out. While consolidation and closure of camps will be feasible in some parts of centre south governorates of Iraq, this will be to a smaller scale in Ninewa and the camps in Kurdistan Region of Iraq. Since the remaining camps are likely continue to host substantive numbers of IDPs, UNHCR will continue to advocate to the Camp Management government organization in KRI to have the camps connected to the government-run utilities including the electricity grid. Planning for consolidation and closure will require close collaboration and coordination with the government, local authorities and other clusters. Provision of accurate and timely information on availability of services in camps and in areas of return is required to ensure the sustainable and voluntary nature of return, where IDPs are considering return to areas of origin. Ensuring effective and continuous coordination with other clusters and local authorities on relevant issues and humanitarian responses remains pivotal so that the gaps are addressed in a cohesive manner.

Activities

In 2019, UNHCR will continue to lead the CCCM Cluster at the national level at the subnational level through sub national Cluster Coordinator. A system of coordination will also be established at the governorate level through CCCM cluster focal points. UNHCR in collaboration with other CCCM cluster members will support the Government to fulfil its responsibilities related to camp management and coordination, with the overall aim of ultimately phasing out of camp management and coordination. Accordingly, actors and service mapping led by the CCCM Cluster will continue to equip camp managers with appropriate tools to ensure effective service delivery through appropriate and efficient planning, and in line with applicable international standards. UNHCR will ensure effective camp coordination mechanisms through its activities as CCCM Cluster lead and in the camps directly managed by UNHCR and its implementing partners. Through their field and protection staff, camp managers will also be encouraged to promote broad-based community participation in all sectors, integrating as much as feasible an AGD approach. In collaboration with partners, further efforts will be devoted to improve information management products, providing timely and appropriate information (through the use of Activity Info in particular) for more effective coordination and agencies’ programmes planning. IDP camps will be supported with shelter and infrastructure repair and maintenance as needed. UNHCR will continue to advocate for improved conditions in camps as lead of the Camp Coordination and Camp Management Cluster, but also for non-camp shelter solutions. It is assumed that as a result of camp closures and consolidation, repair and maintenance will be required across all remaining 124 camps in 2019, while UNHCR will prioritize repairs and maintenance in 44 camps across Iraq. UNHCR will support partners in the provision of basic services and infrastructure maintenance in camps and track distributions using the Assist tool and maintain up to date population data through appropriate tools. Support will be rendered to authorities and partners to provide information on services available in return areas. Where required and conducive, provide support towards the coordinated camp downsizing/consolidation and potential phase out in collaboration with authorities and camp management partners. UNHCR will conduct regular protection monitoring of the informal sites to prevent forced returns, identify persons in need of assistance and conduct referrals to respective services. UNHCR and partner will also continue with cash for work in 2 camps in Ninewa i.e. Salamiyah 1, Salamiyah 2. This will be achieved by continuing the cash-for-work scheme for vulnerable camp residents to conduct site maintenance, awareness raising and other activitiesUNHCR will implement camp management activities in the following formal camps ; Dohuk: Bersive-1 IDP Camp, Bersive-2 IDP camp, Darkar IDP Camp, Chamishku IDP Camp, Bajet kandala-1 IDP camp, Bajet kandala-2 IDP camp, Rwanga IDP Camp, Khanke IDP Camp, Kabarto-1 IDP Camp, Kabarto-2 IDP Camp, Shariya IDP Camp, Garmawa IDP Camp, Esian IDP Camp, Sheikhan IDP camp, Mam-Rashan IDP Camp, Mamilian IDP Camp and Dawudia IDP CampSulaymaniyah: Arbat IDPs Camp, Ashti IDPs Camp, Surdash IDPs Camp, Tazade IDPs Camp and Qoratu IDPs CampErbil: Harsham, Baherka, Hassansham U2, Hassansham U3 and Khazer M1 Kirkuk: Daquq, Laylan 1, Laylan 2 andNizrawaMosul: Salamiyah 1, Salamiyah 2, Hammam Al Aleel 1, Hammam Al Aleel 2, Transit Site and Nimrud Camp

Protection Risk Analysis

With the conflict declared over in December 2017, IDPs increasingly face pressure and even coercion to return to areas of origin. Example of coercive measures have included mass arrests, forced eviction or threat of eviction, confiscation of documentation, and forcible relocation or even expulsion, violating the principles of voluntariness, safety and dignity in relation to some return movements. Other IDPs face administrative and physical obstacles in returning and securing their right of return. Treatment of both IDPs and returnees across Iraq is heavily influenced by religion and ethnicity. In camp environment, the risk of under representation of minority groups and persons with specific needs in the camp management structures and committees. Linked to the above is people with specific needs are particularly vulnerable. Women, girls, orphans and unaccompanied children, the elderly, and persons with disabilities, may struggle access to water, food, shelter, or health services. Many IDPs have faced sexual and gender-based violence or other serious human rights violations prior to or during their flight. UNHCR reports and assessments conducted in 2017 and 2018 indicate that IDP women and girls (especially adolescent girls) face daily protection risks, including the risk of forced marriage, denial of resources and opportunities (access to school for girls), risk of harassment in public spaces, risk of having to exchange sex for money/ or assistance as well as exploitation as they strive to meet basic needs of their families. The aforementioned risks are further exacerbated by the presence of security actors inside and around IDP camps and return areas. Under-reporting of SGBV incidents remains high, due to social stigma, reprisal, discrimination and weak law enforcement.During conflict and displacement, many children were exposed to traumatic events and subjected to sexual violence, child marriage, detention and child labour. Large numbers of children formerly associated, or perceived to be associated with ISIL face stigma, discrimination and security risks. Across IDP camps and host communities, child marriage and child labour have reportedly increased as negative coping mechanisms due to multiple factors including challenging economic circumstances and lack of educational and livelihood opportunities. A number of camps is located in the areas with heave presence of armed elements, i.e Ninewa, Anbar, and humanitarian access remains precarious

Mitigation Measures

Continued protection monitoring will be undertaken in the camps as part of UNHCR overall protection monitoring of IDPs. UNHCR and its partners will aim to ensure equal participation and representation of persons with specific needs in the camp management, and through the network of community outreach workers will monitor protection situation in the camps, identify persons in need of assistance and facilitate their access to appropriate services. UNHCR also regularly conducts focus group discussion in the camps with various gender and age groups to ensure that voices of all population groups are heard and are translated into programming. As part of risk mitigation against sexual exploitation and abuse, UNHCR and partners will CCCM staff working in the camps. Follow up training will also be provided to the guards on how to handle the camp residents in dignity. UNHCR will continue to progressively strengthen community-based protection and involvement of the community in SGBV prevention and response through, amongst others, training community outreach volunteers and establishing a specialised network of volunteers working in the camps. The incidence of sexual violence and domestic violence require particular attention in camp-settings. UNHCR will maintain 24/7 guards, as well as liaise and coordinate with local authorities and security forces (federal police, local police and military) to ensure unimpeded supply of goods and services, while at the same time preventing access of unauthorized personnel to the camps

How is your project accountable to affected population

Both in UNHCR managed camps and as a cluster lead UNHCR will ensure that camp-based committees are established and are inclusive and participatory. By giving particular emphasis on the meaningful inclusion of women, children, elderly and persons with specific needs, for example, UNCHR will receive feedback from these groups and make sure it is heard and incorporated into decision-making processes. Communication with Communities initiatives, such as community-based complaint mechanisms, hot lines, and information sharing channels will be strengthened and consolidated. This will also include ensuring that there are safe, confidential and accessible complaints mechanisms for SEA, and ensuring that UNHCR and partners, refugees and host communities are aware of the complaints mechanisms, and have confidence in reporting and investigation procedures. Starting 2019, UNHCR will mainstream PSEA in all its projects requiring that the partners to actively plan PSEA awareness and accountability measures in all the activities. This will be informed by a mandatory questionnaire that will be completed detailing the measures each of the partners has in place to prevent SEA. As a follow up, multifunctional teams will monitor compliance during joint monitoring visits to project sites and offices. UNHCR will also conduct annual participatory assessments with the displaced population and IDP returnees in addition to thematic FGDs to improve the participation of the affected population in programme planning and delivery.
