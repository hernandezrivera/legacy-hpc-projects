Project Summary

In Area C of the West Bank, the living conditions are still deteriorating because of the prolonged political crisis resulting in various negative impacts to the whole population, particularly for vulnerable communities and families. After more than 17 months of the COVID-19 outbreak in the West Bank, most of communities in Area C continue to suffer the negative social and economic impact of this pandemic in many ways. In the West Bank, tens of thousands of families fell below the poverty line due to COVID-19 which is an additional challenge to the communities as well as to the humanitarian response. In this context and in line with the Food Security (FS) Cluster 2021 HRP Framework, the proposed intervention led by Action Against Hunger, Palestinian Agricultural Relief Committees (PARC), and the Union of Agricultural Work Committees (UAWC) will target the food security and livelihoods needs of the most vulnerable communities affected by protection threats in Area C of the West Bank. In addition, the proposed project will provide specific and tailored assistance to targeted communities in response to COVID-19 negative consequences. The proposed intervention will thus have two major implementing dimensions and activities: non-agricultural and agricultural activities. Specifically, the activities will contribute to protecting vulnerable communities in Area C from forcible transfer and COVID-19 negative consequences by improving the main factors involved in food production (roads, water, livestock shelter), income generation, and agricultural inputs. By doing so, the proposed intervention will contribute to improving the food security of marginalized families living under the poverty line. The project will also participate to the increase of income for vulnerable women and men in non-agriculture sectors. This will include cash for work schemes as well as support to existing small businesses and different Income Generation Activities (IGAs) aiming to assist targeted communities to face the COVID-19 negative impacts. The project activities will benefit all members of the household and will be carried out on two levels: 1. At household and individual level through the rehabilitation of animal shelters, provision of cash support (cash for work), and providing support to existing IGAs as well as through the provision of agricultural inputs. 2. At community level by improving the access to water and the communities’ accessibility (road rehabilitation). The non-agriculture-based activities will be designed to serve the targeted communities including individuals, with focus on women affected by the COVID-19 pandemic. The proposed intervention will deal with the protection threats to the livelihoods of the targeted families. Fomenting a community participation approach in the assessment, design and implementation of the activities, all the community’s sub-groups will participate in different focus group discussions, technical assessments and in the implementation process. The proposed project is in line with the FSS Strategic Objectives (SO) number 1 (The resilience of vulnerable households are protected, and their livelihoods, food security and productive capacity restored/enhanced) and SO2 (Households suffering from limited access to food and to income opportunities are able to meet their basic food needs). The communication with communities (CWCs) will mitigate potential security and protection risks and, ultimately, empower the beneficiaries at community, family and individual level. As outlined, the proposed intervention ensures protection mainstreaming as it is guided by protection principles and considers safety and dignity; meaningful access; accountability to affected population (AAP) as well as participation and empowerment.

1 - Needs

The West Bank faces a protracted crisis, with repeated shocks and restrictions on freedom of movement, constrained productive capacities and lack of economic opportunities resulting in high unemployment and low household incomes especially in Area C. This, together with the high cost of living leavetwo million people (36.7% of the population) moderately or severely food insecure, making almost two out of five Palestinian suffering from food insecurity (HNO 2022). More than 1.5 million Palestinians are in need of assistance (27% in the West Bank). In addition, the negative consequences of COVID-19 have added an extra burden on the economy, communities and families following the Palestinian Authority’s (PA) measures imposed in 2021 in terms of the partial and/or full lockdowns to contain the virus spread. The restrictions are causing a severe disruptive impact on the entire food system.. Despite the slight improvement in the labour market in the West Bank in the first two quarters of 2021, the situation remains fragile. Based on the oPt MSNA carried out by REACH in 2021 to inform the HNO 2022, 36% reported losing their jobs permanently or temporarily because of COVID-19, 68% of surveyed HHs reported a decrease in their income in the West Bank and 68% reported an increase in their debts (Area C 74% and H2 92%). Based on a survey for Area C conducted by Bir Zeit University (2019), 25% of households in Area C rely on herding and agriculture as a main source of income, 40% rely on irregular daily wages, less than 25% rely on stable wages, and almost 75% rely on agricultural products and animal products as main part of their income. Affected by the pandemic restrictions and its consequences, small famers and producers have been hit by the reduced demand, challenges in securing basic inputs, and the impediments in accessing credit and markets. Families who are already food insecure and have limited resilience are unlikely to be able to retain (or rebuild) their livelihoods. Additionally, communities in central West Bank are under threat of forcible transfer (OCHA). 2021 has recorded an increase of 38% in the number of demolitions and confiscations compared with the equivalent period in 2020. In 2021, 311 structures, or 57% of all structures targeted in Area C, have been seized without, or with very short, prior notice, utilizing various military orders, effectively preventing the affected people from objecting in advance. Out of the total demolished structures in Area C, 34% are agricultural related including livelihood infrastructures (OCHA monthly report, August 2021), which affects the vulnerability of families. Winter could bring high winds, storms, flash flooding and frosts, exacerbating the already poor humanitarian status for women, girls and boys. The ongoing assessment of AAH’s interventions in 2020 and 2021 enabled AAH to identify communities requiring priority emergency support due to their vulnerability (unemployment rate, income, demolition/eviction orders, and sensitivity of their location). The current political and economic negative consequences disproportionally affect the different gender and age groups. Female headed households were identified as particularly vulnerable due to their limited access to livelihood opportunities. Women and children are the most disadvantaged regarding food security status, women are responsible for cooking, while children are affected most deeply by malnutrition. AAH mainstreams PSEA actions to mitigate any risks to these vulnerable groups, especially in light of the disparate needs. AAH’s experience along with the previous and continuous assessments to animal shelters, particularly for 6 specific governorates, confirm that herding is the main source of income for most herding families (90% of the targeted families) - especially in Area C. Most of the animal shelters in the targeted areas are not adequate for the flock size resulting in overcrowded shelters. In addition, a proportion of the flock spent extensive periods of time in the open thus resulting in high abortion and mortality rates (25% in most of the targeted communities).

2 - Activities

The project was coordinated with all relevant stakeholders including the MoA and other cluster partners to ensure coherence and no duplication. The activities including the specific locations and governorates were shared and some adjustments (removing and replacing specific communities) were reflected based on the coordination with other applicants/partners to ensure no duplication. The project will contribute to enhancing the protection of the targeted beneficiaries from forcible transfer and COVID-19 negative consequences by improving the main factors involved in food production (roads, water, livestock shelter), income generation, and agricultural inputs. The project will contribute to achieve the FSS objectives and contribute to the SO3. The intervention will contribute to improving the food security of marginalized families living under the poverty line and to increasing the income of vulnerable women and men in non-agriculture sectors. AAH, PARC and UWAC will coordinate the final selection of localities and beneficiaries with MoA, WASH Cluster, the Palestinian Water Authority (PWA) MoSD and MoL directorates, the FS Cluster and with organizations working in the same area. To ensure diversity and a balanced gender and age perspectives, women children and elderly family members will participate in all stages of the project. Each partner will cover certain areas and AAH lead the entire intervention. The interventions will be divided into agricultural-based and non-agricultural based activities. Partners will adopt the protective measures against the risk of COVID-19 transmission and avoid doing harm. Agricultural-based interventions: A.1 Water infrastructure rehabilitation and provision of water storages systems (Hebron, Jerusalem, Ramallah, Jericho, and Tubas) the intervention will reduce the farming input costs. It will cover construction and rehabilitation of water pipes (20 Km), 80 agricultural cisterns and bonds, and rehabilitate 3 springs (2 springs in Batteer and Artas and 1 spring in Halhoul). A.2 Construction/Rehabilitation of livestock shelter for 200 vulnerable herders (Hebron, Bethlehem, Jerusalem and Tubas). It will help ensure hygienic conditions and maintain production levels in the context of COVID-19 pandemic. A.3 Supply of agricultural inputs: tools and material for rehabilitating 260 green houses and 50 irrigation systems to improve productivity of farming (Jericho and Tubas). A.4 Rehabilitation of road to improve safe access to livelihood assets and services, agriculture land and grazing areas (20 km roads in Hebron, Bethlehem and Ramallah) A.5 Rehabilitation of lands located in areas under threat of confiscation in Hebron and Bethlehem (rehabilitation of 100 Dunums). This activity will support vulnerable farmers to protect and improve lands productivity. A.6 Provision of drought tolerant seeds to improve access to locally produced pastures (20 dunums per herder, for 500 herders in Hebron, Bethlehem, and Tubas). Female herders and farmers who own livestock, land, greenhouses will be supported to improve the productivity of their land and to increase household income. The non-agriculture-based interventions: A.1 Cash based support (cash for work) & employability for families with limited access to income opportunities in Hebron, Jerusalem, and Tubas. The project will mainly target workers in most affected areas who lost their jobs due to closures and restrictions followed the spread of COVID-19. This component will assist them in securing basic cash assistance to support the household income and, therefore, contributing positively to the entire household’s food security. The planned work activities will include participating in constructing and rehabilitating public buildings (school rooms and facilities) within the community, roads, agricultural roads, land rehabilitation etc. 50 individuals will be supported for a 75 day period based on a daily salary rate of NIS 80 for unskilled workers and NIS 120 for skilled workers. A.2 Support deteriorated IGAs and small-scale businesses affected by COVID-19 (Hebron, Bethlehem, Tubas, and Jerusalem) by assisting 40 small-scale businesses headed mainly by women. During implementation, partners will ensure active participation by the targeted beneficiaries through a clear participatory monitoring and evaluation approach that feeds the process of activities adjustment/improvement. Additionally, PSEA is always considered during AAH projects, there is a well-established feedback and complaints mechanism for processing any concerns that participants or community members might have about the direct implementation or staff related to the project. These are processed sensitively, particularly for vulnerable groups and we make allowances to gender, age and disability, accommodating to those groups with home visits or private spaces to discuss any concerns. AAH have Standard Operating Procedures to systemize implementation across all our projects. PSEA related complaints will be considered as highly sensitive by AAH and thus directly addressed by the Head of Base and Country Director or by the organization HQ. AAH has safe and accessible beneficiary feedback mechanisms in place which allow beneficiaries to provide their inputs, complaints, and satisfaction regarding the activities implemented. Beneficiary knowledge of this system is promoted during field assessments and monitoring visits during which AAH and partners staff explain how the mechanisms work.

3 - Indicators

AAH has a well-established MEAL unit where all projects are monitored during the entire project life cycle. The MEAL unit will follow up on all activities and work progress to ensure adherent to the main set goals and indicators. The process involves periodic reporting based on several means of verifications including field visits, focus group sessions, questionnaires which follows a clear participatory approach by the targeted beneficiaries. A baseline survey will be conducted at the start of the project by the three partners which will be updated and compared to an end line survey after the activities’ completion. Information will be collected through semi structured interviews, household visits and field observations. Focus group discussions with female family members and female head of HHs will take place separately to eliminate any cultural and social barriers preventing them to actively participate in the project activities. Selection criteria will be identified by the partners in coordination with MoA, MoSD, MoL and the related local councils and the partners will coordinate with the related directorates in each governorate to benefit from their assessments and data. Particular attention will be paid to beneficiary selection based on diversity including their economical and displacement vulnerability, methods of working with village councils, mechanism of asset distribution and gender sensitive approach. Ongoing monitoring will allow AAH, UAWC and PARC to evaluate methodologies and the impact of the activities, to identify challenges and best practices. Accountability and transparency will be ensured through of AAH’s standard beneficiary feedback mechanism. In general, the project will benefit from a diversified population in terms of age and gender. The percentage of beneficiaries age will be: males age between 0-18 years 24.08%, females age 0-18 years 23.36%, males age between 18-65 years 24.67%, females age between 18-65 23.67%, males above 65 years old 1.59%, and females above 65 years old 1.91%. The expected objective verifiable indicators at outcome level will include: • The livestock mortality and abortion rates for the households targeted by livestock accommodation decreased by 30%• The agricultural water cost for the communities targeted by water interventions will decreased by 35%• The targeted farmers targeted by agricultural inputs will improve their productivity by more than 25% To further ensure protection is mainstreamed throughout the project implementation, the indicator “% of communities/project beneficiaries reporting that humanitarian assistance is delivered in a safe and dignified manner” will be measured through an end line survey of the beneficiaries. A total of 12,484 beneficiaries will benefit from the project agricultural-based activities (F 18-65 yrs old: 3,101 M 18-65 yrs old: 3,186 B: 2,920 G: 2,803 Elderly Female above 65: 256 Elderly Male above 65: 218 PWDs: 227). For agricultural-based activities, the following are the main output indicators will be used• 310 households of farmers, herders, breeders, fishers receiving inputs and services (1,519 individuals: 741 females and 778 males) • 820 female and male headed households (4,018 individuals: 1,961 female and 2,057 males) receive technical assistance to rehabilitate the water resources (pipes, springs and cisterns) • 4 communities with improved physical access through the rehabilitation of 20 km of agricultural roads (2,880 individuals: 1,406 females and 1,474 males). • 200 herders (980 individuals: 479 females and 501 males) receive technical and material assistance to rehabilitate their animal shelters. • 500 herding HH (2,450 individuals: 1,196 females and 1,254 males) received climate adapted (drought tolerant) seeds for locally produced pastures (20 dunums per herder)• 35 farmers (172 individuals: 84 females and 88 males) benefited from the rehabilitation of 100 dunums. For the non-agriculture-based activities, output indicators will include:• 50 (37 female headed and 13 male headed families) families (245 individuals) mainly women receive support through the cash for work component. • 40 (23 female headed and 17 male headed) small businesses and IGAs (196 individuals) affected by COVID-19 negative impacts mainly women will be assisted.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

Through the whole project cycle AAH, PARC and UWAC ensure protection mainstreaming is guided by protection principles of Do No Harm. The partners consider safety and dignity, meaningful access, accountability as well as participation and empowerment of paramount importance. Overall AAH seeks to complement its own actions with for instance legal support to inform risk mitigation measures especially in Area C and consequently to increase complementary humanitarian action. For activities that include physical interventions (including constructions of animal shelter and roads, water pipes rehabilitation), AAH will coordinate with the relevant legal entities to evaluate how this intervention will affect the legal status of the targeted communities. AAH, PARC and UWAC will do the hard construction works during Fridays and the holidays of the ICA to attract minimal attention from them, using low visibility implementation and coordinating with beneficiaries (especially animal shelter rehabilitation) to cover the new structures while ensuring that the new works will not change the overall appearance of the communities. In order to inform its assessments and implementation AAH, PARC and UWAC will continuously work on understanding gender roles. By understanding gender roles and household dynamics, AAH believes that the organisation mitigates the risks of doing harm. AAH has in place mechanisms to prevent and respond to sexual exploitation and abuse by all staff. All AAH staff has the obligation to adhere to the organisation code of conduct. AAH has in place focal points at base and mission levels. as well as safe and confidential ways to report abuse. Moreover, all staff received specific in person training on the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

AAH has conducted in its Middle East country offices a Protection Mainstreaming Internal Evaluation. Particular attention was paid to the way AAH includes vulnerable groups in its work, for instance through inclusive participatory assessments. As one of AAH’s core values, AAH thrives to prevent discrimination and to ensure equal and impartial access though transparency and accountability to affected populations. AAH aims for all community members to be involved and consulted as much as possible in project design and implementation, as well as evaluation, including through household and community consultations and focus group discussions, accessible to all. In order for their participation to be meaningful, AAH focuses on ‘qualitative inclusion’ meaning safe spaces for beneficiaries to participate. Also, AAH started engaging with Humanity & Inclusion as inclusion experts to sensitise and inform AAH’s programming. For instance, instead of asking directly if people have a disability, AAH is increasingly using ‘The Washington Group Short Set of Questions’ to detect people with disabilities. AAH is reporting under the HRP disaggregated data by sex and age; though pending the reporting format, AAH usually includes further disaggregation on vulnerability such as data on people with disabilities. In addition, AAH started in its assessments to consider ‘barriers’ of particularly vulnerable groups and ‘enabling factors’ for people with specific needs. AAH has safe and accessible beneficiary feedback mechanisms in place which allow beneficiaries to provide their inputs, complaints, and satisfaction regarding the activities implemented. Beneficiary knowledge of this system are promoted during field assessments and monitoring visits during which AAH and partners staff explain how the mechanisms work. With regards to access to assistance and in this specific project the partners will 1) Use their respective data and knowledge of the areas to prioritize in a transparent and relevant manner more at need communities 2) Consult widely with community members, community leaders and MoSD, MoL and MoA directorates 3) Call for applications with a fair competition mainly for the income generation activities to select most relevant and promising business to be established and access business ideas based on criteria made public.
