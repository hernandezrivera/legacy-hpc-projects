Project Summary

Heavy rain and flooding created historical overflow of the Nile and Sobat Rivers since July 2020, creating the worst flooding in 60 years. The flood destroyed homes and livelihoods of people - damaged settlements, agriculture (crops and cattle), horticulture, and market access which are crucial for peoples’ livelihoods. 40% of the crops and vegetable gardens in GOAL operational areas have been affected and no yield has been harvested during harvesting in 2020. People are also displaced and feared to turn to negative coping mechanisms such as informal borrowing, selling productive assets, and depletion of working capital which might be used to protect from the impact of the disaster. The flooding crisis came at the back drop of very grim hunger situation in these areas, where already 163,000 were suffering from acute and severe hunger (about 70% of population in Baliet, and 85% in Ulang) (IPC Phases 3, and 4). And 59,462 in IPC4. This situation was further impacted by the current economic downturn and higher inflation rates, in addition to food price hikes exacerbated by the impact of COVID-19 pandemic. The floods severely affected the opportunities for income generating schemes leading to loss of household income and inability to afford food and meet basic needs. Negative coping mechanisms of poor and most affected households have been reported, such as taking fewer meals per day (mostly amongst women and girls), selling assets or borrowing money at high rates to purchase food items, causing unmanageable debt. The flooding also resulted in increased incidences of human diseases such as malaria and acute watery diarrhoea that will affect productivity and deplete savings in the form of increased medical expenses – this will further contribute to household vulnerability and reduce resources available for purchasing food.
GOAL is requesting access to the HRP to cover the immediate needs of 25,800 vulnerable individuals severely affected by the current flooding of the Sobat river. The individuals targeted will be from existing GOAL beneficiaries who have been flood affected. The flooding affected 68,502 people in Baliet and Ulang counties. Under the GOAL programme, GOAL has been providing support to build the resilience of 160,000 people in Melut, Baliet, Ulang, Rubkona, and Panyinjar counties since 2016. Prior to the current flooding GOAL program has been implementing various flood risk preparedness and mitigation activities including: dissemination of Early Warning Information, and development of an early response strategy to mitigate the impact of flooding, stakeholders coordination, formation of flood response committees, identification of most vulnerable people and high-risk areas; distribution of empty sacks to aid dyke construction, dyke construction and monitoring implementation of flood response actions. This request for HRP is sought as the scale and magnitude of the current flooding and livelihood crisis it has compounded requires additional interventions to help vulnerable households meet their immediate needs, reducing the hunger gap and mitigating negative coping mechanisms. This intervention will also provide support to host communities that had to cope with people displaced.
There is a significant gap in the response to these needs. WFP food distribution covers only 50% of the monthly households’ food needs. This presents an urgent need for emergency livelihood interventions. The HRP will support households through Cash for Assets to address their immediate food needs, while building assets, such as constructing flood control dikes that will improve their livelihoods, reduce risks and impact of shocks, increase food productivity, and strengthen resilience to natural disasters. The cash for assets will be conditional cash transfer with two components of conditionality – engagement on Cash for Asset, to rebuild community assets, for able bodied households; and mandatory attendance on trainings.

1 - Needs

GOAL is requesting access to the HRP fund to cover the immediate needs of 25,800 vulnerable individuals severely affected by the current flooding of the Sobat river. The individuals targeted will be from existing GOAL beneficiaries who have been flood affected. The flooding affected 68,502 people in Baliet and Ulang counties (Inter-Agency Assessment, Ulang October 2020). High food prices prompted by damaged crops and vegetables, are also contributing to the high levels of acute food insecurity. The flooding is likely to result in increased incidences of human diseases such as malaria and acute watery diarrhoea that will affect productivity and deplete savings in the form of increased medical expenses. The macroeconomic deterioration which led to high commodity prices and depreciation of SSP against USD. The oil price crash also translated into heavy losses in export earnings, which served to widen the trade deficit and indeed supply shortage of needed commodities. Accordingly, the South Sudanese Pound (SSP) continued to lose its purchasing power against the US dollar - during the third week of September, the South Sudanese Pound (SSP) was exchanged at 620/USD on the parallel market in Juba, representing a depreciation of 30% compared to the beginning of September 2020 . This has negatively impacted the purchasing power of market dependent and resource constrained households. The cost of a food basket was also increased by 38%. According to the IPC analysis of November 2020, Ulang is classified # 1 Ranking IPC phase 4 county with 85% of population (117,038 people) in IPC3 and above; and Baliet with 70% of population (39443) in IPC3 and above. These two counties require immediate lifesaving cash assistance and scaled up FSL response to meet immediate lifesaving and emergency livelihood needs. Unless timely interventions to save lives and livelihoods are made some 59,462 people already in IPC 4 may slide to IPC Phase 5 ‘Famine’, indicating extreme human suffering with alarming consequences including significant food consumption gaps, extreme asset depletion, and unacceptably high rates of acute malnutrition. This figure is anticipated to increase in May 2021 (beginning of lean season) and will further worsen the food security situation unless measures are taken to address the immediate needs and increase the capacity of the community to absorb further IDPs.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

In July 2020 GOAL has conducted detailed market assessment in both Baliet, and Ulang counties to determine the feasibility of cash transfer over vouchers. Accordingly, the market analysis done it was identified that cash transfer as feasible intervention. Unconditional cash transfer through Service provider (Bank) will be considered. Similarly, GOAL has been monitoring the market and market functionality on monthly basis, and this data is used to determine intervention modality. Whenever the situation and market functionality changes negatively, GOAL has the flexibility to shift into voucher system through the traders it has been supporting over the last four years. GOAL has supported 35 traders in Ulang and 20 traders in Baliet.

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

When local markets are functioning and able to meet demand cash transfers serve as an appropriate and powerful alternative to direct distributions of food and non-food items or in-kind assistance. Cash transfer programs can also be a more dignified and flexible form of assistance, since program beneficiaries have the freedom to choose what they need. Cash transfers o deliver timely and cost-effective assistance to crisis-affected populations, while at thesame time supporting the local economy. It is also fast ensuring timely humanitarian response as compared to the in kind transfers.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

GOAL believes that the investment in staff competencies and periodic trainings on PSEA and Accountability is critical. On annual basis (in February and August) all staff (including newly recruited) are given on-job training on PSEA, Gender Equality & Inclusion, Community Feedback Mechanisms, Child Protection, SGBV, Safeguarding, Risk Management and ethics (Codes of Conduct, Trafficking in Persons, Whistleblowing, Conflicts of Interest Declaration, Fraud, Corruption & Harassment), and attendance list are filed in HR Department. Communities are given awareness creation training on PSEA during the planned beneficiaries meetings, Women’s Action Groups meetings, and through Project Management Committees. Staff are trained on gathering PSEA, reporting channels and reporting timelines and confidentiality related to PSEA cases. The information gathered through PSEA mechanisms are sensitive. The PSEA mechanism is designed exclusively to address sexual exploitation and abuse allegations; hence reported immediately (less than 24 hours) in a timely manner to CD or Area Coordinators in a way that the investigations are started straight away, and the victim is updated on progress of investigation and given timely response. The CFM mechanism is mainly meant to address issues related to project implementation, but it can also pick feedbacks and allegations sexual exploitation and abuse.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

The project will make sure that the feedback and compliant mechanism serves all community members including women, girls, individuals with disability and not literate. Some specific feedback mechanisms might not be appropriate for all community members like suggestion boxes to not literate individuals. In fact, majority of the community are not literate and that is why they prefer to address their feedbacks and complaints via their chiefs and community leaders. This may not also work for all members of the community, as girls and women may find it uncomfortable to discuss about project issues with elders or chiefs. Taking these in to account the project will organize a review meeting with community representatives, chiefs and administrators to discuss about progresses, issues and complaints. The community representatives will be drawn from women, disabled persons, beneficiaries, and youth. This provides the platform for vulnerable groups of the community to complain and give feedback on their issues. This is proven to be working in the implementation of GOAL’s project activities in the field.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

The Community feedback mechanism (CFM) has been established and strengthened in all field locations since 2012, and all complaints and feedback are logged, tracked, and reported in the CFM tracker (CommCare mobile data collection app) on regular basis. This has enabled GOAL South Sudan (GSS) to identify any trends or issues, track progress and future plans, and challenges to improve on effectiveness during project implementation. All feedbacks and complaints lodged are dealt in a timely manner, according to the project’s guidelines, and provided timely clear outcomes, referrals, responses, and thereafter closed. GOAL staff encourages continued consultation with local authorities, Payam and Boma chiefs, community and camp leaders, women and youth group leaders through regular meetings and field visits to ensure that the community is involved in programme implementation and targeting.Since early 2016, GOAL started the AAP approach with the creation of 22 community-based project management committees at the Payam level that are functional and informing programme decisions/management; all elected members are trained on CFM. These committees are functional in each village where GSS is implementing activities. They meet on a Monthly basis, and one of our field officers or assistants in each Payam attends the meeting. During those monthly meetings, they discuss planned activities, achievements, operational issues, challenges faced, and ways of collaboration for improvement. On top of that, they look at all complaints and feedback logged, discuss, and follow-up on the timeline and responses given to the complainant. Representatives of the community and programme participants use the meetings to hold the organization accountable for inputs, the behavior of staff, and activities being implemented in the community. The Community Feedback Mechanisms (CFM) helps the field staff and management to respond to and address feedback received in a timely manner. The aspiration and desire to monitor not only the use of aid and resources provided but also related behavior changes in the affected communities and program participants, in a continuous manner over longer periods of time. The CFM helps the organization to ensure that we are engaging communities and targeted groups by generating and receiving reactions, comments, and criticisms. This is a critical mechanism for observing humanitarian standards and principles as part of GOAL's accountability to the community. The process of reviewing the accountability reports from field teams, responding to the feedback received, and documenting them for future improvement and learning and enables the project to adapt to prevailing contexts. This has also helped the team to develop sense of accountability and ownership. The communication tools and channels established as part of CFM within the communities are periodic in-person visits, community-wide meetings, and the use of monitoring, assessment, and survey tools.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The feedback from affected communities is used to inform decision-making on GOAL's programme delivery. The Community Feedback Mechanism (CFM) utilization is crucial al as it is used consistently to improve programme implementation and increase accountability to targeted populations. Periodic reviews of beneficiaries’ feedback by managers, and interactive team sessions at the field level are done with documentation of all information from CFM data collection process. This provides critical information and learning that can be used to understand how the programme activities are being implemented in the community. Apart from addressing concerns of current issues of beneficiaries, the feedback are used to improve project implementation, generate learning and as inputs for the design of future projects. Community participation is the integral part of GOAL's programming. During the entire phase of design and implementation of activities, GSS always encourage inclusive participation by communities, including women, elderly, disables, and non-beneficiaries. During the various meetings with these community members, relevant information are captured and inform decision-making at field as well as HO level. GSS ensures the availability of Help Desk or Suggestion Boxes visible with appropriate banners during implementing project activities. During distribution of project inputs, MEAL Officers at field level conduct on-site monitoring for accountability and reporting purposes. Initially, before 2016 beneficiaries and communities preferred community leaders with 78% as the best channel of sharing their feedbacks with GOAL, the second best being meeting GOAL staff in person. However, GOAL's annual survey findings of June 2020 revealed that, 97.8% of GSS beneficiaries are aware/familiar with CFM and subsequently, in 2020 around 90% of CFM cases recorded prefer to talk to GOAL staff and express/voice their concerns during the community meetings or visiting our field officers, as the most appropriate feedback mechanism. This is because of the excellent working relationship, openness, and the trust built among the beneficiaries. To conclude, the data collected are triangulated across different sources, from daily routine feedback cases, through surveys, suggestion box sheets, logbook entries and community meetings. Compilation and analysis will be made to respond to the complaints and feedbacks based on their natures. The lesson from this analysis will be taken as inputs to understand changing contexts and inform project decisions like beneficiaries' preference of project inputs. The status of FCM implementation is shared first to the community, then to the donors and GOAL HQ.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

GOAL recognises the dynamic, interlinked and multi-level nature of conflict in South Sudan. It believes that community resilience is closely related to community cohesion, which has been markedly affected by conflict at national, sub-national and local levels. Therefore, it is critical to ensure that conflict drivers are understood and continued to be analyzed, and that all programmatic tools (planning, monitoring, evaluation etc.) take into consideration the conflict context, with the overall aim of 1) avoiding harm, 2) contributing as much as possible to reducing conflict drivers and 3) increasing community cohesion. The breakdown in community cohesion is often cited anecdotally as a reason for households not undertaking community-led action, and anxiety related to the unpredictable conflict is thought to be a reason for some people’s reluctance to invest in longer-term livelihood strategies such as planting of food cash crops. The long-standing geographical and current operational presence of GOAL in Upper Nile State ensures that the project is rooted in a deep understanding of the context - the local cultural, socio-ethnic, economic and conflict dynamics in the target areas – and strikes the balance between providing support to government and opposition-held areas, and to IDPs, returnees and host communities. GOAL has been present in these areas of operation for significant amount of time, and its established presence has ensured strong community relations and existing mechanisms are in place to ensure the project learns from its interaction with the context. Established beneficiary committees are active, meeting monthly to deliberate on the direction of the project, with representatives from chiefs, sub-chiefs, women, youth, and special-needs groups. A feedback and complaints mechanism (FCRM) is also in operational in all sites to ensure the beneficiary feedback is incorporated and there is a forum to raise any concerns. Local procurement and local staff recruitment is prioritised where possible and coordinated with direct input and supervision of local authorities, while restricting undue influence in the process.
