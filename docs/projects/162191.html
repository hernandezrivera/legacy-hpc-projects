Project Summary

Early Childhood Education (ECE) is underdeveloped in Chad, and minimally present in the 12 Sudanese refugee camps in the east. These communities face several barriers to ECE access:
- Limited access and institutional management of preschool structures;
- Limited didactic programs and materials;
- Lack of qualified preschool teachers; and
- Inability to finance monitors’ incentives.
As of May 2019, there are 35,278 (18,776M, 16,502F) preschool age children living in the 12 Sudanese refugee camps in the UNHCR sub-delegations of Iriba and Farchana. Currently, only 38% (13,505) of them have access to ECE. Of these, most (88%) go to community preschool centers that more resemble a child friendly space or a day-care than a preschool. There are just 16 qualified preschool teachers in all 12 camps.
ECE in Eastern Chad has been unsupported by donors since 2017. JRS is the only partner supporting ECE in the Sudanese refugee camps, doing so through private funds mobilized for an extremely limited number of preschool centers in Djabal, Goz Amir, Milé, and Kounoungou. After UNHCR ended ECE support in 2017, the idea was for centers and monitors to become community-supported, but acceptance and success of this approach varied by camp – some have managed to keep centers operational, and others have ceased preschool education altogether.
In addition, one of the biggest challenges to ECE is the lack of an updated preschool curriculum. Chad’s most recent preschool curriculum dates back over 30 years. In September 2019, JRS contributed to the development of a national preschool strategy, led by UNICEF and the Ministries of Children and Education, that seeks to revise this curriculum in 2020.
ECE is widely accepted in the education community as the most critical time in a child’s cognitive development. Most of the brain’s development takes place during these years, which then shapes a child’s personality and ability for the rest of his or her life. This is when a child’s brain is most apt and open to learning, behavioral development, and the development of critical thinking and creativity. Development is not only physical and intellectual, but psychosocial as well. At this critical age, children need a structured environment that provides ample opportunities to explore and grow. This is especially critical for children in conflict or post-conflict settings, such as refugee camps, as it creates an element of stability and structure among the chaos. Preschool can provide children aged 3-6 a nurturing, safe environment where they can not only learn, but also be protected and receive emotional support. Lastly, preschool is an easy entry point into education. The cultural and economic barriers that might later prevent children from attending lower or upper secondary school are not yet present. Preschool provides safety and care, and often even a meal, to children of a young age, which incentivizes parental support. Creating the habit of school is critical to do early – children who go to preschool are over twice as likely to finish primary school and even more likely to finish secondary school.
Considering this proven importance of ECE, JRS seeks to implement a project that will increase access to and quality of early childhood education for at least 35,304 children ages 3-5. This program will aim to address the lack of safe and secure infrastructure, materials, and supplies needed for quality ECE. It will also support teacher incentives and increase the number of qualified preschool teachers via teacher training. Finally, JRS will advocate to the refugee communities to support ECE, to increase enrollment but also to foster support for a sustainable, community-led system.
To achieve these outcomes, JRS plans the following activities:
- Payment and training of preschool teachers;
- Rehabilitation and construction of preschool infrastructures/latrines;
- The purchase of teaching materials, students’ learning materials, and hygiene materials.

Besoins, Activités et Resultats Attendus

Activities- ECE teacher trainings and certification in collaboration with the Chadian authorities- Payment of ECE teacher stipends in camps that have not yet developed the ability to support this cost themselves- Reopening of preschool centers previously closed due to lack of funding- Rehabilitation and construction of infrastructures and latrines, where needed- Supply of teaching and learning kits- Set up of a pre-school cafeteria on a community-based approach (part of the food will be provided through community agriculture and cooks will be parents)- Improvement of the community participation in pre-school management through the Parent-Teacher Association- Community awareness raising on the importance of early childhood education Desired Outcomes- 259 ECE teachers are certified and their pedagogical competencies are strengthened- 35,304 pre-primary school aged children have access to preschool education- 10,500 pre-primary school children develop critical life skills and knowledge to advance to primary school- Improved attendance rate of pre-primary school students- Improved protection for children aged 3-5- Increased ownership of school management by the community, which assures that following the eventual withdrawal of humanitarian actors, communities are not only motivated to, but able to take on the responsibility of preschool education- Participation of preschool families in IGAs- Improved quality of education through improved working conditions and increased capacity of teachers- Strengthened capacity of Chadian education authorities to monitor preprimary schools in the refugee camps To run this program, JRS will use a community-based approach, by involving the community at all stages of the program and engaging them in decision-making processes. This approach is appropriate because it enables the community to maintain the schools’ functionality even after humanitarian actors have left. In this case, community-based approach means JRS will train and empower preschool communities to implement revenue generating activities (IGAs) to introduce the idea of financial independence and autonomy; employ refugees at every level of the project, from preschool teachers, to guards, to cooks; and use of refugee grown and produced food to feed preschool students. However, the level of community-based approach will vary by camp, as certain camps have welcomed this principle more than others. Some communities have managed to keep paying their preschool teachers, while others have not. Others still have been forced to drastically reduce the number of preschool monitors, while for others, the lack of external financing forced the preschool centers to close entirely. JRS will implement widespread awareness raising and capacity building efforts throughout the 12 refugee camps to encourage their eventual responsibility for preschool education, but JRS has learned from experience that this type of value and behavior change is a long and laborious process that needs to be done slowly, tactfully, but with determination. Via a private donor called iACT, JRS has run 12 preschool centers in four camps whose teachers have been trained by iACT in pedagogical and protection aspects of preschool teaching. A selection of these teachers are qualified to train other teachers, a mechanism which can be deployed throughout this project, as well, especially as a vehicle for providing continuing training apart from the certifying training detailed above.
