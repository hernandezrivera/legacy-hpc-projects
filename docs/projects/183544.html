Project Summary

Palestinians are increasingly less able to cope with sudden shocks, manmade or in connection to natural events, such as spikes of violence or heatwaves and winter storms. These sudden shocks occur alongside, and exacerbate the impact of, already critical conditions, characterized by demolitions in the West Bank, trade restrictions and persistent electricity shortages in Gaza, natural and environmental hazards, and inadequate water and sanitation services.
These circumstances and constraints inevitably hinder economic, social, and urban development, while also preventing freedom of movement and access to basic services and affect aid actors as well, preventing them from delivering effective assistance.
Specific humanitarian and development needs coexist in this context, whereby strengthening the coherence between humanitarian, development, and peace efforts to effectively reduce the humanitarian caseload is critical, as funding for humanitarian action is outstripped by growing needs. Better coordination across the Humanitarian Development Peace (HDP) nexus is therefore crucial to effectively reduce people’s needs, risks, and vulnerabilities, supporting prevention efforts and thus, shifting from only delivering humanitarian assistance to contributing to ending need as well.
Two million people (36.7% of the population) are estimated to be moderately or severely food insecure (FIES estimates based on the data collected as part of the MSNA survey), making almost two out of five Palestinian suffering from food insecurity, in 2021. Of these, more than 1.5 million are in need of assistance (73% in the Gaza Strip and 27 % in the West Bank), with nearly 70 percent (1,090,700 people) suffering from poor access to nutritious and sufficient food.
The Food Security Sector (FSS) in Palestine, created in December 2012, brings together UN and NGOs organizations on an equal footing to improve the food security of the needy and vulnerable, by ensuring immediate direct access to food and protecting/restoring their livelihoods, while enhancing resilience. The FSS works toward building stronger partnerships to support sustainable, comprehensive, and locally owned food security interventions in Palestine. The FSS is composed of circa 45 members, leveraging the existing expertise of stakeholders, working as open fora that need to synergize efforts across three main pillars – food assistance, agriculture/livelihood, and cash-based programming. To enhance coordination and provide stronger analysis and rsponse, the FSS is provided with a single coordination structure tailored to the specific protracted crisis in the WB and GS, that needs inter-linking humanitarian, recovery, and development interventions while working at the inter-cluster level to contribute to a holistic response. As such, currently, more than ever, it is crucial to ensure an adequate level of dedicated staffing to: 1) Support strategic planning of FSS by coordinating a participatory and consultative platform to increase eﬃciency/effectiveness of FSS partners assistance;
2) Support and provide generation of data, analysis and information for planning and coordination of FSS interventions and informing policy adaptation;
3) Support strengthening national capacities to contribute to effective coordination of and advocacy for humanitarian interventions within a HDP nexus approach

1 - Needs

Two million people (36.7% of the population) are estimated to be moderately or severely food insecure (FIES estimates based on the data collected as part of the MSNA survey), making almost two out of five Palestinian suffering from food insecurity, in 2021. Of these, more than 1.5 million are in need of assistance (73% in the Gaza Strip and 27 % in the West Bank), with nearly 70 % suffering from poor access to nutritious and sufficient food. Notably, 82% of people in need in the WB (about 350,000 people) live are in Area A &B. Israel’s military occupation and its coercive environment in the WB, Gaza’s over 14 years of blockade and recurring rounds of violence, remain the root cause of food insecurity. They exert a detrimental impact on food production, availability, and economic access. They impede food production by restricting access to land, water, production inputs, and markets. They constrain productive capacities, and limit economic opportunities, contributing to the high rates of unemployment and low household incomes, which, together with poverty, are the immediate cause of food insecurity in Palestine. Palestinians are increasingly less able to cope with sudden shocks, manmade or in connection to natural events, such as spikes of violence or heatwaves and winter storms. These sudden shocks occur alongside and exacerbate the impact of, already critical conditions, characterized by demolitions in the WB, trade restrictions and persistent electricity shortages in Gaza, natural and environmental hazards, and inadequate water and sanitation services. In the WB (where 16.8% are food insecure) destruction/confiscation of structures, restrictions on access to farming and grazing land, and lack of adequate law enforcement on violent settlers all contribute to undermining livelihoods and standards of living. In the Gaza Strip (where 57.9% are food insecure). The continuous blockade and sustained undermining of potential economic rehabilitation, in addition to the impact of the last hostilities in May 2021, make the need for humanitarian assistance remains high, as the livelihoods of many farmers, breeders, herders, and fishers are negatively impacted by the destruction and damage to their land and productive assets and farming infrastructures, as well as their eventual inability to access such resources. These circumstances and constraints inevitably hinder economic, social and urban development, while also preventing freedom of movement and access to basic services and affect aid actors as well, preventing them from delivering effective assistance. Specific humanitarian and development needs coexist in this context, whereby strengthening the coherence between humanitarian, development, and peace efforts to effectively reduce the humanitarian caseload is critical, as funding for humanitarian action is outstripped by growing needs. Better coordination across the Humanitarian Development Peace (HDP) nexus is therefore crucial to effectively reduce people’s needs, risks and vulnerabilities, supporting prevention efforts and thus, shifting from only delivering humanitarian assistance to contributing to ending need as well. In a country whereby focusing only on the delivery of material subsistence and interventions substituting local capacities is demonstrated to contribute to exacerbating the chronic vulnerabilities of the communities, dedicated efforts are needed to provide strategic coherence between humanitarian and developmental schemes. The nexus has the potential to make aid more effective and efficient. It also provides a good opportunity to work with all stakeholders towards a common goal. Coordination efforts are therefore a point of entry to achieve this synergy and alignment. The Food Security Sector (FSS) in Palestine, created in December 2012, brings together UN and non-UN organizations on an equal footing to improve the food security of the needy and vulnerable, by ensuring immediate direct access to food and protecting/restoring their livelihoods, while enhancing resilience. The FSS works toward building stronger partnerships to support sustainable, comprehensive, and locally owned food security interventions in Palestine. The FSS is composed of circa 45 members, leveraging the existing expertise of stakeholders, working as open fora that need to synergize efforts across three main pillars – food assistance, agriculture/livelihood, and cash-based programming. To enhance coordination and provide stronger analysis and response, the FSS is provided with a single coordination structure tailored to the specific protracted crisis in the WB and GS, that needs inter-linking humanitarian, and development interventions while working at the inter-cluster level to contribute to a holistic response.Through bottom-up-approach that ensures wide participation of the concerned stakeholders, the FSS as a multi-stakeholder platform is a cornerstone to inform joint analysis of underlying causes of food insecurity and provide needed services of assistance to the population. However, coordination between FSS actors is needed to facilitate their interventions are complementary and data analyses are generated to properly inform and guide interventions, to reduce vulnerability and maximize resilience building within the HRP. As such, currently, more than ever, it is crucial to ensure an adequate level of dedicated staffing to • Strengthen coordination and prioritization of humanitarian interventions, and facilitate linkages with developmental programs. • Support the gathering, production, and sharing of information required for effective planning and coordination for food security. • Improve cooperation with line ministries and institutions in charge of coordination at the central and decentralized levels to progressively absorb FSS coordination functions.

2 - Activities

1. Support strategic planning of FSS by coordinating a participatory and consultative platform to increase eﬃciency/effectiveness of FSS partner's assistance. • Humanitarian Programme Cycle: collect, analyze and plan for FSS component needs, gaps, and responses with the involvement of all FSS stakeholders • Inform the Humanitarian Pool Funds (HPF) and other ad-hoc funding opportunities. • Coordinate response to demolitions of agricultural assets and settlers’ violence towards farmers in the WB and other ad-hoc responses. • Contribute to inter-sectorial coordination at the central and governorate levels: Identification of needs and response modalities that may require an inter-cluster ad-hoc harmonized response. • Coordination with other international agencies & NGOs: additional collaboration will be elaborated with Health, Protection, WASH clusters as well as the Cash Working Group (CWG). • Set minimum acceptable standards of deliverables, streamline interventions, facilitate operational communication and coordination of inter-sector and intra-sector operations, and review technical specifications. The FSS will continue supporting thematic/ operational/ situational working groups that were established according to needs. 2. Support and provide generation of data, analysis and information for planning and coordination of FSS interventions and informing policy adaptation: • Engage FSS actors in sharing information on planning, priorities, and findings, and assessing gaps and duplications, to promote complementarity and joint programming using the 5Ws platform. • Identify priorities,possible gaps, or duplications for improved information and data sharing to inter-agency and government plans on food security analysis issues and operations, considering the dynamic scenario and impacts of COVID-19 and any other shocks • Monitor and provide technical support to assess the possible impact of COVID-19 and other shocks on the Palestinian agro-food sector to inform planning and operational responses. • Analyze needs related to increasing gender mainstreaming in food security programming. • Maintain/improve FSS components of the inter-cluster contingency plan in coordination with OCHA and line ministries. 3. Support strengthening national capacities to contribute to effective coordination of and advocacy for humanitarian interventions within an HDP nexus approach • Facilitate roundtable discussion among all partners for better understanding of nexus approach, as well as better engagement in related policy discussions at the national and global level. • Strengthen the capacity of NGOs, line ministries (central/decentralized) about practicing and leading coordination functions. • Ensuring greater dialogue on food security emergencies such as COVID-19, especially in the most vulnerable areas, among food, nutrition, and health officials, civil society, donors, partners, and other key stakeholders. • Support participation of the FSS partners in national efforts towards the achievement and monitoring of SDG2 (like in the activities of the SDG2 Working Group), also contributing to the process of developing FNS Governance institutional capacities, as needed. • Foster qualitative programming components like Protection and People-Centric issues also by delivering training and supporting learning opportunities for FSS partners. Supporting government-led coordination structures, LACS, and AIDA, PNGO, NGOs consortia, to facilitate the humanitarian-development nexus approach. •Incorporate the commitments on Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) into all relevant policies , operational guidelines and promote them among FSS partners. • Develop and/or support resource mobilization and advocacy strategies to strengthen relations with other clusters, UN agencies, governments, donors and enhance humanitarian interventions within an HDP nexus.

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

To ensure that the interests of the FSS partners are continuously addressed and in line with FSS approach and Humanitarian Accountability Framework, the FSS has elaborated a complaints mechanism that its members and relevant stakeholders are informed about the mechanism verbally regular basis and have easy access to. The feedback mechanism will -mainly- track the performance of the FSS coordination team in terms of 1) request for information, 2) request for assistance, and 3) the major satisfaction/dissatisfaction with the behavior/coordination of the FSS team. The regular and quick feedback will ensure the quality and transparency of services and gaps to enable FSS to respond accordingly. The feedback cycle will channel input into changes and innovations that improve the quality, relevance, and effectiveness of the project. The feedback mechanism will constantly make the intervention more rapid, effective, safe, and dignified, as well as strengthening and enhancement of the relations between FSS team and its members. On the other hand, FSS will invest in information management tools consider conducting a regular survey to determine how activities are viewed by partners.A Cluster Coordination Performance Monitoring (CCPM) is a self-assessment exercise. The FSS will monitor the performance and get feedback against the six core functions of the FSS and AAP . The CCPM is supported by (gFSC) and is being undertaken annually targeting all stakeholders, including UN agencies, national and international NGOs, national authorities, and representatives of crosscutting issues. The key findings of the CCPM will be discussed identifies mitigating factors and explanations of performance and agreeing on actions that could be taken to improve performance if necessary. Finally, Organizing a consultative process and meetings including all main stakeholders to agree on the broad areas that need to be explored, the specific questions that need to be answered, and how to proceed.

The FSS systematically involves persons of concern in participatory assessments. 10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

The Food Security Sector coordination system are designed to strengthen protection mainstreaming, by decreasing threats, reducing vulnerability, and enhancing the involvement of the affected people. FSS will work closely with partners to improve food security status at the household level, thereby reducing the use of negative and often-irreversible coping strategies, and, by also adopting Do No Harm approaches through informed decisions around distribution and implementation modalities in program design.Particularly vulnerable communities in Area C struggling to access basic services such as water and sanitation, and health, because of violations of IHL and IHRL, will be supported with livelihoods support interventions, income generation opportunities and direct access to food. FSS partners will support vulnerable households in urban communities, farmers and fisher folk in the Gaza Strip in response to the coercions and deprivations to which they are exposed.Working closely with the protection and advocacy focal point, the FSS team and partners will advocate on issues that drive food insecurity, such as lack of access to sustainable employment and income opportunities, settler violence against farmers in the West Bank, and the limitation of access and dual-use restrictions for importing materials into the Gaza Strip, in order to promote increased accountability of respective duty bearers and assist them in fulfilling their obligations.Through the gender focal point, the FSS team will encourage partners to contribute to gender equality and gender sensitive programming by prioritizing identification of and response to gender-based vulnerabilities, in order to support the eroded coping capacities of gender-sensitive groups like female-headed households.FSS will ensure all partners to build on the success and learning of their implemented food, cash, and livelihoods programming, in addition to its standard procedure of gender and protection mainstreaming. FSS team provides refresher trainings to all partners on protection mainstreaming in the FSS projects. FSS strongly supports partners regarding the COVID-19 mitigation measures during the design and implementation of the projects. FSS works together with the UN agencies and the Access Unit to ensure the compliance with the local authorities measures if movement restrictions were imposed due to the pandemic.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

By using proven assessment tools that include all categories of persons with special needs and allow objective identification of needs; The FSS in close coordination with the protection cluster will encourage partners to develop community-based strategies to ensure that the affected population are fully informed and consulted and can participate in decision-making related to the rehabilitation of disrupted livelihoods. The FSS will integrate the efforts to ensure access for all the affected population, including women, taking into account the often hidden role of certain groups such as older persons in the formal and informal economy.The FSS will make sure that partners are working in distribution activities will ensure the maximum participation of affected communities, in particular women, in the planning, design, and implementation, e.g. by organizing focus group discussions, and using community organizers to identify women representatives. Moreover, ensure providing women, as well as persons with special needs, with viable economic opportunities to protect them against trafficking, sexual exploitation, and abuse, or other abusive and dangerous sources of income. The FSS will work closely with partners to ensure that persons with disabilities have full access to employment and training opportunities without discrimination of any kind, and do not reinforce existing social or stereotypical gender divisions.
