Project Summary

Despite the Iraqi Security Forces retaking Islamic State of Iraq and the Levant (ISIS) by December 2017, Iraqis affected by the conflict remain vulnerable and exposed to numerous protection threats. As the humanitarian response transitions from emergency to early recovery, the protection response enters a critical phase. Displaced and conflict affected people face barriers accessing rights and the discrimination faced by families of individuals with alleged or perceived links to ISIS is increasing. The ability for displaced people to find their own durable solutions and harness their own resilience mechanisms is curtailed. In this climate, the IRC will implement a project that is targeted at breaking down some of these structural barriers by conducting multi-pronged protection interventions aimed at supporting vulnerable people and harnessing community based modalities. This project will focus on the provision of legal assistance to vulnerable people, particularly women and children, protection monitoring, awareness sessions, advocacy, capacity building authorities and community leaders, engaging with Community Representation Groups and providing psychosocial support.

Needs

In 2019, key protection concerns in Ninewa, Anbar, Kirkuk, and Salah al-Din are predicted to be related to displacement, including limited freedom of movement, forced evictions, camp consolidation and closures, barriers to returnee reintegration, secondary displacement, and arbitrary detention. In September 2018, 13% of the IDP population in Ninewa and 15% of the IDPs in Salah al Din were at risk of being evicted while 37% of the assessed population cited HLP related issues as a reason for not returning to areas of origin . In the last months of 2018, the process of camp consolidation and closure has become a government priority, and with this it is expected that protection concerns will flow from forced or premature returns and include the threats and insecurity in areas of origin and the potential for secondary displacement. A lack of civil documentation significantly contributes to the acute vulnerability of conflict affected communities as it represents a barrier to return. in Ninewa and Kirkuk about 11,5% of the IDPs outside the camps lack at least one form of civil documentation while in Anbar the percentage of IDPs inside the camps who lack access to Civil IDs has been raised to 9% of the targeted population . Individuals without documentation cannot receive the required approvals to return, nor can they travel to their areas of origin as they risk being stopped at security checkpoints, and potentially detained for alleged ISIS affiliation. This disproportionately affects women and children as the judicial process in Iraq is underpinned by patrilineal notions of nationality and custody, which is compounded by the high density of female headed households in displacement . Having legal documentation and access to legal assistance is key to long term resilience, reintegration and the ability for people to exercise their rights, because it can act as a gateway to many other services. For example, being able to secure employment will be a challenge without having official identity documents. This challenge is most acute amongst populations which saw heavy fighting and destruction. Not only does a lack of documentation create a barrier to effective reintegration, it can also lead to arbitrary detentions for extended periods of time, where people are routinely tortured to extract confessions of alleged ISIS affiliation. Human Rights Watch and humanitarian agencies working in detention sites report that many detainees, including children, are subject to abuse and torture. The IRC’s needs assessments conducted in 2018 found that in west Mosul 11% of respondents had a missing or detained member of their household. In Anbar, this rose to 18%. In Telafar, while returnees did not report that there were movement restrictions, the many check points in the town that require the correct identification to pass amount to de facto restrictions to the freedom of movement. Inter-communal tension and insecurity has also reportedly increased, which is further complicated by the process of displacement and return. New divisions have developed between returnee populations and those who remained (‘remainees’). Attacks are being carried out by community members against returnee families who are perceived as having ISIS affiliations, or those with ISIS-affiliated relatives. The IRC documented the killing of five returnees in western Anbar in October 2018 alone. Such incidents are common in areas of return and have led to a number of families becoming secondarily displaced due to fear of revenge attacks. Female headed households are at a high risk of being sexually exploited in such circumstances. The IRC teams have also received reports of community members exploiting allegations of ISIS affiliation in order to further tribal conflicts. These all highlight the need for strengthening community based protection mechanisms, and implementing programs that improve social cohesion.

Activities

The IRC’s teams of lawyers in Ninewa, Anbar, Salah al-Din, Kirkuk, Diyala and Baghdad will provide direct representation to vulnerable people who are in need of legal assistance to regain and assert their rights. Vulnerability criteria will form the basis for eligibility for legal assistance, and will include intersectional considerations such as displacement, gender, age, civil documentation status, minority status, and socio-economic situation, amongst other factors. This can include in-camp, out-of-camp, returnee and host populations. Targeting of beneficiaries will take into consideration gender roles and norms within the Iraqi context, particularly the access of women to social spaces. Taking this into consideration, legal awareness and one-on-one counselling sessions will take place in women’s centers in addition to other community spaces. Assistance will be aimed at reducing vulnerability and will include a wide range of cases including civil documentation, family law matters, detention, access to the Social Safety Net including PDS, Ministry of Displacement and Migration (MoDM) and Ministry of Labor and Social Affairs (MoLSA) assistance, if available) and Housing, Land and Property matters. In addition to operating from static sites, the IRC will continue to operate mobile legal clinics which will target newly retaken areas that are hard to reach, where consistent access and lack of infrastructure remain a challenge. The IRC’s team of Protection Officers will implement a number of monitoring and response activities to better support vulnerable populations and increase the protective environment as a whole in all targeted governorates; Ninewa, Anbar, Salah al-Din, Kirkuk, Diyala and Baghdad. The Protection Officers will conduct protection monitoring in the target areas, which includes referrals and engaging in local level advocacy – leveraging well established relationships with local authorities – to intervene on behalf of vulnerable community members. These activities will be complemented by conducting protection mainstreaming trainings to strengthen the capacity of local authorities to respond to needs within their communities. These activities will be conducted where IRC has a static presence, and can also form part of the IRC mobile legal clinic to conduct outreach activities in hard to reach areas. Under this project, the IRC will seek to form and engage with Community Representation Groups (CRGs) in each of the target governorates, which bring different members of the community together to identify needs in their locations and advocate for support from local authorities. After conducting a thorough review of the key areas within the governorate, the team will identify a target area where such an intervention can be most effective. It is expected that the teams will form CRGs, in the most strategic location, where the impact can be the greatest. After identifying the location, the IRC will go through each of the stages of formation, and then conduct the Leadership and Representation Training and Protection Core Concepts training which includes the identification and safe referral of protection cases to the IRC and other service providers. Ongoing capacity strengthening of the CRG will focus on short message service training, community mobilization, ToT training, conflict mitigation, mediation and de-escalation of tensions. Psychosocial support (PSS) group sessions will be conducted by PSS case workers in Ninewa, Salah al-Din, Kirkuk and Anbar and will consist of weekly group sessions of between eight to fifteen participants, over an eight to ten week period. The IRC will target conflict affected people who are in need of PSS, particularly people with disabilities and their caregivers, and adolescent boys with signs of trauma related behavioral changes. These sessions, and the development of concepts from week to week, are crafted to enable the participants to understand the link between trauma and behavioral change, enhance positive coping strategies, reduce social isolation and dislocation from cultural life, and by reducing their feelings of distress, improve their capacity to reengage in social behaviors and in turn improve social cohesion. At the end of each round of sessions, the participants will provide feedback in a short session conducted by the PSS Team Leader or Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) Advisor where they can be involved in revision, improvement and adaptation of the curriculum in order to ensure ongoing accountability to beneficiaries.

Protection Risk Analysis

1. Interventions, particularly community based interventions, may exacerbate pre-existing tensions and cause harm to the intended beneficiaries2. Protection monitoring expose beneficiaries to harm or targeting for providing negative information about the government/security services 3. By working to provide civil documentation to beneficiaries in camps, those who cannot obtain security clearance become more evident and stigmatized 4. Camp consolidation and closure becomes accelerated, forced evictions and returns continue, and severe movement restrictions are maintained, leading to camp populations being in de-facto detention sites 5. Detention lawyers become targeted by security services and accused of ISIS affiliation 6. Staff become targeted and accused of providing assistance to families of ISIS affiliated people

Mitigation Measures

1. Comprehensive actor mapping and conflict analysis will be conducted before determining where to establish CRGs and engage in other community based activities2. Confidentiality is maintained and conflict sensitive programming is conducted to ensure that all beneficiaries are not exposed to harm 3. Civil documentation work is supplemented by strident advocacy efforts to highlight durable solutions, individual criminal responsibility and the need to return to civil governance4. Organisational policies and procedures, particularly on programmatic red-lines, are adhered to in order to ensure programming in line with humanitarian principles 5. Clear operating procedures and eligibility criteria, and IRC engagement with the authorities and judicial officers, in order to ensure that detention lawyers work within the space available to work on high risk cases. 6. Organisational procedures on engaging with families of people who are perceived to be ISIS affiliated as disseminated and enforced.

How is your project accountable to affected population

The affected population has been involved in program design through consultation during needs assessments, and feedback provided from field teams who interact with clients on a daily basis and conduct protection monitoring to understand needs and protection concerns. Regular consultation with the community and community leaders throughout implementation will ensure that program information is disseminated, as well as feedback and complaint mechanisms. Regular satisfaction surveys/monitoring and evaluation activities of key outcome indicators will ensure that beneficiary feedback is incorporated into program implementation, allowing for course correction. Through the technical oversight provided by the Protection Coordinator, with support from the MHPSS Advisor, beneficiary feedback will be incorporated into program implementation and M&E by regular reviewing the strategic direction of the program, including the theory of change, and making relevant course corrections. Responding to critical or sensitive protection issues that arise, in a confidential manner, will be guaranteed through effective training of staff on identifying such issues and escalating them when necessary in order to ensure response. By regularly conducting key informant interviews and focus group discussions with beneficiaries, within the targeted areas, and in an environment where confidentiality can be ensured, the IRC is able to ensure that feedback mechanisms are safe and accessible. Based on the attendance of these sessions, the teams can assess whether this is the preferred mechanism for feedback, and if this is not the case, following further consultations, can amend the mechanism as needed. This may be by altering the location, time of day, the demographics of the FGDs.
