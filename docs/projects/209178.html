Project Summary

Sudan is faced with one of the worst humanitarian disasters in human history, with multiple protection issues driven by disease, epidemic and violation of human rights by state and non-state actors. Children and women are the most affected. OCHA estimates that 4.85 million people are internally displaced in Sudan. The country has the highest number of child displacements, with a total of 3.2 million children living in IDP camps and host communities. Children have not been spared from grave child rights violations. At least 79 per cent of grave child rights violations verified since the conflict began involved the killing and maiming of children, while 8 per cent of grave child rights violations verified since the conflict began involved sexual violence against children. This is recorded from accessible communities, and the overall situation could be worse than what is reported. A breakdown in social service delivery due to unpaid social workers and government officials has left vulnerable children, especially unaccompanied and separated children, exposed to abuse and exploitation. Lack of livelihoods and the weathering away of the traditional social support network triggered recourse to negative coping mechanisms, deepening the suffering faced by children. UNICEF and its partners aim to stop the suffering of 368,000 children by providing child protection services and preventing recourse to negative coping mechanisms. The deteriorating mental health and psychosocial support needs will be met through structured and semi-structured psychosocial support interventions in fixed and mobile safe spaces. Identification of unaccompanied and separated children and provision of alternative care such as placement in foster care, group homes and family reunification will be the core of the programme. Risks of killing and maiming by remnants of war and unexploded ordinances will be reduced by delivering child-friendly messages that build children's and caregivers' knowledge of prevention techniques. Child survivors of rape and sexual violence will receive medical care, livelihood support, safe housing, and mental health support through direct support and referral to specialised services. To ensure accountability to affected communities, reporting mechanisms will be set up in the communities. Communities will receive PSEA training on reporting to ensure an end to the misuse of power by humanitarian workers.

3 - Protection mainstreaming
3.1 - Explain how your project aims to ensure protection mainstreaming during its design and implementation

Protection mainstreaming principles are at the core of our child protection response. The project will prioritise safety and dignity, while at the same time avoiding causing harm to children and their communities. This will be achieved through consultation with communities at each stage of programme implementation. Displaced communities in targeted locations will be provided with a user friendly feed back mechanism that managed by trained staff. The fundamental principles of protection mainstreaming of active participation and community empowerment and ownership will hence be achieved.

4.1.1 - How will the project use community feedback to improve and adapt activities? (open-ended/free text, required)4

On receipt of feedback from the community or an individual, a confidential meeting will be held with the individual/community members to develop a response strategy and ensure improvements are made and new initiatives are take that mitigate risks on beneficiaries.

4.2 - Please outline how the project or your agency will support Inter-Agency approaches to accountability to affected people and feedback mechanisms, including Sexual exploitation and abuse complaints?

UNICEF is a member of the IASC and ensures AAC and PSEA are core components of its programmes. UNICEF will set up a reporting mechanism in the communities (where this is not available) , train beneficiaries on reporting , and ensure mandatory training for all our staff, consultants and services providers
