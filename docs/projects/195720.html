Project Summary

Yet again, 2022 has been a year of continued suffering to the people of South Sudan, with reports of the humanitarian situation deteriorating since independence signing of the revitalized peace agreement. The situation has been worsened by endemic violence, conflict, access constraints and operational interference, public health challenges such as direct and indirect effects of COVID-19 and climatic shocks resulting in the dual phenomena of extraordinary flooding and localized drought, which have a severe impact on people’s livelihoods, hampers access to education and water, sanitation and hygiene and health services. The population for the people in need for humanitarian assistance in 2022 is estimated at more than two-thirds of South Sudan's population, 8.9 million people, being in need of humanitarian assistance, an increase of 600,000 since 2021. Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) prevalence above the emergency threshold of 15 per cent was reported in people in twenty-six counties in six states (Jonglei, Unity, Warrap, Upper Nile, Northern Bahr el Ghazal and Eastern Equatoria). The needs projection for 2022 estimated 8.3 M people to experience severe food insecurity at the peak of the lean season, a representative increase of up to 7% from 2021. The situation was noted to have been exacerbated by the increasing humanitarian caseload and economic crises. The ongoing conflict along the Upper Nile-Jonglei border has resulted in an increasing number of armed attacks, causing large-scale displacement and disruptions to food access, and has impacted negatively on the main season crop production, livelihood activities, and market and trade flows. The level of food prices has risen, resulting in an increase of 51% in the cost of food basket in Malakal, from February to October 2022. River transportation. Fashoda County, for this matter has alarming figures for the IPC projections form October 2022 to Jul, 2023, with high figures of up to 31,000 and 27,000 in Crises and Emergency for the last projection of Apr-July, 2023. National Relief and Development Corps identified a great need to upscale its intervention in the County as its current Multispectral Project in the County cant absorb the consciously increasing caseloads following recent displacements in October caused by conflict in the areas of South of the County, including flooding that affected peoples livelihoods. Within this context, National Relief and Development Corps with support from the South Sudan Humanitarian Response Fund will provide some hybrid support to affected population through distribution Agricultural and fishery kits, restricted conditional cash and market support. This project will target 44,753 individuals in Fashoda and Manyo Counties projected to be in Crises and Emergency phases in as indicated in the South Sudan 2022 HNO. The emergency livelihood support will be guided by the Food Security and Livelihood Cluster Objectives: Cluster Objective 1: Provide food assistance to prevent famine and improve dietary diversity and coping strategies for vulnerable people facing IPC-Phase 5, 4 and some 3, Cluster Objective 2: Enhance and sustain emergency food production through complementary vegetable and crop livelihood inputs and fishing and livestock support in IPC 3, 4 and 5 and Cluster Objective 3: Reduce dependency on food and agricultural inputs to support and strengthen households’ ability to absorb shocks across 78 counties. With the vast experience of over 10 years food security and cash programming in Fashoda County, the level of capacity and synergy available is adequate to implement the proposed plan to effectively meet it intended goals.

1 - Needs

The Humanitarian Needs Overview for 2022 documents that people in South Sudan continue to face deteriorating humanitarian conditions. Their situation is worsened by endemic violence, conflict, access constraints and operational interference, public health challenges such as direct and indirect effects of COVID-19 and climatic shocks resulting in the dual phenomena of extraordinary flooding and localized drought, which have a severe impact on people’s livelihoods, hampers access to education and water, sanitation and hygiene and health services. Protection concerns remain high, with people impacted by violence having limited access to justice and the rule of law.According to the recent IPC food insecurity remains high with 62.7% of the population (7.76 million people) facing Crisis. According to the intersectoral severity of needs analysis, humanitarian needs are most concerning in Fangak, Duk and Pibor counties in Jonglei and Cueibet and Rumbek East counties in Lakes, which were classified as in catastrophic need. People in 71 counties face extreme needs, while two are in severe need.The primary challenge driving needs is food insecurity or lack of food, which weakens people’s health and nutritional status and exposes them to greater protection risks. The IPC projections for Dec, 2022 to July 2023 indicate that 6.31 M and 7.76 M, representing 51.0% and 62.7% percent of the population in South Sudan as people who will be facing severe acute food insecurity (IPC Phase 3+) for the analyses periods of Dec, 2022 to Mar, 2022 and Apr, 2023 to Jul, 2023 respectively. The food insecurity levels remain elevated due to the impacts of the economic crisis, flooding and dry spells, low agricultural production, conflict and insecurity. This analyses present a dire need of support to Fashoda County as being among those projected to be crises and emergency counties. The planned support with this project is intended to save lives, improve people’s livelihoods and enhance their capacity.While a market-based response cannot cover all needs, then it presents an important, innovative complement to more traditional responses during a time where humanitarian actors are still struggling with dwindling resources and the transition from unsustainable NFI and food distributions to responses that recognises the protracted and cyclical nature of crisis in South Sudan. The project locations have all been chosen based on an overlap of critical needs (IPC Level 3-4, conflict, displacement/returnees), functioning or recovering market structures and presence of DCA and NRDC. Do No Harm and conflict sensitivity approaches will be applied to the maximum extent seeking to negative effects of the project, decrease community tensions and capitalize on opportunities to support protection and positive community relations. In this regard, local partners are all running protection programs in the targeted areas, which will help informing targeting of the most vulnerable and link this with other protection responses. Risk related to inflation and price increases will be mitigated by price monitoring and adjustments to the project design if a) markets cannot provide needed commodities or b) price inflation makes cash distribution less cost-effective than other response modalities, such as in-kind or voucher. As long as markets can supply people’s basic needs in a safe and affordable manner, we will work through an unconditional cash modality. Through regular monitoring and presence in the communities, we will shift to a voucher system if there are safety issues, items are not available in the market or prices become too high compared to the cash grant. 5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

The project has been designed in such a way as to stimulate and promote market functionality. We understand that markets contribute to both food security and livelihoods and are critical features for community’s social-economic survival and progress – even in humanitarian settings! Humanitarian actors now generally agree on the critical role of markets in humanitarian interventions! Planned activities such as ensuring market system actors become involved in emergency response, functionality of savings & loans groups, household-oriented market access interventions, cash-voucher transfers, training & capacity building including for on-farm production (farmer/agro-pastoral field schools, demonstration plots), income generation (including business skills), issuance of small grants for petty trade…are all geared towards market functionality! To the extent possible, the project encourages and supports both local hire of staff as well as local procurement (including for some of the farm inputs). NRDC shall remain vigilant about market dynamics and shall participate in planned partner assessments and market analysis – and where necessary, spearhead such exercise! The rationale is such that, to effectively assist the affected populations, a good understanding of critical market systems is important. Indeed, Practical Action (‘The Emergency Market Mapping and Analysis Toolkit’) has cautioned that, “lack of this market analysis in humanitarian programmes may be damaging the livelihoods, jobs and businesses upon which people’s long term security depends.” The design of the project is such that it stimulates market functionality by ensuring affected target groups access food, essential household items, fuel and other types of relief or services; in addition, beneficiaries may be able to access additional and urgently required agricultural inputs and services…..and afford them opportunities for wage labour, or link them to buyers for their produce. The project design is envisaged to result in better efficient use of humanitarian resources, curtail dependency on outside assistance (including for agricultural inputs) and stimulate transition to economic recovery, especially in the post COVID-19 pandemic dispensation. The project takes cognizance of the gender and power dynamics and addresses these in the selection of target groups; as we have alluded to elsewhere, NRDC shall continue to be part of local peace and security initiatives as stability and communal co-existence is critical for market functionality, growth and sustainability. Through other partner-supported, and as part of the market support options, NRDC shall carry out public works projects to clear debris and waste (also through WASH interventions!) using cash-for-work mechanism. Finally, NRDC shall be at the forefront in facilitating links between farmers on the one hand, and resources and service providers on the other (e.g. seed traders, agro-input dealers, transporters, veterinarians, business loan providers….). Last but not least, the various cadre of trainings shall also focus on capacity-strengthening for sustainable market functionality…

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

It is instructive to point out that over last couple of years, NRDC has gained significant insights and capacity in cash-voucher programming through strategic partnerships with established entities like Dan Church Aid (DCA)… The tradition in humanitarian response, until in the recent past, has been through in-kind support. The use of cash or vouchers is a relatively new, but increasingly growing, phenomenon that is said to have numerous advantages, not least convenience and affording choice to would-be beneficiaries! The use of cash or vouchers in lieu of in-kind assistance now appears to be standard practice among humanitarian actors, and the practice is now popular in food assistance programming, and is increasingly becoming a popular choice in emergency shelter, education, and several humanitarian response options and initiatives! Proponents of Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA) argue that offering beneficiary recipients cash affords them an opportunity to use cash in a manner that they deem fit and is appropriate in meeting their range of needs, that the use of cash and or vouchers enables to stimulate markets and provides the beneficiaries with an assistance that is accessible via a local market system but restricts to a certain extent what recipients can buy or where they can make their purchases. One of the chief benefits of cash usage in the present COVID-19 dispensation is that it can be done through a telecommunications provider - i.e. some money transfer platform - and, therefore, not only help to overcome transport and supplies storage logistical constraints and be hastened/done in a timely manner, but also be compliant with COVID-19 protocols, as it can be cash-less! The usage of cash and or vouchers is particularly feasible and appropriate where markets can be stimulated and are functional. Cash transfer programming enables households to take care of their priority needs, is cost-effective, affords beneficiaries their dignity, supports livelihoods, strengths local markets and promotes financial inclusion. However, there may also be some associated negative impacts such as intra-household conflict (for example, brought about by competing needs and priorities, can result in communal tensions and may undermine local markets, but all these can be mitigated through appropriate programme design (For example, ensuring that programming is based upon reasonable price forecasts, ideally using all-season time-series data, instead of prices on the day the calculation was done. Cash transfer-voucher usage can become more effective and impactful if deliberate action is taken to ensure security, guard against corruption/diversion, and mitigate potential delays in providing transfers and market failures. Cash and voucher usage can be done in a manner as to ensure equity and gender equality, ensuring female beneficiaries as well as all those with constraints related to gender, age and other factors that might affect access to assistance become effectively addressed.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

NRDC remains committed to accountability to affected populations and designs responses in a way as to ensure information provision to beneficiaries, as well as ensuring appropriate feedback/ complaint mechanisms. In so doing, we work closely with local structures, for example, fisher-folk and farmers groups, project Management committees (PMCs), women and youth associations and indeed, the local leadership, community volunteers…...NRDC deploys a number of approaches in ensuring community consultation, engagement and information provision, for example, i) Ensuring initial consultation with community members, that is, giving people a say, and getting them involved prior to the of planning a project. This happens in many ways including face-to-face conversations and engagement with their representatives; ii) On‐going engagement through-out the implementation process, indeed ensuring that beneficiaries also become actively involved in activity implementation; iii) Keeping people informed and up to date with activities and achievements through various means, including community meetings. NRDC has the programming practice of assembling project-specific feedback/complaints mechanisms and also deploys the following methods to consult and engage with target communities:-  Local media campaigns – especially local radio such as Miraya FM…  Use of leaflets, flyers, posters….sometime with translated versions  Customizing the organization website to make it an effective medium of information provision and community engagement;  Use of Organization and project-specific Newsletters as well as email updates  Deployment of surveys or questionnaires (now can use social media owing to COVID-19)  Conducting competitions for project ideas / designs, soliciting and providing information at the same time  Holding of Seminars / conferences / workshops (with the project-entry and closure Stakeholder Workshops become popular with beneficiaries…  Public meetings/forums/ Space meetings…  Use of Case Studies to disseminate vital project information, lessons and profiling key feedback /complaints encountered during implementation  Use of beneficiary Comment Cards (e.g. ‘I wish this was’ ) deposited at vantage points like local authority offices, distribution points, training workshops….  Use of Focus Groups Discussion (FGDs) Forums/ User Panels  Scenario planning / community visioning (e.g. ‘Future Search’)…  Community led planning often using community facilitators. NRDC also deploys SMS messages, mobile voice calls and individual one-to-one outreach with beneficiaries and also ensures consultations at the start of the Project, and making sure that we are open and genuinely consider people’s needs and priorities.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

NRDC has the requisite PSEA Policy, complemented with a Gender Policy and a Staff Code of Conduct as a well as an established, fully fledged Protection Department. NRDC develops and or adapts context-specific PSEA outreach and communication materials targeting communities during project implementation, such as the following:  Awareness-raising materials (including print materials) that explain core concepts, such as what can constitute “sexual exploitation and abuse’; the obligations of all actors to prevent and address such acts; protect witnesses, victims and survivors and clarify that protection, assistance and access to solutions are never conditional on sexual favours or relationships;  Materials (including print, video or audio) that share information on where to safely report and seek help, in line with established complaints and feedback mechanisms (CFMs) and referral pathways;  Community engagement tools, such as outlines and facilitators guides to lead community dialogues, role plays or focus group discussions that increase awareness on SEA risks, and the rights of communities and people affected by crises to receive protection and assistance free from sexual exploitation and abuse; and  Other appropriate PSEA information, education and communication (IEC) materials, tools and approaches, as relevant to the local operational and cultural context. NRDC also ensures that activities are planned to ensure that the products and approaches developed are suitable for communicating with women, men, girls, and boys of diverse backgrounds, through means that are appropriate and accessible to all groups in a community. NRDC emphasizes some specific standards of behaviour - and at all times - amongst the organization’s staff, volunteers, community committee members (such as Project Committee Members..), consultants, service providers that NRDC deals with, interns…..; this includes both within working hours as well as outside of standard working hours. NRDC abhors sexually exploitative relationships on account of humanitarian assistance and vigorously disseminates information on the same! It is also the case that NRDC makes it clear to all and sundry (during meetings, staff dispatches /memos, public gatherings….) that any violation of the set standards constitutes gross misconduct. Failure to adhere to these standards or failure to report sexual exploitation or abuse is enough reasons for administrative disciplinary action! As standard practice, NRDC also ensures that PSEA materials developed under the various humanitarian responses become “open source” and publicly available for the benefit of other humanitarian actors and communities (for example, through depositing at various strategic locations like food distribution centres, stakeholder workshops and training venues..). We are also well alive to the fact that the project acknowledges that different groups (the disabled, IDPs, returnees, the chronically ill….) within the same community will have different communication needs and capacities. It is important to point out that for purposes of ensuring adherence, NRDC has established a PSEA Policy Steering Committee (under the stewardship of the Protection Program Manager) charged with monitoring and regularly reporting on progress to implement and observe the PSEA Policy; the report becomes available to NRDC’s Board of Directors and through the Executive Director! In summary, NRDC envisages fulfilling its commitment to protection from sexual exploitation and abuse through some strategic approaches as follows: Awareness, Prevention, Reporting, Response and Promoting a culture of openness, sensitivity and support.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

NRDC very well understands and appreciates that Feedback mechanisms are valued by beneficiaries as avenues through which they can exercise voice, and indeed, influence the quality of responses! For this reason, therefore, and as standard practice for NRDC, we endeavor to the extent possible to consult beneficiaries at the start of the project design period…including through/during needs assessments, stakeholder workshops and consultative meetings with their representatives including local authorities. For this reason, therefore, and following from this exercise, we ensure the original project design as envisaged is adapted based on the initial feedback. Such consultations and feedback adaptation practices ensure that projects then address need that are relevant for the beneficiaries, for stakeholders and for the context! The Project Steering Committee (PSC), the feedback and complaints mechanisms, community volunteers…..shall all be important structures for collating beneficiaries’ feedback, documenting the same and ensuring it is shared with the Project Team for purposes of adapting project assistance. Through the regular reports filed during the implementation process (weekly, monthly, quarterly….), we shall discuss and agree on modalities for adaptation of beneficiaries feedback. NRDC also often uses Case Studies as part of the reporting process and will, in doing so, also track vital beneficiaries feedback that shall then be ‘ploughed back’ in the continuing implementation process as a means to improve on accountability and quality of the response. Owing to the COVID-19 pandemic, we shall endeavor to be a little creative and will deploy technology, including platforms like social media, to gather feedback and adapt the same for project assistance. NRDC shall continue to deploy established practices and standards, including for example, the SPHERE standards in ensuring beneficiaries’ feedback become adapted to project assistance. By capturing beneficiaries’ feedback through an ongoing process, NRDC shall promptly respond to emerging beneficiaries’ issues in real time and act upon it before the project ends. Due to the fragile and dynamic humanitarian context in which the response happens, there may be some instances that may require us to fine-tune or make bigger changes to the project mid-stream. Some examples of these instances include:- i) Changes in types of interventions for the context we operate in; ii) Changes in the quality of the services provided; iii) Changes related to the needs of beneficiaries that were not previously identified. It is important to mention that (also in line with counsel from the HNO 2021), “…....the new reality of COVID-19, risk communication and community engagement requires continuous efforts to ensure a two-way dialogue with refugee and host communities and inclusion of COVID-19 messaging through various platforms and feedback mechanisms, including rumour tracking mechanisms and addressing misinformation.”
