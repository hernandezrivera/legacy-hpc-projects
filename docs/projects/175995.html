Project Summary

The proposed programme aims to provide protection from malaria and other vector-borne diseases (VBDs) to the highly vulnerable Internally Displaced People (IDPs) population in the Rubkona/Bentiu Protection of Civilians (PoC) site and the host, IDP and returnee community in Renk. To achieve this, The MENTOR Initiative will focus on preventing disease-transmitting vectors from multiplying and entering the malaria and other VBD transmission cycles, which cause a great deal of morbidity and mortality. Indoor Residual Spraying (IRS), mosquito larviciding and fly control measures will be employed as tools to disrupt disease transmission. In addition, MENTOR will adopt an integrated approach and the target population will also be educated on the diseases itself, their signs and symptoms, and how to prevent infection, as well as on the impact of water and sanitation on disease transmission, (mal-) nutrition and associated disease severity. Owing to the still fragile nature of the COVID-19 pandemic, respective awareness messages will also be disseminated. All of this will be achieved through delivering targeted IEC/BCC campaigns and health education sessions. MENTOR will incorporate messages in the IEC/BCC campaign in order to ensure consistent use, care and maintenance of LLINs through IEC/BCC in the target locations after distribution by NMCP and other implementing partners. Lastly, health facilities in the target locations will be supported by offering comprehensive trainings on malaria and other VBDs differential diagnosis and case management together with Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) of Covid-19 for nursing and medical staff to improve the quality of health service delivery.
MENTOR is an HRP member and the mentioned activities were planned and implemented in SSHF Round 1 and 2 in Bentiu PoC in 2019 and Round 1 in Ulang and Renk in 2020-21 with significant results achieved.

1 - Needs

Malaria is endemic in all parts of South Sudan with the entire population at risk of infection. The country experiences year-round transmission with peaks towards the end of the rainy season from July to November. In Epidemiological Week 52 of 2020, Malaria accounted for 53 % of all morbidity and 12.7 % of all mortality in South Sudan (1). The security situation at national level has improved but inter-communal clashes are still reported at various location at sub-national level. The insecurity together with two years of consecutive flooding increased the number of IDPs, leaving them vulnerable to a variety of diseases and severe food insecurity. Currently 6.3 million people in South Sudan are facing high levels of acute food insecurity, which has been projected to increase to 7.2 million by July 2021 (2). The same report has placed 5 counties in IPC 5 (catastrophe) and 1 in IPC 5 (famine) (2). Rubkona has been placed in IPC 4 phase, whereas Renk comes under IPC phase 3. Chronic malnutrition has consistently been associated with severity of malaria (3) which doesn’t bode well for health of the people living in high-transmission areas in South Sudan. Malaria cases increased in Rubkona from 88,300 in 2019 to 154,688 in 2020 while in Renk it increased from 18,871 to 39,112 in the same time period (4). Renk county experienced flooding last year and is currently hosting IDPs, returnees and is a crossing point for refugees traveling between South Sudan and Sudan. The squalid and cramped conditions condition in Bentiu PoC make the population of the camp vulnerable to various diseases. Malaria in Bentiu PoC steadily decreased from 188,751 in 2015 to 48,332 in 2019 due to the timely implementation of Integrated Vector Control interventions by MENTOR. However, in the absence of those interventions, malaria cases almost doubled in just one year. The capacity to screen, diagnose and then treat malaria cases were stretched to the point of failure and dramatically increased the patients’ risk of developing severe forms of the disease that would otherwise be easily prevented with a correct diagnosis and immediate treatment. IDPs camp setting also face the threat of water and faecal-borne diseases due to poor sanitary conditions. There is a significant burden of diarrhoeal disease in South Sudan, and this is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality, especially among young children (5). The causative organisms are both water-borne and vector-borne and in poor camp sanitation, open defecation sites and temporary shelter living conditions contribute to the high caseload (6). Rubkona and Renk recorded a combined 35,000 cases of AWD cases in 2020. The MENTOR Initiative is currently implementing malaria and other VBDs prevention and control activities in refugee camps in Maban and Jamjang and the host communities and IDPs/Returnees in Ulang and Renk. MENTOR remained operational in Bentiu PoC from 2015 to 2019 and briefly in 2020 to tackle a malaria situation in the PoC that had spiralled out of control. (1) World Health Organization (2020). 2 - Project activities

The MENTOR Initiative proposes to respond to the HRP call Objective 1 Reduce excess morbidity and mortality of epidemic prone diseases. Indoor Residual Spraying:The control of adult mosquito disease vectors through IRS is key in reducing the risk of malaria infection. MENTOR plans to use the newly WHO-prequalified insecticide Fludora® Fusion. Local community workers will be recruited and trained in implementation of IRS by following WHO guidelines. To ensure an optimal level of protection in line with WHO recommendations, MENTOR will aim to achieve at least 85% coverage of shelters prior to the onset of the rainy season. Impact of the IRS intervention will be measured through weekly epidemiological assessments of the malaria incidence rate as compared to the corresponding week in 2020. Empty sachets/bottles of insecticides will be disposed off properly. Larviciding for mosquito control:Complimenting IRS, reducing the vector breeding capacity through Larval Source Management (LSM) ensures that the disease transmission capacity of Anopheles, Aedes as well as Culex mosquitoes is limited through preventing larval development. The WHO-recommended larvicide Abate® 500E will be applied to all stagnant water containing mosquito larvae. The prevention field staff will routinely assess all potential breeding sites and 90-95% will be treated in 3 rounds of activity. Fly Control:Flies can act as mechanical vectors for a multitude of diseases. The WHO-recommended safe chemical Pyriproxyfen will be used for the application in latrines, rubbish piles and open defecation sites. The prevention field staff will routinely assess all potential breeding sites across the camps and target host communities and 90-95% will be treated in 3 rounds of activity. Staff will also work closely with the WASH cluster and WASH partners to coordinate the timing. Capacity building for health care workers:The capacity of health care workers will be improved through trainings of health care workers in health facilities. They will be trained on the pathology, symptoms, correct differential diagnosis, and case management of VBDs that contribute to morbidity and mortality amongst the target population. On the job training will be provided to serve as follow-up supervision visits. A module on COVID-19 IPC will also be taught. Information, Education and Communication:This will be implemented through door-to-door IEC campaigns as well as health education sessions for community leaders. Integrated IEC will focus on delivering a variety of health messages, such as the promotion of bed net usage, signs and symptoms of VBDs and promotion of early treatment seeking behaviours, prevention of mosquito breeding sites, good waste management, good hygiene and sanitation practices to reinforce the relationship between WASH and VBD control, and the importance of adhering to COVID-19 preventative measures. Disease surveillance and outbreak response:MENTOR regularly collects data on the incidence of several vector-borne and epidemic-prone diseases from health partner facilities and as a result can report on the frequency and accuracy of data submissions and possible disease outbreaks, of which partners will be informed so that appropriate counter measures can be implemented. The proposed activities are most effective when whole populations are covered regardless of the target population set in the HRP log frame as all the prevention activities are implemented on a community level. 7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

All MENTOR staff are enrolled in Preventing Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) training and MENTOR will enrol all newly recruited staff in PSEA training as well. MENTOR will make sure to analyse any Gender Based Violence (GBV)-related risks and work with the responsible NGOs, INGOs, governmental bodies to address these risks, and report and refer potential GBV cases to the related stakeholders. Referral to specialized services is essential for cases which are identified through protection monitoring, at the community centres or through other forms of outreach activities. It is also an integral part of case management and is therefore critical despite potential limited availability of services. It is essential that referral pathways and service/s mapping information are regularly updated at local level to facilitate referrals and related activities. MENTOR will also ensure that all beneficiaries are aware that all MENTOR staff have been trained appropriately and that they can be approached at any time with any protection issues. Staff will then refer them to specialised services.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

During IEC/BCC and IRS campaigns MENTOR teams visit community members door-to-door and have the chance of directly meeting and engaging with individuals living with disabilities, illiteracy, as well as old people and women and girls. MENTOR staff also encourages people who are left behind to attend in health education sessions. This ensures that every beneficiary has the chance of voicing concerns and giving feedback as well as suggestions without having to travel or overcome other barriers.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

MENTOR has its own Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) policy that is guided by WHO, the Inter Agency Standing Committee (IASC), and the Sphere humanitarian standards. The essence of MENTOR’s AAP document is that the organisation is committed to: 1. Taking Account – through participation (enabling affected populations to play an active role in the decision-making processes) feedback and complaints (actively seeking the views of affected populations to improve policy and practice in programming) and design, monitoring and evaluation (Design, monitor and evaluate the goals and objectives of programmes with the involvement of affected populations) 2. Giving Account – through transparency (providing accessible and timely information to affected populations on organizational procedures, structures and processes to ensure that they can make informed decisions and choices) 3. Being Held to Account – through leadership and governance (demonstrating their commitment to accountability to affected populations by ensuring feedback). At the beginning of each program, introductory meetings are held with all the beneficiaries. In such meetings, extensive project activities details are shared, which include the project goal, objectives, name of staff, the duration, activities, office addresses, as well as the name and contact details of the program manager and other relevant individual. MENTOR also actively engages with the camp management feedback and reporting structures that enable the target communities to make complaints and / or suggestions without having to meet with MENTOR staff directly. This contributes to ensuring an environment in which target communities feel able to give feedback without any fear of a negative reaction/consequences.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The nature of the MENTOR activities is community-based. Indoor Residual Spraying, larviciding, fly control and health education sessions are carried out at the community level. Capacity building of health staff and medical commodities are provided to health facilities run by different health partners. Beneficiary feedback is key in all of this and continuously collected throughout the programme. As mentioned before, MENTOR engages with the camp management feedback and reporting structures that enable the target communities to make complaints and / or suggestions without having to meet with MENTOR staff directly. All of MENTOR’s staff are also trained in being focal points for feedback and can be approached at any time during the programme implementation. Any feedback is then immediately fed back into the programme management cycle and used to continuously improve service delivery and impact.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

8.1 Likelihood: Medium Impact: Medium MENTOR’s activities are carried out, wherever possible, directly in the communities, exposing beneficiaries to as few risks as possible. Before the start of IRS, Larviciding and Fly control activities, the community workers will be selected from the target communities and then trained on implementation of these activities The training of spray teams will be based on the latest WHO operational manual for IRS for malaria transmission, control and elimination (2015). The training will entail the safe handling of the WHO approved public health insecticides, the correct use of protective clothing and equipment (PPE), the maintenance of equipment, the correct application of the insecticide, waste management, as well as health education topics related to IRS such as the removal of house items before spraying and precautions after the shelter has been sprayed. The training will be conducted for two days just before launching these activities. Each worker will be provided with a standard set of PPE including an over all, gum boots, face visors, base cap, face and cloth wash soap, rubber gloves and face masks. Participants will also be taught about the Covid-19 risk communication strategy which emphasizes the importance of maintaining physical distance of 2 meters between individual, avoidance of crowded place, wearing of mask and frequent hand washing with clean water and soap. 12.1.4. Quality, timely and inclusive protection services (including medical care, legal support, safety and mental health psychosocial services to children, women at risk and GBV survivors

The programme will not provide protection services by design, but has an inherent component of delivering services in such a way that they benefit protection. Improved medical services lead to better health of all vulnerable groups, decreasing the likelihood of excessive out-of-pocket spending for medical care, temporary or long-lasting unemployment and stigmatization. The availability of improved services can also lead to shorter ways when seeking healthcare, decreasing the risk women in particular are facing when having to travel long distances. The same principle applies to community-wide protection through IRS, which leads to better health overall and protects every vulnerable sub-group from infection and subsequent loss of health, mobility and independence.

12.1.2. Decreased proportion of populations facing IPC 3,4 and 5

Empowering vulnerable communities and providing work and income to populations suffering from poor nutrition/malnutrition provides individuals with capital to provide nutritional and medical care to their families as well as to invest in local businesses that they can sustain long-term. Furthermore, reducing the heavy burden of malaria will lower the financial burden on communities. Money that would have been spent on healthcare and medication related to repeated malaria illness can now be spent on food, nutrition and other commodities

12.1.3. Equitable access to cross-sectoral basic services to vulnerable populations (including women, children, the elderly, persons with disability and older persons)

MENTOR is committed to programmatic gender mainstreaming, and by their very nature, MENTOR’s programme activities do not discriminate against people based on gender, age or disability. Malaria and most other VBDs affect all ages and genders, so entire communities are targeted in order to ensure maximum protection, reduce the human pool of infection and onward disease transmission. The prevention activities provide bed nets to all household members. During IRS, larviciding campaigns and IEC sessions, MENTOR encourages equal participation of all genders, age groups and people with disabilities in planning, implementation and monitoring of core activities. Previous and current MENTOR records indicate a balanced participation of women/men and youths (both boys and girls), and vulnerable groups are identified and prioritized in terms of service delivery. The training for IRS will also include information on gender-related issues related to MENTOR’s policies on anti-harassment guidelines. Through engaging community members from the outset of programme activities, MENTOR ensures that local buy-in is assured. As part of community mobilisation, meetings with community leaders are held on a regular basis, providing an opportunity for MENTOR to share and discuss proposed actions and to actively discuss community feedback.

12.1.1. Decreased prevalence of global acute malnutrition among children under age 5 in severity levels 3 and 4

Studies have shown a synergistic relationship between malaria and malnutrition. It has been suggested that malnutrition may predispose children to malaria infection or make it more difficult to recover from infection. Different forms of malnutrition have been relatively consistently associated with high-density parasitaemia and anaemia, both associated with severe malaria. Moreover, severe acute malnutrition is believed to be linked to intestinal malabsorption of medicines which can cause sub-optimal drug exposure and contribute further to poor patient outcomes and accelerated selection of antimalarial drug resistance. At the same time, malaria infection may predispose a child to acute weight loss or impair response to standard treatment for malnutrition and previous studies have shown malaria infection to be associated with poor nutritional status. Therefore, Effective collaboration between malaria control and nutrition intervention programmes is essential for proper case management and decreasing the prevalence of global acute malnutrition in children.
