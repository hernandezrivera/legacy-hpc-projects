Project Summary

The food security and livelihood sub-component of the program is aimed at improving the overall food security and economic status of 62,260 vulnerable people, particularly children, 8094 internally displaced persons (IPDs) pregnant and lactating women (PLW), the elderly and people with disabilities (PWD) thus improving resilience. The project will be implemented in two counties in Unity State, that is Mayom county with 23,760 targeted individuals (11,405 males and 12,355 females), and Mayendit county will have 21487 beneficiaries (10,314 males and 11,173 females) as well as in Upper Nile State’s Maiwut county where 17, 013 people (8166 males and 8847 females) will be reached. The project will enhance food security and economic status through implementing interventions under the Agriculture and Economic Recovery & Market systems development (ERMS) sectors. Complementary activities under the Agriculture sector will include emergence staple and vegetable production support, including training and inputs/tools provision, cooking demonstrations & nutrition awareness, block farming support for youth and young women, trainings for community-based agriculture extension workers (CBAEWS) and farmers in agricultural production techniques, training in post-harvest handling loss reduction including demonstrations for improved granaries and disaster risk reduction in agriculture. Activities under the Economic Recovery and Market systems development will include training and support for village savings and loans associations (VSLAs) as well as training in financial literacy and business management for participating households.
It is expected that by improving HH agriculture production and fishing techniques, HHs will rely less on humanitarian assistance and will be able to sufficiently meet their own food needs. Furthermore, local economies will be strengthened, and communities will become more resilient against current and future shocks. This is achieved through layering and sequencing of interventions across the two interlinked and mutually supportive sectors, i.e. agriculture/food security and economic recovery and market strengthening. This integrated approach ensures that beneficiaries not only receive the resources they need to thrive, but also the training and knowledge they need for their coping mechanisms and to sustain livelihood activities.

1 - Needs

In 2022, some of the SP's FSL program gains were reversed in Unity State's Mayom, Mayendit Counties and Maiwut county in Upper Nile State due to natural and man-made shocks. Food insecurity in Mayom, Mayendit and Maiwut Counties was exacerbated by the combination of shocks, including three years of flooding that has destroyed crops and livestock, conflict and the macroeconomic crisis leading to inflation. In the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) analysis period of October to November 2022, an estimated 6.64 million people (53.6% of the population) in South Sudan are facing Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity, of which 2.22 million people are facing Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. The most food insecure states between October and November 2022 where more than 50% of their populations are facing Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity are Unity (66.7%), Upper Nile (58.5%), Warrap (57.1%), Northern Bahr el Ghazal (61.9%), Jonglei (68.0%), and Lakes (56.9%). The Food insecurity situation as denoted by the proportion of households in IPC Phase 3 or worse is projected to worsen for Mayom, Mayendit and Maiwut Counties from the October-November period, through the Harvest/Post harvest period December-2022 to March 2023 with Mayom having 50%, Mayendit (79%) and Maiwut (60%). In the lean season from April 2023 to July 2023 household food insecurity further deteriorates with Mayom having 65% of its population in IPC Phase 3 or worse, Maiwut (70%) and Mayendit with 89% of which 6% are in IPC Phase 5 (Famine). Mayendit is among the 5 Counties projected to face Famine/Catastrophic levels in the country despite having at least 25% of its households relying on Humanitarian Food Assistance to meet over 50% of their caloric requirements.Farmer challenges across the country include pests such as the desert locust and fall armyworm which cause extensive crop damage. SP internal surveys indicate that farmers practicing appropriate crop protection procedures increased from 67% in 2019 to 82% in 2021. This suggests that farmers have observed and embraced the benefits of pest control practices. SP will build on this success in 2022/2024 programming to ensure farmers continuously control field crop pests, with increased focus on storage pest control. In areas of current programming, only 47% of HHs interviewed in 2022 had access to sufficient seeds to plant, indicating the need for additional distribution. SP seeks to continue equipping farmers with knowledge on seed multiplication and reduction of post-harvest losses. The program will support emergence food production through staple and vegetable seeds and farming tools supoort. The national biodiversity strategy and action plan (2018-2027) shows that approximately 78% of all HHs in South Sudan earn their livelihoods from farming, fishing, pastoralism, or a mix of all three with 17.3% of the population in South Sudan directly dependent on fisheries. The fishing industry is not well developed, and it is believed that fish is an underutilized resource in the country. Most fishermen use simple fishing techniques such as gillnets, throw nets, and hooks, with women often utilizing baskets to catch fish. SP surveys indicate that in Mayendit, an average of 37 kg of fish per HH were harvested in 2021, down from 43 kg harvested per HH in 2020. SP sees an investment in this sector as an opportunity to further strengthen livelihoods. A 2019 study with women in one county in South Sudan showed that Village Savings and Loan Associations (VSLAs) were able to increase business income for 93.9% of the women, improve decision making for 97.6%, and increase asset ownership (99.3% owned assets). SP will continue with the VSLA approach in the 3 counties to improve economic status of participants.

2 - Project activities

The agriculture and food security sector’s objectives are to enhance HH agricultural production through promotion of good agronomic techniques including integrated pest management (IPM) and post-harvest loss reduction (PHL), small-scale irrigation, seed system strengthening, adoption of sustainable fishing practices as well as economic recovery and market systems strengthening through linkages to financial services using VSLAs model. Complementary staple and vegetable seeds support and fishing kits will contribute to increased production (CLFSCO2). It is expected that by improving HH agriculture production and enhancing knowledge on sustainable agriculture and fishing practices, the HHs will be more resilient. This aligns with cluster object CLFSCO3 by seeking to reduce HH dependency on humanitarian assistance. The staple and vegetable farmers will be provided with tools such as malodas, hand-hoes, axes, pangas and irrigation support (treadle pumps, watering cans). Additionally, HHs will be supported to engage in profitable climate smart and nutrition sensitive agricultural production incorporating training in nutrition practices and cooking demonstrations. To achieve this, agricultural training groups will be formed in Mayom, Mayendit and Maiwut. All groups will be trained in good agronomic practices, organic pest control and post-harvest handling. To encourage adoption of sustainable fishing practices, HHs will be provided with fishing kits (twines and hooks) with aim of improving fish harvesting, consumption and sales. These HHs will be trained in sustainable fish harvesting, processing and marketing. The activities planned under Economic Recovery sector are designed to improve the social economic status of the host community and IDPs living in the three counties. This is based on the understanding that improving economic status will directly contribute to the improvement in overall food & nutrition security and living standards of the affected HHs. To achieve this, SP will carry out carefully planned interventions across the three mutually supportive sub-sectors of market system strengthening, financial services/inclusion, and new livelihoods development. Market system strengthening activities are aimed at enhancing opportunities for income generation among IDP, returnees, and vulnerable resident populations through strengthened engagement with local markets. A series of activities are planned which include (1) conducting a market assessment to help understand the dynamics and entry points for vulnerable HHs, (2) creation of agriculture/business groups, (3) establishment of VSLA groups, and (4) improving post-harvest handling and processing to broaden the benefits arising from agricultural production. The financial services intervention will increase access by non-bankable HHs to financial services through the VSLA model. Specific activity targets Agriculture: Staple crop production: 40.28 MT of staple seeds; 2.2025 MT of vegetable seeds; 5306 pieces of farming tools and 10800 fishing kits for fisheries support beneficiaries. Kitchen Gardening: 270 vegetable groups formed (6750 HHs). Training in staple and vegetable agronomy, IPM for kitchen gardens and staple production beneficiaries. Establish Youth Block Farm Groups: 325 members participating in 13 groups. Post-harvest handling training and demonstrations on improved storage for 5325 staple crop growers. Seed system security through community seed multiplication and PHL training for 225 trained local seed multipliers. Provide fishing kits to 2700 HHs. Training on sustainable fish harvesting, processing and marketing. Establish and support 3500 people in 140 VSLA groups also training on financial literacy. Establish County and Payam level community managed disaster risk reduction committees and train them on Disaster Risk Reduction. Select and support 24 Community Based Agriculture Extension workers, 270 Lead farmers, 18 VSLA Agents. Gender and protection mainstreaming.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

Samaritan’s Purse agronomists, in close collaboration with the state level Ministry of Agriculture technical staff, conducted seed quality and viability tests on seeds from potential suppliers from Mayom and Abiemnhom market. In addition, the project team conducted a market assessment to ascertain market functionality and availability of seed in the area. The study also confirmed that the proposed voucher approach will not disrupt access to seeds by other local farmers who are not direct beneficiaries of this project. Building on this experience, additional project beneficiaries will be provided with seed vouchers and will be able to access 5kgs of preferred locally adapted maize variety per HH in Mayom county.

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

In 2020, SP piloted seed vouchers in Abiemnhom County after the feedback from program participants indicated that beneficiaries preferred to plant local seeds rather than imported seeds that are not adapted to the local condition and needs. The provision of seeds through seed vouchers was then proposed to enable farmers to access seed varieties they needed, specifically the staple crop(sorghum). A market assessment and seed system security assessment conducted by SP in Mayom and Abiemnhom counties indicated the presence of functional markets, high contribution of local farmers as a source of seed and local seed traders to support such interventions. In 2021, the community seed multiplication intervention was successfully extended to Mayom County I addition to Abiemnhom. The seed voucher also strengthens the local market system given that most HHs buy seeds from within the counties.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

As stated above, SP has put in place a number of mechanisms through which feedback and complaints from all project participants, including vulnerable members, can be collected. These include suggestion boxes installed at project meeting venues/distribution points, complaints desk manned by a trained officer at each input distribution sites, and community project committees, through which beneficiaries will be able to submit their complaints or feedback about the services provided by SP. Vulnerable members such as persons with disabilities, those who are not literate, women, and girls will be educated on how best they can share feedback using any of the above channels without fear of their information being disclosed.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Samaritan’s Purse will seek feedback from affected populations in order to address any problems that may arise during program implementation and to improve how activities are designed and carried out. Beneficiaries will be informed about how they can register complaints or provide feedback in a variety of ways. For example, during distributions, a helpdesk will be set up where beneficiaries can provide feedback or register complaints with a trained staff member. Beneficiaries are then notified about availability of such services. The staff member at the help desk will be a member of the monitoring and evaluation (M&E) team, rather than the distribution team, in order to ensure adequate separation of duties. The helpdesk staff member will have a standard form for receiving and recording feedback/complaints. At each of the distribution sites, there will also be a complaint box where community members can submit written complaints if they prefer. In addition, project steering committees will be established in each community to receive and submit to SP any complaints/feedback community members bring them. Beneficiaries will be informed about the availability of these complaint and feedback channels on a regular basis (i.e. during community meetings, training sessions, before and during distribution exercises) so that they are well informed.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

Before program activities commence, beneficiaries are informed of their right to file complaints, including reports of SEA and sexual harassment, through established reporting mechanisms such as the SP hotline. Samaritan’s Purse highly safeguards safety, confidentiality, and dignity of the reporting beneficiary and handles the matter with utmost respect and in a non-discriminatory manner. Program-specific complaints are directed to the PSEA focal point in the country office. Any report of SEA or sexual harassment is reported immediately to the Country Director who will communicate with SP’s international headquarters, per the Whistleblower Policy, for an appropriate response and/or investigation. Per the Whistleblower Policy, the International Compliance Officer will enlist support from various individuals to ensure a prompt, diligent, and confidential investigation appropriate to the complaint. For allegations of SEA, the International Compliance Officer will involve the Director of Global Safeguarding to coordinate the investigation efforts with appropriate personnel in the country office, including the focal point(s).

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The feedback submitted in boxes will be regularly collected on and combined with the feedback received by the committees during distributions. Non-sensitive feedback will be summarized and shared with beneficiaries and staff at the field level. The information generated via feedback and complaints system will be used to continually improve program implementation. In particular, sector managers will meet at least quarterly to discuss general program feedback received and to agree on action plans to address any concerns raised. Program teams will then provide feedback to beneficiaries about what will be done to address their concerns and will then carry out the agreed action steps. Program staff will provide feedback to beneficiaries at the start of program activities to inform them about steps taken to address their concerns (e.g. through pre-distribution messaging for distribution activities, at the start of trainings for training activities, etc.). Where applicable, sector leaders will also inform the relevant local authorities about plans to address general concerns beneficiaries have raised. Non-sensitive complaints that do not involve general program feedback, but which are specific to an individual or individuals, will be reviewed by the Complaints Manager, presented to the Area Coordinator, and shared with relevant sector leaders as part of the Program Manager’s monthly reporting. Where such feedback necessitates a response, Program Managers, in consultation with the Area Coordinator, will agree on action steps and the individual(s) who raised the complaint will be notified about what will be done to address their concerns. Feedback involving critical or sensitive protection issues, however, will be reported to senior management at the country office level. The country office will also notify SP headquarters. In such cases, an action plan will then be created to address the issues raised and a team unconnected with the complaint will investigate.

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

The program will provide emergency assistance to beneficiaries with a particular focus on those in IPC 3, 4 and 5 living in Unity state (Mayom and Mayendit) and Upper Nile state (Maiwut). The assistance will ensure that critical inputs are available, accessible, and utilized in a manner that reduces the suffering and vulnerability of those affected by conflict and natural flooding. They will be provided with emergency food production assistance including main season agriculture seeds & tool distribution and fishing, vegetable, and crop kits. With this type of support, affected HHs will be empowered to produce sufficient food to last them through the lean months when food is normally scarce. Dry season response kits, fishing kits and vegetable kits, which will be provided just before the onset of the dry season, will supplement this. Vulnerable groups; women (including pregnant and lactating women), the elderly, people with disabilities (PWDs) and youth will be targeted reducing their vulnerabilities to GBV at the same time ensuring food security in their households.Samaritan’s Purse acknowledges that there are gaps between males and females, including unequal access to education and income opportunities, unequal access to land and other productive resources, and discriminatory social practices, that limit rights and opportunities for women and girls to fully participate in society. This has resulted in different levels of exposure and sensitivity to shocks and stresses. Samaritan’s Purse also recognizes that the conflict has impacted women, men, boys and girls differently, and will continue to prioritize meeting their specific needs. Although women and girls play a critical, and potentially transformative, role in addressing food insecurity within their HHs and communities, they continue to face cultural, political, and financial obstacles that limit their time, mobility, access to and control over resources, and influence.Samaritan's Purse through its Protection/Safe programming department will continue promoting gender equality in its actions and interventions. Samaritan’s Purse will ensure that activities in the community are gender sensitive in all interventions. To ensure that issues affecting the lives of the most vulnerable women and girls are captured, SP will collect, analyze, and use gender-disaggregated data for planning, targeting, and setting performance indicators. Building on the previous lessons learned by staff in gender and protection, field trainers and LFs will be sensitized on gender and protection mainstreaming. Capacity building training will focus on incorporating gender analysis and mainstreaming into beneficiary targeting, selection, and participation, as well as M&E systems. The exercise will aim at ensuring that quality responses, with equality for men, women, boys and girls as a guiding principle, are observed throughout the project period. To increase the access to and control of capital, and the empowerment of excluded gender groups (particularly women and girls), SP will continue to consider gender roles in agricultural production and fisheries throughout the implementation of activities. . Samaritan’s Purse will not establish a case management system, but will engage in meaningful awareness-raising through existing community structures and refer any cases of GBV through the locally established referral mechanism.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

 The project adopts an integrated multisectoral approach with interventions occurring in other sectors (WASH, Health, agriculture/food security, economic recovery, etc.) that directly contribute towards the reduction of global acute malnutrition (GAM) rates and severe acute food insecurity among targeted HHs. This will be achieved through support for emergency food production in the form of provision of complementary staple and vegetable seeds (including early maturing vegetable such as kudra), training in better agronomic practices and provision of farm tools to support production activities. To support emergency food and livelihood for HHs in IPC 4 and IPC 5, Samaritan’s Purse shall provide fishing kits in addition to facilitating training on sustainable fish harvesting, processing and marketing. To ensure that the project support reaches out to the most in need, SP shall incorporate the needs of and target the most vulnerable HHs and populations: women, girls, men & boys, elderly, disabled, people with special needs, children U5, HIV & chronic sick. By encouraging beneficiaries (women) to join VSLAs, the project will offer a platform for additional group-based activities and information sharing on topics such as nutrition and health. Field supervisors and village agents will be trained to layer these topics into VSLA group meetings.Samaritan’s Purse plans to conduct cooking demonstrations with a focus on the vegetables promoted by the project. The purpose of conducting the cooking demonstrations will be to enable the mothers/caregivers to gain new food preparation skills, develop their confidence in preparing new dishes, and create awareness of underutilized locally available vegetable/staple crops. All HHs participating in dry season vegetable production activities will be instructed on how to prepare nutritious meals in addition to other topics (e.g. washing or cutting vegetables, food hygiene, meal frequency, etc.).
