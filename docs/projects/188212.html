Project Summary

The proposed intervention aims to provide NFE, MHPSS and LSCE activities for school-age children (5-17 years) and youth (18-24 years, girls/boys) in Aleppo (Jabel Saman, Zerbeh), Raqqa (Maadan, Sabka), Idleb (Abul Thohur, Khan Shaykun), Hama (Hamra, As San), Homs (Talbiseh, Ar-Rastan), Rural Damascus (Zabadani, Markaz Darayya), Dar’a (Izra, As-Sanamayn). Jebel Saman, Zabadani, Darayya, and Khan Shaykun are ranked 4 on HNO 2022 severity scale, Zerbeh, Talbiseh, Ar-Rastan, Hamra, As Sanamayn, Izra, Sabka, Maadan and Abul Thohur are ranked 3, As-Saan is ranked 2. Locations and targets have been identified based on the ongoing areas of intervention in Hama, RDam, Raqqa, Homs, Idleb, Dar’a where COOPI is currently present with protection and FSL programs and intends to integrate them with education activities to ensure holistic assistance to the most vulnerable. COOPI focuses mostly on remote and/or underserved areas where the access of education is hampered by a severe lack education services and infrastructures, and on 1 urban neighbourhood (Jebel Saman), as detected during ongoing projects implementation. As per education Sector Severity scale, the estimated PiN in the targeted areas is of 608,650 children, of which 10% living with disabilities, and 20,288 educational personnel, for a total of 628,938, of which 53%women and girls. Parents will be involved in parenting programs. Access to schools is granted after signature of MoU with Ministry of Education in January 2022. The activities are designed in synergy with other projects submitted to HRP 2022-2023 and based on the ongoing intervention sand assessments in the districts targeted. In the 5 Governorates, the following proposal intends to provide education assistance to a total of 9,000 beneficiaries. COOPI will consider adopting CVA modality within its programming to alleviate the economic pressure preventing parents (especially women-headed HHs) to send children at school and to adapt negative coping mechanism such as child labor or child marriage. Activities will be also focus on non-formal education-including remedial classes, Accelerated Learning Programs (ALP), catch-up classes, literacy and numeracy classes, and Self Learning Program - to support the public education system, not able to cover all the needs. All the educational programs will include PSS for children. Taking in consideration of the presence of EO, also mine risk education will be provided to children in centers, schools and through outreach activities. Health and hygiene awareness activities will be also provided through distribution of hygiene kits. To ensure sustainability of the assistance, COOPI will stress on capacity building of both trainers/teachers and staff on leading and monitoring education services and basic principles related to CPMS, EiE and INEE MS. Unit cost per child is based on implementation modality, estimated within a range between 80USD (in presence) and 150USD (blended/distance learning) to serve children leaving in hard-to-reach areas and children with disabilities. The proposed activities will be adapted to the education needs in the different areas of intervention detected during ongoing and previous programs and assessments, with different approach regarding urban and rural or remote areas, as well as the use of the static facilities run by COOPI in Aleppo linked to mobile staff to serve hard-to-reach areas. COOPI proposal will therefore focus on existing resources and capacity to provide NFE in an efficient way, through in-presence, remote and blended earning modalities that will be identified in each location and involving the local community to find suitable but less economically demanding solution, as already ongoing in other sectors of intervention. COOPI will also coordinate with ERL /FSA Sectors and actors for further support such as livelihood/IGA opportunities to support low-income HHs with children drop-out of school.

4 - Needs

The estimated PiN of education in the areas targeted by COOPI are 625,676 of which 487,581 in Jebel Saman and 377 in Zerbeh (Aleppo), 11,441 in Zabadani and 17,190 in Markaz Darayya (Rural Damascus), 3,497 in As San and 4,026 in Hamra (Hama), 5,550 in Maadan and 13,124 in Sabka (Raqqa), 836 Abu Thohur and 1,169 Khan Shaykun (Idleb), 41,479 in Talbiseh and 30,038 in Ar-Rastan (Homs) and 51,913 in As-Sanamayn and 27,475 in Izra (Daraa). As highlighted in the 2022 HNO, on 50,183 school aged children (5-17), 18% are not attending schools, with Ar-Raqqa (35%), Idleb (28%), Aleppo (26%) having the highest rate of non-attendance. The main reason is the economic status of families and the protracted exposure to conflict, causing drop out and increasing protection risks (child marriage and child labour) and psychological stress affecting their learning outcomes. In addition, harassment, bullying, violence, and poverty push children to drop out. Children under 4 are the most affected, with the lowest attendance. Moreover, there is a decrease in the attendance of 61% for children aged (12-18). A particular effort is needed to assist children living with a disability who are routinely the most marginalized. Therefore, the project will respond to the need of overcoming the barriers, in term of physical access and lack of inclusive education systems or special education curricula for people with disabilities out of the education system in Syria.

5 - Implementation of activities

In consultation with stakeholders and in coordination with MoE, COOPI aims at improving access to education and promote the enrolment, retention and reinsertion of dropout children, focusing on: 1. Sustain formal and NFE, through VT, remedial classes, ALP, SLP; 2. Provide material support, including classrooms rehabilitation, furniture, maintenance, running costs, school supplies, winter clothes, recreational materials. COOPI aims to take action against some concern preventing access to school/NFE such as distance, price of transportation, absence of schools and use of resources to meet other basic needs, by supporting vouchers/cash for transportation and for education materials; 3. Support Covid-19 response by providing PPE and awareness raising in formal and non-formal settings and disinfecting schools and learning spaces, but also promote more widely health and hygiene awareness; 4. Complementary activities such as BTL campaigns, MRE, life skills and citizenship education, PSS programs, based on COOPI expertise in providing protection services, and supporting/establishing PTAs. Learning achievements and targets will be verified through tools such as CMS, summative learning assessment, student attendance logs, lesson plans, will support COOPI monitoring of the quality of education activities. COOPI will participate in Sector 4Ws and collect disaggregated data. Complaints/feedback mechanisms will be to monitor accessibility, adequacy and equity of services.

9 - Protection Risk Analysis

RISK: Protection risk faced by girls and boys may persist if education personnel do not play an active role in CP prevention. L: Medium; I: High; MM: Train education personnel on basic CP principles, risk identification and response. RISK: Girls, boys, women and men’s physical security is put at risk as a result of the violence associated with civilian populations being targeted by armed groups L: Medium; I: High; MM: Ensure safety audit is conducted to ensure safe access (route and location) Agree with community on safe space where to implement the activities. RISK: Tension within the family, including risks to physical violence (eg. cash assistance) L: Medium, I: high MM: When targeting individuals for services, ensure a holistic family-based approach where possible and proper consultation and communication. 10 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

Risk: Exploitation or abuse perpetrated by staff members or service providers when delivering assistance. Likelihood Medium. Impact: High. Mitigating measures: Establish formal and informal complaint and feedback mechanisms in each area of intervention; Provide staff with training on Code of Conduct and PSEA, according to COOPI Global policies in place and PSEA zero-tolerance policy; Ensure the proper communication to the communities about the gratuity of the services; Standard operating procedures are in place for submission and receipt of complaints, reporting, investigation and victim assistance. PSEA focal points have been nominated and trained in COOPI sub-offices, Syria Country Office and headquarter and all staff members will continue receiving training and information on preventing and responding to acts of sexual exploitation and abuse. When intervening in new areas, COOPI will ensure to nominate and train PSEA FPs in charge of supervising the use and dissemination of PSEA reporting tools among communities, staff and third parties.


11 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

RISK: Direct access to the target areas and beneficiaries is limited, suspended or becomes unsafe for humanitarian workers. ASSUMPTION: Escalation of the hostilities in the targeted areas/COVID spread. MITIGATION MEASURES: Maintain constant communication with other humanitarian agencies, GoS and security monitoring networks. All staff receive security training and apply COVID prevention measures and it is provided with PPE. RISK: Education services provided are not fully coordinated with other humanitarian actors. ASSUMPTION: Other humanitarian actors might not have the capacity to assist additional beneficiaries or the referral pathway or specialized services needed is not fully designed/implemented. MITIGATION MEASURES: COOPI will advocate with the sector to fill the gap and finding exit strategies. RISK: Explosive incidents ASSUMPTION: Lack of knowledge about risks related to EO and adoption of unsafe behavior by communities and staff. MITIGATION MEASURES: Contribute to incident reporting; awareness raising sessions for staff, volunteers, parents and children; RISK: delay in project approvals or in access to locations. ASSUMPTION: Extensive involvement of national stakeholder in the project implementation. MITIGATING MEASURES: Advocate to maintain the direct implementation. Advocate with donors for the inclusion of specific requirements in implementing and monitoring specialized services; advocate with national counterparts to set up feedback and complaints mechanisms.
