Project Summary

The Barefoot Counsellors programme is a unique community-based approach to protection as it connects individuals to capacities and resources in the community to strengthen their resilience and recovery. In the Rohingya emergency response, Caritas Bangladesh is implementing the BFC program in Camp 4 extension. The barefoot counsellors are volunteers from the refugee community. They conduct daily household visits where they listen to grievances, share information, identify vulnerabilities and community-oriented solutions to protection issues and refer community members to existing protection services if needed. They are trained to provide Psychological First Aid (PFA) and are able to identify the most vulnerable for further referrals. As part of the BFC program model, Caritas Bangladesh runs a community centre in the camp which is used by all members in the community as well as other sectoral partners. In addition to household visits by barefoot counsellors, the field team (national staff) conducts specific awareness raising sessions at the community centre on a range of topics including gender-based violence prevention, anti-trafficking and early marriage. The BFC community centre also provides a safe and confidential entry point for women and girls, men and boys to access GBV case management and counselling.

Needs

"As Rohingya refugees continue to wait in limbo over prospects of their long-term safe, dignified and voluntary repatriation to Myanmar, the lack of access to livelihoods, education, formal justice mechanisms as well as movement restrictions have negative impacts on the overall protection environment. As a result, many refugees resort to harmful coping mechanisms, inadvertently leading to child protection and gender-based violence issues. Push factors such as poverty, unemployment and promise of better future further increase their vulnerability and exposure to trafficking, exploitation and abuse. Based on findings from the Child Protection Sub Sector’s needs assessment for JRP 2020, there is a pressing need to scale up specialized MHPSS for those most vulnerable and severely affected. Boys and girls, including adolescents, face a wide range of protection risks including violence, neglect, abuse, separation from caregivers, child marriage, child labour, trafficking and psychological distress. Results from UNHCR’s 5th SPP and ISCG’s MSNA conducted in 2019 indicate that approximately 5-10% of Rohingya households engage in child marriage with two-thirds of these households considering it a normal practice. The studies also highlight child marriage as a coping strategy – approximately 1/3 of household resort to this practice to protect girls or to reduce the economic stressors on the household. Furthermore, inadequate service coverage bars access to life-saving care and specialized services for survivors of GBV. Data from the GBVIMS report from October 2018 – June 2019 by the GBV Sub-Sector in Cox’s Bazar show that domestic violence constitutes 76% of total reported GBV cases. Therefore, scaling up awareness-raising session on GBV services, entry points, referral pathways and working on social norms programming to address patriarchal norms that perpetuate GBV remains a top priority. Physical access to protection facilities is challenging because of the nature of the field, which disproportionately affects women, elderly persons and persons with disabilities. Therefore, there is a need to work on access, information and trust in protection services at the community-level, together with providing quality prevention and response programming. There is also a need for social system strengthening and peaceful coexistence initiatives in host community. Host communities are also in acute need for protection, which should be provided in close partnership with the local authorities and according to the gaps identified with them. In addition, there should be a focus on activities encouraging peacebuilding approaches and mutual learning. The priority challenges identified in the project area include Polygamy, Early Marriage, and Trafficking/Exploitation. Social cohesion within Camp 4 Extension is also fragile, being a relocation camp where the Rohingya community is exceptionally diverse. Some families are of mixed marriage (Bangladeshi/Rohingya), others have been living in Bangladesh as far as Chittagong for more than 10 years, and some others arrived in the 2017 influx. These concerns might increase in 2020, and service providers need to better understand the social structure in this camp, including the issues related to race/ethnicity and diversity, to be able to promote a protective and inclusive environment. Exploring innovative and community-led approaches to address these issues will be key in 2020.

Activities or outputs

"The Barefoot Counsellors (BFC) project is closely aligned with the Protection Sector’s JRP strategy for 2020 in that it promotes a sustainable and rights-based approach to community-based protection through empowering refugees to gain knowledge and skills in various areas, including but not limited to psychosocial support, community engagement and awareness raising, so that they can support individuals in need. In 2020, the project will continue in Camp 4 Extension where it is well-established. The following project activities will be undertaken:1.1. Training Rohingya refugees, men and women in protection principles, PFA, GBV, anti-trafficking, PSEA and PSS to become Barefoot Counsellors (BFCs). 1.2. Deploying BFCs to conduct household visits to deliver basic PSS; share information on referral mechanisms, services and support available to community members; special focus on female-headed households, individuals with disabilities and unaccompanied children as well as child-headed households. 1.3 BFCs identify specific protection risks in the areas and share with the Protection Focal Point for protection monitoring purposes. The protection monitoring that is done at the field level is meant to inform Caritas Bangladesh (CB) management in Cox’s Bazar of the current needs and concerns of the community, and to ensure programming on the ground addresses these.1.4 The GBV case management team consists of two GBV officers (including a senior psychologist). The case management services that are provided through the community center are safe, confidential and survivor-centered. 1.5 CB will roll-out GBV prevention sessions targeting men and boys, structured and gender-sensitive PSS sessions with facilitators using No Strings International (NSI)’s film (written and produced in Rohingya language), theatre and puppetry as well as other creative arts approaches. These sessions would also include cyclone and monsoon seasons preparedness activities, and practical measures to mitigate the effects of landslides and flooding (to be co-facilitated with our Shelter team). 1.6. CB is the Protection Focal Point and Child Protection Focal Point in Camp 4 Extension. CB’s protection team attends and actively participates in the Protection Working Group (PWG) and its sub-sector meetings. CB’s engagement with the PWG sending monthly 5W reports, SitReps and participation in RPAs. BFC Community Center in Camp 4 Extension has been used by other partners and civil society organisations in the camp for their activities (this includes BRAC and the MHPSS WG). CB also works closely with the CiC in seeking approval prior to piloting new activities in the camps and participates in coordination meetings with Site Management and Block Leaders.1.7. The project has conducted an assessment in the following host communities: Palongkali and Rajapalong Union, Ukhia Upazila, on the feasibility and modalities of community-based protection programming.1.8 CB has a feedback mechanism for the Rohingya response with different entry points for feedback collection: electronic devices, hotline, and complaint boxes. Feedback is received and regularly analyzed by the M&E team and shared with program team to re-design the programming.1.9 Caritas Bangladesh has a national policy for PSEA. Complaints can be collected through the several entry points mentioned above and are directly processed by the PSEA Focal Point. BFCs and CB staff are trained on PSEA and reporting mechanisms.
