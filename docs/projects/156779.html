Project Summary

In the eighth year of the conflict, less than half of Syria's health services are operational. Displacement continues to drive health needs. According to the rapid needs assessments conducted in North West Syria, health issues are a major concern that the communities face; lack of access to health services, lack of supported health facilities and high cost inhibiting access to services. Protection remains to be another area of concern within Syria, where lack of services and poor community level coping mechanisms continue to have especially negative impact on the most vulnerable groups of the affected population - women/girls and children.
To address the identified gaps, the action proposes to meet primary and secondary health care needs through provision of services to vulnerable communities and those within the communities with particular acute vulnerabilities such as women, children, elderly and people with disabilities.

Needs

In Syria, within the overall 13.1 million people in need there are 5.6 million facing particularly acute needs (HNO 2018). According to World Bank the conflict has devastated Syria’s economy. 69% of people now live in extreme poverty , up from 5.5% before the conflict . Two thirds of health facilities have been damaged or destroyed. The conflict has seen persistent targeting of health facilities and health workers. Over 50% of health workers have fled the country. Health conditions in the targeted areas are further exacerbated by bad hygiene practices; according to HNO 2018 WASH services and hygiene supplies are available but are relatively expensive. Access to healthcare is severely restricted, with vital medical infrastructures destroyed and medical personnel deliberately targeted. Hospitals and medical facilities in the country that are still operational continue to be critically understaffed. Most hospitals are not receiving enough supplies and medicine, nor funding for salaries and running costs to meet their needs, significantly hindering both the availability and the continuity of services. The 2018 HNO cited six groups as the most vulnerable and require specific consideration when planning and prioritizing the response, Children are especially vulnerable, particularly those who are unaccompanied and separated or living with older/disabled caregivers or out of school. Children engaging in child labour or recruited for military purposes will require specific consideration, People with chronic illness, disabilities and injuries have specific needs which cut across sectors and may face difficulties in accessing health services, Women and girls may face particular risks due to their gender and situation, including harmful survival mechanisms such as early marriage, trafficking and sexual abuse and exploitation. Unaccompanied girls, pregnant and lactating women and female-headed households all have specific needs which require consideration for accessing services and facilities, participation, empowerment and safely accessible feedback systems Needs in Syria have remained relatively consistent throughout the 8 years of the crisis; as reaffirmed by the findings of the needs and baseline assessments conducted by EPDC; critical gaps in health services for IDPs, returnees & host communities have been and continue to be prevalent across Syria including the project targeted communities: - Multi-sectorial Rapid Needs Assessment Report communities in Aleppo governorate, December 19, 2018, by EPDC.- Syrian HNO 2018

Implementation of activities

The project proposes to fill current gaps in health services through the provision of primary and secondary health service. This will be achieved through both mobile and fixed solutions, designed in line with SPHERE and Core Humanitarian Standards and tailored to the local context. Result 1: Provide essential primary and secondary health care services Activity 1.1: Establishment of PHC CentersThe project will rehabilitate 2 PHC centers in Aleppo, 1 in Dar'a and 1 in Rural Damascus including structural renovation and medical equipment as per WHO standards. The PHCs will provide consultations covering six major services that the Essential Primary Health Care (EPHC) Package addresses. These are: Child health, Reproductive health, prevention and control of communicable diseases, prevention and treatment of Non Communicable diseases (NCD), Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (outreach and referral, not clinical support) and Nutrition (screening and referral not treatment). Activity 1.2: Establishment of Medical Mobile Units (MMU)In Rural Damascus and Dar'a, 2 MMUs will provide primary healthcare services to those who cannot reach the PHCs or hospitals, including NCD package and case management for diseases like diabetes and hypertension. The MMUs will also identify serious cases for referral to other healthcare providers. Each MMU will be staffed with a physician, nurse, midwife and social worker. Through the mobile nature of these units, the project is able to target communities with mobility issues and isolated villages and adapt to changes, including security issues, population movements and new crisis. Activity 1.4: Health awareness campaign targeting women, pregnant and lactating women (PLW), The project will provide health and hygiene promotion sessions targeting pregnant and lactating women and most vulnerable heads of HHs. The various topics will include access to adequate safe water, HH sanitation and personal hygiene, hygienic methods for water collection and storage, hygienic food storage and preparation. The topics will always include diabetes, hypertension, gout, osteoporosis, first aid and reproductive health subjects. Result 2: Provide health facilities with essential medicines and medical supplies Activity 2.1: Provision of medicines, treatment and dialysis supportThe project will provide support to 180 diabetes patients per center in 2 PHC centers , and support to 27 dialysis patients in Dar'a and Rural Damacus (2 locations) due to the high cost of treatment for the most vulnerable assessed families. Provision of diabetes machine will be accompanied by training and health awareness sessions for patient focus on healthy diet. Result 3: Strengthen the capacity of health care providers and community health care workers to provide essential health services Activity 1.3: Recruitment and training of professional staffThe project will recruit medical staff in the 3 governorates. Trainings on PHC center management and other medical topics such as IMCI, CMAM, IYCF, RH, IPC, Pharmacy management, mhGAP, NCD management, GBV core concepts, psychological first aid, disclosure and safe referral will be provided by the project at the beginning of the intervention to the recruited staff. The provision of health services is a crucial element to ensure the safety and security needs of women, girls, boys and men are met. Furthermore, the project will take into account the specificities in relation to the gender, age, disabilities, amongst others.

Protection Risk Analysis

GBV seriously impacts survivors’ immediate sexual, physical and psychological health, and contributes to greater risk of future health problems. Possible sexual health effects include unwanted pregnancies, complications from unsafe abortions, and sexually transmitted infections, including HIV. Possible physical health effects of GBV include injuries that can cause both acute and chronic illness, impacting neurological, gastrointestinal, muscular, urinary, and reproductive systems. Possible mental health problems include depression, anxiety, harmful alcohol and drug use, post-traumatic stress disorder and suicidality. Survivors of GBV may suffer further because of the stigma associated with GBV. Community and family ostracism may place them at greater social and economic disadvantage. GBV can affect child survival and development by raising infant mortality rates, lowering birth weights, contributing to malnutrition and affecting school participation. It can further result in specific disabilities for children: injuries can cause physical impairments; deprivation of proper nutrition or stimulus can cause developmental delay; and consequences of abuse can lead to long-term mental health problems. Many of these effects are hard to link directly to GBV because they are not always easily recognizable by health and other providers as evidence of GBV. This can contribute to mistaken assumptions that GBV is not a problem. However, failure to appreciate the full extent and hidden nature of GBV—as well as failure to address its impact on individuals, families and communities—can limit societies’ ability to heal from humanitarian emergencies.To mitigate the above EPDC will following the below mitigation measures: • Employ female health staff members with skills and experience working with women and children• Put in place guidelines and mechanisms for monitoring and reporting instances of abuse and exploitation. • Ensure that health staff know how to respond to the specific needs of victims of grave human rights violations, including rape and physical abuse. • ensure that the health facilities are accessible to all• Ensure that beneficiaries know their right to health care, and where/how to obtain it. • Monitor access, discrimination, and whether any services are being diverted.• Identify what are the power dynamics within the intervention area. Who has access to health care? • And The protection services will be main streamed through mobile clinics, as well as various feedback mechanisms and referral pathways

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks:● Conflict lines shift due to conflict activities, affecting the safety/mobility of program staff and areas of proposed intervention● Loss of support from the local governing authorities or councils● Beneficiaries do not want to participate in the project.● Duplication or gaps in activities due to miscommunication amongst humanitarian agencies and stakeholders● High fluctuations in prices of items needed for the project as the waring context escalates● Limited availability of medicines ● Delays with distributions caused by checkpoints/delays in obtaining necessary approvals● Lack of full time medical staff and specialization Assumptions:● The local security situation remains stable within this current time and beneficiaries will not face large scale displacements from the target areas.● The local governing authorities support the project in a continuous manner● Coordination and communication lines with other agencies, other services and with local authorities stay in place● Acceptance and engagement of targeted communities. It is assumed that through investment of resources, sound project design and use of appropriate techniques, the benefits of the action will be accepted by the community. Mitigation:• EPDC will implement a robust contingency plan to fluidly relocate selected vulnerable project locations in the event of a shift in security context which could compromise the selected areas.• EPDC will continue to utilize their strong relationship and reputation with the Ministry of Health and Directorate of Health to identify other opportunities although the risk is deemed very low.• EPDC will meet local government officials on a regular basis and update them on the progress of activity implementation. They will also involve them if there are any modifications from the initial plan.• EPDC will disseminate information about projects within the community and will also organize community events to build acceptance and provide services, in coordination with local authorities.• EPDC will remain an active member of the coordination system at the national level led by UN OCHA. At the local level, EPDC will continue to coordinate with humanitarian actors already implementing projects in the areas of operation.• EPDC continues to strengthen its on-site Field Monitoring capacity to ensure a standardized but flexible approach for verifying programmatic and operational aspects of each hub-level program
