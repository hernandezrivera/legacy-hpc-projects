Project Summary

NRC seeks to improve the overall protective environment in South Sudan by not only responding to people’s most acute material needs during a crisis, but also by promoting their safety and dignity and to prevent, reduce or mitigate the protection risks they face. It is also to address the underlying causes of displacement and to contribute to creating conditions conducive to durable solutions for displaced populations. To this end, NRC proposes a graduated approach to support the protection of displaced and conflict-affected populations, including static, mobile, and emergency responses.
The project will offer the following services:
• Protection context assessments in all locations and, where possible, community-based protection monitoring, in which community focal points will be trained to report on protection needs and risks. Protection incidents will be documented and analysed to understand trends and evolution in the protection context, and results disseminated to communities and relevant stakeholders.
• Provision of individual protection assistance (IPA), to persons with urgent protection needs.
• Creation of community-driven protection (CDP) mechanisms in hard to reach, underserved areas through mobile, emergency response teams. NRC teams will work with communities to establish a series of local committees, including marginalized groups, through careful selection of representatives to ensure effectiveness and inclusiveness. CDP teams will mobilise community volunteers and solidarity networks to implement community protection initiatives and actions plans.
• Facilitate improved access to legal identity documentation, including nationality certificates for displacement affected persons who cannot access basic rights and services, especially school-aged children who have challenges applying for state exams. NRC will provide both awareness raising sessions to at-risk populations and individualised assistance in accessing national identity documentation.
• Foster improved ability to exercise HLP rights for individuals and groups affected by displacement with priority given to the most vulnerable, including female and child-headed households, the elderly, individuals with physical and mental disabilities, and those who are extremely poor. NRC will target conflict-affected areas and areas of potential return. NRC will provide awareness raising sessions to communities, as well as offer tailored individual counselling to vulnerable persons requiring further support including cash assistance to access legal services and representation in court.
• Enable peaceful coexistence and support inclusive resolutions of local conflict through training and mentoring in collaborative dispute resolution (CDR) mechanisms. CDR trainings will target chiefs, youth groups, older people, opinion leaders, customary authorities, local government authorities, and include members of host, returnee, and IDP communities. NRC will ensure that women are well-represented. Trainings will be complimented with monitoring and mentoring, and support to see inclusive solutions realized.
NRC is in the process of building up this suite of protection interventions, and will focus on piloting projects in selected locations, with the intention to ultimately offer all protection services in all areas of existing static implementation. All services will be offered in Jonglei (specifically Bor and Duk), as well as in Kajo Keji. NRC will focus on expanding community based protection responses in Unity (Mayom).However, NRC will focus exclusively on HLP response in Northern and Western Bahr el Ghazal. In areas subject to recurrent support from mobile teams a narrower version of the project will be offered, based on local needs and prioritisation and other services available. Emergency responses will focus on protection needs assessments, awareness raising and information, and provision of IPA.

1 - Needs

Decades of recurrent conflict and instability have resulted in a protective environment that is almost entirely eroded and a humanitarian crisis that remains acute. Even should the R-ARCSS agreement hold, the country will remain politically divided and fragile. A nominal peace at the national level is unlikely to address local-level conflicts, ethnic divisions, mass human rights violations, and associated displacement, while at the same time prompting increasing in-flows of IDPs and refugee returns and settlement in new areas. Indeed, inter-communal tension and violence was identified as the single greatest protection concern for 58% of NRC survey respondents. Should the peace deal collapse, the nature of the conflict will shift, resulting in further fragmentation of armed groups and front lines, often along ethnic lines. Unintentional and deliberate attacks on civilians are likely to continue, including conflict-related sexual violence, targeted killing and destruction of property. Large-scale and widespread displacement, affecting approximately one in three South Sudanese, have fractured social norms and solidarity networks, and weakened community based protection mechanisms, limiting available protection for the population. Ethnic segregation continues to be concerning even under the revitalized peace agreement, specifically related to the redrawing of administrative boundaries and the pressuring of IDPs to return to particular locations, actions which could ethnically reengineer the country with worrisome potential protection outcomes. The struggle for access to and control of land has shaped the nation’s history and will affect the realisation of durable solutions. Land acts as an identifier of community, belonging and place, as well as a source of income, subsistence and survival. With such strong cultural and historical significance, land is inherently contested and politicized, and access to housing, land and property is unequal, well beyond economic constraints. Gender and ethnicity are significant determining factors in who can exercise their rights to housing, land and property (HLP), When it comes to women’s rights to land, the gap between law and practice is significant; despite legal provisions recognizing the equal rights of women, widespread knowledge and application of these laws remain limited. Across all assessments, respondents reported that food insecurity, lack of WASH services, and absence of livelihoods opportunities were due to protection risks. Farmers are consistently unable to access their fields due to insecurity; to gather crops for sale or subsistence risks physical assault and death. The conflict exposes all segments of the population to protection risks. Men and boys are at risk of forced recruitment, restrictions on freedom of movement, ill-treatment and killings. Some 36% of respondents reported these as major protection issues. Girls and women of every age are at heighted risk of displacement. They also face abduction, injury and killings in addition to all forms of gender based violence in their homes and public spaces. Women reported high rates of sexual harassment and assault by armed actors, with little ability to access medial support. Domestic and intimate partner violence was identified as a common risk among 68% of female respondents. As internal displacement is likely to continue, along with increasing refugee returns, there will be a commensurate strain the very limited resources of host communities, including for water and health care, in areas of high return like Bor and Kajo Keji. Likewise, in Aweil, host communities expressed their inability to support the needs of flood-related IDPs. Although communal tensions in all surveyed locations are reported to be at a low level at the moment, resource strain could prove a source of inter-communal tension.

2 - Activities

NRC will implement a community-based protection monitoring project. NRC will synthesize actionable information products relevant to the protection and assistance of displacement and conflict-affected populations and inform further decision-making, prioritisation, advocacy, and programme design for both NRC and the Protection Cluster. The project will train community focal points to collect data and report on protection incidents. Protection monitoring teams will verify incident reports and trends with supplementary key informant and household interviews, as well as focus groups discussions. Utilisation of focal points will result in a greater immediacy of information gathering, as volunteer enumerators will be able to collect and report data in near real time. NRC will offer individual protection assistance (IPA) to persons with urgent protection needs. IPA focuses on addressing a central, immediate protection risk through one-off support with limited follow-up. IPA interventions will engage a client-centred approach aimed at empowering the client – and where appropriate the caregiver – by giving them increased awareness of choices they have in dealing with protection risks, and assisting them to make informed decisions about how to address the risks they face. IPA will usually include some element of material assistance, including facilitate refer, procurement of NFI, or unconditional cash transfer. Through awareness raising and protection monitoring activities, NRC teams will work to identify community members who are active on issues of protection and resilience to serve on gender and age disaggregated community-based protection committees. While individual committees will be gender-specific, in order to foster more open communication between participants on sensitive issues, NRC will ensure that the committees overall are gender-balanced and non-proportionally representative of larger community make-up, ensuring that otherwise marginalized groups are included. Committees will be empowered to identify and analyse protection risks in their own communities, addressing negative coping and promoting positive community-wide coping mechanisms through engagement with community leaders, awareness-raising sessions and sensitization campaigns. NRC staff will conduct awareness raising campaigns informed by protection monitoring findings and HLP assessments targeted to meet specific protection and HLP needs identified by communities. This will be done through community outreach, mass awareness campaigns, as well as Information Education and Communication (IEC) materials. Sessions will be adapted to target specific groups, including women, youth, community and religious leaders, and IDPs, based on subject matter, location, and timing. Minimum sessions at each location will include basic rights and entitlements to HLP under South Sudanese law, means of securing land tenure (especially for female-headed households), and rights of those facing forced eviction. Other session curricula will be developed in response to assessed local needs. NRC will compliment HLP awareness raising sessions with tailored individual counselling to vulnerable persons requiring further support, including legal assistance. Individual counselling will be complemented with a cash based intervention to support extremely vulnerable individuals to access legal support.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

On a case-by-case basis in targeted communities, NRC protection and ICLA teams will make efforts to formally establish linkages with relevant government systems, particularly with regard to legal assistance and HLP interventions. Community-based protection activities will link directly to existing community systems, and work with affected communities to rehabilitate community protection systems that have been damaged by conflict and displacement.

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

The project is in-line with national objectives, including the National Refugee Return Framework as well as other relevant frameworks.It is also aligned to the Work plan of Ministry of Land and Urban Development formerly Ministry of Land, Housing, and Physical Planning, Ministry of Justice, The TNLA Standing Specialised Committee on Lands and Physical Infrastructure and the Housing Land and Property Technical Working Group under the Protection Cluster with regards to the priority areas to be considered and major HLP issues to be addressed.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

In certain of the targeted communities, the protection environment is sufficiently stable to begin linking emergency responses to durable solutions efforts. In particular, emergency HLP activities including conflict-sensitive assessments, awareness-raising, legal assistance, and collaborative dispute resolution will be linked to efforts to strengthen local systems, including traditional courts. Similarly, community-based protection mechanisms will be supported to consider both immediate protection needs and localised peace-building, as well as considering more long-term risks and needs, and envision potential risk mitigation and resilience plans.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

Wherever possible, particularly in emergency response locations, NRC teams will seek to handover cases to local actors, building capacity when it is feasible to do so. In static locations, handover of IPA and legal assistance cases will be considered on a case-by-cases basis, according to need and capacity of local actors. If NRC has not found a suitable local partner for handover - including the communities themselves - NRC will continue to seek funding to ensure that essential support remains in place until such a time as communities are able to respond to their own protection concerns.

8.18 - Please indicate how the risks selected above will be mitigated.

8.12 - In previous interventions, NRC teams have observed that formal and informal community leadership can often act as gatekeepers to affected populations. Some leadership can attempted to direct aid according to local political or cultural conditions, rather than humanitarian need. NRC teams will take several precautionary measures to mitigate this risk. First, teams will engage extensively with leadership to clearly articulate our humanitarian principles and the goals of our intervention, as well as working with leaders to better understand their own accountability to the communities they lead. Second, NRC will explicitly target vulnerable and excluded persons in assessments and protection monitoring, utilizing a 'snowball' sampling technique and similar methodologies to ensure that vulnerable persons are not only canvassed, but proactively engaged in programme implementation and evaluation.8.13 - Any intervention proposing to handle protection information must take special care to mitigate risks of disclosure. The proposed project will handle the personal data of vulnerable persons, including legal information. In order to mitigate against the risk of mismanagement, all staff will receive training in NRC's data protection protocols, as well as on PIM principles and best practices. NRC is currently piloting several data gathering tools for protection monitoring that have been designed to support high levels of data protection, as surveys are not able to be opened upon submission, and all data will be analysed centrally in Juba. For legal and IPA cases, all file will be stored by case number and personal data will be housed separately.8.15 - In the past, NRC has struggled to make its complaint and feedback mechanisms accessible to all populations, especially those in hard-to-reach locations. NRC is currently piloting a hotline reporting mechanism, which will compliment in-person options that will be put in place in consultation with affected communities.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

In line with NRC’s global commitment to ensure that all aspects of humanitarian intervention – including monitoring and evaluation – are informed by and reflect the voices of the communities with which we work, NRC will ensure that monitoring and evaluation activities are inclusive and accessible. Whether utilising IPA case closure and evaluation forms, PDM tools, a centralized feedback hotline that can receive both texts and calls, or more general community evaluation FGDs and KIIs, NRC teams will seek to engage a range of community members from both sexes and across a range of diversity characteristics. In assessment stages and throughout implementation, NRC teams will discuss with communities the ideal mechanisms for monitoring and evaluation to ensure inclusive approaches, including of persons with low literacy levels. In hard to reach areas, or those with emergency responses that might mitigate the kinds of monitoring and evaluation approaches possible in more static locations, NRC will take particular care to discuss with communities safe, realistic options for monitoring and evaluation and means of improving accountability to affected populations.
