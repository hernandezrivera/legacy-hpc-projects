Project Summary

The proposed Child Protection program aims to provide critical child protection services through an integrated approach to address the priority needs of 27,000 children and parents (8,459B, 10,210G, 3,820M 4,520F) at high risk and in hard-to-reach priority geographical areas of Panyijiar, Pariang, Koch and Aweil East in the former Unity and former Northern Bahr el Ghazal States. The proposed interventions contribute to the higher level HPR objectives, the protection cluster as well as the CPSC objectives. It is also directly aligned with the three objectives of CPSC which include: comprehensive case management and family tracing and reunification (FTR) services for UASC according to the South Sudan Case management SOP; improve the social-emotional well-being of children using a combination approaches and tools including center-based, school-based and community-based approaches using PSS Guide (UNICEF), Safe Healing and Learning Space (SHLS) guide, Families Make the Difference- parenting skill curriculum, Supporting Adolescents and their Families in Emergencies (SAFE), and IRC’s Brighter Futures Model. The program shall also build the capacity of IRC child protection workers, protection workers, government officers, and community-based child protection structures in CPiE in order to promote longer-term and sustainable change. Furthermore, the child protection rapid assessment (CPRA) will be conducted with the CP sub-cluster and the information will be shared widely.

1 - Needs

Over 2.5 million people are projected to face acute and severe child protection (CP) risks and in need of some form of child protection intervention (CPSC, Sep 2019). According to the same source, HRP 2020 will target 525,100 people in need of the CP intervention which exceeds 148,600 people from the last year (CPSC, 2019). Unity and the NBeG States are identified as one of the greatest states of concern in 2020, as a result, boys and girls will continue to be exposed to abuse, neglect, exploitation, and violence. Among those in need 36,218 children located in Panyijiar, 20,660 children in Pariang and 20,660 children in Aweil East are in Catastrophe need while 9,506 children in Koch in Severe need. According to the CP sub-cluster priority locations, Panyijiar is in the 1st Tier; Pariang, Aweil East and Koch are in the 2nd tire priorities. Moreover, the January to April 2020 IPC projection indicated that the population in Panyijiar will be in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) food insecurity while the other three Counties of Aweil East, Koch, and Pariang are in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) food insecurity. Furthermore, Panyijiar, Koch and Aweil East are affected by flooding due to the heavy rain which is cutting off-road networks. The communities including displaced people have faced the most complex challenges in accessing various services and children are the prime victim. The child protection concerns prevailing in the area are exacerbated by such a crisis of food insecurity and flooding. Families in such situations adopt different coping mechanisms that significantly impact children’s lives. It presses families to make difficult survival decisions affecting their children such as early marriage in exchange for bride price, child labor to reduce food scarcity in the household, and withdrawing children from school so they can participate in income generation activities. According to the CPSC (Sep 2019), 1421 UASC are found in Panyijiar, 179 in Koch, 58 in Pariang, and 59 in Aweil East living with their relatives and foster parents. The IRC identified 408 UASC in its operating location and reunified 75 children with the support of the UASC Working Group (IRC’s CPIMS, 2019). The IRC has been providing a CP service in Panyijiar, Pariang, and Aweil East, but the coverage is limited due to funding constraints as compared to the high need in the location. In Panyijiar, only 3/4 of the Payams are covered by 2 CP agencies. The most common child protection concerns in 5 counties are UASC, neglect, child labor, physical abuse, emotional abuse, and child marriage. Even though, there is a high need for CFS participation in four Counties where IRC is currently operating only six CFSs established due to the constraint of funding. As the IRC baseline survey in Panyijiar (September 2019) showed most of the caregivers lack parenting skills and knowledge of child development to support their children. Moreover, the members of community-based child protection mechanism structures are strengthened by various training but the members still lack a sense of ownership and commitment. Furthermore, the cattle raiding and communal violence (revenge killings) in and around Koch have hampered the coordinated delivery of protection and health services. As a result, organizations operating in this area are very few while the need is very vast. Therefore, children in Koch County are vulnerable to violence, abuse, labor exploitation, and have been suffering from psychological trauma and distress. The current pressing need, the high rate of child protection concerns, and the limited capacity of the child protection structures in Panyijiar, Pariang, Aweil East, and Koch continue to be a critical priority for this anticipated HRP 2020. Therefore, IRC will expand its geographic locations within Panyijiar (Pacheinjock, Pachack, and Chuk), Pariang (Biu, Nyeel and Pariang), Aweil East and Koch (Gany, Poul, Rier) through static and mobile child protection interventions.

2 - Activities

The IRC proposes life-saving child protection interventions focusing on both protective and preventive modalities through static and mobile service targeting the most vulnerable and affected populations, including children in the host community, IDP, returnees and with a disability which is 27,000 children and parents (8,459B, 10,210G, 3,820M 4,520F). This proposed intervention directly contributes to the achievement of the 3 CP objectives, 2 objectives of protection (PO1 & PO2) and the 3 objectives of the HRP (SO1, SO2 &SO3). The intervention will focus on 3 major components of child protection which include: comprehensive case management/FTR services; psychosocial support through community-based interventions and strengthening community-based child protection mechanisms. The IRC will use the SS Comprehensive Case Management SOP, PSS Guide (UNICEF), Safe Healing and Learning Space (SHLS) guide, Families Make the Difference- parenting skill curriculum, Supporting Adolescents and their Families in Emergencies (SAFE), and IRC’s Brighter Futures Model. Planned activities include (a) Comprehensive Case Management: The IRC will continue to provide case management services including family tracing and reunification (FTR) to the most vulnerable children aligned with the SS vulnerability and risk assessment criteria matrix and affected by the food insecurity and flooding. The IRC will use the customized national inter-agency case management forms to manage individual cases, conduct child and family assessments, work with caregivers and children to establish care and safety goals, create plans to achieve those goals, provide direct and indirect services to meet needs identified in assessments, monitor progress toward achievement of those goals, and close cases when goals have been achieved. In addition, revise the existing service mapping and referral pathway in all the operational areas in collaboration with the CP working group. Un-restricted and conditional cash-based intervention (CBI) is one of the services for vulnerable children and parents to fulfill their basic needs as a package of case management. Economic and social reintegration for formally released CAAGAG will be prioritized. (b) Psychosocial Support: The IRC will also establish PSS service using different approaches of SHLS, PSS clubs in school, SAFE, and Parenting Skill training. This program will rehabilitate and establish new SHLS and enroll children for PSS/SEL. It has been shown to mitigate the effects of adversity, by providing children with the tools to focus, regulate their emotional responses, interact with others, and cope with stress and challenges. The SHLS will be staffed by community facilitators who will receive training on UNICEF’s PSS manual and IRC’s SEL and SHLS management standards. (c) Community-Based Child Protection Mechanism (CBCPM): Finally, the CBCPC will be capacitated to better protect from and respond to abuse, neglect, exploitation and violence of children. The CBCPC members will be trained on CPiE, case identification, and referrals to the CP actors. CP help-desks will be rehabilitated or established in schools, health and nutrition facilities. In addition, continuous community awareness of CP will be conducted using different platforms. Child Protection Rapid Assessment (CPRA): the IRC will conduct the CPRA with the support of the CPSC, CPWG and other CP implementing agencies in the operating locations using child-friendly tools. Besides, the CP team will continue to coordinate with the IRC’s nutrition, health, livelihood and GBV to ensure comprehensive care. The existing health and nutrition facilities serve as a screening and awareness-raising spots and will establish CP help desks. IRC’s GBV centers will serve as a point of referral for rape survivor girls and the livelihood section will support the cash-based intervention. Moreover, the CP team will be part of the integrated mobile team in all locations where IRC is operating.

6.6 - Will beneficiaries' rights be legally recognised?

Yes, the South Sudan government formally recognized the rights of children using international legal binding documents like;-UNCRC, international human rights law and its Child Act, 2008. Also, the IRC aligned with the frameworks of humanitarian principles.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

Yes, this program will target both IDPs and the host community, so that the displaced affected people's perspective definitely will be included and valued throughout the project cycle management.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

Yes, the proposed program has a community-based child protection structures which included local government/ IO administration, community gatekeepers, religious leaders, women representatives, youth representatives, police, and other service providers in the community. The program will build the capacity of this structure so as to prevent and respond to violence against children.

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

Yes, the program is aligned to both the National Refugee Return Framework and the South Sudan Humanitarian Response Plan 2020 SO3- access to critical services and resilience building. It is aligned with the CPSC 3rd objective of strengthening the capacity and resilience of children and communities to prevent and respond to violence through community-based mechanisms, coordination and evidence-based response.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

Yes, the IRC proposed this program to both relatively stable and unstable areas in NBeG and Unity state respectively. The majority of Counties in Unity state are controlled by SPLA-IO and conflict-affected areas. However, with an anticipation of the revitalized peace agreement the area would be relatively stable.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

Yes, the capacity-building intervention of the community structure is one of the sustainability strategies.

8.18 - Please indicate how the risks selected above will be mitigated.

IRC anticipates the risks of marginalization of the minority groups while doing consultations, beneficiaries’ lack of access to referral services for survivors of protection risks, interference of external stakeholders like local government/administration in the impartial selection of beneficiaries and risk of exposure of violence as a result of the distance to services. This program will promote accountability, meaningful participation, access, safety, and dignity. IRC will continue to work with CP sub-cluster and other humanitarian agencies to ensure all minority groups are incorporated in the consultation and service provision. To mitigate this, the program will set clear targets for the assessment and service provision which is disaggregated by gender, age and vulnerability status including disability so that anyone will not be left out during consultation and service provision. In addition, the child protection team will join IRC’s integrated mobile team to deliver holistic service for the children and community members who live in the hard-to-reach areas, and at the same time community-level awareness-raising will be conducted and it would help the community to know where the services are available. Moreover, the Community-based Child Protection Structure will have the role of overseeing the targeting and work to avoid biased selection of beneficiaries. After the beneficiary selection, this structure will disclose the list of identified beneficiaries for open community critics. If the community members come up with a complaint, the responsible IRC CP staff will investigate the issue and take the appropriate measures up to substituting from the waiting list. Any complaints or feedback coming from the beneficiaries will be taken seriously based on the IRC’s client responsiveness approach. The mobile service approach also will reduce the exposure of beneficiaries to violence as a result of the distance to the location of humanitarian assistance and services.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

The IRC has developed as part of its good and great standards a mechanism of Client Responsiveness to ensure that the target population is involved in the whole project cycle. Accountability to the affected population will be realized through various approaches including country leadership, emphasis on transparency, feedback and complaints mechanisms, participation in project implementation, monitoring and evaluation. The IRC will adapt and coordinate a client-responsiveness approach integrated into the IRC programs. Through the client responsiveness approach, the IRC will provide and manage context-appropriate communication and feedback channels that are safe, accessible, transparent and confidential for clients to provide feedback and complaints on IRC services as well as seeking information and receiving timely, appropriate responses about IRC’s actions and decisions. Moreover, specific to this program there will be mass awareness-raising sessions where the community members including children and adults can participate and express any concerns regarding the entire project as well as the child protection concerns in the community. Additionally, there will be monthly meetings with the CBCPM members to understand the feedback received from the wider beneficiaries during their day-to-day interactions. The complaint and feedback mechanism will be set up during the project initiation stage with beneficiaries being sensitized on beneficiary accountability standards. Views and feedback will be collected from community beneficiaries, school authorities, teachers and children from the start of the intervention. Feedback and complaints will generally be collected through FGD’s (with groups of men, women, boys, and girls including those with disabilities), during community awareness sessions and individually.
