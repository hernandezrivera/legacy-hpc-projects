Project Summary

IOM priority is to ensure that programming is accountable to the affected populations of the Rohingya refugee response. The program includes over 30 Feedback and Information Centers (FIC) across 18 camps in IOM Area of Responsibility (AoR), the development and management of the Common Feedback Platform (CFP) and regular community engagement and awareness sessions. A team of Rohingya Researchers conduct consultations to ensure community voices are included in strategic planning. Gender, Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) and Accountability to Affected Population (AAP) principles are mainstreamed throughout all activities. Data is sex, age and disability disaggregated (SADD), and vulnerable populations receive tailored programming approaches. IOM participates in regular DRR information campaigns, early warning, and response efforts. The affected population is consulted at all stages of the program implementation cycle, and findings from consultations contribute to program design and are communicated across the response.
Feedback mechanisms & community engagement: Through FICs and door to door visits IOM routinely receives and responds to community feedback. IOM is also committed to uphold the Community Feedback Mechanisms (CFM) standards and has developed the CFP application which enables safer and more efficient processing and management of community feedback data. The CwC technical specialists will continue to improve the CFP and provide technical support to agencies and sectors across the response to ensure CFM smooth operation and adherence to standards. To ensure the CFP is accessible to all, weekly meetings are held with different camp committees and leaders, including imams, women, girls, boys, and men through the Rohingya-led Block Engagement program. This program also includes outreach visits to single female headed households, elderly headed households, households containing member/s with disabilities and child headed households. These meetings link the affected population to site management and the CFP system and ensure they regularly receive important information related to camp operations, health, WASH, distributions, site development works and emergency preparedness. The aim is also to increase the affected populations' understanding of humanitarian programs and provide avenues for the affected population to regularly participate in the identification of priority needs and inform program design and implementation.
Consultations and research: IOM has teams of Rohingya male and female qualitative researchers, transcribers, and quantitative enumerators. Through these teams IOM will produce multi-sector assessments, track community perceptions and regular thematic consultations that elevate community voices and increase understanding of Rohingya people and culture. The focus is to provide a space for the affected population to openly express their concerns, experiences and preferences.
Capacity development: IOM will support the CWC working group partners to adopt the CFP and adhere to APP standards through providing technical guidance and camp trainings such as trainings on Rohingya language and respectful communication for non-Rohingya IOM staff and trainings on facilitation and humanitarian systems for Rohingya volunteers. IOM together with BSF will engage youth groups to provide a series of trainings to support youth to design and implement their own micro community projects.
IOM will also conduct conflict-sensitive media engagement trainings to foster social cohesion and peaceful coexistence between Rohingya and host community. IOM will organize workshops and cross-community visits aimed to strengthen media capacity regarding documenting and framing aspects of the crisis in a conflict-sensitive manner and produce media content such as information and multimedia campaigns, photography and art.

1 - Identified Needs

IOM has continued to help reveal systemic shortfalls of the response that hinder the affected populations' ability to communicate their needs, access assistance and participate in the delivery of aid, as documented in various reports such as the COVID-19 Explained series in 2020, Anarar Bafana: Our Thoughts and Rohingya Hobor. The overarching finding from the reports is that the majority of Rohingya feel overwhelmingly frustrated and helpless as passive recipients of aid, with participants in 72% of female FGDs and 67% of male FGDs report not feeling engaged in decision-making processes. Mahjis, volunteers, literate people, religious leaders, and older men are more likely to be included in decision-making consultations than illiterate people, younger people, and women and girls. Quantitative needs assessments are not interpreted as inclusion in decision-making because they do not give space for people to voice opinions, raise issues, and discuss solutions. Many people are uncomfortable sharing opinions and engaging with humanitarians. This is especially the case for women, older persons, and members of the community that have less access to public space. The lack of culturally appropriate and respectful greetings and language and cultural differences between the Rohingya and those providing assistance is hindering access to assistance. The capacity of agencies to appropriately engage with the community needs to be improved and more attention is needed on how assistance is delivered. Rohingya lack the understanding of their rights as recipients of aid and want more clarity around program delivery and certain decisions. This creates frustration, dissatisfaction, unmet needs and distrust between providers and recipients, and prevents meaningful participation in aid delivery. Rohingya communities need regular engagement with operational actors to develop a relationship with those working in their area. This would increase their understanding of program delivery, participation in decision-making, ability to communicate problems and receive adequate and timely feedback. Agencies and sectors also need to harmonize their approaches to CFM. Currently, referral of community feedback and the response back to the community lack consistency and take longer time to process and resolve. According to the Our Thoughts report, in around 50% of FGDs people recalled negative experiences when trying to report problems with services and assistance. The main problem is there was no response provided despite repeated complaints, resulting in a lack of reliance and trust in CFMs. The humanitarian response needs a collective push to use common operational principals for community feedback. Social research on cultural communication practices, community structures, and religious traditions is required to ensure appropriate and successful interventions. Consultations with different Rohingya stakeholders are hampered by a lack of representational structures, as the consultations should be participatory and tailored to gender, age, and disabilties. Many people are unable to fully comprehend key messages related to disaster preparedness, health and WASH. The lack of coordination between actors, poor engagement strategies, failure to test messaging prior to dissemination with different sex and age groups and weak facilitation skills of outreach volunteers and staff hinder successful message delivery.Moreover, the relationship between the Rohingya and Bangladeshi communities has deteriorated over the last four years. The COVID-19 containment measures increased economy hardship, while also restricting the interaction between the two populations. The increase in competition is fueled by news outlets that repeatedly use negative stereotypes to depict the Rohingya, which increase tensions and impact opportunities for cooperation.
