Project Summary

Nine years of crisis have had a catastrophic impact on Syria’s economy and the well-being of people. This has resulted in an economic environment characterised by high basic commodity prices, shortage in life necessities, low wages and high rates of unemployment and poverty. Since the onset of the crisis, the significant downscaling in the government social welfare programmes – as a result of fiscal pressures – has further led to a lack of support for many vulnerable children and families. People with disabilities are among the most vulnerable sections of the population, both socially and economically. Global evidence shows that they are more likely to be poor and have more difficulties in accessing services – especially when services are already depleted. In a context where poverty is widespread, families with children with severe disabilities have even less opportunities to provide for their livelihoods – mainly as a result of the full-time care requirements and disruption of social care services.
Social protection interventions can be key to enhance the resilience of the most vulnerable families in the face of adversities of crisis and displacement in Syria, to strengthen the continuum of humanitarian and early recovery interventions, and to preserve the social protection space in the country. This programme focuses on children with severe disabilities and complements regular cash transfers with professional case management services. Therefore, the programme seeks to address both economic and social vulnerabilities of children with disabilities and their families. While the cash transfer responds to the “demand side” barriers in terms of financial barriers to access services, the key role of the case managers is to ensure that certain “supply side” barriers, such as unfavourable attitudes or reluctance of schools to enrol children with severe disabilities, are being addressed. In addition to public sector services, the case managers also have a role in maximising the enjoyment of children with disabilities and their families of other types of assistance available through other national and international relief organisations. This includes devising individual action plans for girls and boys in the programme to address their specific needs and circumstances. The services of the case managers also include facilitating the referrals of care givers to livelihood support initiatives, such as vocational and entrepreneurship training or microfinance services, available at the local level. Therefore, case management is an integral component of this programme that seeks to empower the family to better respond to the needs of their children with disabilities beyond the life of this cash transfer intervention. The findings of monitoring surveys in 2018-2019 has registered important positive effects of the programme on the well-being of the children and their families. The households’ investment in education and health of their children with disabilities increased after the cash assistance by 130% and 90% respectively. According to the surveys’ findings, the programme had helped children with disabilities continue education, and reduced school drop-out rate by 70 percentage point on average compared to before the cash assistance. The monitoring surveys also reported high level of satisfaction among the beneficiaries regarding the amount and modality of the cash transfers. In-depth case studies also demonstrated the positive role of the case managers assigned to individual families in enabling them to better respond to the multiple needs of their children with disabilities, and better access the available services. The monitoring surveys registered a decrease in negative coping strategies among the beneficiary households, with over 50% reporting that they no longer needed to cut on their essential health expenditures and around 30% reporting that they no longer needed to cut on their food intake.

4 - Needs

Crisis has taken a toll on children; Syria's human development indicators have regressed by almost four decades since the beginning of the crisis. According to the UN estimates in 2019 around 11.7 million people were in need of humanitarian assistance in Syria, including 5 million children. According to the national SDG report, it is estimated that over 63 per cent of the population live below the poverty line. During the last quarter of 2019, the Syrian currency lost over half of its value against the US Dollar. The fast depreciation of the national currency compounded by deterioration of the security situation in the north of the country and the reinforcement of the sanctions on oil imports resulted in higher fuel prices. In the 2020 budget bill of the country, government is foreseeing further reductions in the subsidies for fuel, oil derivatives and other essential items. The effects of subsidies reduction combined with further depreciation of the national currency is expected to result in higher commodity prices which in turn might push the already poor and vulnerable sections of the population deeper into poverty. Under economic duress, households adopt different coping responses that are magnified by the multiplicity of shocks and are often found to be disproportionately affecting women and children. Selling household assets, borrowing, reducing food consumption and relying on child labour are among the most common negative coping strategies in the face of economic hardship. People with disabilities are among the most vulnerable sections of the population, both socially and economically. In a context where poverty is widespread, families with children with severe disabilities have even less opportunities to provide for their livelihoods – mainly as a result of the full-time care requirements and disruption of social care services.

5 - Implementation of activities

The programme combines regular cash transfers with professional case management services to address both economic and social vulnerabilities of children with disabilities and their families. While the cash transfer responds to the “demand side” barriers in terms of financial barriers to access services, the key role of the case managers is to ensure that certain “supply side” barriers, such as unfavourable attitudes or reluctance of schools to enrol children with severe disabilities, are being addressed. In addition to public sector services, the case managers also have a role in maximising the enjoyment of children with disabilities and their families of other types of assistance available through other national and international relief organisations. UNICEF uses services of a third-party financial institution for the distribution of the cash and has set up a solid monitoring system based on clear segregation of roles to ensure transparency and effectiveness of the programme. All case managers in the programme receive professional training based on a comprehensive package that has been introduced by UNICEF and endorsed by the national government. The beneficiaries are identified by the implementing partners based on the defined eligibility criteria applied to the entire geographical area where the programme is implemented. A randomized verification of the eligibility of beneficiaries is conducted at the beginning of the programme by UNICEF field staff, while case managers conduct on-site monitoring throughout the implementation. The case management component is critical in identifying and addressing the distinct needs of girls and boys with disabilities. The eligibility criteria and the purpose of the programme are clearly communicated with the local communities through the implementing partners. UNICEF also uses periodic monitoring surveys to document the beneficiaries’ feedback and perceptions and to assess the efficiency and effectiveness of the programme.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

The main risk that the population involved could be exposed to is social tensions generated either by lack of clarity on eligibility criteria, or harassment towards those receiving the cash transfer. Mitigation measures include: (i) clear communication strategy on eligibility criteria and selection modalities; (ii) strict verification mechanisms (ii) continuous on-site monitoring; (iii) complaint mechanisms, and (iv) rigorous monitoring schemes. UNICEF’s approach to social protection emphasizes the need to prevent and reduce economic and social vulnerabilities. The linkages between social protection and child protection are embedded in the design of the cash transfer scheme through the case management component.

7 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Assumption: The overall assumption of this project is that the economy will recover as the overall situation in the country stabilizes. Risk: The security situation in the country deteriorates and the country falls back into the state of emergency; Mitigation measures: Preparedness and contingency plans in place for scaling up the emergency social protection response.
