Project Summary

The project will provide clean water, sanitation and hygiene knowledge and practice assistance to conflict-affected households, in and out of camp settings. This seeks to enable vulnerable households and communities to meet basic human needs and avoid morbidity/mortality due to related factors, whilst displaced and upon return to their places of origin.

Needs

Critical WASH needs continue into 2019 for both IDPs and for returning households. Whilst many households have left displaced peoples’ camps to return to their places of origin, the returns survey indicate that many are likely to stay on in camps for the foreseeable future. This is due to their places of origin having continued insecurity, remnants of ERW and destroyed infrastructure and services. Other households are perceived to be affiliated with IS and either are unlikely to receive security clearance to return or not be welcome back into their communities. In Kirkuk governorate, 2 of the 6 IDP camps have now closed with a third (Nazrawa) likely in the next few months. It is anticipated that Laylan 1 camp will remain open throughout 2019 and have a continued reliance on humanitarian assistance. Tearfund currently manages the WASH services in both Laylan 1 and Laylan 2 camps. The population at present is 7,778 people. There is a very slow trickle of returns with only a few people leaving the camp each week, and the population likely to increase with the consolidation of Nazrawa camp. The full range of WASH services is needed in the camp to ensure continued health for its residents. This includes the provision of clean water, sanitation, solid waste management, vector control, drainage, provision of basic hygiene items and hygiene promotion to ensure that people know how to and are using the facilities well. With constant interaction between residents and basic living conditions in tents, the control of disease such as diarrhoea, scabies and head lice can also be a challenge and needs continuous follow up, messaging and encouragement of residents to change behaviours. One of the key needs is ongoing maintenance. Due to poor design and construction, infrastructure in older camps such as Laylan 1 is prone to frequent breakages and needs constant follow up for maintenance to quickly restore services, as well as needing retrospective re-design and amendments to avoid ongoing problems. For example, the lack of earthing on electrical wiring on toilet blocks has resulted in electric shocks during rain, or plastic pipes easily broken due to their placement. The frustrations arising from their protracted displacement has caused some residents to engage in destructive behaviour which is sometimes expressed against the WASH infrastructure. Tearfund is working with Mukhtars and communities to try to reduce deliberate breakages or theft by adults, and unruly play by uncontrolled children - and to encourage the community to take ownership whilst ensuring ongoing basic services. WASH continues to be a critical need in areas of return. In a survey of Mukhtars across 482 conflict-affected villages in retaken areas across Hawiga, Daquq and Dibis districts, the number one priority need (stated by 38.7% of Mukhtars) was water supply (Tearfund Assessment, September 2018). Many communities are therefore still reliant on untreated river water for their household needs, whilst others are using shallow boreholes which are sometimes saline, and when consumed maybe contributing to the alarmingly high rates of kidney disease (30-55%, Tearfund Assessment of 2300 households, in Hawija, June - September 2018). Whilst some water treatment systems have been repaired, many are still not producing clean water. Tearfund’s previous experience in certain areas has indicated that the Directorate of Water does not necessarily have the inclination or ability to prioritise the rehabilitation and/or take ongoing ownership of water treatment systems, with the supply of consumables, especially chlorine proving particularly challenging. Household water filters (both standard and reverse osmosis) in this context may therefore provide the most effective way to provide safe water and give individual households more control over their water supply. Water storage is also an issue, with communal and household water tanks systematically looted by IS, damaged by shrapnel, or fallen into disrepair during the years of IS control. Whilst the need for water supply is the greatest WASH challenge in areas of return, in some villages, the lack of household latrines, destruction to sanitation infrastructure by armed conflict or the chronic lack of solid waste management also pose needs that is requested and prioritised by Mukhtars for attention. Public institutions such as health centres and schools also have latrines damaged or fallen into disrepair. As not all schools are yet open, those that are, have an increased number of students which contributes to the insufficient latrines numbers. This is particularly important to ensure access for girl’s education. Tearfund has also seen the need for sessions on menstrual hygiene management, which is often poorly understood and is viewed in conservative contexts as a taboo subject, with women and girls struggling to access basic items due to insufficient local markets.

Activities

1). Provision of safe water: Tearfund will provide sufficient access to safe water to conflict affected households.Within camps, this includes the training and monitoring of borehole operators to ensure that the correct quantities of chlorine are added to the water, regular water tests are conducted and repairs made on the water supply network, including the replacement of broken pipes.Out of camps, Tearfund will conduct assessment and analysis to ensure that the solution to provide safe water is appropriate to the local context. If previously existing infrastructure can be repaired and maintained, then Tearfund will rehabilitate damaged water treatment plants or boreholes or replace specific broken parts such as pumps, as previously done both in Kirkuk and Nineveh governorates. Tearfund will also repair/ install community water tanks (e.g. 50m3) in areas such as Salamiya, and ensure sufficient quantities of water reaching communities through water conveyance infrastructure such as pipelines and water networks. In other areas, household led solutions may be more appropriate such as as the provision of water filters and household water tanks. 2). Sanitation provision: Ongoing sanitation services within the camps includes daily desludging, provision of latrine cleaning kits and maintenance or construction of latrines and cesspools/soak pits. Out of camp, work may target household without basic sanitation, in community buildings such as schools and health centres, ensuring that there are sufficient latrines, handwashing stations and waste management facilities. The design for the latrines are agreed with the community and institution, and includes access for disabled people. 3). Hygiene Promotion and provision of hygiene items: Tearfund provides hygiene consumables every month within Laylan 1 and Laylan 2 camp, with full hygiene kit and water kit distributions every 6 months as well as other kits (disabled, baby, etc) when needed. The kits are based on cluster specifications and on beneficiaries stated needs. Whilst these are currently in-kind, Tearfund is assessing whether vouchers or cash modalities would be more appropriate.Hygiene Promotion is conducted both directly through the Tearfund hygiene team, and training community based hygiene volunteers such as school teachers to run sessions. Topics include handwashing, keeping water safe, sanitation practices and menstrual hygiene management. Dignity/MHM kits will also be provided where appropriate and where it is not possible for households to access basic items locally. 4). Environmental Sanitation: Tearfund will work to ensure that the environment is clean and hygienic. Currently within the camps, this includes ongoing garbage collection and the provision of garbage bins.

Protection Risk Analysis

Tearfund is aware of protection concerns relating to households that are perceived to be affiliated to ISIS. In out-of-camp settings, such as Hawija, Tearfund has been pressured by governmental authorities to share lists of beneficiaries to block assistance going to these households. In line with OCHA and national practice, Tearfund’s stance is not to share lists as this is breaching the mandate that NGOs have for independence. All civilians are impartially entitled to receive humanitarian assistance. This is also a concern within camps, as households perceived to be affiliated to ISIS may not be granted security clearance or welcomed back into their communities of origin. Lacking other options it is likely that they will remain longer-term in camps, with a risk that these turn into informal detention facilities. Gender dynamics continue to raise protection issues, with the power dynamics between men and women often denying women representation or decision-making. This is particularly the case in some of the communities where Tearfund works - in the Kirkuk camps Tearfund has found that women can be restricted to certain areas and are limited with whom they can interact.

Mitigation Measures

Gender is a key issue within Tearfund’s WASH programmes. Tearfund will continue to hold gender-specific focus groups, and ensure that input into design and evaluation is gathered from both genders. For example, in evaluating the modality for hygiene kits it may be that male decision-makers in the household will not appreciate the importance of female-only products, and that cash or unlimited vouchers may therefore not be appropriate. Tearfund seeks to have a gender balance amongst the hygiene promoters, and both male and female Mukhtars exist within the camps.

How is your project accountable to affected population

Understanding the local context: In the assessment and analysis of community’s needs, FDGs are carried out with women, youth and different ethnic/religious groups to get a better understanding of their vulnerabilities and abilities. Key Informant Interviews are used to better understand the impact of the crisis on their activities and their ability to meet basic needs for their families and to identify bottlenecks for successful project activities. For example, hygiene kit samples are shared at FGDs and beneficiaries given the opportunity to feedback on the type of items, quantity and quality. Beneficiary accountability: An independent MEAL team runs a helpdesk at distributions, carries out out spot checks and provides input to the project teams to ensure inclusion of beneficiary feedback into project design details and decisions. The team also meet with beneficiary communities receiving infrastructure to make them aware of avenues to feedback. Individuals of beneficiary communities can contact the free hotline number to report issues and complaints related to the project activities; with each call reviewed and responded with recommendations to improve the project systems or to indicate any action to be taken. Monitoring Evaluation Accountability and Learning (MEAL): As part of the project cycle the teams will be supported by a system of monitoring, evaluation, accountability and learning through the MEAL team who operates independently to the project team. The MEAL team will take responsibility to manage beneficiary database to cross-check transparency and accountability of vulnerability assessments and registration process. Lessons learned workshops will ensure that learning is incorporated into future work.
