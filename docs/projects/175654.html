Project Summary

Pilgrims of Hope, seeks to integrate nutrition to food security and livelihood activities aiming at ensuring most vulnerable HHs that are affected by the worsened Overall food security and where communities are facing catastrophic needs and crisis and where children continue facing acute malnutrition in the past three years and where People’s coping mechanisms have been weakened as a consequence of the cumulative shocks, which had led families to adopt negative practices such as forced labor and child marriage and where the populations have been affected by the economy as it continue to spiral downwards, pushing people to the brink. Through this project POH would like to triangulate support of food production to particularly vulnerable households in Unity State across current operational locations including Mayom, Mayendit and Panyijiar, areas that according to the last IPC report of November 2020 are some of the Counties most severely hit by the increasing food insecurity reaching overall levels of emergency (IPC 3, and 4.
People’s physical and mental wellbeing, living standards and coping mechanisms are expected to further deteriorate in 2021. Some 8.3 million people in South Sudan are estimated to be in need of humanitarian assistance in 2021. These include 8,000,000 South Sudanese women, men, girls and boys and 310,000 refugees and asylum seekers.
An estimated 5.6 million people will be negatively impacted by lack of or inadequate access to clean water and improved sanitation and hygiene practices. Poor access to WASH services and high levels of food insecurity continue to have a detrimental impact on the health of children, as seen through the high prevalence of malnutrition and water-borne diseases. By the end of October, 2020, 593,000 acute watery diarrhea cases were reported, a 60 per cent increase compared to 2019. Low response rates to the flooding in 2020 and upcoming dry season 2021, are anticipated to further exacerbate WASH-related morbidity and incidences of diarrheal diseases, particularly in young children.
Nutrition cluster estimates 1.9 million people will need treatment for acute malnutrition in 2021 and around 1.4 million South Sudanese children under age of 5 and another 483,000 pregnant and lactating women are expected to be acutely malnourished, the highest number in last three years which are related to multifaceted, including severe acute food insecurity, sub-optimal feeding practices, high prevalence of disease, inadequate sanitation conditions and hygiene practices, and limited access to health and nutrition services driven by sub national conflict, floods and Covid-19. The proxy prevalence of Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) among under-five children ranges from 1.3 per cent to 33.4 per cent and in total, 47 counties out of 78 have a proxy prevalence of GAM above the emergency threshold of 15 per cent.
POH integrated programming and linkages to WASH and Nutrition will implement the project linking cluster objectives to enhance and to prevent further deterioration by improving access to safe water and promote safe hygiene practices in communities showing high prevalence of GAM under WASH linking Sectoral approaches hence contributing to the overarching strategic objective of the humanitarian community in providing dignified and accessible sanitation services, and equitable access and utilization of quality preventative nutrition-specific services for children, and women in the prioritized locations.

1 - Needs

According to the IPC messages released on Dec 2020, the scale and severity of acute food insecurity continue to rise at the peak of the lean season in South Sudan. Emergency (IPC Phases 3 and 4) outcomes are widespread, indicating that a high proportion of the population is experiencing either large food consumption gaps or engaging in severe coping strategies to mitigate food consumption gaps. In the first projection period of December 2020 to March 2021, an estimated 5.82 million people (48.3% of the population) will likely face Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity. During this period, an estimated 1.79 million people are likely to face Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. In addition, as the economic crisis persists and in an existing context of sub optimal market integration, affecting especially urban based and other market dependent persons (vulnerable food deficit farming households reliant on day labor). The effect is most severe where there is convergence of these multiple shocks (floods effects, communal conflict and population displacement, economic crises and indirect effects of COVID-19 in terms of disruptions to the supply chains of both commercial and humanitarian assistance, that have resulted in very high levels of chronic poverty, vulnerability and acute malnutrition/ food insecurity with populations resorting to the use of negative coping strategies as their livelihoods are disrupted, kinship support mechanisms over stretched and remaining assets depleted. In order to respond to this dire situation, as per Famine Early Warning Systems Network (FEWNET) Food Security Outlook for October 2020 to January 2021 period, the selected counties of Panyijiar (population in IPC 3 -64,408 and 29,276 in IPC 4 are food insecure representing 80% of the county population), Mayom, (total population in IPC 3 -91,727 and 30,576 in IPC 4 are food insecure representing 80% of the total county population) and Mayendit, ( population in IPC 3 – 37,991 and 13,815 in IPC 4 are food insecure, which represents 75% of the total county population), POH proposed FSL project intervention in Panyijiar, Mayendit and Mayom aims to increase food availability and access by providing emergency livelihood support kits and adaptive training to most flood affected vulnerable IDPs, host community and returnees, subsistence farmers and fisher-folk households to increase fish and vegetable production, mainly for consumption at the household level and sale of surplus at local markets. The proposed project targets of 9,000 beneficiaries in the three counties with emergency fishing, livelihood kits, vegetable kits, and skills training in modern agronomy. The location was chosen in accordance to the Food Security Outlook Update for South Sudan (IPC December 2020) in which Mayendit County which has about 51,806 people and Panyijiar had 93,684 affected by the floods and Mayom and are in IPC phase 3 & 4.The project will support the beneficiaries with fishing kits, crop kits and vegetable kits which avails immediate benefits by providing white meat for the HHs (fish) as well as providing an alternative source of income through the sale of surplus fish. This income will be used to supplement the HHs food requirements as well as address basic HHs needs. To ensure maximum realization of benefits as well as ensure sustainability, HHs will be trained on improved fish production practices such as catch hygiene, fish post-harvest and preservation as well as utilization and marketing techniques to promote market access. Vegetable kits will help them to address their nutritional requirement in the short-term. The Income from the sale of surplus produced will be used to purchase alternative food requirements such as sorghum as well as supplement the HH dietary needs. Farmers will receive basic training on improved agronomic practices with regards to small-scale vegetable production.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

POH has zero tolerance to any form of inappropriate behavior involving its staff, partner staff, contractors and visitors. First, the staff, partner staff, contractors and visitors are required to sign the code of conduct that spells out the standard of behavior expected of them. Secondly, all those mentioned have an obligation to report any form of witnessed or suspected case of such as outlined in our policy on reporting channels. Thirdly, POH has an obligation to report to donors any form of abuse that has taken place and measures that are taken to address it. Finally, there are safeguarding staff and committees in each location where we operate that are responsible for disseminating the good practices that are expected of staff and so all staff to sign PSEA code of conduct and adhere to it as well as putting in place SEA reporting mechanisms and conducting awareness on its utilization and usage to the beneficiaries is critical in protection against sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA).

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

By using the already systematic procedures for reviewing, investigating and responding to feedback or complaints. We have always ensured that, this occur in a consistent, timely manner for the beneficiaries to believe in the system and use it. This is because system for processing and responding to feedback is crucial regardless of what feedback mechanisms we do use. For instance, we have developed procedures for responding to anonymous feedback that are submitted through complaints boxes. Most non-sensitive issues, such as suggestions for changing delivery times or basic changes to project operations are handled locally. The resulting changes that occur due to this feedback are announced public-ally through beneficiary meetings. And for sensitive complaints, such as allegations of corruption, we have a divined procedure where an independent team is allowed to carry out an investigation on the feedback received and then communicates the information and decisions back to the community members. The process is set up to channel these issues up the organizational chain (field level) or to the headquarters level if necessary. The decision is then channeled back down the chain and changes are made by the local management.We also uses a process for setting up a complaints and response procedure which includes the following questions and considerations: • To whom should the complaint be forwarded? Guidance on distinguishing and sorting of sensitive complaints versus non-sensitive complaints, e.g. ‘sensitive’ could include sexual exploitation and abuse. ‘Non-sensitive’ could include late delivery of non-food items. • Which level of management needs to be informed and when? When will HQ need to be informed? • What is the time frame for responding to complaints? • Who will communicate the response to the complainant and how?The procedure also includes an appeals process in the event that the need arises for this. POH do suggest that other national organizations and partners should be clear—with their own staff and with recipients—about what types of feedback they are able to deal with or influence. For instance, if someone complains about an issue outside the agency’s jurisdiction (i.e. areas not in an agency’s control or location of operations, or about services or actions that are beyond the agency’s capacity and mandate), the organization should refer the feedback to the appropriate authority or structure that can respond or help in dealing with it. By deciding ahead of time how to handle feedback, staff should be able to respond more efficiently and effectively back to the beneficiaries.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

For instance, when designing our livelihood programs (kitchen gardening) , we do ask local communities and our partners for ideas on what kind of livelihood projects would be helpful and how to make them sustainable instead of the traditional approach of providing prepared "menus" of potential livelihood projects. We conduct one or two listening activities every year and hold workshops to gather feedback and lessons learned with our partners. By regularly listening to feedback from those affected by our programs or policies, we have been able to design better programs and to implement them with less external support. In some locations, we have realized that, people/beneficiaries fear to report or raise a concern and so in such locations, we have chosen not to call it a “complaints mechanism” given that most of our people/beneficiaries are afraid to lodge complaints for fear of reprisals. Instead, we have called the process “SINFOR” which stands for “Suggestions, Information, Reclamation,” and placed emphasis on gathering and responding to recipient suggestions.And in other locations, we have worked with suggestion boxes, a dedicated phone number and an email address to receive feedback, and a “help desk” and staff during program cycle so that people/beneficiaries could give feedback and get answers to questions about the project. Beneficiaries provided feedback and if they leave their contact information, they do receive a response within two weeks to avoid delay. And when staff receive repeated or surprising feedback related to problems with the project, they investigated whether programming changes are necessary and if so, what they should be doing as a right mean to meet the beneficiary’s expectations. Other approaches that we have used to handle complaints and influenced our decisions, including a free telephone number for recipients to call with feedback and complaints, and providing beneficiaries with a tape recorder to lodge complaints especially where education and language barrier exist and where these can affect the quality and origin of the reporting complain/concern. We also hold community meetings with recipients to hear their complaints, opinions and suggestions and to discuss the steps that we took to resolve the issues.
