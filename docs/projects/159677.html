Project Summary

The proposed project (approved 2019 HRP HPSE19-WSH-150501-1) will target the most vulnerable families in Area C and in Hebron’s H2 area. These groups suffer from no, or limited, access to water and sanitation services due to Israeli restrictions on construction and development in the area. This makes them particularly vulnerable to the threat of forced displacement. The target communities are farming and herding families dependent on livestock and agricultural activities to maintain their livelihoods and, as such, heavily reliant on water availability, quality and affordability. Lack of water has forced families to adopt negative coping mechanisms such as reducing their consumption to an average of less than 30 l/d/c (Area C Water Master Plan 2017, updated in April 2019) depending on unsafe water resources or reducing their expenditure on basic needs such as health and education to afford the high cost of water.
(1) By constructing and rehabilitating water networks, ﬁlling points and household cisterns, the proposed project will decrease the target communities’ dependency on trucked water as well as deter them from sourcing unsafe water.
(2) The project will also improve the communities’ access to sanitation facilities through the construction and provision of household WASH facilities such as latrines and plastic tanks. Families often disregard these facilities and prioritise other basic needs such as food and water. This negative coping mechanism has left many households in poor health, hygiene, privacy and dignity conditions.
(3) Thus, to complement these activities, AAH will also carry out training and awareness workshops on subjects ranging from hygiene promotion to water saving techniques and cistern management.
Ultimately, by mitigating the effects of the Israeli occupation on water access and thus improving the target communities’ livelihood prospects, the proposed project will reduce their exposure to forced displacement and provide them with the means to show resilience in the face of these challenges. The proposed intervention ensures protection mainstreaming as it is guided by protection principles and considers safety and dignity, meaningful access, accountability as well as participation and empowerment.

1 - Needs

According to the 2020 Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO), 294,000 people across the West Bank are either unconnected to a water network or receiving water once a week or less. Area C communities in the West Bank, particularly Southern Hebron, East Jerusalem periphery and the Jordan Valley, are particularly affected by this issue. Approximately 300,000 Palestinians in Area C are directly affected by the Israeli restrictions and control over WASH-related infrastructure, such as water and sanitation networks. Most recent assessments completed by AAH in April 2019, in Area C, indicate that water scarcity is one of the main factors leading vulnerable families to poverty and food insecurity, as well as further deteriorating living conditions and hygiene practices. Most Bedouin and herding communities in Jericho, Ramallah and the East Jerusalem periphery receive water through old water pipes laid over-ground. Irregular water supply, the small size of old water pipes, high water losses and the destruction of water pipes by settlers and the ICA (e.g. in the R317 communities of South Hebron) restrict access to safe, suﬃcient and affordable water. This places families in a situation of great ﬁnancial vulnerability and forces them to displace closer to ﬁlling points and water sources during the summer. This movement complicates their return to the communities and places them at risk of permanent displacement due to settler harassment and settlement expansion. Data from AAH’s 2017 Area C Water Master Plan and Water Distribution Modeling (updated in April 2019), validated by the PWA, highlighted that most of the targeted communities consume less than 50 l/c/d and use rain harvesting cisterns and trucked water as coping mechanisms for the water shortage. Thus, adequate water storage capacity is vital for vulnerable families to harvest rainwater in winter and store trucked water during dry months. In August and September 2019, AAH, through the West Bank Protection Consortium, used a community-based approach to assess 78 communities in the southern, central and northern West Bank. It established that many families in Area C lack latrines and do not have suﬃcient water storage capacity (< 5m3). Most of them must buy water using small tanks (3.7m3 tractors) instead of large tanks (10m3) with prices up to 30 NIS/m3 as the trucked water cost is affected by road conditions, tank capacity, distance and ﬁlling time. Distance from consumer to water source and road conditions also considerably affect the price of trucked water. High water prices and limited income force families to reduce their water consumption to as little as 30-50 l/c/d. Women, many of whom are widowed, disabled or divorced, must borrow water from relatives and neighbors, which is then stored in small containers or kitchen pots. According to AAH’s sex and age disaggregated data and gender analysis in August-September 2019, men and women have distinct tasks: men provide payments for water truck services, while women are responsible for the storage and use of water. Women are often the most disadvantaged when families face water shortages or when latrines are not available, speciﬁcally for speciﬁc needs such as menstrual hygiene. AAH and educational directorates conduct regular assessments and coordination meetings on WASH facilities in schools in Hebron, Bethlehem, East Jerusalem, Tubas, etc. These meetings, ﬁeld visits and interviews with women, men, students and teachers indicated that many schools are not connected to a regular water supply and/or have poor WASH facilities. Substandard sanitation facilities in schools increase children’s exposure to health risks, unhealthy social environment and the school dropout rate among girls, specifically because of privacy issues.

2 - Activities

Coordination meetings with the PWA, Ministry of Education (MoE), UNICEF and other international agencies (GVC, CISP and PAH) will avoid gaps and overlapping. Local partners in the targeted locations (municipalities, village councils and RCSD), will collect baseline and end line data. Said partners have been fully involved in the project’s design (identiﬁcation of target communities and development of technical components). Together with AAH, they will be responsible for monitoring the implementation of activities and for the collection of feedback from project beneﬁciaries. RCSD staff will be trained on designing and monitoring WASH activities in sensitive areas, their ﬁeld oﬃcers will work directly with AAH technical staff and the targeted groups to ensure the quality of implementation and further empower the beneﬁciaries. Based on their extensive experience in implementing cistern and road rehabilitation activities, RCSD will take the lead in assessing, designing and implementing said activities. RCSD will also be involved in the ﬁnal handover of the implemented activities. (1) Rehabilitation and construction of ﬁlling points and simple water networks for Bedouin and herding communities in southern Hebron, Bethlehem, Jordan valley, and East Jerusalem periphery. The pipelines will be connected to the PWA and municipality systems according to the Area C Water Master Plan. AAH will also rehabilitate and/or install 20 simple water networks using high-density polyethylene (HDPE) and steel pipes. Wherever possible, pipes will be buried below ground level by the village council or the beneﬁciaries themselves as a protective measure against ICA uprooting or cutting, for H2 AAH and RCSD will target the families who have poor domestic WASH facilities (internal pipes and ﬁttings) and will also target the water distribution network in the movement restricted neighbourhoods of H2 area (2) Construction and rehabilitation of household cisterns in Area C and seam zones. According to the Area C Water Master Plan endorsed by PWA, 25 household cisterns will be constructed in South Hebron and the Jordan valley for communities that cannot be connected to a piped water service. Based on a socio-economic baseline survey, female headed households and families with disabled members will be prioritized. The activities will be implemented directly by the households to reduce the risks and increase time and cost eﬃciency. (3) Rehabilitation of 11 km of roads in Masafer Yatta, road 317 and north Jordan Valley clusters. This will facilitate access to the community and reduce the cost of trucked water by enabling access of larger volume water tankers, which are signiﬁcantly cheaper than smaller tractor tanks. (4) Construction and rehabilitation of toilets, 6 cisterns and drinking fountains for 8 schools in Hebron H2 and Area C communities. This will improve student’s access to adequate, safe and healthy WASH facilities, and will also improve the general educational environment, thus upholding the children’s right to education. (5) Supply and installation of WASH facilities for the most vulnerable groups in Area C: - 130 latrines (compliant with SPHERE standards and with gender and age disaggregated analysis of hygiene practices and community social attitudes); - 141 plastic tanks (1.5m3/tank) provided to families affected by drought or when security constraints prevent the construction/rehabilitation of cisterns. (6) Community training and awareness workshops on (i) good hygiene practices (prevention of water borne diseases such as diarrhoea by promoting handwashing); (ii) water saving and recycling techniques and; (iii) and cistern management (chlorination). (7) Increase duty bearers’ awareness on causes and solutions to water scarcity, and the related health and economic impacts, through evidence-based advocacy tailored to inﬂuence their policies and practices.

3 - Indicators

These interventions will contribute to the safety and dignity of women, men, girls and boys, to do no harm and improve the overall protection of the targeted communities. They will do so by improving access to basic WASH services to sustain livelihoods and avoid/respond to displacement, support the WASH advocacy strategy and improve the coordination platforms. The expected results are the following: 1. Improved water quality for vulnerable families: construction and rehabilitation of water networks will decrease the dependency on trucked water and will deter families from sourcing unsafe water. Catchment area improvement, chlorination of cisterns, hygiene promotion campaigns on hygiene practices and cistern management will encourage behavior changes and contribute to a reduction in waterborne diseases. 2. Construction/rehabilitation of ﬁlling points and water networks to increase the availability of water supplies, particularly for women and children in southern and central West Bank. Men and women will participate in the planning, design and implementation of the activities. Families will reduce their expenditure on trucked water, consequently they will have additional disposable income for other basic needs. Women will also need less time to fetch water and will be able to spend more time caring for their children. 3. Improved awareness, knowledge and hygiene practices: Hygiene promotion activities and awareness raising will be provided at household level, with a special focus on women for topics regarding domestic consumption management, as per the previously conducted gender analysis of household roles and responsibilities. The core hygiene promotion messages will be hand washing, eﬃcient use of water, domestic hygiene, cleaning and maintenance of WASH facilities and water storage tanks. 4. Strengthened capacity and resilience of vulnerable communities to respond to adverse environmental conditions resulting from natural disasters: The distribution of water, mobile latrines, shelters and non-food items (plastic tanks, kitchen kits), as well as maintenance training for cistern management and chlorination will improve and strengthen the capacity of vulnerable communities to withstand adverse environmental conditions. 5. Improved information management, coordination and dissemination of information among the cluster members and other stakeholders: The use of GIS and statistical software in data collection will facilitate data comparison and analysis; ad hoc databases will be set up to collect and compare information collected during the project implementation; complemented by AAH’s data management system. Elaborated data will be shared with relevant stakeholders and made available through regular reporting. KAP surveys and general project monitoring and evaluation will be carried out throughout the entire project in coordination with other agencies operating in the area; AAH will contribute to exchange of information/lessons learned, fact sheets and stories among the cluster community and to be used for advocacy purposes. Expected indicators and targets for the project are: • Water expenditure decreased by 30% • Water consumption increased by 30% • Interruptions of water supply in the network decreased by 30% • Water loss in the network decreased by 30% • Number of small expensive water deliveries (with tractors) decreased by 60% • Water storage capacity for 160 families with be improved through the construction/rehabilitation of water cisterns and the provision of PE tanks • WASH facilities in 8 schools improved through construction/rehabilitation of toilets, cisterns and drinking points • Living conditions for 650 women, men, girls and boys are improved through the supply of mobile latrines • 25 communities improved their access to water through the construction/ rehabilitation of water pipelines • 7 communities improved the accessibility to water resources through the rehabilitation of the water pipes.

9 - Protection mainstreaming
9.1 - Do No Harm

AAH considers safety and dignity, meaningful access, accountability as well as participation and empowerment of paramount importance. AAH is in the process of developing its own protection mainstreaming toolkit and to start working with its own sector specific gender and protection mainstreaming checklists. Up to date, AAH has made use of cluster specific Protection Mainstreaming Tip Sheets. Overall AAH’s aim is to take decisive humanitarian action with attention to risk mitigation thereby avoiding doing harm to the greatest extent possible. AAH in oPt prioritises this through context specific (risk) analyses as well as the protection coordinator position stirring questions and discussion. AAH and RCSD (and community representatives, legal organizations and local authorities) will conduct a thorough risk analysis for proposed activities in each targeted location to identify potential risk factors and propose mitigation measures. The following factors will be taken into consideration: 1- Community legal status: to assess if there are military orders or other legal concerns, and to ensure that the proposed intervention will not trigger the community legal case in a harmful way.2- Community location related risks: to assess the community surrounding risks, like the existence of settlements, military base, firing zones and bypass roads, and to ensure that the proposed interventions will be applicable and operational in such context, not exposing beneficiaries to further risks3- Relations and sensitivity inside and outside the community: to assess the different cultural and social relations inside the community and in its surrounding, to ensure that the proposed interventions will not increase the social sensitivity or tension between the community, households, and with the surrounding communities. 4- Type of activity: to assess the community capacities and needs to follow up on activities such as the communities’ ability to operate and maintain the water networks which can contribute to the sustainability of the delivered assistance.In addition to doing no harm, the participatory approach contributes to the safety and dignity of beneficiaries. In order to further inform its assessments and implementation, AAH continuously works on understanding gender roles and household dynamics. By doing so, AAH believes that the organisation mitigates the risks of doing harm. In 2019, AAH conducted a masculinity study in West Bank, focused on identities/roles of men. AAH has in place mechanisms to prevent and respond to sexual exploitation and abuse by all staff. All AAH staff have the obligation to adhere to the organisation code of conduct. AAH also has in place focal points at base and mission levels, as well as safe and confidential ways to report abuse. Moreover, in spring 2019, all staff received specific in person training on the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse. Even more, most of AAH staff have gone through protection cluster facilitated trainings with the aim to know how to detect and refer potential cases of GBV. The indicator “% of ccommunities/project beneficiaries reporting that humanitarian assistance is delivered in a safe and dignified manner” will be added and will be measured through an end line survey of the beneficiaries.AAH will ensure that its activities, especially connecting communities with the pipe water service, will not cause overcrowding of the limited water sources and will not cause competition between the communities regarding water supplies, through the coordination with PWA and water service providers. Moreover, and in cooperation with community representatives, AAH will ensure that the project fully covers the water and sanitation needs of communities and households (each household has the right to have 1 latrine, 1 household water connection and 1 PE tank). Hence the project will aim not to create more tensions between the community families and other surrounding communities.

9.2 - Equal and impartial access to aid

In 2019, AAH has conducted in its Middle East country offices a Protection Mainstreaming Internal Evaluation for which the consultant spent the month of March in oPt. Particular attention has been paid to the way AAH includes vulnerable groups in its work, for instance through participatory assessments. As one of AAH’s core values, AAH thrives to prevent discrimination and to ensure equal and impartial access though transparency and accountability to affected population. AAH aims for all community members to be involved and consulted as much as possible, including with households, community consultations and focus group discussions accessible to all. In order for their participation to be meaningful, AAH focuses on ‘qualitative inclusion’ meaning safe spaces for beneficiaries to participate. Also, AAH started engaging with Humanity & Inclusion as inclusion experts to sensitise and inform AAH’s programming. For instance, instead of asking directly if people have a disability, AAH is starting to use increasingly ‘The Washington Group Short Set of Questions’ to detect people with disabilities. AAH is reporting under the HRP disaggregated data by sex and age; though pending the reporting format AAH usually includes further disaggregation on vulnerability such as data on people with disabilities. In addition, AAH started in its assessments to consider ‘barriers’ of particularly vulnerable groups and ‘enabling factors’ for people with specific needs. AAH has a safe and accessible beneficiary feedback mechanism in place which allows beneficiaries to provide their inputs, complaints, and satisfaction regarding the activities implemented. It is designed to increase their protection and complies with sensitive data management guidelines to uphold the principle of ‘Do No Harm’. Beneficiary knowledge of this system will be promoted through several field assessments and monitoring visits in which staff explains how the mechanism works. In addition, a paragraph will be included in each agreement with beneficiaries explaining the steps to report any complaint through the AAH beneficiary feedback mechanism which is accessible by phone or email according to AAH protocols. AAH will process and treat all information collected with ad-hoc committee composed of project staff to understand the situation, identify appropriate solutions and include technical adjustment or modifications if needed. A monthly meeting will be conducted at community level to update beneficiaries and community leaders relating to project development and challenges. All formally received complaints and feedback are registered by the MEAL unit, considered for further investigation and adequately addressed. Additionally, the AAH anti-corruption and Anti-Fraud Policy and PSEA guidelines are implemented. With regards to access in this specific project, AAH tries to achieve equal and impartial access through for MEAL measures. AAH’s MEAL unit uses results-based monitoring tools in accordance with AAH’s M&E guidelines. A detailed M&E framework will be developed to ensure that beneficiary selection is conducted in a transparent and accountable manner and prioritizes vulnerable families. For this project AAH will use its own criteria for WASH interventions to measure each household vulnerability regarding water and sanitation services. The response will therefore be designed to cover each household vulnerability using the same standards, extracted from the WASH cluster minimum standards and HRP prioritization. M&E measures will be participatory, both developed and conducted collaboratively with key stakeholders, target groups and beneficiaries, and with emerging findings and results shared transparently throughout the project. The aim is to ensure equal participation of all, sustainability and follow-up beyond the project’s lifetime.
