Project Summary

DRC intends to undertake an integrated child protection response to increase the availability of specialized CP services. More specifically: DRC will continue to provide specialised CP case management and referral services to support the immediate and acute needs of children at risk of violence, exploitation, and neglect, as per CPSS guidelines. Support services provided under case management will include individual and group PSS sessions (both case management and non-case management) with children and caregivers, alternative care support and FTR for UASC, lost child reunification to meet their safety needs and wellbeing. These consist of positive parenting sessions for caregivers, age-gender specific recreational activities through CFS/MPCAC activities for boys and girls to cope with and heal from trauma.
Additionally, life-skills development training sessions will be provided to adolescents with a particular focus on adolescent girls, who face unique barriers in accessing learning and other opportunities. DRC’s work on prevention of protection risks that includes sessions on sector-specific awareness packages to children and adults including prevention of child marriage, child labor, family separation, physical and sexual violence, child trafficking, personal hygiene, and COVID 19. A key focus of DRC CP interventions will be to strengthen existing CP community mechanisms. DRC already has established gender-age specific community groups (girls, boys, mother, father, and CBCPC) and will strive to continue building the capacities of these groups to identify, prevent and respond to CP risks. This includes leading awareness-raising, identifying the needs of children, and referring them to appropriate services. Apart from natural disasters and cyclone preparedness and response, they will lead on developing contingency plans for DRR and conduct regular demonstrative sessions on these plans. DRC will continue to sensitize communities on gender equality and prioritize the women volunteers whenever possible. A pool of ‘Para Social Workers’ to identify through CBCPM, will be trained and to engage for case follow-up especially in COVID-19 and emergencies. DRC also intends to increase the number of women community volunteers to ensure greater outreach to adolescent girls. DRC is committed to adopting an APP and AGD approach across its programming. Accordingly, it is expanding its protection monitoring activities to include child protection risks so that the evidence generated directly informs CP interventions. This includes revising the protection monitoring tools as per the guidance of the CPSS, building capacities of protection monitors and volunteers.
DRC is also committed to ensuring an AGD approach and child protection principle (best interest of children, confidentiality, do no harm) across its programmatic cycle. For this purpose, data collection tools and sampling approaches are being revised to ensure data collection can be disaggregated by AGD profiles (including CwD, adolescent girls, other vulnerable profiles). Data collected through protection monitoring will be directly managed by DRC child protection programme and shared with the CPSS and other partners. DRC is an active participant of the CPSS and regularly shares information during coordination meetings and participates in joint initiatives. DRC is committed to the principles of AAP and mechanism has been put in place to ensure these principles are respected during implementation and while interacting with partners. DRC has established CP quality benchmarks for monitoring implementation. DRC has an established PSEA reporting mechanism and policy as well as CoC and child safeguarding policy and train all staff and volunteers on these policies is on progress. DRC is an active member of the PSEA Network with whom it regularly shares information. DRC also regularly delivers CP mainstreaming ToTs as per the CPSS guidance.

1 - Identified Needs

The humanitarian situation in Cox’s Bazar, coupled with the global coronavirus pandemic has intensified the need for critical lifesaving and specialized child protection services. During the pandemic it has been observed that exposing to physical violence at home and community; psychosocial trauma; and different forms of negative coping mechanisms trafficking, violence, and abuse of rohingya children were significantly increased. The origin of these challenges are insecurities around the household economics and safety concerns such as child marriage and child labor. Girls are at greater risk of sexual exploitation and abuse and gender-based violence including rape and domestic abuse. According to the J-MSNA (May 2021) VAC increase at home as parents do not have the capacity to provide adequate care and supervision of children. On the other hand, according to the CPSS Child Protection Assessment (October 2021), risks like child labor (64%), negligence (59%), child marriage (57%), and trafficking (32%) have increased due to COVID-19 restrictions and the absence of service providers. Moreover, risks related to the separation of children (25%), sexual and gender-based violence (12%), and physical abuse of the child (23%) have increased during this period, along with reports of child depression (27%) and psychosocial disorders due to lack of mental and psychosocial support. Further, without adequate protection mechanisms, children can be easily exploited by criminal gangs. DRC’s protection monitoring and CM reports proof, ‘young adolescents being forcefully recruited by armed groups, and child physical abuse, child marriage, child labor, sexual violence, abuse, and exploitation and neglect are increased, compared to the pre-lockdown period. The upturn of such incidents was likely due to the closure of child-based facilities and children remaining unsupervised. More specifically, girls are at greater risk of sexual and gender-based violence including rape and domestic abuse, and less likely to return to child centers and learning centers. Prior to COVID-19, the children with disabilities absence in learning centers; only 19% of children aged 3 to 5 and 53% of children aged 6 to 14 with a disability attended learning centers, compared to 65% and 73% of their peers without a disability (REACH 11/2019). Despite the paucity of studies on mental health, the few available studies conducted prior to the onset of COVID-19 have indicated high levels of psychological distress among the refugees, including children which are negative psychological reactions including sleeping problems, nightmares, flashback memories, loss of interest in playing, appetite problems, sense of loneliness, somatic complaints, feeling sad, tense, or nervous. More alarmingly traffickers and gangs are presence in the camps and kidnap children (mostly adolescent) and adults for financial gain. Children are showing increasing signs of vulnerability to a range of threats in host community areas surrounding the camps. According to DRC need assessment in September 2021, revealed high rates of child labor among host families and drug addiction/abuse by adolescents. People are isolated and difficult to reach to accessing services, and often inadequate services. The May 2021 J-MSNA also reported a high prevalence of child labor and early marriage in host locations. Anonymized DRC’s case management data covering January to August 2021 record high number of cases of child neglect and child labor. Case management data records a significant spike in cases of child labor during 2021 compared to the previous year (accounting to 5% of total host cases in 2020 upwards to 36% of cases in 2021. This also points to the need to produce further evidence on protection needs in these areas to inform interventions.
