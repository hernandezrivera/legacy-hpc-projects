Project Summary

In line with the Humanitarian Needs Overview for 2019, and the planning for the Humanitarian Response Plan for 2019, DRC plans to implement Sexual and Gender Based Violence (SGBV) prevention and response activities in hard-to-reach conflict areas in South Sudan, through static and mobile responses. DRC strives to contribute to the two Protection cluster strategic objectives:
- Provide immediate lifesaving assistance to address priority protection needs for the most vulnerable populations in hard-to-reach areas
- Prevent, mitigate and respond to protection risks through enhanced preparedness and resilience
DRC will provide SGBV response activities for identified cases, including case management and psychosocial support (PSS). The SGBV response element will follow a survivor-centered approach, following the Inter Agency Standing Committee (IASC) GBV guidelines, and ensuring DRC staff are well trained and qualified to handle sensitive and confidential cases. The prevention activities will focus on promoting community engagement and ownership in addressing sexual and gender-based violence, while ensuring communities are aware of GBV services available. Awareness raising sessions, community activists’ trainings, mass awareness campaigns and information campaigns will be organised to ensure GBV messaging is consistent and effective, as well as community-led. DRC uses the SASA! methodology, developed by Raising Voices, which aims to address one of the root causes of GBV and HIV, the power imbalance between men and women, through community mobilisation and activism. DRC will also engage in advocacy with the GBV sub-cluster at the state-levels, and with the relevant authorities, such as the minister of gender, to ensure buy-in and support from the main and relevant stakeholders. These activities will be linked with the food security and livelihood activities, which will focus on women empowerment and resilience, while engaging with men on improving gender equality.
DRC has static presence in Unity State, operating from Bentiu, and in Upper Nile, operating both in Maban and in Malakal, Aburoc and several locations on the West bank of the Nile. In addition, DRC operates three mobile protection teams, which conduct integrated protection analyses, identifying SGBV risks and issues in hard-to-reach areas and among populations affected by conflict and displacement (targeted locations identfiied by GBV sub-cluster). In Upper Nile and Unity States, the GBV program will focus on increasing access to services for survivors and setting up safe spaces for women, where they can discuss their concerns, and alleviate stress and trauma through PSS activities. In all target locations, beneficiaries engaging in awareness raising and activism will be selected based on their motivation to support to behavioral change in the power relations between men and women, while survivors will not be sought out but supported based on need and referrals. Through the intervention, DRC intends to ensure women and men are equally sensitised on the issues concerning gender based violence and gender inequality, while providing life-saving assistance to survivors, encouraging help-seeking behavior, and support to vulnerable men and women. Overall, DRC aim to reach approximately 96,500 vulnerable conflict and displacement-affected individuals from host communities, IDPs and refugee communities in South Sudan.

Needs

Conflict, displacement, and poverty do not affect men, women, boys and girls in a homogeneous manner, and tend to aggravate the gender gap and inequality in South Sudan. Multiple reports and studies have found that women and girls are directly targeted for killing, rape, and sexual assault during active conflict, leading to displacement and suffering populations. Traditional and often discriminatory practices, such as bride price, child and forced marriage, wife inheritance, polygamy, and adultery, as well as violence within the family, have been exacerbated by years of conflict and instability. The gender related impact of the war and displacement is intrinsically linked to traditional gender practices. For example, women tend to suffer more from food insecurity than men, due to cultural and social roles as food providers in the household; women are more likely to pass on their food ration to the children, when there is not enough food to feed the entire household. In general, women are also at a high risk of gender-based violence (GBV) due to the significant cultural power imbalance between men and women, which is intensified during conflict displacement. Women are often harassed and raped by men, often armed men, while collecting firewood, fetching water, and receiving or harvesting food, which are mainly female-assigned chores. In addition, inter-partner violence increases during conflict and displacement, with increased pressure on both male and female to survive and provide for their families and reduced opportunities for income generating activities. Within the household a shift in gender roles is witnessed whereas decision-making power traditionally belongs to men, women have taken over this role in case of a deceased or absent husband. Families have resorted to various coping mechanisms, which include marrying girls off young in order to receive the bride price for the family; women collecting and selling firewood for money, while running the increased risk of harassment and rape, and prostitution is also used as a way to cope with extreme poverty by women and girls. DRC’s strategy to address gender inequality, as well as to prevent and respond to GBV is multi-faceted. It includes training community activists on the SASA! Methodology, which is a community-based program designed to address the core driver of violence against women; the power imbalance between men and women, designed by Raising Voices . In parallel, DRC works with survivors to help them in their journey to recover and receive the services they are entitled to. Throughout its interventions in South Sudan, DRC recognises the need to reduce the power imbalance between men and women, to support and encourage women to take lead and participate in household and community-level decision making, while at the same time work with men to battle the inequalities and stand up against violence against women. South Sudan is in dire need of tackling impunity, lack of legal options and accountability for survivors calls for widespread response in terms of access to justice programming and legal aid. Other services, such as counselling, immediate medical attention, such as post-rape treatment, and psychosocial attention are scarce in South Sudan, particularly in hard-to-reach areas, and even more for survivors fleeing conflict. The high need for services in hotspot locations identified by the GBV sub-cluster for the 2019 HRP. These locations include: Koch, Mayendit, Rubkona, and Leer, for Unity State, and Malakal, Maiwut and Nassir for Upper Nile State. For DRC’s integrated protection mobile response, DRC can reach other hard-to-reach areas for assessments and short term GBV prevention and responses, focusing on strengthening local capacities and identifying services for referrals.

Activities

SGBV response activities for identified cases, will include case management, PSS, individual and group counseling, and will be combined with FSL activities when possible. The SGBV response component will follow a survivor-centered approach in line with the Inter Agency Standing Committee (IASC) GBV guidelines. The prevention activities will focus on SASA! training, advocacy, and information campaigns promoting community engagement and ownership in addressing SGBV, while ensuring communities are aware of SGBV services available. In all target locations, beneficiaries will be selected based on their motivation to support behavioral change in the power relations between men and women, while survivors will not be sought out but supported based on need and referrals. Through the intervention, DRC intends to ensure women and men are equally sensitised on the issues concerning SGBV and gender inequality, while providing life-saving assistance to survivors, encouraging help-seeking behavior, and providing support to vulnerable men and women. DRC uses the SASA! methodology, which aims to address one of the root causes of SGBV; the power imbalance between men and women, through community mobilisation and activism. Through SASA!, DRC SGBV teams will train male and female community activists in strategies for de-normalizing cycles of violence and abuse and in community awareness raising. Community activists will be selected based on their willingness to engage in the program and their capacity to conduct outreach activities. Female and male youth in particular will be engaged as agents of change for gender equality, to ensure continuity of the activism and enthusiasm among young people, who are still developing their societal views. The approach also seeks to effect change through multiple entry points to address power imbalances, ensuring that activists are drawn from different age, gender and ethnic parts of the community for a holistic approach. DRC’s SGBV prevention officers together with the identified activists will further engage the community to increase their awareness of gender power imbalances, shared power and non-violence. The activities will be community based and focus on empowerment of existing and new community structures, such as women’s networks, community safety committees, and women’s groups. DRC will ensure community-based awareness raising focuses on available services, legal awareness, and GBV prevention messages. Trainings will be provided for humanitarian actors and community structures on GBV mainstreaming and prevention of GBV. In static locations, the GBV program will focus on increasing access to services for survivors and will link with FSL to set up safe spaces for women where they can discuss their concerns and alleviate stress and trauma through PSS activities. Case management will be provided to survivors and individuals at risk of SGBV; some may be referred through referral pathways, while others might seek assistance directly with DRC caseworkers. Case management for survivors will include linking survivors who request legal aid with the available justice mechanisms, as well as providing legal counselling upon need and with consent from the survivor. As part of the case plan, identified women at risk may require livelihood support in order to mitigate the protection risks linked to their vulnerabilities. These activities of skills training, tools and material for income generating activities, and fuel efficient stoves trainings. These activities will be linked with the food security and livelihood activities, which will focus on women’s empowerment and resilience, while engaging with men on improving gender equality.DRC will engage in advocacy with the GBV state-level and national-level sub-cluster, and with the relevant authorities, such as the Minister of Gender, to ensure buy-in and support from relevant stakeholders, to advocate for reduction of SGBV.

Consultations with affected people

Yes

Provide breakdown by women, men, boys and girls

Approximately 5,000 ([2,400 women; 2,100 men, 250 boys, 250 girls) people have been consulted over the months of September and October 2018. This consultation took place during regular monitoring visits, FGDs with men, women, boys, and girls, protection assessments conducted on mobile missions, PSN identifications, community based protection networks, etc. In general, DRC consults communities about their needs, the risks they face, their displacement history, their intentions, their safety situation, as well as identifies specialised protection risks (SGBV, child protection and mine action). These consultations take place on a daily basis, and are captured in DRC’s monitoring reports, protection assessments, context snapshots, monthly reports, and camp management satisfaction surveys and information products. DRC engages with communities on a daily basis to understand their needs, concerns, and analyse the protection environment. This project design is based on these discussions and observations within the communities, including separate discussion with women, men, boys, and girls, persons with disabilities, persons with special needs, and community leaders.

Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse

Yes

Briefly describe how

DRC has put in place global organisational policies and procedures to safeguard against harmful behaviour; DRC is in full agreement with the UN zero tolerance policy relating to sexual exploitation and abuse. Violations constitute acts of serious misconduct and are grounds for disciplinary measures up to and including dismissal, as described in sections 3.7 and 3.9 of DRC’s Code of Conduct (CoC). DRC implements awareness raising in project sites on PSEA, provides training to staff on PSEA and CoC, and has complaints and feedback mechanisms in place in all operational areas. Moreover, DRC also commits to organisational diversity and gender equity through adherence to the DRC Age, Gender and Diversity (AGD) Vision and Standards policy.

Does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes the project measures safety of beneficiaries by ensuring safety questions are mainstreamed into FGDs and PDMs. DRC also ensure safety of beneficiaries by monitoring the protection environment, having regular discussions and meetings with the community based protection networks, talking to women, to children, to men, and to community leaders. When safety concerns are identified, DRC raises these issues with specific agencies who can respond adequately (safety providers or authorities).
