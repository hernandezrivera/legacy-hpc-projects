Project Summary

The project will target the most vulnerable groups that includes children(6-59 months), pregnant and lactating women & including people with disabilities) in Leer, Mayendit and Panyijiar Counties both displaced, returnees and host community on one side and mother to mother support groups on kitchen gardening at the facilities and in the communities in Kajokeji County on the other hand. The main goals of the project are: to increase equitable and dignified access to quality malnutrition-specific curative services for children and women, while enhancing integrated sustainable preventive measures to halt prevalence of acute malnutrition among children and women in Leer, Mayendit and Panyijiar Counties; and to raise awareness and train women and their households in the cultivation and utilization of nutrient rich food both for self-consumption and for the communities’ markets. Having this goal, POH will try to address the main issues related to nutrition acting on two different plans: address the gaps of the malnutrition assistance providing emergency and mid-term services to those in need, and work on the prevention of future cases of malnutrition by strengthening the dietary diversification of communities of Kajokeji. Nutrition service delivery will prioritize provision of comprehensive and timely community management of acute malnutrition at both Nutrition centres at Boma levels and in hard to reach locations without static centres to access service. These areas includes payams like ( Mayom, Pachak and Panyijar in Panyijiar County, (Dablual, Pabuong and Thaker in Mayendit and (Bou, Dok and Padeah in Leer County.
The project will as well emphasize on promoting breast feeding and optimal feeding practices among children and women through scaling up of MIYCN counselling services. Nutrition for mother to mother support groups on kitchen gardening will focus on raising awareness on the importance of producing, best cooking methods/practices and while consuming specific food items: households will be sensitized and trained on the use of new vegetables and crops that will be selected together with the communities in a participatory process; focus groups discussions will be held with the mothers groups in order to discuss the importance of diversifying the cultivation for nutrition purposes. With the lessons learnt from compounding effect of COVID-19 pandemic to already existing challenges, several revised/new guidelines, and approaches such as family MUAC shall be adhered and implemented in 2022 to ensure continuity of equitable access and utilization of quality nutrition services in the context of COVID -19. Given the multi causative contributing factors to malnutrition, the project will ensure existent of operational linkages and joint programming with other relevant sectors such as WASH, health, protection and food security through effectives coordination mechanism that will stimulate integrated approach and dignified access to multi-sectoral basic service which will further reduce prevalence of acute malnutrition and eventually reduce morbidity and mortality among the vulnerable groups (children and women). Accountability to affected population (AAP) will be mainstreamed by ensuring that programming of nutrition services approach is community/people centred in such a way that those in needs are fully engaged throughout the program implementation to ensure that the intervention is responsive enough to the arrays of the changing needs. Consideration of gender and age variation of the vulnerable groups will be prioritized as well for efficiency of the response.

1 - Needs

While South Sudan continues to face overlapping crises including floods, conflict, and food insecurity all contributing to persisting high levels of malnutrition the preliminary results from Food Security and Nutrition Monitoring System (FSNMS) Round 28 and the 2022 SMART surveys show that the proportion of children 6-59 months suffering from acute malnutrition remains above the emergency threshold of 15 percent (very high acute malnutrition level- WHO classification) at 16.0% (15.4 -16.7) in lean season. This indicates no improvement since 2019, when the GAM prevalence was 16.2% (13.8-18.5) in lean season. The 2022 Nutrition Program data also shows lack of improvement - with increased admission of children and women with acute malnutrition by 24% compared to 2021. As per the FSNMS Round 28 and the 2022 SMART survey results, it is estimated that a total of 2,175,546 people will suffer from acute malnutrition (AM) in SS in 2023. These include a total of 1,437,735 million children under five years of which 353,606 will have SAM, 1,084,129 will have MAM, and 737,812 pregnant and lactating women (PLW) will have acute malnutrition (AM-PLW). Sadly, more children and pregnant and lactating women in SS will suffer from acute malnutrition in 2023 compared to 2022 (increased by 158, 201). The three Counties of Unity State as Leer, Mayendit and Panyijiar has estimated GAM of 24.6% (SAM of 6.9% (severity 4) and MAM of 18% (severity 5) and as per South Sudan IPC analysis conducted in Juba between 3rd -15th October 2022, an estimated 513,000 and 251,000 of the population in Unity State is classified in crisis and emergency food insecurity situation between Dec 2022 and March 2023 which shows an increase in figures. Further deterioration is seen occurring during the second projection period (April – July 2023), 507,000 in crisis and 354,000 in emergency which is 77.2% of crisis, emergency and catastrophe. The three mentioned counties above are amongst the affected ones. Based on the FSNMS Round 2028, SMART nutrition surveys and admission trends for 2022, it is estimated that 2,175,546 people need treatment for acute malnutrition in 2023. This includes: 353,606 children from SAM, 1,084,129 children) from MAM, and 737, 812 PLW with acute malnutrition. In addition, 2,157, 514 children will require Vitamin A Supplementation (VAS), 1,015,301 Children (0-23 months) and 507,650 pregnant will require MIYCN, 1,769, 276 Children (12-59 months), will require deworming, and a total of 1,776,776 children and women will require BSFP. The major factors contributing to acute malnutrition include high prevalence of diseases. 62.5% of children reported to have been ill two weeks prior to the assessment. Diarrhea, fever, and cough were the leading symptoms reported. Existing poor sanitation conditions are likely contributing to the high incidences of illness, with open defecation reported by as high as 50% of households in 56 counties. Inadequate feeding practices of infant and young children is another major contributing factor. All Infant and Young child feeding (IYCF) indicators remain suboptimal. At national level, Minimum Acceptable Diet was 4.6%, Minimum Dietary Diversity was 18%, and Minimum Meal Frequencies was at 25%. Only 4.6% of children aged 6-23 months are receiving the required food quality at the required frequency. Additionally, High food insecurity, and lack of hygiene, health and environmental sanitation services, along with inadequate maternal and childcare in South Sudan continue to drive malnutrition levels higher and impact negatively on children and women’s health and well-being.

2 - Project activities

The main project activities will be: 1 - Capacity building: recruitment community nutrition volunteers, training of health and nutrition worker’s/community volunteers on growth monitoring; Integrated Malnutrition prevention package and integrated package on prevention of malnutrition and training of health and nutrition worker’s/community volunteers on maternal infant and young child counselling. Training of community nutrition volunteers and lactating mothers on kitchen gardening and on growing of crop varieties. 2 - Nutrition education and awareness: conduct routine MIYCN counselling to mothers/caretaker of children under five in the community; conduct routine Integrated socio-behavioral change communication campaigns on nutrition, hygiene and health promotion including global breast-feeding week campaign; conduct cooking demonstration to mothers and caretaker of children under five on nutritious dense meal in the community. 3 - Micro nutrient supplementation: facilitate Vitamin A and de-worming campaigns in the communities, promotion locally produced fruit trees and diversified micro nutrients rich food crop e.g., orange sweet potatoes, okra, soybeans, amaranths among others. 4 - Provision of safety net: provision of food assistant to mothers/caregiver of children in the communities, those that are yet attended to, and linkages and referral of mothers to children in OTP/TSFP to GFD. 5 - Protection mainstreaming: Conduct GBV safety audits in the communities; training of health and nutrition staffs on GBV/protection; training of health and nutrition staffs on ECD; and distribution of ECD kits. 8 - Monitoring: provision of monitoring/evaluation tools and national guidelines to community nutrition volunteers; conduct routine on job.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Concerning protection, POH will work collaboratively with other actors (UN agencies, NGOS, FBOs and CBOs) involved in child protection issues to safeguard the delivery of protection services in the schools and communities. POH will be accountable to the beneficiaries by enabling them to participate in the project at the planning, implementation, and evaluation phases. Moreover, appropriate mechanisms through which affected populations can measure the adequacy of interventions, and address concerns and complaints will be set up. On regularly basis, POH conducts community engagement meetings such as awareness campaigns, innovative youth events that allows open participation/discussion over the quality of progress of the activity conducted. POH embrace a last-mile approach through field-based personal and daily visits to the field and conducts impact analysis and post monitoring reviews, which are analyzed distinguishing relevant categories of people with the objective of providing detailed information about the targeting of the intervention and beneficiaries barriers to access services. Subsequent projects are designed to address such barrier and put in place a system of monitoring that puts special emphasis on monitoring the targeting of those groups of beneficiaries found to face particular barriers

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

The capillary and continuous presence that POH has in the communities targeted by the intervention (POH has more than 80% of its staff, including senior staff), and the extensive use of staff recruited locally among people recognized by the communities, ensures that such fruitful cooperation with project beneficiaries continues throughout the implementation of the project. All project’s beneficiaries (female and male IDPs and returnees’ children, CWDs and host communities’ children) will be considered as capable and active resources rather than as passive recipients who do not have a say in the project. Thanks to a very localized approach with POH staff regularly present and usually coming from the same community of project locations, accountability is pursued also directly through its staff that can act as bridge between top management and beneficiaries. Furthermore, considering the purposely field-oriented management team (officers and also project managers are field based), beneficiaries and affected population at large as constant possibility to interact and file complain to staff at different level. Yearly, POH conducts surveys which results are discussed through open meetings with the community, the county and payam officials and traditional leaders (Boma and sub Boma Chiefs). During these events, POH explains to the community its plans for the incoming years as a means to an informed discussion among community members and receive solid suggestions. POH extension officers, AAP committees before selecting a person/group/community as beneficiaries, establish a case specific dialog where POHs expectations are compared with the one from the beneficiaries in order to evaluate possible challenges/ intervention failure chance. Beside the surveys, POH’s beneficiaries have access to information during participatory meetings which provided platform to address issues linked to power-relations whereby some strata of the population culturally marginalized can find the opportunity to express themselves, include the view of beneficiaries in programming and allow intervention in case of issues reported thus increase transparency and accountability through awareness of beneficiaries on protection-related issues as well as project-design.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

POH has always and will place protection and gender issues at the centre of the intervention by engaging directly with the IDPs, returnees and host communities’ children and CWDs so that their needs, priorities, rights are addressed within the scope of the project. POH will always create conducive and interactive environments that encourage them to express their needs, rights and priorities. Apart from the active involvement of the rights holders in the project, POH will ensure that the duty bearers (Government, PTAs/SMCS, institutions and non-state actors) play an effective role to promote the rights of the CWDs and girls. Activities will be developed with the active participation of women and parents in all stages of the implementation process, to ensure that girls’ priority needs are taken into consideration and that WaSH infrastructures meet the right gender standards (IASC, 2015, Guidelines for Integrating Gender-Based Violence Interventions in Humanitarian Action: Reducing risk, promoting resilience and aiding recovery). Moreover, prevention of GBV (Sexual Exploitation) is pursued by displaying SEA code of conducts at service access points (schools, health facilitates, distribution points and community). On the other hand, safety audits are conducted, and multiple locations selected to avoid far travel of beneficiaries and reducing risks. Community members, staff and stakeholder’s capacity will be strengthened through specific PSEA trainings.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

POH has in place a tested system of yearly multi-sector surveys that through the employment of more than 300 questions (including question on FSL, nutrition, GBV, hazards, protection, disabilities, education, gender inclusion, etc.), a large number of interviews (more than 300 HHs) capable of providing detailed reference at the county and payam level, an analysis centred on specific groups (single mothers, families with members with disabilities, poor farmers, etc) and the participatory discussion of such findings with the staff and representatives of the communities ensures not only that projects are designed on the base of the specific needs of the different groups of the population, but also that such knowledge is internalized by POH staff and becomes the base of an informed conversation and a common understanding with local communities, ensuring feedback from the most vulnerable within the community and such is aimed at monitoring the results of POH’s projects. Beside such surveys, POH has a tested system of detailed post distribution Monitoring which is used to gather precise feedback over the targeting, timing, transparency and overall implementation of activities oriented at the community level. During each field visit, POH personnel is required to fill apposite forms that include a section for open-ended feedback and complaints. Finally, the numerous permanent bases that POH has across the targeted communities are equipped with mail boxes, suggestion boxes; focal point protection help desks for unanimous and isolated reporting of abuses or complaints.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

• Risks that beneficiaries are exposed to violence during registration/distribution as a result of the distance to or location of humanitarian assistance and services: Nutrition sites will be established equitably in all the Boma at PHCU which is easily accessible by the local communities, and in Boma without static nutrition services the project will ensure vulnerable groups are reached through mobile services; Reducing waiting time of beneficiaries at nutrition service point by adhering to the effective flow in service provision as per the guideline and ensure every nutrition sites has got adequate staffing (nutrition workers) attending promptly to beneficiaries. In areas where case of violence is high ration provided to beneficiaries will be adequate enough to reduce on frequency of follow up visits at nutrition sites. Engagement of local and traditional leaders during mobilization to provide security in their area for their people accessing services and protection of service access points; Women are always more exposed than men, therefore service provider will make sure women are encouraged to be accompanied by their husbands while accessing services. • Risk of no access to or awareness of protection assistance/ referral services for survivors of protection incidents ( GBV, child protection, evictions, explosive hazards,): the project will ensure initial involvement of women and local women groups representatives in AAP committees and in planning activities implementation; Nutrition facilities will act as a valuable entry point for disseminating information about available GBV services and collecting feedback from women and girls about their needs/safety concerns; ensuring CNVs are trained/oriented on how to safely respond to a disclosure and that they have the most up-to-date referral pathway. With support and coordination with GBV specialists from available local actors, household nutrition screenings will be used as a modality to proactively disseminate information on available GBV services. Health and nutrition workers/volunteers will be equipped with pocket cards containing relevant contact information; Representations of the GBV referral pathways will be posted in all nutrition sites. • Risk that beneficiaries are not accessing/using complaint and feedback mechanisms: POH has managed to install suggestion boxes in most parts of the nutrition sites however very few feedbacks were received majorly because majority of beneficiaries are illiterate (not able to write) especially in Unity. Beneficiaries access to information in form communicating complaints and receiving feedback will be enhanced through formation of AAP committees at Boma level with representatives from each village who will be in touch with beneficiaries to receive compliant and feedback. POH will meet with the AAP committee to discuss on the complaints and feedback from time to time (Monthly); On quarterly basis there will be a help desk at Nutrition sites attended by one personnel from POH at a higher position to receive complaints and feedback from beneficiaries. • Risk of interference from external stakeholders in the impartial and needs-based selection of beneficiaries thus negatively affecting social cohesion and sparking intercommunity conflict over access to resources: POH will ensure Local stakeholders are oriented on the program in term of its objective/purpose, targeted beneficiaries and justification of selection criteria like those with acute malnutrition (children and PLW); Involvement of Local stakeholders and community organs/committees or groups and tradition leaders in selection procedures e.g. vulnerability assessment; Visual presentation of targeted beneficiaries and entitlement at distribution points/service access points in form of charts, poster; Engagement of community committee/groups or local leaders to participate, supervise and monitor distribution; Distribution points should be centrally and equitably allocated accordingly.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

The project will contribute to the collective outcome 3 acting on different levels. The emergency activities will focus on the most vulnerable, among which flood related IDPs represent a priority group; these activities, such as emergency distribution of agricultural inputs and basic trainings, will help IDPs in finding relief from the emergency situation while building the basis for a new system of self-reliance. On the other hand, POH will work with Host Communities to strengthen the support network that take care of those displaced ensuring they have all the support needed. POH intervention aims at building the collaboration between host community and displaced people as a synergy tool to ensure the growth of the whole community and the reduction of internal conflicts over the use of natural resources.

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

POH will ensure that the assistance during the project does not harm the safety, dignity and integrity of women, men, girls and boys receiving it, and is provided in a way that respect their rights. Protection sensitive services will as well be mainstream in the project, in fact the activities will include: facilitation of child stimulation and ECD in all nutrition sites, GBV/PSEA awareness to workers and beneficiaries, referral of GBV cases to access service at the management sites. Likewise, POH will be making sure that trainings targets at least an equal number of female and male, that the monitoring will include sex and age disaggregated data, that the project personnel is trained on gender/protection topics including GBV identification, handling and referral. Equal participation will be ensured through the involvement of women and men, equally in decision making right up from induction meetings, and routine focus groups discussions. For instance, Inclusive selection process with set % for specific population components typically victims of inequalities (min 60% of females in project implementation, inclusion of elderly and people with special needs, and returnees). POH’s projects include activities of livelihood and capacity building especially tailored to women, individually and as women group, and is matched by educational projects that always include GBV and peace building sensitization for pupils and training on GBV and referral pathways for teachers and PTA/SMCs members. And finally, POH will have a gender & protection specialist, a gender officer and a protection officer dedicated to the analysis, supervision and adjustment of activities from the gender perspective.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

In Central Equatoria State-Kajokeji, POH will focus its intervention in the field of food security and livelihoods with the main aim of reducing the proportion of population facing famine, emergency or crisis situations. All POH activities will be oriented to delivering basic agricultural assistance to the most vulnerable while building the capacity of the communities towards an ever-growing resilience and independence from humanitarian aid. To improve on production and utilization of nutritious locally available food at household for optimal MIYC feeding, the project will integrate nutrition to food security component, establishing sustainable solutions as demonstration kitchen gardens at household level, as well as at nutrition sites, schools, institutions (as County, Payam and Boma-based offices). POH will continue engaging in cereals largescale production through progressive farmers’ groups and enhancing the mechanization of cereal production in the targeted areas with common/group farms of above 10 feddan of dimension and supporting in production management and post-harvest methods. POH will also facilitate cooking demonstration to enlighten the mothers, fathers, and caregivers on how to prepare nutritious foods harvested from the kitchen gardens and encouraged to replicate the same at home. Mothers and caretakers of children with acute malnutrition will be link to community general food assistant to improve on household food security. People with chronic illnesses and mothers of children admitted in SC for management of severe acute malnutrition will be link to institution feeding program to receive food assistance accordingly.
