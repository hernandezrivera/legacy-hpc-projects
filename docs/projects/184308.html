Project Summary

Conflict, natural disasters, and COVID19 deterred Somalia from making significant development strides to benefit the most vulnerable population. The humanitarian context remains dire. Displacement, conflict, drought and famine have triggered family separation and exposed children to abuse, violence and exploitation as families resort to harmful coping mechanisms. Boys and girls endure multiple protection risks such as sexual and gender-based violence (GBV), child recruitment, mental and psychosocial distress. A trend analysis by the Country Task Force on Monitoring and Reporting covering 2016 to 2021 shows an alarming trend on grave violations, with 21,560 violations verified against 18,079 children (3,291 girls, 14,788 boys).
In 2021, the protection cluster reported a 25 per cent increase in people in need of protection compared to the previous year, with children’s needs increasing exponentially. At least 607,546 children are in extreme need (26 per cent), 1,449,144 in severe need (61 per cent) and 302,440 in distress (13 per cent), and require humanitarian child protection assistance. Adolescents between 12 and 18 years are exposed to extreme violence, including recruitment and use by parties to the conflict, killing and maiming, child marriage and FGM. In 2021 alone, 631 children (97 per cent boys aged 11-17) were recruited and used, representing a 15 per cent increase compared to the same period in 2020. Preliminary findings of a UNICEF study on drivers of child recruitment show that Al-Shabaab explicitly targets children through forced recruitment and abduction. Rape and sexual violence remain a threat to children’s survival. At least 1 in 3 people in Somalia have mental health-related issues. In 2021 at least 40 per cent of child protection incidents reported to caseworkers were related to mental illness, physical abuse, and neglect.
COVID19 precautionary measures such as closing schools and social services worsened the situation, exposing children to further risk. During the same period, the CTFMR recorded a 14 per cent increase in rape and sexual violence, with the Southern part of Somalia (Bay, Bakool, Gedo and Middle Juba) recording the highest number of cases. IDPs and minorities, including children with disabilities, remain particularly vulnerable. Integrated community-based responses addressing the multiple protection needs of children remain a priority in Somalia.

4.6 - Mitigation Measures

Exposure of women and girls to sexual violence, including sexual exploitation and abuse in relation to access to humanitarian assistance-Mitigating measures: The risk to survivors of further stigmatization, abuse or isolationis high even when they access professional support services. To mitigateagainst this risk, all partners will undergo mandatory PSEA and Child Safeguardingtraining and sign a code of conduct as a testimony to protect beneficiaries from abuse.PSEA and child safeguarding focal points will be identified and their rolesinstitutionalized. UNICEF will ensure partners apply fundamental principles in thedelivery of services such as absolute confidentiality (in both consultation as well asrecord keeping); providing survivors with the opportunity to decide what is in their bestinterest, and involving them in the management and care process through theapplication of informed consent procedures. Conduct SGBV training at the community level and set up referral mechanisms to identify and report cases of SGBV. * Agree with communities and service providers on safe hours and locations where protection services and other humanitarian services can be provided to children and women. Exposure of displaced boys and girls to violence caused by living in highly insecure settlements: Mitigating measures include: * increasing knowledge on child protection and child rights for parties to the conflict, the army, police and clan leaders. * Conduct training for vulnerable boys and girls on protection risks in conflict zones and how to remain safe.
