Project Summary

Displacement, insecurity, flooding and economic challenges have compounded the impact of historically low coverage of water infrastructure in South Sudan. The economic downturn has disrupted water services, limiting resources for maintaining wells and boreholes. In displacement-hosting areas, excessive demand has strained water points, reducing access to safe drinking water, especially as displacement increases. Insecurity has further restricted access to water sources.
The onward movement of approximately 378,000 refugees and returnees—representing 45% of arrivals in South Sudan—has intensified the pressure on scarce resources. Notably, nearly 20% (69,000) of these individuals are in Upper Nile State, and 13% (47,000) are in Northern Bahr el Ghazal State, compounding existing WASH, nutrition, and health challenges (FEWS NET 2024). Akobo and other parts of Jonglei State face similar challenges, with returnees and IDPs from Ethiopia exacerbating vulnerabilities in areas already burdened by limited resources.
Acute malnutrition remains a critical concern, with 2.1 million children aged 6–59 months and 1.11 million pregnant or breastfeeding women (PBW) projected to experience elevated levels of malnutrition between July 2024 and June 2025. Among these, 650,000 children are expected to suffer from Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM). The situation is particularly dire in Malakal County (Upper Nile State), where 10,000 people are in IPC Phase 5 (Catastrophe), alongside 31,000 South Sudanese returnees fleeing conflict in Sudan. Alarmingly, 67% of the acute malnutrition burden is concentrated in Jonglei, Northern Bahr el Ghazal, and Upper Nile, where SAM rates remain critically high (IPC 2024).
Access to basic WASH services remains critically low, with only 42% of the population accessing improved water sources, and 45% walking over 30 minutes to fetch water. In Malakal County, over 90% of residents rely on unsafe water sources such as rivers and puddles (FSNMS Round 28). Untreated surface water, coupled with widespread open defecation, exacerbates public health risks, particularly during floods, when latrine pits contaminate water sources. Additionally, only 16% of households have adequate water storage containers, further hindering safe water access.
This response aims to reduce WASH-related morbidity and mortality in priority counties through integrated WASH, Nutrition, and Health programming. Targeted WASH support will be provided in OTP and SC facilities, focusing on mothers/caretakers of children with SAM to mitigate waterborne diseases linked to poor sanitation and unsafe water. Emergency WASH services will prioritize high-risk and displaced populations in IPC 4 and 5 counties, including repairing and maintaining critical water points, ensuring safe water access, and improving sanitation facilities to prevent disease outbreaks. Capacity building for water pump engineers will also be a key component.
To enhance safety and inclusivity, GBV-sensitive WASH interventions will include constructing gender-sensitive latrines and bathing facilities, strategically placed to ensure privacy and security for women, girls, and persons with disabilities (PWDs). Community engagement will focus on raising awareness of GBV risks linked to inadequate WASH infrastructure. Flood-prone areas will benefit from disaster-resilient WASH infrastructure, including improved latrine designs and elevated water points to reduce contamination risks during floods, enhancing community resilience.

1. Needs

In 2025, an estimated 5.01 million people required WASH assistance across South Sudan. Displacement, insecurity, flooding and economic challenges have compounded the impact of historically low water infrastructure coverage. The economic downturn has disrupted water services, limiting the resources available for maintaining wells and boreholes. In displacement-hosting areas, excessive demand has overwhelmed water points, reducing access to safe drinking water, particularly as displacement increases. Insecurity has further restricted access to water sources. The arrival of approximately 378,000 refugees and returnees—representing 45% of arrivals in South Sudan—has exacerbated the strain on limited resources. Notably, nearly 20% (69,000) of these individuals are in Upper Nile State, and 13% (47,000) are in Northern Bahr el Ghazal State, compounding existing challenges for WASH, nutrition, and health services (FEWS NET 2024). Akobo and other parts of Jonglei State face similar pressures, with returnees and IDPs from Ethiopia further straining already limited resources. Acute malnutrition remains a critical concern, with 2.1 million children aged 6–59 months and 1.11 million pregnant or breastfeeding women (PBW) projected to experience elevated levels of malnutrition between July 2024 and June 2025. Among these, 650,000 children are expected to suffer from Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM). Malakal County (Upper Nile State) is particularly dire, with 10,000 people in IPC Phase 5 (Catastrophe), alongside 31,000 South Sudanese returnees fleeing conflict in Sudan. Alarmingly, 67% of the acute malnutrition burden is concentrated in Jonglei, Northern Bahr el Ghazal, and Upper Nile, which report critically high SAM rates (IPC 2024). Access to basic WASH services remains critically low, with only 42% of the population accessing improved water sources, and 45% walking over 30 minutes to fetch water. In Manyo County, over 90% of residents rely on unsafe water sources such as rivers and puddles (FSNMS Round 28). Untreated surface water, coupled with widespread open defecation, increases public health risks, particularly during floods, when latrine pits contaminate water sources. Additionally, only 16% of households have adequate water storage containers, further hindering safe water access. Protection concerns persist at water points in Greater Upper Nile, with 18% of households, both rural and urban, reporting they felt unsafe collecting water within two weeks of data collection. Among these, 12% cited GBV risks, with women and girls avoiding water points due to safety concerns. The situation is particularly acute in counties like Akobo, Mayom, and Baliet, where over 40% of households reported feeling unsafe while collecting water. The vulnerability of IDPs, coupled with limited resources, heightens the risk of GBV. Integration of IDPs into host communities remains a challenge, as both groups rely heavily on humanitarian support. Long distances to boreholes and lengthy queues force communities to fetch water from unsafe sources like rivers. The absence of night lighting prevents safe access to latrines after dark, increasing risks of GBV. Additionally, road blockages due to flooding hinder access to water points and disrupt children's education. The lack of sanitation facilities, such as latrines and bathing spaces, poses significant GBV risks, particularly for women and girls. Sexual exploitation and abuse are frequently reported, driven by the need to survive in these precarious conditions. Addressing these gaps is critical to ensuring safety, dignity, and improved WASH outcomes for affected populations.
