Project Summary

The food insecurity level in South Sudan is dire. The people facing severe acute food insecurity are 6.64M currently, 6.31M in the first projection and 7.76M in the second projection at the peak of the 2023 lean season (April – July).(IPC_Nov2022). This intervention aligns with the 2023 FSL Cluster Strategic Objectives (SOs); SO1: Lifesaving, SO2: Centrality of protection and SO3: Resilience/ Coping mechanisms. As well as the FSL Cluster Objectives (COs) CO2: Enhance and sustain emergency food production. CO3: Reduce dependency on food and agricultural inputs to support and strengthen households’ ability to absorb shocks considering MOH/WHO COVID 19 guidance. Out of the cluster target of 6,040,483, RuCAPD targets 525,299 individuals = 87,550HHs segregated by age and gender as well as considering persons with disability. Dec 2021 inflation rose by 12.7 %, resulting from increase in the price of food and non-alcoholic beverages triggered by fuel shortages, supply chain disruptions linked to COVID-19 restrictions, and floods (S/2022/156). The rate has remained high throughout this year. South Sudan hunger rate is serious (GHI 2022). Pockets of populations in IPC 5 in Jonglei and Unity (IPC,Nov2022). The food insecurity has been exacerbated by constrained humanitarian access, conflict, effects of COVID-19, economic challenges and elevated food prices. Additionally, there has been dry spells in some areas and flooding. Severe three year flooding has led to widespread displacement, damage to crops and pasture and destruction of livelihoods. Therefore, the already existing high levels of different vulnerabilities caused by complete asset depletion and impoverishment experienced since the start of the protracted crisis & compounded shocks experienced by South Sudan driven by insecurity, multiple population displacements, declined crop production, diseases and pests, the economic crisis has greatly affected food security. Constraints to humanitarian access experienced in 2021 and early 2022 really hampered the work. (HNO/HRP2022/ CFSAM DEC2021/ UN OCHA/ DG ECHO). Additionally, South Sudan 2023 INFORM Index for Risk Management is 8.5 which is very high. Combating climate change should also be prioritized (COP27). Other factors – (which are also the main drivers of food insecurity) that make South Sudan a high risk country are: low HDI and poverty indices at 185 (SDG2, SDG 3, SDG 4.3 & SDG 4.6, high intensity of national and sub-national violent conflict, high economic dependency on aid (HNO2022/ HDI Report2020). In the period of Dec22-Mar23, the IPC3+ in Fashoda is 50,000, Manyo 56,000, Malakal 137,000, Baliet 35,000, Maiwut 80,000, Pigi/Canal 80,000, Morobo 23,000, Lainya 34,000 Panyikang(N/A). This is expected to rise further at the peak of lean season Apr-Jul2023 with Canal having 5,000 in IPC5 (IPC,Nov2022). Most affected and most vulnerable are Child HHs, persons living with disabilities, elderly, women-HHs. Youth are affected too. Agriculture, fisheries and livestock are the most cost effective humanitarian frontline interventions. With Gender, age & disability lens, sufficient nutritious food production to enable the most vulnerable beat the protracted deepening food crisis. Issues of combating climate change are also key.

1 - Needs

The food insecurity level in South Sudan is dire. The people facing severe acute food insecurity are 6.64M currently, 6.31M in the first projection and 7.76M in the second projection at the peak of the 2023 lean season (April – July).(IPC_Nov2022). This intervention aligns with the 2023 FSL Cluster Strategic Objectives (SOs); SO1: Lifesaving, SO2: Centrality of protection and SO3: Resilience/ Coping mechanisms. As well as the FSL Cluster Objectives (COs) CO2: Enhance and sustain emergency food production. CO3: Reduce dependency on food and agricultural inputs to support and strengthen households’ ability to absorb shocks considering MOH/WHO COVID 19 guidance. Out of the cluster target of 6,040,483, RuCAPD targets 525,299 individuals = 87,550HHs segregated by age and gender as well as considering persons with disability. Dec 2021 inflation rose by 12.7 %, resulting from increase in the price of food and non-alcoholic beverages triggered by fuel shortages, supply chain disruptions linked to COVID-19 restrictions, and floods (S/2022/156). The rate has remained high throughout this year. South Sudan hunger rate is serious (GHI 2022). Pockets of populations in IPC 5 in Jonglei and Unity (IPC,Nov2022). The food insecurity in Greater Upper Nile, sust like the rest of the country, has been exacerbated by constrained humanitarian access, conflict, effects of COVID-19, economic challenges and elevated food prices. Additionally, there has been dry spells in some areas and flooding. Severe three year flooding has led to widespread displacement, damage to crops and pasture and destruction of livelihoods. In Central Equatoria, low crop production, insecurity/inter-communal conflicts particularly between cattle keepers and farmers that have led to displacement and movement restrictions, crop and livestock pests and diseases, and high food prices continue to drive food insecurity. Additionally, degraded road conditions affect market access and functionality (IPC,Nov22). Therefore, the already existing high levels of different vulnerabilities caused by complete asset depletion and impoverishment experienced since the start of the protracted crisis & compounded shocks experienced by South Sudan driven by insecurity, multiple population displacements, declined crop production, diseases and pests, poor infrastructure, the economic crisis has greatly affected food security. Constraints to humanitarian access experienced in 2021 and early 2022 really hampered the work. (HNO/HRP2022/ CFSAM DEC2021/ UN OCHA/ DG ECHO). Additionally, South Sudan 2023 INFORM Index for Risk Management is 8.5 which is very high. Combating climate change should also be prioritized (COP27). Other factors – (which are also the main drivers of food insecurity) that make South Sudan a high risk country are: low HDI and poverty indices at 185 (SDG2, SDG 3, SDG 4.3 & SDG 4.6), high intensity of national and sub-national violent conflict, high economic dependency on aid (HNO2022/ HDI Report2020). In the period of Dec22-Mar23, the IPC3+ in Fashoda is 50,000, Manyo 56,000, Malakal 137,000, Baliet 35,000, Maiwut 80,000, Pigi/Canal 80,000, Morobo 23,000, Lainya 34,000 Panyikang(N/A). The people in need is expected to rise further at the peak of lean season Apr-Jul2023 with Canal having 5,000 in IPC5 (IPC,Nov2022). Most affected and most vulnerable are Child HHs, persons living with disabilities, elderly, women-HHs. Youth are affected too. To realize food security, livelihood inputs and training in basic agronomy as well as livestock and fisheries services are needed. Components of agroforestry, irrigation and floods management are also needed to combat effects climate change.

2 - Project activities

CO2:Emergency food production: - Main season agriculture seeds & tool distribution; fishing, vegetable & crop kits; - Nutrition vouchers (plus poultry & shoat distribution) - Emergency food & livelihood support: fishing kits/ vegetable kits; rapid response kits (fish kit/ cow pea & three variety of fast maturing vegetable seed) . - Livestock treatment and vaccination; livestock disease surveillance, Community Animal Health Workers Training and inputs support, cold chain establishment and maintenance, prevention & treatment.- Livelihood support to returnee households. Provision of timely food security information. - Support value chain addition through agriculture, fishery and livestock products/ by-products preservation & marketing. - Training & capacity building: both adaptive and absorptive on-farm production (farmer/agro-pastoral field schools, demonstration plots), income generation (business skills), vocational training etc. -Multi sectoral models: livelihoods, nutrition sensitive food security, hygiene, sanitation, agroforestry. - Reducing pest and crop disease including ; Reduction in postharvest losses and introduction of appropriate preservation technologies through training and provision of hermetic storage bags. - Diversification & restoration of livelihoods CO3: capacity building & training- Training HH on proper safe management of food supply chain. - Training natural resource management and agroforestry- Training on agronomy, vegetable and fish production and nutritious utilization and marketing.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

This project takes into consideration all the feedback from people ( men, women, boys and girls) with different vulnerabilities including the elderly, persons with disability. And using available referral pathways to close the loop on all feedback and complaints registered by the the different categories and vulnerability of the people we serve. Rural Community Action for Peace and Development clearly recognizes that more substantive efforts toward inclusion of persons with disabilities will need to continue concurrently in relation to how disability issues are not only considered but holistically included in all our projects / intervention designs, monitored and evaluated. This involves recognizing the need for a wider capacity building effort focused on disability inclusion not only throughout all our interventions. One of the selection criteria for registration as a vulnerable household is having or headed by someone living with disability and elderly persons and therefore will be eligible to receive inputs during the distribution. Both formal and informal feedback/ complaint collection strategy will be used by RuCAPD. Since our staff are within the community, the field visits and home visits to the beneficiaries is one of the avenues we use to obtain feedback.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

RuCAPD will continue all the stakeholders especially the communities we serve to shape all the decisions and actions in relation to this intervention. Be it: challenges, problems, opportunities, outcomes as well as solutions. It is about having the communities actively participate and be consulted throughout the project cycles. During assessment and project initiation and indeed throughout the project cycle, it will be clearly communicated that their feedback or complaints are highly welcome. The setting up of feedback/complaint desks at the different distribution sites is also key. We will have phones and numbers dedicated to receiving feedback (positive/ negative). Our AAP staff will have suggestion boxes and will also gather feedback from the beneficiaries during the Throughout our intervention, RuCAPD is guided by the following community engagement principles: • Integrity: RuCAPD upholds integrity in our engagement with the community. We are transparent and clear in scope and purpose of all our interventions. • Inclusiveness: Community engagement is accessible and balanced therefore capturing a full range of values and perspectives in the community. • Dialogue: in our Community engagement, we promote dialogue as well as open genuine discussions supported by timely and accurate information; which we use to weigh the options we have, and develop common understanding on way forward. • Influence: Community engagement is always reflected in outcomes. The community is able to see and clearly understand the impact of their involvement in the intervention.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

RuCAPD ensures that all our staff get a copy of the PSEA policy and they undergo training to understand what it entails. On the same note, all staff read and sign the Code of Conduct policy.We also ensure that we have PSEA focal points in all our areas of intervention throughout this project. Above all, RuCAPD endeavors to reinforce community participation and do more awareness. During this project, we will run awareness sessions with boys, women, girls, and men in the communities we serve as well as with other stakeholders. It is our role to make the people we serve aware of their rights as well as their entitlements. E.g., they are not supposed to give any form of sexual favours, and such requests/ demand by anyone such favours is proscribed. The how and where to report SEA incidents confidentially and safely explained to the community. RuCAPD PSEA focal points will coordinate with other partners on the ground ( especially those fully engaged in Protection and GBV responses) to help close the loop in the feedback and complaints mechanisms concerning SEA. Our feedback mechanisms are aggregated by age, gender and diversity (AGD) and accessible to all including persons living with disabilities.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Feedback, (positive and negative) from the population we serve is invaluable. It is one of the AAP pillars too. RuCAPD, has mechanisms in place to help with this. In case of any problems, RuCAPD will be able to address them on time thus save time and money. Because of the transparency and accountability brought about by the feedback ( and closing the loop), the people we serve are not only empowered but the relationship between RuCAPD and the community as well as other stake holders grow positively. Improved quality of programming will come about as RuCAPD addresses people’s feedback thus adapting interventions leading to greater impact. We strengthen our interventions based on learning and best practice from the feedback we receive from the community we serve. We have community communication agents at all the project sites. Their main function is to collect all feedback from the community and channel it to the concerned personnel for further communication and action as well as receiving feedback on the community concerns and closing the loop by reporting back to the community on the way forward/ action taken. Use of local language, chief’s barazas to deal with matters arising as well as use the public address systems to announce issues concerning the interventions. Currently, we have suggestion boxes at our distribution sites (managed by two officers -one male one female) to help people communicate their concerns. Both formal and informal feedback and complaints are duly registered and dealt with to ensure closing the loop. In case of issues beyond our intervention for example health matters, we refer to the relevant partners/institution and ensure necessary action has been taken.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

RuCAPD interventions are geared towards inclusion of all gender, age and diversity- disabled and the chronically ill members of the community. This is key in helping avoid exclusion of women and girls in consultations and decision making. RuCAPD has a working PSEA policy which is upheld at all levels and the principles set forth in this policy apply: To all RuCAPD staff, whether expatriates or national; At all times, i.e. during and outside of office hours and during periods of leave. To all people or entities or stakeholders who have signed a contract with RuCAPD: consultants, day laborers, suppliers as well as partner organisations. On the same note, RuCAPD is part of the founding and steering committee of Nuswan Giddam which is a movement that seeks to change the narrative about the woman and girl of South Sudan. That South Sudanese women and girls are born to lead. Lack of access to/awareness of referral services for survivors of protection incidents (GBV, child protection, for example) - RuCAPD is part of the Protection cluster, child protection sub cluster as well as the GBV sub cluster. and will also work hand in hand with the other protection partners in our areas of operation like Nile Hope, DRC, RI,UNHCR,AHC, HDC, CAFAD, among others to ensure that matters protection are amicably and ably dealt with. Beneficiary registration is done after the criteria has been clearly explained during mobilization and project awareness. This is based on the FAO selection criteria. After clearly agreeing and disagreeing with the different stakeholders including the local authorities, representatives of the different vulnerable groups (women, the chronically ill (e.g. HIV/AIDs) youth, people living with disability, the elderly) as well as religious leaders. Therefore all the selected beneficiaries will be registered accordingly. Contamination of targeted project areas with explosive hazards- Bearing in mind that some of our areas of intervention have faced the brunt of the protracted crisis, the risk of explosive hazards is real. We will continue working together with UNMASS and G4s and other partners in this field to ensure the safety of our staff as well as the community whom we serve. Where land is required for purposes such as group farms and demonstration plots, written consent from the owners if private or local authorities if communal will be sought before use of such lands. Beneficiaries risk to violence during registration and distribution will be minimized by having teams assisting women and men on separate desks. The distribution will also be restricted to between 8am and 5pm to allow the beneficiaries to reach home in time- for the women to perform their family roles and avoid GBV caused by delays at the distribution sites; the timing also helps in safeguarding against abduction ( of boys and girls)as these perpetrators fear daylight. The number of Distribution points will also be increased to make sure beneficiaries do not travel over long distances to access our services. This will also be helpful in this era of covid-19 as it goes a long way into making work flow faster.

Women and girls are stewards of the physical, economic, and cultural well-being of their communities yet disproportionately impacted by climate change. We advocate for their frontline efforts are recognized and funded. RuCAPD will run with this button which also falls under this year's IWD theme as well as CSW66.
