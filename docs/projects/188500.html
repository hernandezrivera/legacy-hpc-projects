Project Summary

In line with the CP Area of Responsibility (AOR) Strategy and the “No Lost Generation” framework, UNICEF will enhance equitable access to quality CP services with a resilience focus, including for Survivors of abuse, violence, neglect and exploitation as well as children with disabilities, targeting 117 high severity sub-districts (3 to 5) and 38 low severity ones.
UNICEF’s CP Strategy in 2022-2023 includes:
• Addressing behavioural, social, cultural and economic determinants of CP violations as a prevention approach.
• Improving the reach and quality of specialized CP services
• Capacity development of CP responders
• Monitoring, evidence generation and advocacy
UNICEF will support the delivery of integrated programming that will encourage social transformation, prioritizing:
• Structured and sustained CP MHPSS and EORE programmes
• Specialized integrated CP services including case management for survivors of violence, exploitation, neglect and abuse (child labour, CAFAAG, child survivors of GBV, child marriages, justice for children, birth registration and unaccompanied or separated children amongst others)
• Parenting programmes to promote positive child rearing skills and experience for caregivers
• Elimination of violence against children, including GBV prevention and empowerment for girls, boys and women, prevention of violence in schools, homes and communities.
• Awareness-raising on CP risks
• Capacity development of CP responders and social workers
UNICEF will generate evidence to inform the humanitarian response and advocacy including setting up an information management system for case management. UNICEF will embrace new delivery modalities and implement cash transfers aimed at child protection outcomes for vulnerable families. UNICEF will also continue to support the Monitoring and Reporting Mechanism on grave violations against children.
CP services will be delivered by partners based on minimum standards and IASC guidelines. 7,200 CP and social workers will be trained on case management, MHPSS, EORE, advocacy, evidence generation and coordination across sectors for a comprehensive CP response.

UNICEF will support MHPSS for 364,000 children to benefit from improved mental health and social wellbeing. UNICEF will equip 61,500 parents and caregivers with skills to provide a stimulating and safe environment at home, including reduced violence.
Violence prevention is a key priority. Family violence prevalence is high for girls 12-17 years (47%), boys 12-17 years (44%), boys below 12 (45%) and girls below 12 (44%) (UNICEF 2018). UNICEF will work to protect children from violence and abuse through prevention activities in schools, homes and communities and providing survivors of violence with child-friendly and gender sensitive services. Support will be provided for PSEA mechanisms to protect beneficiaries and build the capacity of humanitarian actors. PSEA programming will be implemented with the Protection Sector AORs.
Based on HNO 2021, one in three communities in Syria is reportedly contaminated by explosive ordnance. One in four victims is a child (Joint MA & CP Analysis, 2020). EORE messages will be provided to 1,460,000 children and adults through humanitarian actors and public service providers using static facilities and mobile teams. UNICEF will build the capacity of partners to provide quality EORE that is age, gender and disability appropriate and to provide child victims with assistance.

UNICEF will strengthen the justice system to ensure that children in contact with the law victims, offenders or witnesses are protected through child-friendly, gender-sensitive services, that are community-based and family focused.
As Lead Agency for the CP AOR, UNICEF will ensure coordination needs at Hub and WOS levels. Two UN-led and one INGO-led coordination hubs in Damascus, Gaziantep and NES, as well as a WOS overall coordination system, to ensure coherence across the CP response.

4 - Needs

ased on the HNO 2022, 6,044,884 children need CP services. Millions of children are under profound psychosocial distress. The 2021 MSNA shows concerns across all governorates: 84% of assessed communities reported child labour; 71% child marriage; and 67% family separation. CP concerns are expected to raised through 2023.From Jan to Sept 2021, 1,440 grave violations were verified. From the activation of the Syria MRM until Sept 2021, the CTFMR has verified 22,375 grave violations. Five million children live in areas contaminated by EO (HNO 2021). Girls and boys experience violence at homes, schools and communities. Family violence affects girls 12-17 years (47%), boys 12-17 years (44%), boys below 12 (45%) and girls below 12 (44%) (Children situation analysis, Syria 2018, UNICEF, unpublished). Over 850 children with perceived affiliation to armed groups are detained without due legal process in NES, many awaiting repatriation to other countries. There are 8,000 children and 3,000 women from 60 nationalities, and over 50,000 Syrians and Iraqis (majority women and children) in camps and subject to arbitrary arrest, violence and forced relocation. Given the scale of the Syria crisis and the increased child protection needs across the hubs, there is a compelling need for a strengthened coordination mechanism to ensure harmonized and high quality response for child protection services at Wos, Hub levels and Sub-national levels.

5 - Implementation of activities

UNICEF aims to reach 1,567,200 children and caregivers with CP interventions. In line with the CP AOR Strategy and “No Lost Generation” framework, UNICEF will strengthen CP structures and systems towards early recovery, ensuring services are at scale and sustainable, reaching children in a phased and context-sensitive approach. The programme will enhance equitable access to quality CP services, including for children with disabilities, through: 1) Community-based behaviour change and awareness raising, MHPSS and parenting interventions; 2) Specialized integrated CP services for survivors of violence, exploitation, neglect and abuse, including case management; 3) Capacity building; and 4) Monitoring, evidence generation and advocacy. UNICEF will expand the availability and quality of EORE for girls, boys, women and men in targeted locations. In line with the MA AOR HRP results framework, UNICEF will provide EORE through humanitarian risk education actors and public service providers. Support for PSEA mechanisms to protect beneficiaries and build capacity of humanitarian actors will be enhanced. The CP AOR will continue to pursue a strategic approach to reach girls and boys with flexibility within a changing operational environment. Coordination structures will work to improve equitable access to quality CP interventions. UNICEF monitors through partners, independent TPMs, its technical staff and feedback mechanisms and uses a WOS IMS to analyze data.

10 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

Protection from sexual exploitation or abuse by humanitarian workers and service providers is high on the UNICEF agenda to ensure that no violations and abuse happen during implementation. UNICEF as co-lead for the inter-agency network (ICN) on PSEA, will continue to support and work closely with other agencies to enhance the PSEA portfolio. The main goal of the ICN is to improve coordination for the benefit of people at risk and ensure access to complaint mechanisms and specialized services for victims. Continuous capacity building for humanitarian workers on zero tolerance for SEA is required, as is awareness raising for the community on the right to humanitarian aid free of any exchange. As a pre-condition for contracting implementing partners, PSEA assurance and capacity building plans are guaranteed. Prevention, mitigation and response interventions include:• Capacity building for humanitarian workers and service providers on zero-tolerance for SEA and commitment to SEA prevention, mitigation, and response.• Trainings and awareness raising activities on PSEA principles to beneficiaries and communities.• Train, mentor, and coach staff on policies and procedures ensuring do not harm principles and PSEA.• Strengthened safe and confidential complaint mechanisms for beneficiaries and communities.• Immediate support to SEA survivors in line with survivor centered approach principles.


11 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks:a) limited access to affected population due to insecurityb) direct exposure of children and families to violent attacks while accessing servicesC) exploitation and abuse by service providersd) services causing unintended harm Assumptions: In carrying out community-based protection services, protection actors devote attention to targeting locations choices and beneficiaries and avoid perceptions that assistance is delivered in a discriminatory/non-equitable manner. As risk mitigation measures, protection actors will be guided by the sector severity analysis; analysis of existing services through sector mapping tools, maintaining a transparent, inclusive and adequate communication with communities on available services; consult communities in planning, selection of services; and ensure feedback after service provision. GBV actors may need overcome cultural resistance from communities on the direct engagement of/ /provision of services to women and girls. Mobile and gender-balanced approaches will be used during implementation. Manage expectations with clear communications, referrals pathways. Quality of service provision may be impacted by staff turnover, lack of adequate technical capacities, particularly in specialized protection interventions. Mitigation measures include: 1) working through community-based modalities including mobile services; 2) consideration of conflict patterns to inform services delivery; 3) diversification of delivery channels.
