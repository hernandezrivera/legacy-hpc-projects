Project Summary

HI’s interventions aim to contribute towards closing the gap in Education in Emergency in South Sudan. The Education Cluster states, in its HRP draft, says that: “Some 2,808,000 girls and boys between 3 and 18 years of age in conflict and crisis affected areas are estimated to not have access to pre-school, primary and secondary education in 2019....”. Whereas many children in the country are out of school, enrollment and retention of children living with disabilities is far worse of, as children with disabilities account for barely 2.5-3.0% of the total enrollment in primary schools, and less than 1% in secondary schools. Disparity between boys and girls is also a major concern. A 2014 survey report on Menstrual Hygiene Management in schools in greater Eastern Equatoria by SNV shows that girl enrollment in upper primary classes (primary 5-8) drops to less than 15%.
In 2019, Humanity and Inclusion (HI) will target at least 30,200 beneficiaries: 20,000 children (girls and boys, singling out those living with disabilities) in at least 30 schools, along with 9,000 parents and 1,200 teachers, PTA members and State Education officials local authority and religious officials as well as customary court officials within 4 old states of South Sudan: greater Unity, Western Equatoria, Eastern Equatoria and Central Equatoria. One of the states (Unity) is ranked as facing emergency food insecurity under the IPC food security classification for the first quarter 2019, two as crisis and one (Western Equatoria) as stressed, but with high humanitarian access constraints. Funding for 10,435 beneficiaries (7,000 boys and girls) in 14 of these schools in greater Central and Eastern Equatoria states is under negotiation with Unicef as at November 2018.
HI’s Education activities will focus on ways to increase enrolment of children (girls and boys) with disabilities in schools; training adolescent girls in schools in safe menstrual hygiene management (MHM) coupled with provision of sanitary pads to the most needy girls; training of teachers/facilitators, school PTAs and county and state education officials in basic inclusive education pedagogy and protection code of conduct in schools; conducting campaigns ((with involvement of local authorities, religious organizations and customary courts) against early girl marriages, bullying, and negative attitudes against children with disabilities. Parents of children with disabilities will also be trained to better cope with and support their children in school. These actions contribute towards realization of South South Sudan HRP and Education Cluster 2019 objectives 1, 2 and 3. The interventions target children in POCs, IDP camps and in host communities, with about 60% of schools targeted delivering Education in Emergency and 40% targeting schools in more or less static conditions.
These actions will be undertaken in schools operated by at least 6 INGOs in the targeted counties with who HI is currently discussing MOUs. At the school mapping and targeting stage, and throughout the implementation phase, HI will coordinate closely with all stakeholders to avoid duplication of activities and resources and to enhance synergy. Closely engaging parents, education officials, local authorities and religious organizations and customary courts enhances resilience and even future sustainability of the project. In parts of the targeted counties where access is extremely difficult, HI will work through Local NGOs/DPOs and where access constraints are less to medium, HI will implement directly, employing and training staff locally. HI already has field offices in the targeted states or will have established bases by first quarter of 2019 using existing donor funding. Although this will be HI’s first intervention in EIE, HI has over 6 years’ experience in emergency interventions across 5 old states of South Sudan, in Protection and Health including MHPSS and disability rehabilitation, and over 2 years in Inclusive Education in static school conditions. HI’s intervention in the 30 schools is estimated to cost USD 1,200,000 (USD 60 per child and USD 40 per beneficiary), with USD 1,000,000 targeted from Unicef and SSHF, and USD 200,000 co-funding raised by HI separately or as in kind contribution.

Needs

Discussions with some of the agencies operating education programs in Unity and Western Equatoria including Intersos, NRC, WVI and Windle Trust International reveal a broad range of needs, which are also vindicated by the South Sudan Education Cluster in its 2019 HRP objective statement as well as HI’s own recent study on enrolment of children with disabilities in Central and Eastern Equatoria. These gaps and needs include limited physical infrastructure/classrooms with classroom enrolments exceeding 100 children in many schools; lack or insufficient earning aids including desks, exercise books, and teaching materials; lack or poor WASH facilities –water supply, latrines and hand washing stations, inadequate or no school feeding programmes, insufficient or poorly remunerated and motivated teachers, low or absence of female teachers, and low or no interest and participation of parents in the education of their children. For schools which demand uniforms and registration fees, parents cannot afford them, hence causing children to drop out. Enrolment and retention is poor for girls, many who drop out at upper primary (classes 5-8) mainly for lack of menstrual towels, and are forced into early marriage. But perhaps the major external factor influencing access to education is insecurity, which according to a recent survey reported by the Cluster affected smooth learning in at least 50% of the schools assessed. But more alarming is the extremely low (or in some schools) no enrollment of children with disabilities. Enrollment and retention of children living with disability is quite low, with disabled children accounting for barely 2.5-3.0% of the total enrollment in primary schools, and less than 1% in secondary schools. A nationwide disability assessment conducted in 2012 and a disability barrier assessment conducted by HI in Torit, Juba and Jonglei in 2017 revealed the top five barriers to children with disability accessing school as: long distances/transportation challenges to access school; lack of special needs learning materials/aids; negative parental and community cultural attitudes, and poverty (parents cannot afford food, school fees, general classroom learning materials, uniform and assistive devices for their children) and ; lack of teachers with special needs education skills . These findings have been corroborated by a more recent (August-September 2018) rapid assessment conducted by HI in 14 schools in Juba and in Torit. The total enrollment in the 14 schools is 12,765 children, of who 296 or 2.3% live with disabilities. The survey revealed that although some of the schools practiced aspects of inclusive education, it was not structured , and children with disabilities were being left behind. Interviews with children, teachers, PTAs, Government education and local administration officials showed that the number of children who were out of school and living right within the catchment of the schools surveyed exceeded that of children enrolled by far . Ranking of the barriers to enrollment of disabled children in the 14 schools mirrored closely those already outlined in the earlier assessments. A 2014 survey report on Menstrual Hygiene Management in schools in greater Eastern Equatoria including Torit County by SNV paints an even grimmer picture: retention of girls in upper primary classes (primary 5-8) drops from the 42% national average to less than 15%. The top three reasons for drop in enrollment and retention of girls in this class category is given as cultural attitudes which regard girls as source of wealth (dowry) hence leading to early and often forced marriages, late enrollment of girls in school (the report says girls attend primary 1 at 11 years on average); and lost school time and shame during mensuration, as over 55% of teenage girls are ignorant on how to handle their menstrual periods, and a mean 60% don’t use sanitary pads, with 43% not using underwear at all.

Activities

Project activities are aligned to South Sudan Education Cluster objectives and activities.SO1: Increase access to inclusive and protective lifesaving education (formal and non-formal) for children affected by emergencies.1.1 Rehabilitation of existing learning spaces: Basic modifications will be done to classrooms entrances, school desks, latrines and accesses to facilitate easy mobility of children with disabilities. 1.2 School gender based violence (SGBV) prevention and management: Conduct sensitization sessions for all children in schools on protection rights, right to education for children with disabilities and their role in promoting and supporting access and retention of children with disabilities in schools.1.3 Assess individual disabilities and provide tailored rehabilitation, psycho-social care and/ or assistive devices to priority cases depending on type of disability.1.4 Facilitate schools to integrate minimum guidelines for implementation of Inclusive Education and observance of protection rights in their SIPs-School Improvement Plans.1.5. Establishment or rehabilitation of basic wash facilities, including hand washing with soap stations to improve girls menstrual hygiene management (MHM) and the hygiene of all children in general.SO2: Improve the quality of education to ensure continuity of relevant education services for children and youth in emergencies.2.1 Training of teachers : adapt and pilot a training module on inclusive education pedagogy with a focus on practical skills for the local context.2.2 Provision of textbooks, learning and teaching materials: 2.2.1 provide inclusive and accessible teaching and learning materials to schools and specific aids to children with disabilities according to individual needs. 2.2.2 Provide Unicef classroom learning kits to needy children with/out disability.2.3 Provision of hygiene kits for girls to support their menstruation: provide reusable sanitary pads coupled with MHM training to menstruating girls (those with and without disabilities) while promoting early school enrollment for girls and discouraging early marriages. For girls in schools in static conditions, their mothers will be trained to make the pads and sell to project.SO3: Strengthen the response capacity of communities and education actors to mitigate the impact of emergencies on children and youth.3.1 Trainings of PTAs/SMCs: Train Parent Teacher Association (PTA) and School Management Committees (SMC) and Head Teachers in Inclusive Education management and protection rights.3.2 Trainings of SMCs and county education officials on Inclusive Education school management, record keeping and monitoring – focus on in disaggregated data collection and reporting systems.3.3 Provide mental health and psycho-social support services to needy children in school and also their needy parents to deal with trauma and social pressures heightened by movement restriction3.4 Improve and facilitate referral cases of children in school for rehabilitation, or psycho-social support to partner agencies or institutions.3.5 Train parents and members of the public as well as local administration officials and local traditional court officials on participation in running of the schools, about the rights of girls and children with disabilities to education and against negative cultural practices.Activities will be implemented directly by HI through recruitment and training of grassroots staff (community mobilizers/inclusive education facilitators, mobile inclusive education officers, or through partnerships with local NGOs. All HI staff and LNGO/DPO volunteers will be trained in inclusive education pedagogy and monitoring, protection and SGBV awareness and prevention, HI PSEA policy and other relevant skills necessary for their delivery of the project. A peer and joint monitoring plan will be put in place and implemented under the over site of education officials.

Centrality of Protection in humanitarian action
Protection Risk Analysis?

Yes

Briefly describe how

HI has recently (in October-September 2018) carried out a desk gender based analysis of the targeted locations. Recent studies and reports by various agencies operating in South Sudan do guide this preliminary GBA. Reports like Amnesty International’s (2017) Do Not Remain Silent provide horrific accounts of brutal rape, beatings, psychological terror, and even killings in greater Unity and Western Equatoria among other regions, perpetrated by armed groups during the renewed fighting of 2016-2017. These studies indicate a serious, and mostly deleterious, disruption of traditional gender norms and societal practices by conflict and displacement, with either gender assuming new roles. Traditionally, South Sudanese households are patrilineal, with men dominating leadership and decision-making roles, as well as having the say on land and other productive resources, while women hold reproductive and caregiving roles, including fetching firewood and water and cooking for the family. Among men and young male adolescents, engagement in conflict (either current or prior) and violence are linked and deeply rooted. In a society entirely unaccustomed to same-sex relations, boys and men have been sodomised and left with permanent psychological scars. The erosion of men’s capacity to economically support their families increases pressure (and consequently violence) on them. Besides cultural norms, families have cited extreme poverty as a driving factor in the decision to marry off young girls (for dowry) or for women to engage in sex for food. Recognizing the deep roots of such practices, HI will draw on its experience of community engagement to accompany and empower women to influence the adjudication of GBV and child-marriage cases in local customary courts. It will include working with and training traditional court officials and community leaders and initiating community-awareness and women leadership confidence building campaigns through local women’s groups

Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

HI will work with various beneficiaries to address the key identified protection gaps from the assessment conducted in August, namely: barriers to children (especially poor girls, boys and those living with disabilities) to accessing education, stigma, inequitable access to humanitarians services by survivors of GBV and women’s’ and girls’ rights violations.

Addresses inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

The project will adopt a gender-equality and disability inclusion lens in all aspects and stages of the proposed work, seeking to identify, describe, and strengthen community capacity to address gender disparities and harmful social norms that contribute to sexual and gender-based violence. In line with HRP GAM guidelines , the project will strengthen and support greater access to comprehensive GBV services (including psychosocial and mental health) for all, with a particular emphasis on the needs of women and girls.

Project activities address inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Activities will be designed and implemented aligned to a survivor-centered approach , with activities informed by the International Agency Standing Committee (IASC) guidelines for integrating Gender-Based Violence intervention in Humanitarian Action. Do No Harm practices will be observed and beneficiaries sensitized and facilitated through dialogue on community-based mitigation measures on environmental hazards that accompany massive displacement and camp settlements. HI staff and staff of partners will be trained in Gender-Based Analysis and the mainstreaming of strategic gender rights in project design and implementation. Given the delicate security access issues in the two states, HI will mainstream Security Risk management in its project implementation.
