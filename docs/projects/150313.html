Project Summary

The Specific Objectives of the project are as follows:
• Support the productive capacity of farmers by rehabilitating open field land and providing access to water through functioning water systems (irrigation, collection ponds, carrier lines)
• Recover and sustain the livelihoods of farmers by providing cash for work activities
The project will address Country Strategic Objective 3 and will be implemented in partnership with UAWC who has a strong presence and history of intervention in the OPT including the Gaza Strip. Oxfam’s partnership approach is holistic and rights based. The project is planned to be implemented for a period of two years to achieve complementary and transitional activities aiming to enhance the productive capacities of farmers at all levels to sustain food security of farmers and increase resilience. Phase one of the project includes activities in Gaza Strip targeting Khanyounis, Middle Area and Northern Gaza Governorates. Activities will include land rehabilitation, planting of field crops and intercropping vegetables, water collecting ponds, total expected beneficiaries will be 920 farmers (5152 ind). The project aims to employ 170 agricultural workers in land rehabilitation activities. Moreover, the project aims to address aspects of gender injustices between men and women in the agricultural sector and safe agricultural practices to enable farmers to follow proper adaptation options. The project is based on Oxfam’s resilience framework; therefore, it ultimately aims to increase their absorptive and adaptive capacities.
The project is in line with FSS’s specific objective 1 which aims to enhance the productive capacity of farmers by improving their livelihoods and enhancing their access to water for agricultural purposes which improves the general food security situation and has a positive impact on overall nutrition and health status.

Needs

The poverty level in Gaza is four times higher than in the West Bank and female headed HH’s in Gaza experience a poverty rate of 1.3 times higher than those of males. Adding to the already deteriorating situation in Gaza, the cuts in funding imposed on the PA and the UNRWA by the US Government will affect the delivery of emergency assistance to vulnerable families. The Great March of Return demonstrations taking place in Gaza have also contributed to higher food insecurity due to the damage of 870 dunums and the large number of injuries and deaths. In September 2018 Oxfam carried out a Vulnerability and Risk Assessment (VRA) in Beit Lahia and Beit Hanoun areas. In addition to unanimously identifying the overarching root problem; the Israeli blockade, many of the issues prioritized by the community group representing the two areas including Um El Nasser were related to the agricultural sector. The findings from the assessment reflect the wider agricultural situation in Gaza, as affirmed by the MoA. The issues were debt, the lack of compensation to affected farmers, the agricultural land bulldozing, urbanisation and increase of population density, and water aquifer and seawater pollution, the lack of accountability of local actors and finally and PA salary cuts. The respondents proposed cash generating activities for youth, women and men to protect their HHs from negative coping strategies. Also, the MoA confirmed the need to further invest in rehabilitation of lands and greenhouses, improving access to water and injecting cash amongst farmers.According to Oxfam’s Post-Crisis Market Analysis (PCMA, August 2018), multiple, inter-dependent layers of overwhelming debt in a system of credit were identified. As it currently stands, households owe shops for goods purchased on credit, meanwhile shops owe wholesalers, who owe banks. Oxfam led focus groups cited average debt amongst households in the area as between 2000-5000 ILS / 540-1360 USD with an approximate average monthly income of 410 NIS. Borrowing money or food is hence one of the – if not the only - way that a significant percentage of households in Gaza are able to survive. This mechanism is not only unsustainable, but dangerously close to reaching breaking point. During the VRA it was also debated whether or not the community recognizes and values the work of the women in agriculture. The farmers pointed out that although women help their husbands, they are not considered farmers. On the other hand, women in the same group believed that they play a fundamental role through long hours of work on the farm which has to be valued given that as it constitutes additional work on top of the household and reproductive responsibilities. Although women and men have complementary yet different roles in agriculture, men have more access to the benefits emerging from those activities; 48% of these women do not receive paid salary for their work despite representing 65% of these activities.Women said they make joint decisions about what to grow, how much to grow and how to sell their products yet don’t possess control over their own lands nor their husbands. The women’s role is seen as a supportive one to their husbands and merit is often to denied to those women who support in agricultural duties as well as carry the additional burden of domestic and reproductive duties. Forced early marriages were stated as an option for poor farmers despite their belief in education. The same group also stated they had to let their children leave school, and some had girls in college who were forced to drop out due to economic reasons. Both men and women stated that it’s more conducive for their girls to marry at a young age, even to men who don’t necessarily have a sustainable source of income, thus revealing weaker social for girls and young women. Vulnerabilities, food insecurity and agricultural work are therefore deeply gendered in Gaza, generating a devastating impact for women and girls.

Activities

Activities focus on vulnerable farmers within a multi-layered and vulnerable context and take gender disparities into serious consideration. Support to land and irrigation systems will address agricultural land that has been destroyed either by abandonment or the Israeli forces. Moreover, improving the perception of female work in the agriculture sector is a major focus of the activities to raise awareness of both men and women (especially young) working within the sector on labour divisions and agricultural rights of women. The project will also provide women with tailored CfW activities that provide them with cash through a gender sensitive approach. The project is planned to be implemented for the period of two years to enhance the productive capabilities of farmers at all levels. Phase one of lands rehabilitation, water collecting ponds to be followed by phases two in the following year which will complement the work done in the first year in addition to new soft component activities that includes training on best practices, gender awareness, capacity building and linking to local market to achieve the LRRD model. The third phase will explore the potential linkages between the existing beneficiaries and the ongoing activities under Oxfam’s Economic Justice Program. Ultimately the project after two years would restore the productive capacities of 700 dunums of agricultural lands and sustain the livelihoods of approximately 2000 farmers who will be engaged in the design process to best utilize the support towards their livelihoods rehabilitation.UAWC’s role will be liaising with the community and the MoA, conducting HH visits for comprehensive needs assessment, pre selection of BNFs, and distribution to BNFs, and monitoring the day to day implementation on the ground. The list of beneficiaries will be partly provided by MoA through UAWC and the final eligible list will be cross checked with FSS to avoid duplication of assistance.

Indicators

Oxfam is committed to genders-sensitive Monitoring, Evaluation, Accountability and Learning (MEAL) across all its programs and projects which ensure equal access to Oxfam project services for males and females from all age groups. Oxfam also developed a Common Approach to Monitoring and Social Accountability which forms the basis of M&E system for all Oxfam projects. In case of emergency, Oxfam’s minimum standards for MEAL and gender in emergencies will be applied. The CAMSA approach also comprises how gender-relevant needs, actions, and issues are monitored during the implementation and how project reviews and evaluations take gender sensitive issues into serious consideration. All tools, data gathering, data analysis, reporting, recommendations and action points are gender and age disaggregated where required. Throughout the project, MEAL will be undertaken in accordance with a comprehensive M&E plan which will track performance against indicators (outputs and outcomes), timeframe, and encompass key criteria for baseline/end-lines and other related studies. Data collected will be gender sensitive and disaggregated by age and sex for both qualitative and quantitative data using different tools such as questionnaires, Key Informative Interviews (KII), stories of change, and focus group discussions (FGDs). In terms of accountability, Oxfam lunched its Accountability, Feedback, and Response Mechanism (FCRM) in September 2018 which provides wider access for diverse vulnerable groups to raise their thoughts and concerns, provide feedback and submit complaints. This system is using ICT (Information Communication Technology) for receiving feedbacks either by an online link, verbal feedback or recording complains received by other channels such as the hotline and feedback boxes. The system automatically referrs the received feedback to the relevant program staff and is monitored by Oxfam MEAL Officer.
