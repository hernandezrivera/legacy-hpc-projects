Project Summary

Coordination and health information management are essential as the nature of humanitarian needs across the country has not changed. The years of 2022-2023 will be the year of the shift of coordinated response to “severity scale” prioritization. Coordination will focus to identify solutions for access: to deliver health supplies; to expand and carry out health services; to carry out assessments and surveys; to monitor and evaluate needs and levels of assistance. COVID-19 pandemic has only revealed the systematic challenges existing in public health system and in the country as a whole. Coordinating health sector response in a country with divided governance remans a critical task. Issues of standardization, needs’ identification, proper planning, effective response, limited funding, prioritization based on the principles of neutrality and impartiality are very serious.

4 - Needs

Coordination led by WHO will continue to ensure response to the following defined needs: Insufficient number of mobile clinics/teams to areas of acute emergency needs; Disrupted emergency referral system in public health care facilities, preparedness and response levels to improve the management of trauma patients; Lack of medicines, equipment and supplies to support diagnostic and treatment services (including cold chain) in health care facilities and mobile teams/clinics; Deficient reproductive health services; Ineffective detection and response to communicable diseases through community health education/promotion; integration of vertical programming with other services; Limited health care for patients with TB, HIV. A potential for increase of vector-borne diseases across the country; Non-continuous and interrupted routine vaccination of children, insufficient number of vaccination teams; Increasing demand for services for patients with injuries: rehabilitation of persons with disability; strengthen capacity for prostheses and rehabilitation; Limited MHPSS activities and services, including community mental health system; Shortage for diagnosis and treatment of non-communicable diseases, as home care for patients with chronic diseases; A high number of public health care facilities, especially PHC level, being closed and in need of repair and rehabilitation. The capacity of health authorities in health emergency preparedness, planning and operational readiness.

5 - Implementation of activities

The composition of the WoS health sector in Syria requires continuous support and build up within CCPM (cluster coordination performance management) parameters. In 2022-2023, the health sector’s priorities will be guided by the severity of needs. The health sector will aim to overcome the challenges of 2012 related to further buildup of operational capacity to adapt operations to meet emerging needs. Through improved dialogue with the health authorities at all levels (and while acknowledging their leading role), the health sector will work to secure faster, more flexible approvals to support the humanitarian response in areas where needs are greatest (e.g., patient referrals and evacuations, the delivery of medicines and supplies, vaccination campaigns and the continuum of care for chronic disease patients). Main activities will include the provision of a minimum and essential package of integrated health care services at primary and secondary levels. The package will include emergency and trauma care, the management of communicable and noncommunicable diseases, maternal, neonatal and child health, mental health and psychosocial support, vaccination, disease surveillance and outbreak response. The sector will continue enhancement of mechanisms to eliminate duplication of service delivery/activities: updating reporting tools (4Ws) to the IMOs of the health partners; provision and consolidation of bi-weekly inputs (health sector operational updates).

9 - Protection Risk Analysis

Protection, including violence against health facilities and healthcare providers: • There are continuous shifting lines of conflict. The situation (political, security) is fluid. There are de-factor four governance structures. There is a large degree of uncertainty. • Essential health services are largely not accessible to people. • Information on functioning and supported health services should be continuously updated and validated and verified. • Health care staff, patients, health facilities are subject of violence with a large degree of neglect of existing protection mechanisms in place. • Protection related activities should be continuously mainstreamed throughout health programming, coordination fora, training/workshops with health partners. • Hard-to-reach areas and areas of higher severity scale must be clearly prioritized and accessed through different modalities of response. • Advocacy should be in place to register and report on all incidents related to the protection of health. GBV activities are not mainstreamed into health while health services are often the first, and in many occasions the only point of contact for GBV survivors seeking assistance, or coming for health care for GBV related emotional or physical conditions, including injuries, without necessarily disclose the abuse due to shame or fear. • There are limitations related to the pro-active role of civil society, grass-root organizations, community-based approach in health sector planning.

11 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

• The sector will ensure to have a balanced approach between different parties to the conflict. The emphasis of health sector coordination will be to empower health response on the ground, at a community level. The operational framework developed for health sector will enable tracking down humanitarian and development defined activities but with clear reference to “severity scale” approach. A quarterly review of severity scale prioritization will be produced with development of necessary recommendations. • There is a pressure and perceptions to disconnect emergency and development imperatives/activities while NEXUS (“humanitarian” and “development” priorities) should be equally addressed and funded in health). Current health sector funding is structured to support short term interventions. There are different donor-driven initiatives with regards to policy and strategy development, but these were not necessarily being coordinated with the health sector. While immediate emergency response continues to be required, strengthening of largely disrupted public services (especially at PHC level) and strengthening the health system is needed across the country. • Syria is characterized by a large private sector which has been functioning throughout the crisis with population diverting its funds and seeking care in private sector while the public health sector has kept its fast erosion, especially in rural areas. • Rapid response mechanism should be more effectively continued.
