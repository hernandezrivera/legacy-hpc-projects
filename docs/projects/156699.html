Project Summary

The purpose of the WASH intervention is to deliver humanitarian WASH supplies and services to most vulnerable people and support to water, sanitation and sewage systems to ensure continuous services for affected people in Syria. NRC will respond to the diverse problems and needs faced by vulnerable populations throughout Syria through the delivery of a multi-faceted WASH response. The different components of NRC’s WASH project are intended to contribute to HRP WASH Sector Objectives 1 and 2.
NRC will deliver this project through a combination of direct, partner-led and hybrid modalities. Partner names have not been included in this submission for reasons of confidentiality
Geographic areas will be targeted based on the severity and scale of needs, inter-agency coordination at hub-level, and access. NRC will provide assistance to vulnerable conflict and displacement-affected populations, including IDPs, refugees, members of host communities, IDP and refugee returnees, and non-displaced households suffering the effects of the conflict. All interventions will seek to prioritize the most vulnerable households. Where appropriate, multi-sectoral vulnerability scoring techniques will be used that integrate vulnerability indicators
This project includes: This
- Emergency response through the provision of one-off support as part of an integrated, multi-sector response in accordance with the Emergency Preparedness and Response Plans (EPRPs). Comprises of three Result Areas: Temporary WASH services, Household assets (Hygiene NFIs) and support to Education in Emergencies (WASH Services for Temporary Learning Spaces).
- The provision of medium-term shelter support at household and settlement-level to populations living in and out-of-camps. Comprises of two Result Areas: Community Infrastructure and Services in Camps, and Household assets (Hygiene vouchers)
- The provision of longer-term assistance at household and settlement-level in urban and peri-urban out-of-camp areas. Comprises of three Result Areas: Community Infrastructure and Services, Housing Rehabilitation for WASH facilities, and school rehabilitation (WASH Facilities) (see NRC’s Education project submission).

Needs

The 2019 HNO estimates that a total of 15.5M people are in need of WASH assistance & 6.2M are in acute need of WASH assistance. This represents 6% increase relative to 2018 estimates. Whilst there are needs throughout Syria, inter-agency assessments indicate high scale and severity of needs in Aleppo, Idleb, Raqqa, Rural Damascus, Damascus and Homs governorates. WASH conditions vary considerably across Syria. In GoS areas, approximately 30% of the population relay on unsafe drinking water, whereas it’s 70% in opposition-controlled areas.Continued displacement coupled with the economic impact of the crisis, conflict damage & lack of maintenance have resulted in limited safe & affordable water, limited waste-water & solid waste management, protection issues due to inadequate & over-crowded sanitation facilities (particularly in IDP sites) and interrupted hygiene practices due to unaffordable hygiene items. Findings by the WoS WASH Sector indicate that approximately 6.8M Syrians are reliant on unsafe drinking-water sources. Whilst 98% of the population has access to functioning toilet, the lack of functioning waste water treatment has resulted in contamination of the water table.According to UN OCHA, 1,299,393 ind. experienced displacement in the period Jan - July 2018 incl. It’s considered that this number will increase throughout 2019, particularly in opposition-controlled NW Syria. NRC’s assessments in NW Syria found that around 10% of individuals had been displaced 6 or more times since the start of the conflict. Based on RNAs conducted by NRC’s field teams throughout 2018, many newly IDPs lack access to hygiene items & are often obliged to live in shelter options of last resort. According to 2019 HNO, WASH needs of newly IDPs are high due to lack of adequate facilities & the availability of affordable hygiene items in displacement sites. Newly IDPs are spending up to 20% of their expenditure on water in some areas. According to UN OCHA, there are 966 IDP sites, including 477 Informal settlements; 445 collective sites and 31 planned camps. NW Syria alone accounts for 336 of these sites, housing 420,000 ind. The NE Syria INGO forum estimates that there are 74,570 ind. (including 24,000 Iraqi refugees) residing in 9 camps; Ein-Issa, Mabrouka, Areesha, AlHole, Roj, Newroz, Abo-Khashab , Twahina & Menbij. They were established by or with the approval of the Kurdish self-administration. The nature & conditions of the “camps” vary considerably from ad-hoc Informal Settlements housing IDPs in NW to effectively formal camps hosting a combination of IDPs & Iraqi refugees in NE Syria.As highlighted by 2019 HNO, IDPs living in camps/informal settlements lack access to safe and adequate water & increase of water-borne diseases is registered in areas with high concentrations of IDPs. In addition, IDPs living in camps/informal settlements face severe sanitation needs due to the over-crowding & lack of operation & maintenance. KIIs & observation by NRC have identified limited segregation of sanitation facilities, lack of lighting & access routes as a particular protection concern for females. In addition, to lack of physical access to WASH facilities for PwD. Despite continued new displacement, other areas are experiencing consolidated political control and high levels of return. Based on precedent from other contexts, it is anticipated that the majority of Syrian refugee returns will be to urban and peri-urban areas. Where 70% of water networks are functional, the majority are operating well below capacity. The primary reason for this is a lack of power, particularly outside of major urban centres. In Urban areas, the sewage network is largely intact. However, the lack of waste water treatment has resulted in an elevated risk of contamination. Also, the strengthening of solid waste management has been identified as a need. NRC’s assessments indicate that many buildings in peri-urban areas lack a connection to water & sanitation networks

Implementation of activities

NRC and its partners will contribute to the inter-agency objective of “Support to water, sanitation and sewage systems to ensure continuous services for affected people in Syria” through the following HRP outputs. These will be delivered in out-of-camp communities (mainly urban and peri-urban) in post-conflict settings. All will be supplemented by the respective software component. 1.1 Repair and rehabilitation of water systems; through installation of and/or upgrade to essential water and sanitation services1.2 Support to water quality assurance and operation and maintenance of water supply systems1.3 Repair, rehabilitation and operational support to sanitation/sewage and solid waste management systems; NRC will contribute to addressing settlement-level needs by supporting the rehabilitation/upgrade of community infrastructure and services. NRC will only target community infrastructure and services which significantly contribute to its objectives for WASH and/or other NRC CCs as part of an integrated response.NRC WASH programme will support the Education programme with support to water and sanitation services in informal community learning spaces and supplementary software in out-of-camp contexts.NRC and its partners will contribute to the inter-agency objective of “Deliver humanitarian WASH supplies and services to most vulnerable people” through the following HRP outputs: 2.1 Distribution of essential WASH NFIs & Hygiene promotion; Household Assets (Hygiene Kits with supporting Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) materials) will be provided through the appropriate modality depending on the particular context; Multi-Purpose Cash. In-Kind or Vouchers/Restricted Cash. NRC will also conduct hygiene campaigns and training to address particular hygiene concerns identified through a population-specific Knowledge, Attitude and Practices (KAP) survey2.2 Improved access to lifesaving/ emergency WASH facilities and services; through provision of water-trucking, temporary water storage facilities, and temporary latrines with hand-washing-points (with de-sludging where required) delivered in camps/informal settlements where NRC/partners already have a presence and when new displacement over-burdens the capacity of existing infrastructure and services. NRC will expand its Permanent Housing programme and this will include the upgrade or installation of household-level water and sanitation facilities.NRC’s WASH programme will support the Creating Welcoming Schools initiative with support to water and sanitation services and supplementary software to formal schools in out-of-camp contexts.

Protection Risk Analysis

NRC has conducted a WASH-specific protection risk mapping as part of its 2019 strategy development process. Major identified protection risks include (this is not exhaustive): 1. The requirement for formal civil documentation (e.g. IDs) in order to receive humanitarian assistance has been identified as one of the main barriers to equitable access to assistance. 2. Status-based provision of assistance may lead to or exacerbate social tensions and distort consistent needs-based targeting of assistance. 3. PDM indicates that in-kind NFI distributions lack appropriate items for women and children and/or the quality/type is inadequate resulting in unmet needs and un-dignified conditions. 4. Lack of sufficient gender modified and disaggregated sanitation facilities within camps/camp-like settings resulting in safety risks, insufficient privacy and un-dignified conditions, particularly for women and girls. 5. Lack of lighting in water and sanitation facilities and along access routes and other communal areas resulting in safety risks, particularly for women and children. 6. FGDs indicate that physical access to and within camps is often limited for people with impaired mobility and others unable to leave the family tent, creating a barrier to equitable access to services provided outside of the family tent. 7. A lack of adequate understanding of HLP rights risks breach of do-no-harm principles and may increase the risk of eviction or other lack of security of tenure for vulnerable populations. 8. NGO activities may have unintended impact on local market (e.g. cost inflation) or contribute to local ‘war economies’ and do harm. 9. Unsafe construction practices expose beneficiaries to avoidable risks of physical harm, particularly to children. 10. A lack of gender balance and gender awareness amongst project teams has been identified as a barrier to equitable access to assistance and the provision of gender-sensitive assistance, particularly for women and girls. Mitigation Measures for identified protection risks: 1. Alternative means of verification of beneficiary identification will be used, subject to donor compliance requirements. 2. Beneficiary selection will be vulnerability-based, rather than status-based, to ensure that assistance targets the most vulnerable. 3. Where possible, market-based approaches will be used in place of in-kind assistance. In-kind assistance packages will be adjusted based on PDM findings. 4. Increased provision/budget for gender modified and separated facilities. Full consultation throughout project cycle with diverse representatives of affected population. 5. Increased provision/budget for communal lighting gender separated facilities, access routes and other areas. Full consultation as above. 6. Programming in camps/informal settlements will include consultation with diverse representatives from the affected population. Interventions will take measures to improve physical access where possible (e.g. wheel-chair access to facilities). 7. All relevant interventions will be contingent upon a full HLP due diligence process conducted by specialist staff. 8. Systematic market monitoring to understand market dynamics and the impact of programming. 9. Code of conduct and training for safe construction practices, based on risk assessment. Plus regular monitoring of construction sites to ensure compliance (e.g. covering and fencing of areas under construction and child labour). In addition, construction works will be phased in order to reduce disruption and risks for the beneficiary population. Project teams will be gender balanced and trained on gender awareness. This includes removing barriers that inhibit the recruitment of women to field teams in particular. 10. General: all activities will require the completion of a participation plan (e.g. accountability mechanisms), in addition to protection risk assessment, monitoring and modification as part of the project-cycle

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1. Escalation of violence and loss of safety and security for staff, partners or beneficiaries: NRC has dedicated Risk and Safety Management staff in the Country who monitor security change triggers and maintain communication with local and regional analysts. All offices maintain updated vulnerability assessments, risk tolerance statements, as well as security plans, for all operational locations. All NRC staff receive proper security and safety training.2. Changes in territorial control. Mitigation measures include good understanding of context, contextual analysis, Security Plans/Security Structure; Community acceptance and good relationship with local authorities; “Red lines” sessions with staff. 3. Large-scale return of refugees and neighbouring states push for large-scale return of refugees to Syria (including coerced return): Collaboration and coordination with other NRC Country programmes in the region. Development of specific contingency plans. Adaptation of programmes. Advocacy efforts.4. Fraud, corruption and bribery: Enforcing NRC’s policy of zero tolerance of corruption through the continuous strengthening of procurement and operating procedures.5. Government permission to operate: Maintain regular and transparent communication with all stakeholders during all stages of the project; continue to build trust and dialogue; cautious and careful liaison work. 6. National/Local authorities withdrawn their approval to conduct WASH activities: Maintain regular and transparent communication with all stakeholders during all stages of the project; continue to build trust and dialogue; cautious and careful liaison work. 7. Interference by armed opposition actors: Continue to engage and meet with relevant actors and ensure the humanitarian imperative and principles of NRC’s work is fully understood. Setting internal redlines and guidance on how to respond to such requests.8. Organisational acceptance: Assessments and initial contact with communities and stakeholders is done, who have good local context understanding and acceptance in addition to well-established networks; principled access. 9. Kidnapping Community Acceptance Recruitment of national staff and LHPs with networks, communication channels, and contacts in a place where interpersonal relationships are at a premium (trust and acceptance). 10. Availability of qualified staff: Mitigation include keeping the qualified staff and advertising extensively for the new positions to be recruited.
