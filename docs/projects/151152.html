Project Summary

The proposed intervention aims to continue answering the most urgent humanitarian needs along the contact line while looking for medium to long term solutions to ensure an improved access to quality GBV response services for the most vulnerable people affected by the protracted crisis in Eastern Ukraine (Luhansk and Donetsk Oblast).
The project will particularly target vulnerable women and girls, affected by the on-going conflict by ensuring access to GBV prevention and response services.
The objectives are as follow:
- Increased availability and access to comprehensive essential GBV services for conflict affected population in Governmental Controlled Areas of Luhansk and Donetsk Oblast
- Improved capacities of health staff and other local service providers for the provision of support to GBV victims and of MHPSS services to conflict-affected men and women, in GCA of Luhansk and Donetsk Oblast
- Improved awareness of the population on GBV and risks and strengthened referral pathways for survivors

Needs

Largely unnoticed, the armed conflict in eastern Ukraine continues to be active and to trigger humanitarian needs. Access to health services for population residing in government controlled areas (GCAs) remains challenging while the de facto authorities in Non-Government Controlled Areas (NGCAs) continue to hamper humanitarian access to those most in need of assistance. The conflict affected population along the contact line is still facing a lack of availability and access to quality services, while experiencing worsening of their health status due to the conflict. Most vulnerable people (mainly women)’s access to health services continues to shrink. Freedom of movement needed by both patients and health care providers continue to be obstructed by insecurity, while transportation means are either limited or too expensive. The separation and the situation along the Line of Contact also heavily affect the mental health of the population. They have or continue to experience various stressors such as exposure to violence, loss (e.g. homes, family members, and communities), unemployment, decreasing incomes, increasing costs of utilities, and difficulties with housing and accessing basic needs and services. This situation affects patients, but also health staff, who live in the same communities as those whom they help and they provide care for. In conflict-affected areas of Ukraine, gender-based violence (GBV) remains a significant protection concern that can impose high life-threatening risk to IDPs and local populations, especially close to the ‘line of contact’. (UNOCHA) Despite the growing nature of its risk, GBV remains a very “hidden and silent” problem in the crisis-affected regions, which further deepens vulnerability. In a study conducted by UNFPA, only 37% of female GBV survivors among IDPs reported that they were seeking protection and services. Many survivors usually do not accept interventions by outsiders when dealing with spousal issues, labelling such problems as a private family matter (Institute of Development Studies, 2017). Victims are often ashamed, distrust the police, do not believe that bringing charges will result in justice, or fear reprisals from the assailant.(OECD, 2014) Survivors of GBV have multiple and complex needs. This includes medical care, safe accommodation, psychosocial counselling, police protection and/or legal advice. Therefore, an effective response to GBV requires knowledge of a comprehensive set of services, which few survivors are likely to have on their own. The referral system in place for survivors is weak, without systemic monitoring and reporting mechanisms. Furthermore, inconsistent implementation of the current system limits a survivor’s access to health services including STI/HIV prevention and treatment, protection, psychosocial support and legal redress. In many locations of conflict-affected areas, provision of outreach-based psychosocial support services, shelter, referral and availability of free-of-charge legal counselling and specialized survivor friendly health services (including post-rape counselling and treatment) remain in high need on the line of contact as many local providers have difficulties to provide a minimum package of services for survivors. In addition, even if there is still a lack of documentation in Ukraine on how the protracted conflict is affecting women and girls, MdM current experience – and especially with the Mobile Units where more than 65% of patients are women – enables to point out difficulties. Besides the dire constrains in accessing primary health care services including qualitative sexual and reproductive health services, additional concerns are being raised for women and girls at risk of domestic violence, rape, intimidation, sexual harassment and survival sex especially in households from disadvantaged economic groups.

MdM involves beneficiaries and local partners (including local authorities) in decision making related to the design of its projects by collecting their feedback and adapting MdM’s strategy accordingly. This also enables MdM to define their role in project implementation and monitoring. Involvement of beneficiaries and local partners includes: • A stakeholders’ consultation process, which enables MdM to gather feedback on current activities and suggestions for improvement and further development from different stakeholders, including the Ministry of Health, oblast and raion level administrations, international organisations, national and INGOs active in the region;• Capacity building components are determined through a consultation process with beneficiaries and local health authorities;• The assessment and selection of health facilities to be supported by mobile units are always done in cooperation with local health authorities (Department of Health and relevant PMSAC);• The weekly work plan of the mobile unit is designed with the head of the relevant PMSAC;• Monthly reports on activities are sent to Department of Health and relevant PMSAC and bi-monthly meetings are organized with Chief Doctors of relevant PMSAC, to discuss the achievements of the project and the challenges faced;• MdM teams are continuously collecting informal feedbacks from patients and beneficiaries of each activity. MdM teams monitor and take into consideration the needs expressed at community level, health facilities staff and local authorities on a daily basis. MdM’s presence and engagement with authorities, communities and patients allow MdM to be able to constantly adjust its support to ensure suitability and relevancy of MdM’s strategy and activities to address the most urgent needs and targeting the most vulnerable population. In this sens, MdM applies the Accountability to Affected Population Framework recommended by the Protection Cluster.
