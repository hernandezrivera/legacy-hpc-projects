Project Summary

In its ninth year, the crisis in Northeast (NE) Nigeria shows no sign of abating. Despite continued scale up in humanitarian assistance, significant gaps remain, as thousands of people affected by the conflict have not been reached. According to Sectoral need assessments nearly 3.5 M individuals will require shelter and settlement related assistance annually with 1.8 M number targeted in 2019 through 2022. This comprises of 1.3 M IDPs’, 916,000 people living in host communities, 822,927 hard-to-reach people and 407,000 returnees in need of both Shelter and NFI support
CRS will assist a total of 15,000 individuals (3,000 HHs) in Kaga , Gubio and Magumeri and Jere LGAs with shelter assistance in Borno State. This support will be delivered through different shelter options guided by CRS’ knowledge and learning, shelter cluster approach which has identified these areas as of high to medium severity in terms of shelter needs and in line with the needs of the target communities. Based on learning from previous interventions, CRS has reviewed its flexible range of shelter options to appropriately respond to conflict-affected populations’ including IDPs, returnees and host families. Safety and dignity of the beneficiaries will be taken into consideration during shelter response interventions These interventions will include distribution of emergency Shelter or A1 Kits, Construction of Modified Bama Emergency Shelter, rehabilitation of Host Family Houses in Exchange for IDP’s Free Rent/Building repair for returnees, To help minimize the loss of life and property, and building on successes from ongoing programming, CRS will continue to support existing DRR committees within targeted communities and CRS plans to assist 10,960 individuals (2,192 HHs) with essential NFIs through e-voucher for the purchase of HH items. The items will include mats, a cooking set, blankets, innerwear for boys, girls, women and men.
All shelter beneficiaries will be sensitized on proposed shelter activities including targeting process, shelter modalities and designs, construction processes, and feedback system. HHs for which land agreements will be developed and signed will receive additional information on the agreement’s content, as well as theirs and landowners’ roles and responsibilities. Relevant authorities, village leaders, and the general population will be sensitized to NFI interventions. Sensitization themes include activity objectives, benefits, assistance modalities, monitoring and evaluation, and targeting process. Information on CRS’ feedback and complaints mechanisms will also be shared. Women will be targeted to reduce their vulnerability especially where a man has several wives to ensure the vulnerable households are covered. Vulnerable members of the community including women and child headed households and those living with disability will be targeted.
To promote transparency in targeting and minimizing conflict, CRS will communicate with male and female community leaders and community members about the selection process, working to reinforce awareness and information sharing efforts, and ensuring security during all registration activities and that feedback is received and integrated into activities.
CRS will continue to prioritize coordination with key actors at National level and in the NE to maximize complementarity and avoid duplication. CRS’s proposed geographic targeting is informed by an ongoing gap analysis based on coordination with other actors, via participation in sectoral working groups/clusters and frequent bilateral meetings and community mapping

Needs

According to Sectoral need assessments nearly 3.5 M individuals will require shelter and settlement related assistance annually with 1.8 M number targeted in 2019 through 2022. This comprises of 1.3 M IDPs’, 916,000 people living in host communities, 822,927 hard-to-reach people and 407,000 returnees in need of both Shelter and NFI support A recent assessment by the Norwegian Refugee Council (NRC) has highlighted that the greatest barriers to accessing safe and adequate shelter in urban and semi urban areas are: high rental costs, HHs’ limited financial resources, housing shortages, threats of forced evictions, refusal by landlords to repair rented houses and discrimination against female tenants. Emergency and provisional shelters, often provided by humanitarian organizations, are constructed on land donated by the landowner, typically without remuneration. Rented rooms and houses are often not fit for purpose: many are unfinished spaces, without roofs, locks doors or windows. Due to the strain on limited resources, disputes between IDPs and host communities around housing, land and property (HLP) issues have increased in frequency, with a particular impact on the most vulnerable amongst the displaced, such as widows and women. CRS Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) with conflict-affected families in Maiduguri, conducted in March 2018, highlighted that while displaced HHs continue to construct makeshift shelters with their own means, using local materials such as wooden poles, grass matting for the walls and roofs, only a few HHs have access to tarpaulins for roof cover. Furthermore, emergency shelters provided by humanitarian actors have a durability of only six months, while HHs are likely to displaced for longer periods of time. Intense sun in the dry season, followed by heavy rain and windstorms in the rainy season, result in the tearing of tarpaulin sheeting. The transitional needs of returnees, though shaped by very different dynamics, are also significant. Nearly one quarter of returnees assessed in return areas (approximately 50,000 HHs) live in inadequate shelters, with 86% (40,000 HHs) of these in partially damaged housing. A lack of capital to purchase building materials undermines opportunities for stimulating early recovery in areas of return. Many returnees also report that in their absence others – often IDPs - have encroached on their properties and land. NFIs remain the second most reported need of affected people in NE Nigeria, in host communities and in camp settings alike. In a recent survey conducted by IOM the second most frequently cited need after food was NFIs. While over 70,000 HHs benefitted from humanitarian NFI support in 2017, significant needs remain, in part due to continuous population movements and the short lifespan of shelter-related NFIs, which implies a constant need for replenishment. CRS’ beneficiaries have reported mattresses, blankets, solar torches/lamps, cooking kits, farming tools and sleeping mats as priority items. WASH NFIs have been identified by CRS beneficiaries as a critical need, including important hygiene items needed to prevent disease outbreak. For example, a lack of containers to carry and store water has been identified by HH respondents as a main barrier to water consumption and the carrying out of basic daily activities. Similarly, CRS’ purchasing pattern data shows that when provided with NFI vouchers, families most frequently purchased laundry soap, bathing soap, sleeping mats, cooking pots and basins.

Activities

CRS will continue to prioritize coordination with key actors at National level and in the NE to maximize complementarity and avoid duplication. CRS’s proposed geographic targeting is informed by an ongoing gap analysis based on coordination with other actors, via participation in sectoral working groups/clusters and frequent bilateral meetings and community mapping The following will be the key activities 1. Distribution of emergency Shelter or A1 Kits (Type 1): 842 HHs living in makeshift shelters, in overcrowded settlements or living with host families in overcrowded shelters, will receive an emergency shelter kit. 2. Construction of Modified Bama Emergency Shelter where 700 HHs living in makeshift shelters or overcrowded conditions requiring or expressing a preference for construction support will receive one constructed modified Bama emergency shelter. 3. Rehabilitation of Host Family Houses in Exchange for IDP’s Free Rent/Building repair for returnees: Particularly in urban areas, many families are hosted by local families and, in some cases, paying monthly rent. A total of 300 HHs living in unfinished buildings or damaged homes, will be supported through rental subsidies and small repairs; indirectly host families will benefit from the repair/improvement work carried out on their houses4. To help minimize the loss of life and property, and building on successes from ongoing programming, CRS will continue to support existing DRR committees within targeted communities. Fire response teams and safety stations will be established and prepositioned in new areas of interventions, with a particular focus on densely populated urban areas and informal settlements. DRR messages related to fire safety and flood/storm mitigation will also be incorporated during house to house visits by Hygiene Promotion.5. Fire stations will be installed across all informal settlements and highly congested urban neighborhoods. CRS will provide and set up clearly identifiable barrels of water and sand buckets around all targeted areas. These will be accompanied by signboards with key messages and images around safe shelter and fire spacing; whistles will be provided to trained members of fire safety committees. For flood and storm preparedness and response, committees will also be provided with wheelbarrows, shovels, pickaxes and metal pans as well as filling soils/sands (or gunny bags) to enable people to create plinths and earth dams within and around makeshift shelters and to channel drainage waters.6. CRS plans to assist 10,960 individuals (2,192 HHs) with essential NFIs through e-voucher for the purchase of HH items. The items will include mats, a cooking set, blankets, innerwear for boys, girls, women and men. A small amount will be given to the beneficiaries to transport items back home from the vendors shop.
