Needs

Poverty and unemployment is a reality for many conflict-affected communities in Syria, which in this region is manifested by people forced to leave their homes, jobs and universities, losing their access to income or an education. In this context, young people (16 to 24 years) do not find opportunities for employment or other ways in which to develop themselves and their skills. This situation of unemployment and a lack of opportunities, paves the way to a life a life of poverty and the adaptation of dangerous coping mechanism. These can include forced recruitment into armed groups as well as into dangerous professions. Recruitment into armed groups is particularly affecting young men, whereas young women are at greater risk of early marriage. It can also lead to long term psychosocial problems such as addiction, aggressive behavior, disappointment, depression and engaging in theft. As the conflict in Syria has destroyed most of the Syrian economy, enterprises of all sizes gradually stopped working and went out of service and ceased to be able to contribute to economic development. Revitalizing micro/small enterprises can complement the production that has come about with a downturn from larger companies. Micro and small enterprises in Syria used to rely on intensive labor and were able to distribute income more broadly and more equitably compared to large institutions. They have the capacity to create job opportunities at reasonable wages for workers from vulnerable families who lack alternative sources of income, as it was stated in Save the Children’s report that poorer families rely on labour and petty trade to subside their needs. Furthermore, if supported they can revive occupations – that might have ceased to exist – and the chance to engage in profitable work for the provision of household livelihoods that can alleviate poverty. The project will begin with a market assessment to identify the specific enterprises to target. This project will focus on building the capacity of youth at risk and vulnerable micro/small enterprises as well as rehabilitating damaged infrastructure (schools). During the most recent relevant assessment of the area using primarily meetings with local councils and key informant interviews. It was found that the majority of the population in this area is hosting community (82%) with a minority of IDPs (18%). The ratio of men and women is nearly equal (47% men and 53% women). Noteworthy is that approximately 46% of the total population is under the age of 25, with the majority of this demographic being young women and girls at 48% of the total population and young men and boys at 44%. In the course of the assessment, it was identified specifically vulnerable people, noting that out of the total population, school drop-out rates (900 M/980F) and unemployment rates (3400M/3215F) are relatively similar for men and women; yet it is noted that there are more male child headed households (312M/133F) and more women than men are illiterate (1080M/1750F). Finally, an assessment of the education sector in the targeted communities has shown that out of the 64 schools assessed, at least 40 are damaged and need to be rehabilitated. Damaged schools significantly contribute to keeping children out of school, due to issues like cold classrooms and insufficient hygiene facilities (toilets).

Protection Risk Analysis

Prioritizing safety and dignity, and avoiding harm by preventing and/or minimizing unintended effects of the program individual vulnerability to both physical and psycho-social risks is a critical foundation for all programming that is guided by a protection perspective. In order to operationationalize this, the four basic Protection Principles will inform this project, specifically: a) Do No Harm; b) the impartiality of assistance in proportion to need and without discrimination; c) protection from physical and psychological harm from violence and/or coercion, and d) assist civilians in claiming their rights, accessing solutions and recovering from the impact of abuse and exploitation. As stated above, and consistent with Global Communities’ standard approach to humanitarian programming, the project is designed using the Do No Harm framework, promoting the dignity, agency, and safety of beneficiaries at all times. Program activities are designed to be culturally appropriate and tailored to the specific context, while preventing competition over resources. We will take great care to broadly disseminate information about the opportunities that the project presents, so that youth and enterprises can equally access the chance to be involved. Our detailed beneficiary selection mechanism, implemented using mobile devices, makes use of project specific vulnerability criteria to select the target group in an impartial way. Also the program will guarantee access through the consistent engagement of community members to identify those who are most in need and targeting of community infrastructure that improves access to essential services that support the dignity of beneficiaries. We have worked to devise ways in which to also provide opportunities for women under this project, in a safe and accessible way. Taking into account the third and fourth principle, specific activities to address these directly: training on safe work conditions and empowerment (leadership and life skills training). Also, activities have been included that will ensure real participation and empowerment of people in the target location, such as involving teachers and students in setting priorities for the rehabilitation of the schools. We will ensure safety and dignity through the implementation of a safe work approach, especially considering the vulnerabilities of young people. It is important that young people everywhere, and especially in conflict-affected contexts, are given the opportunity for meaningful engagement in society and productive employment is a good option. However, there are important considerations that the program itself as well as the employers need to take into consideration in employing young people. Conclusively, we will work with our partner on their approach to communication, i.e. how to present the program transparently in a way that mitigates conflict, anticipating perceptions of unfairness or other issues that could create conflict, and in managing expectations.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

The Bab al Hawa border crossing between Turkey and Syria will remain open and accessible for humanitarian goods and staff, without extended closures, and/or alternate border points are opened and accessible. If the border closes for humanitarian goods or commercial traffic and our ability to receive essential items or funds, we will consider other, less accessible means of delivery. GC staff continue to have consistent access to target locations while maintaining acceptable levels of independence, impartiality, and neutrality. We will ensure this by continuing to utilize our community engagement team to liaise with the relevant camp stakeholders, and continue to reinforce that we are a humanitarian agency that abides by the humanitarian principles of independence, neutrality, and impartiality. Should our level of access change significantly or impact our ability to work within our humanitarian principles, we will look to identify alternative locations or solutions. Active violence does not directly impact GC's offices in Gaziantep, or Qah for a sustained period of time; Violence in the targeted areas remains at a level which allows GC to implement the program, with both armed and unarmed parties to the conflict allowing access to vulnerable HHs for provision of services. We will regularly assess the risk to our beneficiaries and staff of violence in our project locations. If the risk becomes unacceptable we will suspend activities temporarily and close permanently if needed, according to ethical closeout procedures and in communication with the Cluster. Camp managers, Local Councils, relief committees, and community stakeholders will collaborate with GC, and facilitate access for delivery of humanitarian assistance. We regularly engage with local actors to coordinate activities where appropriate. We are also expanding our use of community networks to both implement some activities and encourage local acceptance of our activities.
