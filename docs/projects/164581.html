War Child Holland (War Child) and its consortium of six Syrian implementing partners will provide an education response to 8500 highly vulnerable children in communities in Northwest (Idlib and Aleppo governorates) and Northeast Syria (Deir Ez-zor and Raqqa governorates) across a 12-month action.

Recognising the interconnection between protection and education, the action will increase access to non-formal education and strengthen the protection of vulnerable girls and boys who are out of school or at risk of dropping out, to build their resilience and provide a pathway back into a safe and protective environment in school and within their communities.

The action will include non-formal education interventions including Accelerated Learning Programme (ALP) for out of school children, remedial and homework support for children at risk of dropping out, teacher professional development, and material support in terms of stationery and light rehabilitation. The action will adopt an integrated approach through developing the capacity of teachers on child protection and enhancing the accessibility and safety of the school environment.

This prevention and response approach will address immediate and urgent identiﬁed risks to access to education while contributing to long-term change.

4 - Needs

Assessments within the Whole of Syria demonstrate that Idlib and Aleppo governorates in the Northwest, and Deir Ez Zor and Raqqa governorates in the Northeast, have signiﬁcant areas with a severity rating of 4 or 5 for Education services. In the Northwest, currently 199 schools are out of operation, impacting 59,231 children and 3,703 teachers: 135 schools in Ma’arrat An Nu’man and neighboring communities, impacting 48,649 children and 2,704 teachers; 23 schools in the communities of Ariha, impacting 5,681 children and 384 teachers; and 41 schools in the communities of Saraqab, impacting 4,901 children and 615 teachers (OCHA Situation Report 7,6th February). The education system in displaced areas are already overstretched and suffers from overcrowded classrooms, with 60 to 80 students per class. This continues to limit absorption capacity for newcomers (NWS Readiness and Response plan). In Raqqa and Deir Ez Zor governorates, children have been out of school for at least two years, and there is significantly diminished protection service delivery commensurate with the volume of people in need. Teachers have suffered from a lack of supervision and capacity development support. Reach rapids needs assessment highlighted that education was reported as a priority need in 30% of all assessed communities in Raqqa. While children were able to access schools in 81% of all communities assessed, KIs in 31% of IDP-hosting communities reported that less than half of all IDP children (5-11 years old) attended school in the two weeks prior to data collection. Damage or destruction is estimated to have affected 40 per cent of school infrastructure across the Whole of Syria.

5 - Implementation of activities

War Child and partners will assist the large numbers of girls and boys who have been out of school for over a year by enrolling them in ﬂexible learning classes – basic literacy and numeracy where necessary, and then most signiﬁcantly as part of an accelerated learning programme. Children will be offered two cycles of ALP , reaching eight months in total. In the Northwest, the Curriculum B will be used. In the Northeast, an adapted version of the Kurdish-supported curriculum will be used in Arabic. Where possible, the ALP classes will take place with the formal school environment, to encourage reintegration and avoidance of a parallel system. Placement tests will be used to ascertain the right level, and outcome measured at the conclusion of the cycle. Where the facilities are unsafe or not available, temporary learning centres will be established and equipped. To encourage attendance and address the security risks associated with travelling to the classes, transportation for those living at a distance will be provided (around 50% of children). The children will receive educational kits before the start of the ALP which include exercise books and stationery. To develop the capacity of the educational system to accommodate and properly support children affected by conﬂict, the action will include a coaching programme for educational professionals in the target area. In each of the implementation hubs, a network of coaches will be trained and supported to conduct classroom observations and assist teachers with their professional development through a training plan. Where necessary, light rehabilitation (painting, heating, window repair) and upgrading of WASH facilities will be conducted in formal schools. In Deir Ez-zor and Idlib, remedial and catch-up classes will be offered to students at risk of dropping out. Community-level feedback mechanisms will be used, as well as child-focused Focus Group Discussions and feedback. Monitoring is through Third Party.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

- Sexual exploitation or abuse - Staff safety incident - Burnout or poor health of staff due to activities - Data breach Mitigating measures: - Development and use of own Duty of Care policies (to War Child minimum standards) - Access and effective use of Staff care personnel on War Child staff, including capacity building for partners - IT security measures and training on procedures to prevent data misuse - Contingency planning for change of control or non-renewal/restriction of cross-border resolution. Each of the project locations will be identified and set up using War Child CFS guidelines, which will also be used to train partners on minimum standards for assessing the risk and establishing safe spaces. The spaces will be monitored with contextualised tools, including assessment using the War Child CFS checklist. To ensure they are fit-for-purpose, the spaces will be lightly rehabilitated where required and then equipped with minimum standard safety equipment, winterisation materials, stationery and recreational kits. In all cases War Child will ensure the sites and their surrounding entry and egress points for families and children alike are cleared of ERW. To increase equitable access to the facilities, an inclusion checklist will be applied, so that the needs of disadvantaged groups are considered into programme design (specifically IDPs, CwDs, adolescents, and girls). The checklist will assess accessibility and meaningful participation in the needs assessment; outreach activities and inclusive development of activities; engagement of marginalised groups; andrepresentation in monitoring and evaluation activities.

7 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

As the operating environment continues to be volatile in both the Northwest and Northeast, strong contingency planning is a necessity. This is reflected in the budget, with a shared contingency across both hubs in case of changes in control and access, including non-renewal of the cross-border resolution. As part of the contracting process, emergency response plans will be reviewed with partners to cover staff and information security, Duty of Care policies and flexible programming in case of changes to the context. Partners are supported to create an organisational DoC policy to War Child minimum standards. If relevant, a shared information platform on risks and security reports will be established, at the request of the local partners. As laid out in partner SOPs, senior partner staff in Syria have authority to make any emergency decision about suspending activities, and/or temporarily closing centres or any other protective action on a short term basis. If attacks occur in the local area, partners will directly suspend activities, ensure safe evacuation of any centres, and assess the situation on a daily basis. Relevant safety measures will be put in place at the establishment of the project (safety equipment, evacuation planning, communications protocols, etc). Contigency measures in terms of advancing project costs, paying advance salaries, and remote management of projects will be put in place in case of change of access or control. Travel to and from project sites has been identified as a key risk for both affected populations and project staff. From the project design phase, partners have reduced the amount of transportation needed during the implementation of the project to the minimum, locating services close to target communities as far as possible. Large-scale public gatherings are to be avoided, and invitations to programmatic events are low profile to maintain focus on humanitarian objectives.
