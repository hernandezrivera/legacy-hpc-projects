Project Summary

Mass disruption of livelihoods due to (repetitive) conflict induced displacement, loss of assets and collapsed markets have made a large share of the Iraq population food insecure (estimated 1,9 million people in 2018). 54% of the returnees in Tel Afar and 50% of those in Sinjar (in total 30,477 families) are categorised as high & very high on the return severity index, indicating a dire livelihood- service provision and security situation in their return area.
In response to this humanitarian crises, and aligned with FSC Objective 1 “Provide emergency food assistance to displaced families living in camps and sub-standard accommodation” and FSC Objective 2 “Provide agricultural inputs or services to protect agricultural productive assets and help restore fragile livelihoods of returning or vulnerable families” Dorcas together with World Vision, two active leading NGO’s in the FS sector propose to support 14,400 severely vulnerable returnees and host community in Ninewa Governorate with FS support.
Partners propose to alleviate the suffering of the most vulnerable/food insecure displaced/returning populations in rural Sinjar, Tel Afar (Zummar & Rabea villages) and rural Hamdaniya and in urban Mosul through distribution of four months food vouchers for 2,400 HH - equalling 85% of the minimum SPHERE. Partners will simultaneously start activities to ensure access to income (through Cash for Work for 300 HH) and emergency agricultural assets (chicken packages, farm packages, small greenhouses, fodder & vaccines for livestock) for 475 vulnerable households, with a key focus on female headed households, so they are able to (re)start their livelihoods on a short-term and/or gain access to nutrition. Food support is planned for 4 months since the proposed livelihood activities are expected to create agricultural turnover after a 3 to 4 month inception period.
For the agricultural inputs, specific precedence will be given to female headed households for activities that are culturally appropriate and acceptable for the community. Poultry Gardens and Greenhouses are especially suitable for this target group as activities can take place in close proximity to the house, not only decreasing the risks of violence that women face when leaving their homes but also allowing women to combine agricultural work with other core duties as caregivers. Female staff will be included in all exercises to ensure women’s voices are heard and women specific concerns are taken into account.
Both Dorcas and World Vision have an extensive track record in providing emergency Food Assistance and Agricultural Support to affected communities. Dorcas under the second and third standard allocation of IHPF in 2016, 2017 and 2018 in newly retaken areas (including Hamdaniya). World Vision as key partner of WFP for Food Assistance. These actions, together with other multi-million sectoral projects in i.e. Protection, Health, Education, and WASH implemented in the same areas (rural Sinjar, Tel Afar town, Mosul & rural Hamdaniya) have provided Partners with extensive knowledge of the intervention areas and technical expertise to implement food assistance activities sound and cost-efficient and ensuring a holistic service provision to targeted communities.

Needs

Though poor food consumption scores (FCS) in Ninewa have gone down, still 18% of the population in Mosul, 10% of the population in Sinjar and 16% of those in Tel Afar score either poorly or borderline on the FCS (mVAM #19). Dorcas identified (severe) negative coping mechanism related to a lack of livelihood opportunities in rural areas in Sinjar and Tilkaif (Wana sub-district) through its IHF-funded structured protection monitoring. Increasing (severely) negative food security and livelihood coping mechanisms are used by 41% of the IDP and returnee households in these areas. Main FS negative coping strategies adopted by IDPs/returnees according to REACH’s MSNA (2018) are (1) shifting towards cheaper/lower quality food – 73%, (2)consuming less food per meal-47%, (3) reducing the daily number of meals -41% and (4) borrowing food – 38%. Main negative livelihood coping mechanisms are (1)buying food on credit – 62%, (2)spending savings – 60%, (3) selling household property – 44% and (4) reducing expenditure on non-food items – 40%. Returnees represent the highest percentage of households who divert to negative coping mechanisms as they face adverse conditions in return locations. 54% of the returnees in Tel Afar and 50% of those in Sinjar (in total 30,477 families) are categorised as high and very high on the return severity index, indicating a dire livelihood- service provision and security situation in their return area. Though a national social protection floor exists, it suffers from shortages and administrative flaws mainly related to geographic re-location of households and absence of authoritative support at point of settlement (Emergency Livelihood Cluster Update – February 2018). As such, around 44% of the IDPs and 13% of the returnees didn’t receive their PDS support in the summer of 2018 (REACH MCNA, 2018). The severity of food insecurity differs per household status. Hamdaniya, but also Sinjar & Tel Afar, hosts a relative high amount of vulnerable households. So is an estimated 15%-20% of the household’s in Hamdaniya (Bashiqa) female headed as husbands have fled abroad or have passed away in the battle against ISIS (CRS & USAID MSNA – January 2018). Additionally, analysis of Dorcas’ collected household level assessment data show heightened debts and higher quantities and severity of negative coping mechanisms amongst child- and female headed households (Dorcas HH Food Security Assessments May & June 2018). Moreover, CRS’s MSNA indicated that around 50% of the households have at least one member with a disability or chronic disease in their first-line family. Compared to men; women, children and disabled people are more vulnerable for negative consequences of limited nutritious food intake. Reduced food intake impacts growth and health of children, pregnant & lactating women and elderly, whilst simultaneously depletion of savings used for food also cause school drop out of children (identified negative coping strategy). The school drop-out rate among the IDPs and HC ranges from 23% to 38%.Due to the conflict, an average 68% of acres dedicated to wheat cultivation in Ninewa were lost as well as 57% of the acres used for barley. The large remainder of arable is left unfertile or damaged (FAO, Agriculture and Livelihoods Needs Assessment, 2017). Furthermore, around 80% of the sheep and goats and almost 50% of the cows have gone lost. Poultry suffered the highest average mortality rates at about 90%. Moreover, access to medicine, high-quality animal fodder, vaccines and other veterinarian services are also difficult to access for returned households. Dorcas assessments in Sinjar reported 65% of the landowners lost assets, equipment and/or access to land. Earnings are extremely low, with 39% in Sinjar and Bashiqa (Al-Hamdaniya district) households earning less than 200,000 IQD per month. TF RNA in Tel Afar reported only 12% of assessed households capable to access previous livelihoods and 68% expressing high concerns about lack of income or resources to survive. With the collapse of the agricultural economy the main source of livelihood in rural areas is carrying out daily labour followed by working in small markets and/or employment for the government (CRS & USAID MSNA – January 2018). Main issues identified through FGD’s and KII for restarting agricultural livelihoods are contamination of land and lack of access to financial capital/agricultural inputs.

Activities

Partners propose to alleviate the suffering of the most vulnerable/food insecure displaced/returning populations in rural Sinjar, Tel Afar (Zummar & Rabea villages) and rural Hamdaniya and in urban Mosul through distribution of food vouchers, provision of emergency agricultural assets and cash for work. Food support (vouchers) will be provided to 2,400 HH, to the people most in need, based on a set of vulnerability criteria. These include Female Headed Households, Child Headed Households, elderly and disabled unable to acquire an income and with no or little social support and enhancing severely negative food coping mechanisms. Triangulation and cross-checking will take place when beneficiaries are identified. Markets for basic products (rice, oil, sugar, white beans, iodized salt, chickpeas, burgol, lentel and flour) are re-established in most target locations, allowing for a voucher system to be set in place which supports the local economy and increases the ownership of recipients of aid on how the aid is utilized. Mobile Money transfer (MMT) will be adopted where possible and partners will distribute cash via SMS through a Mobile Network Operator (MNO). The beneficiaries can cash out their monthly cash assistance during fixed disbursement dates at listed POS (Point of Sales), which are readily available across Mosul, Hamdaniya and Telefar. Usage will be monitored and assisted through a cash monitor team. In cases where communities are far away from the nearest POS (10% of target beneficiary), Partners will provide a travel subsidy of 9000 IQD per household per month to ensure access for cashing out. Partners acknowledge that there are particular cases/situations where vouchers might not be a desirable option for beneficiaries. In-kind assistance will therefore remain to be an option for a smaller percentage of the total number of beneficiaries. Partners will simultaneously start activities to ensure access to income (through Cash for Work for 300 HH) and emergency agricultural assets for 475 vulnerable households, with a key focus on female headed households, so they are able to (re)start their livelihoods on a short-term and/or gain access to nutrition. Partners propose to support vulnerable/food insecure households with agricultural inputs that have proven to be most potentially financially/nutritious rewarding and are familiar to their pre-crises livelihood: (1) poultry distributions, (2) agricultural input for small-holder vegetable/wheat farmers (5-15 donnum), (3) mini-greenhouses, (4) vaccines & fodder for small-holder livestock farmers. Agricultural inputs will be made available either through cash/vouchers in locations where desired assets are available in the local market. Where this is not the case, in-kind distributions will be carried out. Subsequent training will be provided on pest-practices to ensure inputs are used in the most cost-efficient way (curriculum will be aligned with agriculture department). As much as possible government extention workers will be incorporated in each step of the implementation to ensure burden-sharing, capacity building and sustainability after the project period has ended. For disabled and elderly community members, appropriate activities will be selected. Especially poultry gardens are highly accessible for people who are, in traditional communities, less mobile (i.e. women) as their work in gardens are more accepted. In the majority of the cases, the poultry garden beneficiaries will be the same as the food assistance beneficiaries. If they are not able to carry out any agricultural activities, Partners will focus on support the family as a whole and will aim to identify possible direct family members who could be eligible for support. HH that are included in this activity are also included in the food voucher assistance, as the proposed livelihood activities are expected to create agricultural turnover after a 3 to 4 month inception period. For vulnerable households who have no access to land, Cash for Work will be offered as a solution to obtain a short-term income (25,000 IQD per day for 15 days). Where possible, CfW activities will be geared towards the rehabilitation of the agricultural sector (i.e. land preparation, irrigation channel digging, etc). Nevertheless, due to high contamination this might not be possible in all locations; as such focus will be placed on rehabilitating community assets that foster the local economy such as school rehabilitation, community garden rehabilitation, road pavement, etc. Coordination on cleaned and cleared structures will take place with MAG and the GoI Demining Department. Where required a request for clearance will be submitted to the Council of Ninewa who forwards it to the Ministry of Environment upon which action is taken by a demining agency or department.

Mitigation Measures

1. All ethnic and religious groups will participate in community committees for the development of selection criteria, as well as religious/informal leaders of different groups. HH visits and a feedback loop on selection will take place to verify vulnerability. Female staff will be included in all exercises to ensure women’s voices are heard.2. Activities will take place during daytime and not on national/religious holidays. Where required different sites for men and women will be used. If a beneficiary is unable to come to the distribution site, Partners provide once-off financial accessibility support for transport or child-care, or a next of kin can be sent, if original civil documentation is shown linking the beneficiary HH PDS number to the next of kin. 3. Waiting areas and training sites will, where appropriate, be separated for men and women. Distribution sites will be in the closest proximity of the beneficiary’s place of residence so limited travel is required. Explanation will be provided to communities on selection criteria that give precedence to women/FHH. Offering poultry gardening and greenhouses as agricultural activities decreases the risks of violence that women face when leaving their homes as these can be implemented close to home and also allow women to combine agricultural work with other core duties as caregivers.4. Explanation will be provided to communities on selection criteria that might give precedence to certain ethnic groups if they were found to be more vulnerable. Nevertheless, aim will be to target beneficiaries based on their ratio within the population, to avoid social conflict.5. Project staff will closely monitor the voluntary support provided during distributions. Suspected minors will be talked with to identify their age and/or their need to be in school. Awareness will be raised about the undesirability of deploying children as (voluntary) support. 6. Distributions will be done at day time at close proximity of beneficiary’s residence. Information about the time and location will be provided in due time but not too long and not to a broad range of people, to avoid unintended “persons with interest” showing up and/or awaiting the beneficiaries. PDM’s will assess if cases occurred. A. Partner’s Security Officers/Managers, in coordination with the project teams, frequently re-assess security threats by working with UNAMI. The project teams are provided with appropriate security orientations upon the beginning of their assignment and will be updated about changes in the security situation. Partners have established Security SOP’s that outline the likeliness and impact of possible security threats. Evacuation routes and plans are in place.B. All staff members undergo full mine awareness education from a certified NGO. The cash for work activities will be held only in the areas that are fully demined. The participants of asset replacement in the rural areas should present how, when and by whom their lands are demined. C. Participants will be trained on safety on how to handle the assets well as risks associated with livelihoods activity. Poultries will be vaccinated before distribution and household visits will take place to check on health status and provide extra vaccine and medical assistance if required. D. Partners maintain the principle of impartiality, neutrality and independence by maintaining a clear communication with communities on selection criteria, as well as involving communities in key decisions of design and planning. Project staff includes people from different groups, including the local target group. Feedback and complain mechanisms will be in place. E. The project will coordinate and consult communities and local authority with the location, date and time of distribution. Information and updates from security, and UNAMI will be taken into consideration. At locations with high security risk, project teams will be accompanied by security staff.

How is your project accountable to affected population

To ensure accountability, Partners will adopt WV’s Accountability to Affected Population (AAP) with reference to its “Communicating with Community” strategy, which has been mainstreamed in all other WV livelihoods and food assistance programming, so as to ensure two way communications between beneficiaries and the project. The AAP is guided by 4 pillars of framework as below:1. Information provision: Partners will provide information of interventions using face-to-face engagements and banners. To raise community awareness on PSEA, related information will also be provided, including rights and reporting mechanisms. Likewise, communities will be informed on available feedback and complaints mechanisms. 2. Consultation: Partners will ensure to consult the local authorities and communities on the key aspects (i.e. targeting criteria) and decisions for the project before the implementation through the FGD method. Separate male and female FGD’s will be organised to ensure all voices are heared.3. Participation: Partners will ensure to collect inputs throughout the project from the most vulnerable people (beneficiaries). At the same time, instead of passively receiving food aid, communities will be given support to actively contribute to generate income to improve food security. 4. Suggestion and Feedback Mechanism: different mechanisms will be used to receive information, complaints, and feedback from people of different backgrounds, including face-to-face meetings with MEAL teams, and suggestions & complaints boxes and the operation of a feedback hotline. This information is classified in 5 categories (Sensitive complaints, non-sensitive complaints, suggestions, inquiries, feedback), and all inputs received will be addressed and followed up appropriately. Monthly summary report of all the inputs will be shared with project staff. MEAL: The consortium will adopt World Vision’s MEAL system that serves to strengthen the program activities through consistent monitoring and feedback on programmatic impact, progress and achievements. 1)Indicator Tracking Table: the project will set indicators at the beginner of project and will measure progress according to the MEAL plan throughout project, as identified through the Means of Verification.2) During Implementation Monitoring: Dedicated staff members will be present at distribution sites to monitor technical and accountability compliance. Moreover, staff members will also be present and identified shops/vendors to monitor services are appropriately provided to the entitled food voucher beneficiaries and their food items are received. Project team and technical leads, will visit agricultural interventions regularly, to provide technical support and ensure asset is used as planned. A vet will be employed to conduct monitoring visit a week after distribution of poultry to provide vaccine or medicine if required.3)Post Implementation Monitoring: After each activity packages is delivered (i.e. voucher distributions, agricultural inputs, etc.) post implementation monitoring will be carried out. This will be conducted by trained MEAL staff in gender sensitive manners to continuously ensure accountability and learning for program improvement. Results of the PIMs and actions taken based on results will be communicated back to beneficiaries and integrated into the project. 4)Accountability Framework: the consortium’s assessment and accountability teams focus on strong communication with beneficiaries and involving in monitoring the project implementation. This involvement will be guided by WV's Program Accountability Framework (which is shared with all consortium partners). Complaints and suggestions from the different feedback mechanisms (hotline, feedback boxes, feedback desks during distributions) are all recorded, compiled and assessed by the MEAL staff and presented to the project staff for relevant action and improvement on a monthly basis.
