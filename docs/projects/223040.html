Project Summary

Targeting the Rohingya refugee camps and host communities, this initiative addresses urgent food insecurity while empowering participants to build self-reliance and economic resilience. Participants, particularly women and youth, are selected based on criteria such as economic vulnerability, gender, household composition (e.g., female-headed households), high dependency ratios, and lack of access to income opportunities. The participants will receive business skills training covering essentials like business ideas, cost analysis, budgeting, and customer and supplier relations, alongside life skills. The project’s core objective is to enhance long-term resilience through climate-smart, sustainable agriculture and livelihood activities. For instance, 600 refugee women, youth, and men will undergo homestead gardening training focused on climate-smart practices, adopting new technologies, and utilizing household resources for production. This approach expands local food sources and offers pathways to income generation. Additionally, a total of 150 refugee clients will be trained in food processing, preservation, safety, and utilization. These skills help reduce food waste and increase food availability throughout the year while creating new income streams. Participants also receive a startup grant, including input and equipment for initiating home gardening and small business activities.
In the host community, IRC will support 500 participants with agriculture training to boost food production through homestead gardening and crop production. With a startup grant in cash and in kind, participants will start growing food and diversify income sources. Training will cover compost preparation, climate-smart technologies, and Integrated Pest Management (IPM) for organic production. Furthermore, digital agriculture extension services will keep farmers informed on best practices. The project also will provide livestock management training, focusing on goat and poultry rearing for secure food production and income to 500 clients. This includes livestock distribution, training on production and feed management, disease, biodiversity, and shed management. IRC will also be building the capacity of livestock service providers to ensure access to veterinary services and livestock businesses, supporting the transition into sustainable businesses. Coordinating with government and private sectors, these agricultural and livestock initiatives will foster households economic, laying the groundwork for improved food security. IRC and partners will follow up with clients to support diverse livelihood activities. In addition, 120 participants will gain skills in food management, including dry fish processing, food preservation, and food processing techniques to reduce waste, extend food shelf life, and create income opportunities. Local market linkage activities help to connect with suppliers and buyers, strengthening farming and income opportunities.
To strengthen community disaster risk management, IRC will establish and train 300 client from self-help groups, community watch teams, and women’s support groups in multi-hazard preparedness. These groups will conduct community risk assessments, develop risk mitigation plans, and raise early warning awareness to support vulnerable households. IRC will also build the leadership capacity of Disaster Management Committees (DMCs) to enhance their ability to deliver essential emergency services, including food distribution, and to coordinate with CiCs, Site Management, and other sectors. IRC also will provide multi-purpose cash transfers to 500 clients for basic needs and assess cyclone shelters for gender-responsive facilities, collaborating with shelter management and local authorities. Moreover, IRC will engage with FSS actors through regular meetings, aligned interventions, capacity building, joint assessments, and resource optimization to ensure effective and high-quality project implementation.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

Cox’s Bazar, a disaster-prone district in Southeastern Bangladesh, with a population of 2.8 million people (BBS, 2022), faces the immense challenge of hosting the world’s largest refugee camp . Since the influx of refugees in 2017, and with no prospects of repatriation by 2024, the protracted crisis is expected to continue. Currently 994,124 stateless Rohingya refugees residing across the camps within Cox’s Bazar (Joint GoB– UNHCR Population Factsheet, Aug' 2024). An additional 345,972 host community members across the Teknaf and Ukhiya sub-districts are also experiencing the effects of this growing crisis (JRP 2024). This demographic shift has severely strained local resources, services, systems and increased competition on food, income and employment opportunities for both refugee and host communities. The refugee crisis has also increased environmental degradation including deforestation and the depletion of natural resources. Combined the crisis has increased poverty levels and reliance on external aid, as well as exacerbated tensions between refugees and host communities. While the basic needs in the camps continue to grow, the humanitarian funding is not on the same trajectory, with the joint response plan perpetually inadequately funded over the years, and the restrictions on livelihoods programming for refugees persisting. There are 45% of Rohingya and 30% of host communities in Cox’s Bazar in Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) Phase 3 or above, with 5% in the critical Phase 4 (IPC Acute Food Insecurity Analysis October 2024) and 97% of Rohingya households classified as moderately to highly vulnerable, relying entirely on humanitarian aid for their survival (REVA-7). The situation has also deteriorated in the host communities, with the poverty rates in Teknaf (30%) and Ukhyia (40%) making them some of the poorest sub-districts in the country. The REVA-7 reports also revealed that a significant rise in vulnerability among Bangladeshi households, with highly vulnerable households doubling from 13% in 2017 to 30% in 2024. In response to food scarcity, 67% of camp households and 50% of surrounding Bangladeshi households rely on less preferred, less expensive foods as a primary coping strategy. Additionally, 49% of Rohingya households report reducing meal portions, and 37% have reduced meal frequency. Common strategies include buying on credit or borrowing food (58%), borrowing money (51%), and spending savings (29%) (J-MSNA 2023). Food remains a top priority for both communities, with 80% of Rohingya and 57% of Bangladeshi households expressing a need for better access to fresh food and ongoing food assistance (REVA-7). Refugee households reported a median monthly expense of $69, significantly exceeding the humanitarian assistance of $40 they receive. As a result, they are compelled to seek alternative sources of income or cash inflows to cover their basic needs. Over half (53%) of refugee households report not having any livelihoods opportunities nearby, limiting their ability to support themselves, and their families (J-MSNA 2023). Frequent climate-induced disasters in Cox's Bazar have compounded the crisis, severely impacting the local economy and livelihoods for both camp and host community populations. These events have led to increased labor demand within a stagnating market with reduced wages, inflation of essential item prices, and loss of livestock, crops, and grazing lands, further straining resources and resilience. This situation highlights the need for resilience-building initiatives to strengthen the capacity of both refugees and host communities to cope with and recover from shocks, promoting self-reliance and sustainability.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

Women and men face significant barriers in accessing livelihood opportunities due to entrenched socio-cultural norms. These norms limit their ability to participate in training, start business, engage with market actors, or manage resources, resulting in restricted participation in income-generating activities and hindering effective asset management. Furthermore, market actors often overlook gender-specific needs, limiting women’s economic empowerment and excluding them from fully benefiting from livelihood opportunities. To address these challenges, the project will adopt a gender-sensitive approach to empower women and ensures their safety and dignity. Central to this approach is the Do No Harm principle, the activities will implement targeting women and vulnerable clients, including training, cash or input distribution, and market linkage engagements. In addition, the approach will protect women from violence, exploitation, and discrimination throughout their participation. Safeguards will be implemented to ensure activities take place in safe, confidential, and respectful environments, protecting participants from potential harm. The project will focus on enhancing women’s business skills, providing market-relevant information, and equipping them with the technical skills necessary to respond to economic opportunities. By ensuring that women are not only participants but also decision-makers in production and marketing activities, the project will foster a more inclusive and equitable market system. Integrating gender-sensitive strategies and upholding the Do No Harm principle will contribute to a safer and more sustainable market, benefiting both women and their communities. This approach will enhance women’s livelihoods, safeguard their well-being, and drive positive social change, promoting long-term resilience and development for both the Rohingya refugees and the host communities.

2.3 - Age & Disability

The IRC and its partners will focus on inclusive food security programs targeting adolescents and youth aged 15-24 and vulnerable women up to 60 years, with a special emphasis on caregivers of youth and adolescents. To promote access for people with disabilities (PwDs), the program will implement a targeted outreach strategy aiming to engage approximately 12% of PwD clients.To identify and support PwD participants, IRC will use the Washington Group questionnaire, ensuring accurate identification and needs assessment. All staff and volunteers will receive specialized training to assist PwD clients, with the program delivery adapted to include accessible equipment, tailored training approaches, and business activities that meet the specific needs of PwDs.Not all participants will receive direct services from IRC; some will be referred to other service providers through a coordinated network supported by service mapping. Caregivers will be encouraged to participate in program activities to support PwD clients, reducing barriers and enhancing program impact. To ensure effective service delivery and meet the diverse needs of all clients, IRC has allocated a dedicated budget for age and disability inclusion, making certain that every participant is supported in achieving their goals within the program.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

IRC and all implementing partners have robust Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) Frameworks in place. The project's AAP approach aligns with the IASC’s commitments on AAP, emphasizing the integration of AAP principles and practices into all aspects of humanitarian programming. Consult with the women, men and youth participants, design feedback and complaints mechanisms, and ensure AAP-related questions are incorporated into templates. IRC's primary goal is to comprehensively understand the level of satisfaction with services, identify community needs, concerns, hopes, and preferences, and incorporate these insights into program design to enhance intervention quality. The participation of affected populations, including marginalized groups, is an essential and ongoing component of the program, involving in-depth participatory research and frequent community consultations. An effective and accessible Client Feedback and Response Mechanism (CFRM) is already established, allowing communities to provide feedback, raise complaints, improve referral mechanisms, and facilitate two-way communication between IRC/implementing partners and the communities. Prioritize strengthening the CFRM for protection from sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA). Maintain PSEA Network membership and provide PSEA training to all staff, volunteers, and contractors. The project will regularly collect community voices to support advocacy, allocate budgets for AAP activities, assess SEA risks, and ensure affected groups can register complaints and provide feedback during implementation. Information on accessing services will be widely disseminated, referral pathways will be developed and shared within PSEA networks and program teams and conduct orientation on AAP-related actions and principles, including PSEA for all.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

The IRC is dedicated to integrating Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) into the Rohingya Refugee Response in Bangladesh through a robust framework that includes IRC’s safeguarding policy, Core Humanitarian Standards, and the Minimum Operating Standards for PSEA (MOS-PSEA). IRC will ensure all staff and partners are informed of and adhere to codes of conduct and safeguarding policies, with rigorous hiring processes that screen for SEA history, including self-disclosures. Policy implementation is championed by the Senior Management Team (SMT), promoting a zero-tolerance culture through ongoing PSEA training. Specialized Safeguarding staff and PSEA Focal Points will coordinate with project teams and the PSEA Network to maintain alignment. Tailored PSEA training for staff and partners, especially frontline workers, will emphasize a “do no harm” approach and confidentiality. Beneficiaries from both refugee and host communities will be informed about PSEA policies and available protections during training sessions, enhancing awareness across communities. Community engagement is central: IRC will create accessible, safe complaint and feedback mechanisms for beneficiaries. A survivor-centered approach ensures survivors’ safety, confidentiality, and support access without mandatory reporting. Feedback channels—such as in-person, hotline, and complaint boxes—will accommodate all genders and ages, with mandatory reporting for minors. IRC commits to notifying donors and stakeholders of SEA concerns, conducting high-quality investigations to support survivors, hold perpetrators accountable, and reinforce prevention. Program quality will be enhanced through regular field visits, interviews, and focus groups, fostering continuous improvement and best practices.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

The project will integrate disaster risk management and climate resilience through community-based approaches, empowering communities to enhance their preparedness and response capacities. By building knowledge and skills in multi-hazard preparedness, the project will involve community members in risk identification, mitigation, and preparedness to reduce vulnerabilities.The focus will be on strengthening disaster preparedness, anticipatory action, and forecast-based early response. IRC will train youth and community groups on disaster risk management and equip them to support their communities. Special training will be provided to vulnerable households in landslide- and flood-prone areas, particularly targeting women and youth, enabling them to lead resilience-building efforts within their communities.To promote sustainable practices, the project will include green skills training and raise clients’ awareness of climate-smart practices, empowering them to adopt environmentally responsible methods. Youth climate activists will be trained to support their communities in adopting climate-smart practices and DRR activities aimed at minimizing the impact of climate-induced disasters. Additionally, IRC will establish information-sharing mechanisms to strengthen the local disaster risk management system and build community resilience.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

The project incorporates Emergency Preparedness and Response (EPR) measures to minimize disruption during natural disasters or emergencies. It focuses on raising disaster preparedness awareness among clients to help protect their businesses from damage and loss, providing early information through project staff and connecting clients with CPP (Cyclone Preparedness Programme) volunteers. IRC will also establish Emergency Response Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs), ensuring all team members understand their roles and responsibilities during a crisis for a rapid and coordinated response. While the project does not include a specific contingency fund, it will be supported by IRC's dedicated disaster risk reduction team, which will mobilize and allocate funds during and after a disaster to assist affected clients and ensure continued operations. These measures are designed to ensure the project's resilience and smooth continuation even in the face of unforeseen events.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

Yes, IRC will actively engage youth, specifically those aged 18-24, with youth participation being central to the project's activities. They will be capacitated through training in areas such as business skill, life skills, green skills, climate smart practices and enabling them to contribute to local community development and economic growth. The project also facilitates youth engagement with private companies for creating self-employment opportunities and volunteering in the camp. Additionally, youth will be involved in civic engagement and disaster risk reduction activities, where they will play a key role in raising awareness, supporting preparedness efforts, and disseminating early warning information in collaboration with community volunteers. This engagement aims to enhance youth leadership, conflict resolution skills, resilience, and inclusion in community-level decision-making processes.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

Yes, the project promotes self-reliance and resilience for refugees in the camps by supporting climate-smart food production activities, income-generating opportunities, and skill-building initiatives. Through homestead gardening, aquaculture, and training in high-value crop production, refugees gain practical skills to produce their own food and generate income. Additionally, activities like food preservation and processing enhance food security and reduce waste, while community-focused initiatives such as cash-for-work and social cohesion projects empower refugees to contribute to and take ownership of their community’s resilience. This approach not only addresses immediate food needs but also builds sustainable skills and capacities for long-term self-reliance.

4.2 Do project activities in the host community link to development initiatives, and/or align with development priorities in the Cox’s Bazar district? If yes, briefly describe.If yes, briefly describe.

Yes, the project activities in the host community are designed to align closely with the development priorities and growth strategies of Cox’s Bazar district, including the District Development and Growth Priorities (Dec 2022). The project supports climate-smart agriculture, market linkages, and food management, which are essential for enhancing local food security, economic resilience, and sustainable livelihoods. Through skills training, access to financial services, and support for market integration, the project strengthens local economies, fosters resilience against climate change, and supports inclusive growth. Additionally, the focus on natural resource management, disaster risk reduction, and community infrastructure aligns with district-level goals for environmental sustainability and disaster preparedness, contributing to long-term development and stability in Cox’s Bazar.
