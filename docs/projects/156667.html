Needs

Shelter needs are acute in Syria, where more than 4.73 million people require shelter assistance. Across heavily crisis-affected areas, internally displaced people, host communities, and returnees face shelter inadequacy issues whereby unsafe shelters is causing protection concerns and health risks to mount, degrading resilience and impeding early recovery even where conflict has receded from the area. Income vulnerability means that affected people are often unable to afford repairs themselves. An increasing number of IDPs are now willing to return homes, but lack of safe shelters acts as an impediment, increasing the need to repair damaged shelters in order to facilitate return home and to protect returnees from life-threatening shelter conditions encountered on return to heavily damaged areas. In the most heavily crisis-affected areas of Syria, including Dar’a (Dar’a al Balad, Yadoudeh, Kherbet Ghazala, and Nawa), Homs old city, Eastern Aleppo city, Deir-ez-Zor city, and Hama (Suran, Hamra), up to 75% of buildings have sustained damage causing resident populations, in addition to newly arrived returnees, to live in dangerous living conditions in the ruins of their former homes. Widespread income vulnerability and food insecurity (50% of households report taking on debt to purchase food) limits the ability of the affected population to make shelter repairs themselves. Lack of safe WASH facilities and of private, lockable spaces acts to degrade the protection of the affected population, especially women and children. Lack of safe shelter in Syria is posing life-threatening risks to different age and gender groups. Exposure to the elements because of inadequate shelter increases the risk of illness amongst the affected population, risking the health and lives of, in particular, young children, children, and older adults. Lack of proper staircases, rubble in and around shelters, and lack of accessibility features such as ramps in particular impacts older adults and young children who may have limited mobility. Meanwhile lack of private and lockable shelters heightens the vulnerability of all age-gender groups but in particular girls and women who are at increased risk of gender-based violence (GBV). Additionally, continued instability and the likelihood of new displacements, particularly from the north of Syria, means that emergency shelter solutions will be continued to be needed in order to save lives. Housing, Land, and Property (HLP) concerns remain acute, especially where lack of access to full HLP documentation and/or pathways to restore documentation impedes the ability of the affected population to establish full legal ownership. Sources: DRC rapid assessments, 2018; Syria HNO, 2019.

Implementation of activities

DRC has field staff present in all areas of operation who will conduct technical assessments and supervise works delivered by private contractors. Wherever possible, local contractors will be preferred, multiplying benefits to local economies. Hand-over of the repaired infrastructure will be made to local authorities/actors (MoLA) after six months of works completed; a warranty period of six months will be put in place to ensure full completion of works in accordance with Shelter sector minimum standards. The end-of-warranty visit will be conducted in collaboration with local authorities facilitating handover.

Protection Risk Analysis

PR1: Inter or intra community tension due to service delivery locations causing perceptions of discrimination or favouritism. Risk: Medium; Impact: High. Mitigation measures: close coordination with authorities (MoLA, DoLA, municipality) and humanitarian coordination with SARC and Shelter sector to include communities and authorities in planning; adherence to minimum standards established by the Shelter sector; field monitoring by DRC personnel. Needed resources: inclusion of unequal distribution of services in standard monitoring activities and monitoring formats; Shelter sector minimum standards. PR2: Theft of Shelter rehabilitation/construction materials such as generators and construction materials (during delivery/storage/ after installation) by groups and used in conflict activities. Likelihood: Low; Impact: High. Mitigation measures: MoUs agreed with local authorities/councils to ensure local ownership of materials and rehabilitated/new facilities. Needed resources: support from Shelter sector in development/application of cost recovery systems. PR3: Selection criteria is influenced by local stakeholders/community members in a way that contributes to nepotism, tension in the community and risk of most vulnerable being left out. Likelihood: High; Impact: High. Mitigation: transparent selection criteria clearly communicated to and agreed upon with all stakeholders; adherence to humanitarian principles and minimum standards set by Shelter sector; triangulation of information and wide community engagement ensuring a range of stakeholders and affected groups are consulted and included in the selection process. PR4: Lack of security of tenure document excludes benefiting from shelter repair/rehabilitation works. Likelihood: High; Impact: High. Mitigation measure: close coordination with MoLA to ensure that alternate documents of ownership are respected at all levels. Needed resources: list of alternate documents which may be considered to prove ownership with all partners, from the Shelter sector. PR5: repair of apartments encourages returns to areas which are not yet safe, for example because of risk of unexploded remnants of war (ERW). Likelihood: Medium; Impact: High. Mitigation: for longer-term, owner-oriented interventions DRC will only intervene where returns have already happened and are gathering pace. DRC will liaise with a range of local actors in addition to other Shelter sector partners to understand ERW risks inherent in the area. In line with the do-no-harm approach, any interventions which are assessed as likely to put the affected population at risk will be cancelled.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1. Should any of the planned areas of intervention become inaccessible due to safety concerns, DRC would consider alternate locations in line with its standard targeting criteria and procedures. 2. Should exchange rate fluctuations between donor currencies and the Syrian pound mean that DRC had less actual funds available in Syria; and/or, should this project not be fully funded, DRC may be unable to deliver all activities as planned. DRC would prioritize those communities in most urgent need of Protection assistance according to verified data from the field. 3. Should all necessary approvals not be received from relevant stakeholders (including and especially SARC) and/or governmental entities for activities, DRC would as a last resort return the related funds to donors. 4. Should community members eligible for Shelter support not have full HLP documents in place, DRC will attempt to make referrals to legal actors able to provide assistance in restoring HLP documentation (e.g. SARC).
