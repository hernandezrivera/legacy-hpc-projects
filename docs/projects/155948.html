Needs

Depending on HNO 2019. Assessments have indicated that gender-based violence, particularly sexual harassment, early/forced marriage and domestic violence (including intimate partner violence and other forms of family violence against women and girls) continue to pervade the lives of women and girls in Syria inside and outside the home, resulting in very few spaces where women and girls feel safe. The length of the crisis and deep-rooted social norms, in conjunction with the mounting lawlessness in some areas, are normalizing this violence, resulting in the detrimental psychosocial wellbeing and continued erosion of women’s and girls’ rights. The fear of sexual harassment (on the way to school, in the market, at distribution sites, etc.) as well as sexual violence, often associated with kidnapping, is a concern raised by consulted women and girls and contributes to psychosocial stress and limits their movements. Additionally, various means of technology—e.g. texting and social media—are used to sexually harass and facilitate early/forced marriages. The restriction on freedom of movement of women and girls also inhibits their access to services, humanitarian aid and ultimately curtails their rights, with widows who have experienced divorce and widowhood and adolescent girls disproportionately impacted. The shame and stigma surrounding sexual violence means that survivors often do not talk about violence when it happens. Families arrange marriages for girls, believing it will protect them and ease the financial burden on the family. Girls are reportedly being married younger, with early/forced marriage- sometimes involving girls of 10 or 11 years old - framed as a protective coping strategy. The socio-economic situation, lack of livelihood opportunities, and increased poverty are ultimately leading to women and girls to be at higher risks of all forms of violence including sexual exploitation and abuse. Adolescent girls and young girls can experience sexual harassment and violence in situations such as when working on the land/ farm, in shops, and in instances when affected by child recruitment. Household level gender roles continue to change with negative implications within families and communities. Although women have more responsibility, this does not infer authority. Women have taken on additional roles traditionally associated with men, including as heads of household and principal breadwinners for their families. This contributes to increased pressure and stress for women to provide for their families as they juggle commitments within the home and workplace. Consequently, they are exposed to increased risks of violence against them within the family and at their workplace. Where GBV services exist, women and girls have confirmed that they have a positive impact on their lives, with women and girls safe spaces serving as a place to seek safety and to receive essential life-saving services (health, psychosocial and legal support) and other needed services (e.g. vocational and skill training). The areas were selected based on a protection severity scale.

Protection Risk Analysis

the following risks are a potential with severity from Medium to high - Lack of quality services and referral expose survivors to harm, enhancing risk of stigmatization reprisal and further trauma. Lack of quality services also lead to fewer disclosures and lack of trust in services. -Privacy concerns along with confidentiality and safety might prevent women & girls to access GBV response services- Resistance or backlash from sections of the community and community leaders on GBV messaging -The backlash from family or community on women participating in recreational, vocational or empowerment ---Persons with disability have difficult accessing GBV services.Mitigation mechanism :-Service mapping including only those that can provide survivor-centered services - Traditional legal systems put women more at risk, the message to be mainstreamed - Self-care strategies as part of programmes and organization HR policy with global standards and the GBV SOPs, ensure data protection protocols and private physical space are in place. Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Assumption 1: We assume that the security situation in the defined area is staying the same.Risk 1 : Changing the security situation that interfere with implementation of the project.Mitigation Strategies: We will establish a response plan that can be activated according to the situation. the plan could include equipping thecenter with needed emergency equipment. Designing the activities in a flexible way where we can change the way of conducting theactivity according to the situation so as to achieve the wanted results. and we can focus on more suitable activities like doing PFA . Stoppingthe activities for short period. moving the center to another place if deemed necessary after negotiation with OCHA.Assumption 2: Local council and authorities are continue to allow for our work, as the case right now with other projects.Risk: The local authorities decide to prevent us from implementing the project or part of it.Mitigation: as preventive measures we will coordinate with the local authorities and negotiate with them about the best way to implement theproject and discuss the importance the project that will help.Assumption 3: Women and girls has no barriers to attend the activities.Risk 3: Some barriers can occur that prevent women and girls from attending the activities, for example, some will be prevented by their family to go to center unless they dress in certain way , which the girls and women do not want, as happend in one of our projects.Mitigation response: we can adapt to overcome those barriers as possible. For example: in the mentioned example, we were successful inimplementing our activities at home for the women and girls by a female staff so they did not have any problem to participate.Assumption 4: The attitudes and needs of the population stay the same as we found in the needs assessments .Risk4: changing in the attitudes or priorities of the population that will affect their willingness to benefit from the project activities.Mitigation strategies: Will work on awareness raising through out the project period and will get the feed back from the beneficiaries aboutthe services and adapt accordingly .
