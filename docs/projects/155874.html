Project Summary

SECD during this program in the northwest of Syria aim increasing resilience and affected people’s access to basic Education Based on recent assessment conducted, the project defines the priority areas and priority schools, maintaining the following two main objectives: enhancing the quality of education services and scaling up equitable access to education and WASH services; within the proposed project SECD will support 20 schools at Idleb and Aleppo governorate , about 14,312 Child (7139 girls and 6861 boys)and 312 teachers will receive education assistance . SECD will conduct the following activities during the program:
- Provide children and youth with formal education
- Provide children and youth with non-formal education programs.
- Conduct Back-to-Learning (BTL) campaigns.
- Establish, expand and rehabilitate new classrooms.
- Rehabilitate, improve or construct gender-sensitive WASH facilities
- Provide students with learning materials (stationary, pens, notebooks), school bags and school in a Carton kit in formal and nonformal settings
- Provide professional development to teaching staff (child-centered and protective pedagogy, active learning, self-learning, life-skills, Curriculum B, ECCE, New Curriculum, psychosocial support and referral mechanisms.
- Provide teachers and education personnel with incentives
- Provide children with school-based psychosocial support programs in formal and nonformal settings
- Provide children with life skills and citizenship education programs in formal and nonformal settings
- Provide children with supplementary learning materials for children attending formal and non-formal education.

Needs

The protracted crisis in Syria, now in its eighth year, has forced 2.1 million, over one-third of children, out-of-school and has put a further 1.3 million children at risk of dropping out. Over 5.8 million school-aged children (including more than 100,000 Palestine refugee children) and about 121,972 education personnel are in need of education assistance inside Syria, 61 percent of whom are in acute and immediate need. The provision of education services inside Syria is limited. Hostilities have resulted in over one in three schools being damaged, destroyed, no longer accessible or occupied for shelter and other purposes. Schools are reported as the most common building type used for IDP sites in Syria, accounting for an estimated 32 percent of collective centers. Due to a shortage of functional classrooms, classes are overcrowded and over one million children required to attend double-shift schools. An extreme shortage and under payment of qualiﬁed teachers also limits the quality and provision of education. Children who are out of school face increased protection risks including child marriage and the worst forms of child labor. The changes in control in central and south Syria in early and mid-2018 as well as increasing hostilities in northwest Syria in September 2018 caused new waves of civilian displacement into Rural Damascus, Idleb and Aleppo. Existing education capacity in these areas was already limited prior to recent displacement inﬂux. With the addition of over 1.9 million displaced school-aged children in these three governorates, many of whom have been displaced multiple times, education capacity has been nearly exhausted, with widespread implications on the ability of children to access quality education. The ability of schools to absorb more IDPs into the education system is very limited. Some schools operate in double shifts (morning and afternoon classes) to accommodate the large inﬂux of displaced children. This reduces the teaching-learning hours per day, adversely affecting learning outcomes for IDPs and host community residents alike. As the conﬂict continues, effective teaching becomes increasingly challenging due to mixed-aged, mixed ability, over-crowded and under-resourced classrooms. More than 40 percent of the out-of-school children in Syria is between 15 and 17 years of age and have differentiated learning, skills development, and livelihoods needs.SECD conducted an educational needs assessment in Bdama and Sarmin in November 2018. Data indicate that schools suffer from the lack of learning materials, teaching staff, and salaries.In addition, the WASH facilities in schools need rehabilitation taken into consideration the gender-sensitive facilities according to WASH cluster recommendations. SECD Supported two schools in Sarmin community with fuel in order to fill the gap in those schools.

Implementation of activities

All the activities will be conducted by the technical staff of SECD, including the volunteers. Regarding the procurement process, SECD will commit to the procurement policy to select the vendors who will only supply the commodities from the local markets if available. SECD has a proven track record of successful project implementation employing both approaches. Implementation for this project will be complete through SECD. The MEAL team will remain present throughout the various activities of the project. Also, SECD will be responsible for the technical, project management, and determine project-speciﬁc workplans, ensuring transparent and clear procurement processes. M&E ﬁeld staff will be charged with collecting information regarding beneﬁciaries, benchmarks and baseline data. The purpose of the M&E Department is to ensure that projects are implemented according to the plan in all respects: meeting benchmarks, following timelines, adhering to the ﬁnancial plan, reaching target beneﬁciaries, etc.). The M&E team will also be responsible for post-implementation monitoring and surveying. SECD coordinate with education Clusters in Turkey by sending 4W each month and before and during the project to ensure there is no overlap, also SECD coordinate with the all stakeholders : before and during all stage of the project to ensure that there is not any overlap with any other organization, The main stakeholders which SECD plan engage with them through all cycle of the project . Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Assumptions: - Security situation allows access to the targeted communities. - The project is accepted by the communities and key stakeholders - Local markets are able to provide needed construction supplies Risks and mitigation: - SECD has a clear policy for risk management, all negatively identified risk has mitigated, transformed and avoid. The following table shows SECD's risk management plan for the project: Risk 1: Outbreak of conflict prevents access to project locations. Likelihood of Risk Occurring: low. Effect on Expected Results: Very High-Risk Management Strategy: Experience from 2013 operations found that while conflict occurred, access could be restored within a few days. A cautious approach that plans for these type of delays is expressed in the project work plan. Risk 2: Armed groups ban specific types of activities such as information-sharing. Likelihood of Risk Occurring: Low Effect on Expected Results: Very High-Risk Management Strategy: In 2014, ISIS banned NGOs from activities related to education, information messages, and any activities with children. As such, the proposed project locations are in non-ISIS controlled areas. Thus far, the armed groups in the project target locations have not expressed an issue with these type of interventions; SECD will continue to, liaise with these groups’ representatives through the NGO Forum and local partners. The project design considers this limit to be a possibility and is as such designed with built-in alternatives to direct engagement or messaging so that project results may still be achieved. Risk 3: Airplane shelling Likelihood of Risk Occurring: medium Effect on Expected Results: Very High Risk Management Strategy: The risk of Security situation (only heavy shelling) and the risk of the local council buildings being targeted by airstrikes are a very low probability according to the historical information for 1 year ago, also SECD will be linked with the de -conflict mechanism which provided by OCHA.
