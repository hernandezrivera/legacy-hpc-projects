Project Summary

More than 15.3 million people are in dire need of life-saving health assistance in 2023, a 3.2 million increase from 2022. The weak, fragile, and disrupted health systems with concurrent public health emergencies and numerous challenges affect not only the access, availability, and quality of health services across Syria but also the physical and mental well-being of the vulnerable population.
In year 2023, the IRC Health Sector remains focused on three response priorities defined through health system, monitoring and community feedback.
The IRC will deliver comprehensive lifesaving primary health care services and support to
secondary level health facilities through direct and partner implementation. The IRC health
services will reach an estimated target 386,914 vulnerable conflict-affected individuals in Raqqa, Hasakeh and Deir ezZor governorates through 21 health care interventions including 10 static primary health care clinics (four will deliver BEmONC services), 11 mobile medical units including the services run by the partner organizations and community health outreach services. The IRC primary health care intervention integrates community health and reproductive health services, and they are provided in line with the essential health service package for northern Syria and WHO guidelines for Syria. This includes prevention and management of communicable and non-communicable diseases (NCD)s, integrated management of childhood illnesses (IMCI), reproductive, maternal and child health, clinical care for sexual assault survivors (CCSAS), provision of pharmacological and nonpharmacological mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS), health education and risk communication and community engagement (RCCE), COVID-19 risk mitigation measures with strict adherence to standard infection prevention and control measures (IPC) and to smaller scale, IRC will also provide COVID-19 Rapid Diagnostic testing services for the population in the camps. IRC will also provide cholera prevention and control measures in operations areas in Hassakah, Raqqa and Dier Az Zor governorates.
In addition to service delivery, the IRC will adopt two referral mechanisms: An internal NGO intersectoral pathway (Health- Protection-ERD), at the health facilities level between the health components (Health- MHPSS- Reproductive health-community health) and an external referral pathway from the MMU and static clinics to secondary health care centers. The referral between the IRC component is tracked and documented through referral tracking tools and focal points from each component who are following the case through a regular meeting. For the external referrals, the IRC uses the ambulances to transport the beneficiaries to the secondary health care and receive documented feedback about the case through accompanying medical personnel.
The IRC will also contribute to disease surveillance through systematic collection, analysis, and reporting of health data and weekly reporting through the Early Warning and Alert Reporting System (EWARS) to inform and support outbreak preparedness and response. Considering the ongoing cholera epidemic and COVID-19 pandemic, the IRC will aim to increase support to COVID-19 measures on isolation units and integrate its screening, triage, isolation, and referral protocols for suspected cholera and COVID-19 cases throughout its health interventions. The IRC will expand (RCCE) across its health programming operations areas.

3 - Needs

Access to quality integrated basic primary and secondary health services, proper supply chain management, emergency preparedness, and response capacities have been affected by the deteriorating socio-economic conditions, poor infrastructure, lack of continued availability of electricity, and poor WASH conditions in healthcare facilities. In addition, food insecurity and the water crises have also contributed to an increase in water-borne diseases and the deteriorating malnutrition status of children and pregnant and lactating women. Al-Hasakeh, Ar-Raqqa and Deir-ez-Zor consistently showed greater barriers, costs, and service non-availability as compared to other areas of Syria, while dense urban settings, crowded and overburdened areas, continue to be at risk of poor health outcomes due to shortages in water and hygiene supplies, risk of communicable disease and the likelihood of overcrowding and long waiting times at health facilities. The health system in Syria continues to be overstretched and under-resourced, with approximately half of PHCCs not fully operational, with only 65% of the hospitals and 56% of public healthcare centers considered fully functional. No districts in NES meet the emergency thresholds of at least ten beds per 10,000 people, and no districts meet the ISAC standard, 1 hospital per 250,000 people. Severe shortages of medicines, and medical supplies and inadequate healthcare workers remain critical needs.

4 - Implementation of activities

The IRC will deliver Primary health care across Ar-Raqqa, Al Hasakeh, and Deir-ez-Zor by static PHCCs, MMUs, and local partners with essential health service packages in line with WHO guidelines for Syria including malnutrition screening and treatment referrals. Will integrate primary, reproductive, MHPSS, and community health across service delivery points to ensure comprehensives comprehensive curative, preventive services and will continue to build on its mhGAP MHPSS services. Each facility will be equipped with an MHPSS counselor, psychoeducation, psychotropics by psychiatrists, and referrals, all MHPSS activities is conducted in line with IASC standards. IPC and quality assessments will be conducted at facilities with refresher training for health staff. The IRC will support 24/7 BEmONC services and work with partners to create a network for BEmONCs centers linked to CEmONC facilities. CCSAS services will be provided for GBV survivors linked with the existing protection programs. Provision of life-saving pharmaceuticals, PPE, and medical supplies will be provided to all supported facilities. The IRC has an internal referral system between health, integrated protection and early recovery and development, creating a case management team for referral follow-up. Ambulances services will be provided to support external referrals and cases are followed up by the medical focal points. Primary and secondary health inter-sectoral and between-service referrals will be provided.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk: Rupture of service delivery during security situations: NGO mobile services will be established in temporary locations while facilities are evacuated and relocated, to maintain health delivery. Risk: Community stigmatization and discrimination of children, survivors of sexual violence receiving specialized services such as case management or health services: Likelihood: High Impact: High Mitigation: Use community acceptance strategy, including non-specialized services as entry-points and trust-building. Ensure specialized services are properly messaged to community. Embed specialized services in broader service-provision to de-stigmatize, using confidential and discrete spaces.MHPSS: There will be awareness sessions and psychoeducation on MH conditions and services, to reduce stigmatization and support awareness of specialized services. Risk: Access barriers due to physical impediments as well as normative access restrictions. Likelihood: Medium Impact: High Mitigation: Conduct inclusion analysis, deploy MMUs to reach vulnerable populations; deploy community outreach teams to support referrals of vulnerable individuals, and integrate health service delivery across other sectors. Risk: COVID-19 and its impact on health and wellbeing. Likelihood: High, Impact: High : mitigation: the NGO will work with its health facilities to create a safe and clean environment at all the health facilities including IPC control measures and PPE adherence for both staff and clients.


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

Risk of sexual exploitation and abuse and sexual harassment of women and girls’ clients by staff and volunteers. Likelihood: Medium/Low Impact: Very High. Mitigation: IEC materials for beneficiaries explaining reporting channels for beneficiaries to report PSEA and ASH. The IRC adheres to a strong internal code of conduct related to the prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA) andsafeguarding beneficiaries, especially children, as well as a safe environment forreporting that allows for anonymity. Each year, all IRC staff attend a full day IRCWay Code of Conduct Training that includes discussions on The IRC’s commitmentsto beneficiaries and The IRC’s zero tolerance for sexual misconduct. NGO will workwith partners utilizing their existing beneficiary mechanisms to establish commonstandards for beneficiary feedback and ensure protection from sexual exploitationand abuse and accountability to beneficiaries and other stakeholders. This willinclude capacity building activities with partners to reach these standards, asnecessary. The IRC is a member of PSEA networks inside Syria and providesregular updates on the number of staff receiving PSEA training and number ofcomplaints received through feedback and complaint channels


9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risk: rupture of pharma and medical supplies due to mass influx: The IRC has master purchase agreements with local suppliers to ensure continuous supply to supported facilities, uses the HAWALA transfer for local payments taking into consideration SYP fluctuation. Risk: KRI border closures resulting interruption of lifesaving health services mitigated through pharmaceutical contingency stock. NGO actively participates in NES.Forum contingency preparedness and response planning to effectively address health needs across NES. Risk: Access and security, NGO regularly reviews contingency plans for Syria operations. This has recently been updated for NES including multiple potentialscenarios regarding changes in territorial control, continuation or interruption of cross border access and access to locations of implementation. Humanitarian access staffconduct negotiations with local actors, work closely with program leads to identify andmanage risks and engage with local councils to avoid any interference or aid diversion,which NGO has zero tolerance. NGO has a Security Management Plan and SOPs to guideoperations and staff to provide lifesaving assistance using the safest possible methods.Risk: Community acceptance, NGO works with the communities to build trust andcommunity engagement through its community health program. NGO also works withlocal actors to ensure services are provided to the most vulnerable and in need. NGOensures program management and implementation
