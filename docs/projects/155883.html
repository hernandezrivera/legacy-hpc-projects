Project Summary

The United Nations (UN) estimates number of crisis-affected population in need of humanitarian assistance in Syria at 11.7 million people (49.8% male and 50.2% female) out of which 4.7 million and 5 million live in areas of major severity of needs (inter-sector severity 3) and acute need (inter-sector severity 4-6). The above include 6.2 million protracted Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs); 1.4 million returnees; 324,000 IDPs in hard to reach areas, 1.6 million-population movement and the rest overburdened host communities. In addition, 480,000 Palestine refugees are considered part of the people in need. 67% of the 11.7 million people in need of humanitarian assistance are accessible from within Syria according to Syria Response Access Dynamic report by OCHA (December 2018). Overall, 9 million of people are estimated to be food insecure and at risk of food insecurity by Food Security Sector.
In this context, FAO has designed a project to assist 200,000 small-scale herders (equivalent to 1,200,000 people) in Syria. The objective of the proposed action is to protect the livestock assets of 200,000 vulnerable herders’ households through provision of vaccinations and treatment of livestock. The project expected outputs are:
Output 1. Access to animal health care against Transboundary Animal Diseases (TADs) and common livestock diseases among 200,000 herders increased. The output aims at providing animal health care, vaccinations for Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD), Enterotoxaemia and treatment against ecto and endo parasites (as per sector response package under emergency livestock vaccination and treatment).
Output 2. Capacity of veterinarian and veterinary technicians for vaccination and treatment of livestock strengthened. This output aims at training of veterinarians and veterinary technicians and equipping them on TADs and zoonosis diseases control and eradication.
It is expected that the support to protect the livestock assets through improving access to animal health care against TADs and common livestock diseases and enhancing capacity of veterinary for vaccinations will ensure the survival of the remaining livestock herd while increasing their productivity which will lead to improvement of livelihoods, increase local availability of food, self-reliance and improve food and nutrition security of small scale herders Syrian especially the most vulnerable ones including women and youths.

Needs

Keeping livestock alive, healthy and productive is vital for the food security and livelihoods of large numbers of poor, vulnerable households in rural areas especially women, youth and elderly people whose livelihoods depends on livestock. Pre-crisis, Syria had a highly developed and effective animal health system that reduced likelihood and impact of animal diseases and ensured that animal populations were generally healthy. The healthier animals are stronger and are less prone to diseases. This helps maintain their value and quality thereby raising their importance for food and nutritional security and household incomes. Animal health services focuses on two broad areas: (1) preventative health treatments to keep animals healthy (mainly through treatments to reduce endo and ecto parasites, provide multivitamins etc.) and (2) vaccination campaign/ programmes to protect livestock populations from diseases outbreaks. It is important to note that healthier and stronger animals are less prone to diseases on the one hand and that weak, unhealthy livestock are often unsuitable for vaccination programmes particularly those that involve live vaccines. Syria’s animal health system has suffered significant damages during the crisis meaning that many livestock owners do not have access to either preventative animal treatments or vaccination programmes. This has increased the scale of livestock losses and has led to the widespread distress sale of livestock to neighboring countries. Livestock is a key factor for food security and resilience for vulnerable people in crisis time due to the multiple benefits. It can supply dairy products, meat, wool, leather for industry, sources of cash, manure which is a source of clean energy and fertilizer. All the above contributions constitute valuable means to resilience building to food security. Since 2011, the capacity for delivery of field veterinary services have been seriously eroded. In consequence the capacity for trans boundary animal diseases (TADs) surveillance and responses have been totally eroded including cold chain infrastructure required to carry out livestock vaccination campaigns. Services such as vaccination campaigns, preventive treatment programs and diseases surveillance are now available only in areas considered as safer security wise and often not to all farmers. In addition, veterinary vaccine production facilities have been damaged which resulted in a shortage of vaccines. Consequently, livestock are left without immunity against contagious diseases and subjected to higher risks of infections. The continuing movement of livestock out of the Syrian Arab Republic into neighboring countries either with refugees or smuggled out to more lucrative markets give rise to increasing concerns about the spread of trans boundary animal diseases (TADs) and zoonosis(being in the same epidemiological unit and considering frequent movements of animals between countries) the risk of diseases spreading across the region is high. This will also threaten the health of livestock in Syria due to lack of vaccination in the country. This threat does not necessarily manifest itself in major outbreaks and sudden mass deaths of animals. When uncontrolled, contagious diseases lead to continuous significant production reductions in the form of increased losses in young animals (e.g. Enterotoxaemia), higher costs of treatments (e.g. Pasteurellosis) and elevated abortion rates (e.g. Brucellosis). The country’s veterinary service is rapidly running out of vaccines and drugs for routine administration and losing the capacity to provide technical and material support to herders. The animal diseases and infections of highest concern to animal health/production and public health are: Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD), Peste des Petits Ruminants (PPR) Enterotoxaemia, Lumpy Skin disease and ecto and endo parasites.

Implementation of activities

Currently FAO is operating in thirteen governorates – Aleppo, Al Hassakeh, Ar Raqqa, Dar’a, Deir e Zor, Hama, Homs, Idleb, As Sweida, Quneitra, Rural Damascus, Tartous and Lattakia. In governorates where UN staff presence is not permitted by UN security regulations, FAO operates through government seconded officers and NGO service providers who are directly supporting implementation of activities and report to FAO.FAO Representation in Syria will be responsible for the overall implementation of the activities, backstopped by FAO headquarters with technical support of FAO’s multidisciplinary team in the Regional Office for the Near East and North Africa. FAO works very closely with MAAR to ensure that the project goals are achieved through proper technical support and monitoring capacity. The implementation will be further supported by:• A central coordination committees composed of FAO, relevant MAAR directorates, community-based organizations and implementing partners. The committee’s role is to ensure facilitation of the project implementation and application of the criteria for beneficiaries' selection.• Local teams at village/district level, led by FAO and composed of local community representatives (traditional leader or “Moukhtar”), extension workers and implementing partners respectively will be in charge of selecting project beneficiaries in line with FAO established set of criteria that take into account gender and age sensitive considerations.• FAO Resilience Officer in the targeted governorates will operationally and technically supervise animal health inputs distribution, training programmes.The proposed actions will benefit from existing capacity which can be scaled up at whenever required.Activities under output 1 1) Finalization of districts selection base on assessments, discussions with other participating sector working group members and relevant Government authorities;2) Identification and contracting of appropriate implementing partners (IPs). 3) Development of beneficiaries selection criteria taking into considerations specific gender and age needs in consultations with key partners and stakeholders. 4) Participatory identification of most needy and most vulnerable groups giving high priority to women headed households.5) Procurement and distribution of 3.4 million doses of FMD vaccines;6) Procurement and distribution of 5 million doses of enterotoxaemia vaccines.7) Procurement and distribution of 5 million doses of ecto and endo parasites treatment.8) Undertake vaccination and livestock treatment across all the targeted areas specifically for small ruminants and cattle.9) Monitor the impact of the various campaigns and collection of gender disaggregated data on beneficiaries.Activities under output 1: 1) Provision and strengthening of veterinary cold chain infrastructure (solar and mobile cold chain equipment) for veterinary vaccination campaigns; 2) Provide technical assistance to beneficiaries, community based organizations (CBOs), government institutions and implementing partners on surveillance and early detection of TADs and infectious diseases to guide early responses;3. Provision of tools and equipment for local veterinarians to support conducting vaccinations campaigns and treatments;4. Provide refresher training to veterinarians and veterinary technicians on basic animal health and veterinary services taking into considerations specific gender and age needs.5. Monitor impact of the various campaigns and collection of gender and age dis-aggregated data on beneficiaries. NB: All the activities will address specific needs of different gender and age groups.

Protection Risk Analysis

1) Risk: Beneficiaries being targeted as a result of being selected for project/Risk of community tension arising from beneficiaries selection; Mitigation measures: Inclusion of different categories of affected population examples IDPs and host communities; returnees and refugees; men, women, boys, girls and youth base their specific needs and clear selection criteria agreed upon by local committees.2) Risk: Inclusion and exclusion errors; Mitigation measures: establishing a local beneficiary selection committee with equitable representation of vulnerable groups; putting in place a clear and precise guidance on beneficiary selection criteria and process; ensuring an effective communication for guidance in local language.3) Risk of diversion of assistance/assets: Mitigation measures: establish a community based beneficiaries’ selection and community based monitoring.The design of the project has taken into account protection elements, in line with the do not harm principles. To the maximum possible extent, all measures will be taken to limit the risk of beneficiaries being targeted as a result of the being selected for the project. Where IDPs are selected, representatives from the host communities will be included as well, to limit possible frictions between the two, and a special attention will be provided to the returnees to help them in their settlement and resuming their production activities through protecting and rehabilitating their asset. Moreover, the project will target those breeders who receive food support (about 15% of the total targeted breeders) in order to help them shift from receiving food support into producing their own food or able to afford it. In addition to this, re stocking and provision of feed can help in sustaining food production (dairy and animal products) in the local community, so more food will be available and accessible to people. An accountability to affected population system including beneficiaries’ participation and appropriate information and feedback/complaint mechanism will also be put in place to provide communities with a mechanism to express their grievance. This will be used to make adjustments and guide improvement on the implementation of activities.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks and assumptions beyond project control and their mitigation measures:1. Risks: Deterioration of the security situation precluding access to areas of implementation; Probability: Likely; Impact: Severe; Mitigation measures: Preventive: continue to gather information from range of sources; Advocate with Resident Coordinator(RC)/Humanitarian Coordinator (HC) and other instances to regain access. Develop contingency plans in coordination with service providers and if necessary move activities to alternative locations.2. Risks: Major displacements/returns that could put more pressure on livelihood strategies and/or limit the impact of activities; Probability: possible; Impact: insignificant; Mitigation measures: Continue with advocacy in order to scale up programmes to cover additional requirements to promote livelihoods opportunities. Advocacy with other organizations to include new arrival in assistance programmes.3. Risks: Changes in the conditions related to cross border movements and operations; Probability: Unlikely; Impact: Moderate; Mitigation measures: Ensure that service provider (SP) have capacities to deliver programme within the changed legal framework. Diversify activities complementing international procurement with local procurement and ensure different delivery modalities (scaling up cross lines to balance reduced cross borders).4. Risk: Limitations on partnership agreements; Probability: Unlikely; Impact: major; Mitigation measures: Continuous advocacy and negotiations with Government and counterparts for partners to have access to targeted areas. Establish agreements with counterparts to facilitate the implementation of project activities. 5. Risk: Deterioration of the relations between IDPs and host communities in the period/area concerned; Probability: Unlikely; Impact: Insignificant; Mitigation measures: Assistance is balanced and not directed only to one specific group. Identification of beneficiaries done in partnership with local communities including IDPs and host communities. Selected implementing partners that are accepted by local communities and aware of conflict sensitivity.6. Risks: Increased control of land and water by parties to crisis; Probability: Likely; Impact: Moderate; Mitigation measures: Preventive: continue to gather information from a range of sources. Accurate choice of target areas ensuring that farming is possible and beneficiaries will undertake the activity. Advocacy with local authorities by SPs. If necessary move to alternative locations previously identified.7. Risks: Possible climatic shocks during the implementation period (drought/floods in particular). Probability: Possible; Impact: Moderate; Mitigation measures: Select vaccination campaign locations in close consultations with the local communities to reduce risk of disruption.
