Project Summary

The multi-layered crisis in Lebanon has significantly compromised the maternal, infant and young child and adolescents feeding and nutrition. The findings of the National Nutrition Survey 2021 confirmed that stunting is still an unfinished agenda in several pockets and among the refugee populations. The prevalence of moderate and severe stunting stands at 25% among Syrian refugees, representing 73% of the total stunted children in Lebanon. The prevalence of acute malnutrition is as high as 4% among children and 9% among Pregnant and Lactating Women in Palestinian camps. Moreover, over 40% of women and children are affected by a degree of anemia which imposes lifelong irreversible impacts on wellbeing and cognitive capital of generations.
In terms of underlying contributing factors, the survey has reported that 70% of young children are missing their best start in life, which is Exclusive Breastfeeding, diets of over 90% of children are lacking the diversity and meal frequency, and Vitamin A and protein rich foods are missing in the diets of almost 80% of children regardless of their origin. This could be attributed to deteriorated food and nutrition insecurity, poor practices and lack of access to quality and adequate services.
As a result of the ongoing economic crisis, over 1.3 million Lebanese citizens, 35.8 percent of the total population, were estimated to be food insecure (including 190 000 severely food insecure) by the end of September 2021. Of the 1.5 million Syrian refugees in Lebanon, 735 000 were food insecure in September 2021. In addition, 88 percent of the Syrian refugee population were dependent on humanitarian assistance, as they were not able to afford the absolute minimum amount required to cover lifesaving needs.
In terms of trends, due to massive changes is population patterns in Lebanon since the onset of the refugee crisis and lack of frequent data points on nutrition, plotting trends and comparing different data points is challenging. Having said this, by comparing the available data for prevalence of acute malnutrition both Lebanese and Syrian population, reducing trend is being observed since the baseline year (see below table). When looking into stunting, while we are witnessing a drop among Lebanese, however Syrian refugee children this trend has been increasing. Worth mentioning that prevalence of anemia among women is showing an increasing trend for both Lebanese and Syrian refugees. And while the rates of exclusive breastfeeding have improved for Lebanese but for Syrian refugees, there has been a significant reduction in 2021 compared to 2014.

1. - Brief description of the specific project activities under each sector objective

Sector Objective 1.  Support 35,000 vulnerable PLWs and caretakers of children aged below 2 years with IYCF counselling integrated with hygiene promotion. Providing preventive nutritional and micronutrient supplementation to 50,000 children under the age of two and 64,000 PLWS to prevent and address micronutrient deficiencies and stunting  Conduct Micronutrient survey: Generate evidence and evaluate the micronutrient status and provide a basis for policy makers to understand variations among different population groups (host communities and refugee populations), to assess where micronutrient deficiencies exist, to evaluate the impact of interventions, to improve micronutrient status, prevent from micronutrients deficiency and to gather the evidence needed to better dictate what interventions are needed to improve the response at country level. The Ministry of Public Health in Lebanon is aligned with the nutrition sector on the need to switch from SMART to a micronutrient survey. The estimated budget for this survey $1.3 million, including human resources. 2. - What is your protection risk mitigation plan?

UNICEF is committed in advancing PSEA and Child Safeguarding (CS) minimum standards within Implementing Partners (IPs) working under the ERP umbrella through the roll-out of the PSEA/CS capacity assessment followed by trainings, provision of technical support on PSEA and CS. Additionally, the communities coping mechanisms will also be assessed to mitigate adverse impacts on their safety. This is in line with Core Humanitarian Standard that encompasses the ‘Do no harm’ principle.UNICEF, through its mandate as sector lead U for nutrition sector will support partners to include the needs and voices of children, PLWs and persons with disabilities at all stages of the response. UNICEF, from a vantage point of co-leading the child protection sub-sector will ensure protection concerns for children are identified and addressed in line with the strategies outlined in UNICEF’s Core Commitment for Children for humanitarian action.

3. - How will the project ensure accountability for affected populations (AAP)?

UNICEF has made Accountability to Affected People (AAP) a priority in its Strategic Plan, UNICEF is adopting an institutional strategy and action plan for scaling up AAP. UNICEF’s responsibility to ensure programmes generate meaningful and relevant results and outcomes for girls, boys, women and men, in accordance with their specific needs, priorities and preferences. It involves working in ways to protect, facilitate and enable them to exercise their rights, including the right to safe, fair, equitable access to quality services and accurate, reliable and relevant information, the right to share their views and opinions about the quality and effectiveness of programmes, and participate in decisions that affect them.UNICEF will scale up the use of the SMS based 24x7 U-report system to ensure community feedback is collected and analyzed systematically. The U-report will complement other forms of community based beneficiary feedback and complaint handling mechanisms.
