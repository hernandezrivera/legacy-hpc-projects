Project Summary

The project aims to Improve the protection environment in northern Syria for at-risk populations through 1) protection monitoring and inter-agency task-force coordination that promotes cluster-level advocacy and informs humanitarian actors’ protection mainstreaming; and 2) Provision of critical information that enables displaced populations to obtain referrals for services or respond to civil documentation concerns.

Needs

As the Syrian conflict heads into its eighth year, 13.1 million people are in need of humanitarian assistance, with over 5.6 million living in acute need. Individuals in Syria face increased protection risks in their daily lives while the capacity to positively cope with these risks continues to be stretched. Indiscriminate and targeted attacks on civilian areas and facilities, grave rights violations, denial of humanitarian access, and violations of international law persist. Violence at household (HH) and community levels continue and vulnerabilities based on gender, age, disability and other factors often intersect. Lack of civil status documentation (CSD) compromises freedom of movement, security of tenure, access to humanitarian assistance, the ability to claim asylum in third countries, and future durable solution possibilities for conflict-affected individuals. The lack of documentation and proof of social relationships (such as marriage registration, death of a spouse or parent, and child birth) is difficult for all individuals, but has disproportionate effects on women and girls, because of legal and social practices that relate many rights to their documented relationship to other family members. According to IRC assessments, approximately 96% of interviewed HHs in Idleb experienced issues with CSD. Most children born in Armed Opposition Group (AoG) held areas since the conflict began in 2011 have been unable to obtain birth certificates. Humanitarian actors remotely managing emergency programming in conflict-affected northern Syria face significant access challenges, resulting in unclear understanding of contextual protection issues. IRC protection monitoring, both through regular monitoring and rapid protection assessments in emergencies, can provide timely and accurate information about protection risks and concerns to generate actionable recommendations for humanitarian stakeholders. In NWS, in addition to the HNO data, the three protection field team collect both qualitative and quantitative data which informs the PMTF bi-monthly protection needs analyses, in conjunction with UNHCR data. IRC uses a mixed methodology to select participants for household (HH)-level assessments with IRC’s protection monitors visiting every fifth house within the location of focus and IRC assessors conducting visits where recommended, based on vulnerability, by members of the community (i.e. utilizing the “snowball” methodology). IRC’s protection needs assessors also conduct Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) and KIIs to gather additional qualitative information on key thematic issues. Due to the limited sample size, data gathered cannot be considered statistically representative, but is meant to highlight some of the main protection issues affecting the population in surveyed areas in NWS.

Implementation of activities

IRC proposes to maintain protection monitoring (PM) in Idleb governorate and continue to co-lead the protection cluster through 2019. PM will result in bimonthly protection reporting that will facilitate improved humanitarian coordination, evidence driven advocacy and response design and support the humanitarian community to continue to anticipate and analyse changes to the protection environment and the consequences of increased displacements. PM will provide IRC and the humanitarian community with regular and context specific information on protection concerns and gaps in service provision. PM teams will continue to collect and track protection trends over time. IRC will continue to provide information to beneficiaries about services and facilitate referrals based on services mapping, which will continue to be regularly updated. The PM activities will identify the most vulnerable persons who are in urgent need of and support and will further be linked to internal and external support and services involved in case management to ensure standardized approaches to beneficiary identification, referrals and case management opportunities and approaches. The IRC legal team will continue to gather information around civil status documentation (CSD) procedures. In 2019, the assessment will be updated to better collect information regarding recognition of documents (nationally and internationally), gender considerations, impact on displaced populations, barriers to accessing clinics for childbirth, availability of birth documents outside of clinics, and the effect of the lack of CSD on children being able to access schooling. IRC will build on existing strong integration of protection and health programming, as well as local NGOs, to target particularly vulnerable populations with specific CSD needs, such as displaced individuals, women and girls. The CSD team will provide targeted trainings for doctors, nurses, mid-wives, and other health professionals to show them how to support documentation of new births through the production of Birth Notifications. In 2019, the CSD program will also conduct targeted advocacy and trainings for community leaders (e.g. Mukhtars), whose support is needed to produce Birth Attestations, another intermediary step needed to prove birth and which is a prerequisite to eventually attaining formal birth certificates. IRC will conduct targeted awareness sessions for education professionals, as many children are unable to attend schools if they do not have birth paperwork, or if they do not have previous education certificates. IRC’s Protection Cash is a joint protection and Livelihoods programme which increases the coping capacity of the most vulnerable individuals and/or families facing unexpected and significant shocks which may be mitigated through a one-time injection of cash. IRC targets households with pressing protection needs, such as a medical emergency; recent eviction; or support for persons with disabilities. Families are identified through protection monitoring activities, and all cases are reviewed by protection and cash program team. Following a successful household verification, the cash is distributed in consultation with IRC’s security team and finance team. Throughout the emergency cash provision process, Protection Needs Assessors will ensure cases are followed up on and responded to in a timely manner. Additionally, IRC’s Monitoring, Evaluation, Accountability and Learning (MEAL) unit conducts Post Distribution Monitoring (PDMs) s with a random sample of protection cash recipients each month, and supports programme learning and development by assessing the impact of the program. IRC’s Protection Cash Guidelines outline proper coordination of protection cash assessments and ensure decision-making is informed by three considerations (sustainability, suitability, and safety), all of which must be met for inclusion.

Protection Risk Analysis

Protection is a core objective of all humanitarian work in crisis affected areas. Given that affected populations heavily depend on humanitarian services for survival, it is crucial that services be delivered in a safe and dignified manner. Due to access constraints in Syria, many humanitarian actors provide remote service delivery from Jordan. It requires actors to carefully consider protection-specific needs and how their humanitarian interventions do not cause harm to beneficiaries, including the prevention of exploitation and abuse. Services will be open to Syrian locals, IDPs, and returnees, but will be targeted based on need and vulnerability, prioritizing women, children, PWD, and the elderly. The protection monitoring team will collect data directly from vulnerable communities to allow IRC to understand beneficiary needs, gaps in service provision, and context specific protection risks related to aid provision, deliberately ensuring that all vulnerable groups can access services. IRC will also conduct regular project review and reflection workshops and utilise child-led clubs in SHLS to assess progress, document learning, and promote children’s participation. The Humanitarian Access Unit (HAU), work closely with program leads and field management to identify risks and trends that may impact IRC to manage, mitigate and prepare contingency plans. The Syria country program developed a Security Management Plan (SMP) and Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) for northwest Syria to guide IRC operations and staff, to enable them to provide lifesaving assistance using the safest methods for IRC staff and beneficiaries. These policies are updated regularly or when necessary. The HAU, keep monitoring the conflict situation and provide analysis and scenarios for potential risks to inform the contingency planning through Bi-weekly situation updates, quarterly humanitarian access updates and the daily security situation reports including one-on-one meetings for specific risks management. These practices gives the IRC the ability to ensure the safety and security of staff, beneficiaries and assets including the IRC obligation in regards to duty of care and staff welfare. The IRC also uses the Feedback and Accountability Mechanism (FARM) to ensure beneficiary accountability and ensures that beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries alike are able to provide feedback to an independent Accountability Team. The FARM is promoted through fliers, posters, in-person presentations, videos, and more. Upon reception, feedback will be categorized for priority and complaint type, logged in an online information system, and instantly uploaded to a dashboard for trend analysis. Feedback will be routed based on their activity and priority level to the appropriate focal point and responses, fact-finding, or corrective action will be taken based on the content of the complaint. The FARM, coupled with monthly satisfaction surveys, recurring focus group discussions, and other ad-hoc studies will enable beneficiaries and non-beneficiary community members to provide regular feedback, complaints, and recommendations to IRC before, during, and after implementation.
