Project Summary

As a result of the ongoing conflict, the population and humanitarian response in northeast Nigeria are affected by the presence of explosive devices such as victim operated IEDs and Explosive Remnants of War. The impact remains unabated. According to the UNMAS database, from January to October 2019, 111 civilians died due to explosions and 181 have been injured. Access is also hampered due to the presence of IED along roads.
Primarily a humanitarian project, the activities will also contribute to the humanitarian-development nexus.
In order to contribute to the protection of individuals and communities from the risks and socio-economic impact of explosive hazards in the context of the Humanitarian Response in northeast Nigeria, the project will be articulated around four pillars. Through the coordination of Mine Action and the management of related information, UNMAS proposes to support the delivery of effective, efficient and safe lifesaving activities by Mine Action partners in close coordination with the humanitarian community and Nigerian authorities and in compliance with the 2020 Humanitarian Response Plan. Through the delivery of Explosive Ordnance Risk Education to 80,000 civilians – including 55% women and girls and Conflict Preparedness and Protection, UNMAS also aims will strengthen community-owned protection and resilience. UN actors and humanitarian actors operating in northeast Nigeria will also benefit from Explosive Hazard Awareness sessions. The delivery of 40 non-technical surveys focused on accessible areas will allow delineating dangerous areas while allowing using safe lands for their primary purpose and in support of the humanitarian-development nexus. The support to the development of Nigerian capacities, through localization and training of institutional mine clearance capacities, will reinforce the global response.

1 - Needs

UNMAS has collated, mapped and analyzed data on incidents involving explosive ordnances from different sources. Explosive hazards increase the severity of the crisis affecting northeast Nigeria. The number of incidents occurring in people being killed and injured resulting from unsafe behavior and the presence of explosives is an obstacle to safe access to lands.As in any conflict, explosive ammunitions that did not function properly or were abandoned, known as Explosive Remnants of War (ERW) contaminate northeast Nigeria. Between 10 to 20% of the fired explosive ammunition malfunction and remain extremely dangerous as their presence impedes freedom of movement, prevents returns as well as resettlements and obstructs the delivery of humanitarian aid. These explosive devices range from small arms ammunition to air-dropped bombs to include grenades, rockets, mortars, artillery and tank shells. The rocket attack against Maiduguri on 23 February, illustrates this fact with one unexploded 122mm rocket landing in the vicinity of a primary school and another one landing in Teachers’ village IDP camp. There is a heightened risk to encounter ERW in newly or temporary accessible areas. With a predominant agricultural sector, access to land and waters is hampered while farmers, herders, fishermen, hunters, and traditional beekeepers are at risk. Returns will increase this protection issue. From January to September 2019, UNMAS recorded 113 events when artillery or airstrikes have been used, augmenting the contamination accumulated since 2009. Out of the 8 Non-Technical Surveys (NTS) conducted under UNMAS funding in 2019, 5 led to the confirmation of contamination of explosive ordnances on the site.There are two predominant uses of Improvised Explosive Devices that affect civilians and humanitarians i) IEDs emplaced along roads, ii) person borne IED (PBIED) attacks (notably carriers are women, girls, and boys). The majority of incidents reported are due to victim operated IED (VOIED) more precisely in the form of Pressure Plates, which meet the definition of a landmine and do not discriminate between security forces, civilians and humanitarian-development actors.Explosive Ordnance constitutes a direct threat to lives and a clear obstacle to safe freedom of movement. CCCM sector is in a continuous process to secure more land for expansion and decongestion of camps and these sites should be surveyed prior to sending IDPs to new camps.From January to September 2019 alone, at least 111 civilians have been killed and 181 have been injured by explosive ordnance. Recorded data clearly indicates that men and boys constitute the biggest number of civilian victims of explosive ordnance. This has a detrimental effect on the whole family, as men are often the sole or main breadwinner of the family and losing them leaves the family vulnerable. It is thus critical to delivering tailored and targeted EORE to men and boys, in order to ensure the security of all the gender and age groups. Many accidents have occurred due to the unsafe behavior of the civilians i.e. detonating ERW due to lack of awareness and knowledge of the danger of these items. Accidents will further increase when IDPs return to their areas of origin and resume their livelihood activities. Fatalities will rise due to a lack of medical assistance in their communities. Concerning victims and survivors, despite immediate medical assistance provided by ICRC and some INGOs it has been observed that the full range of physical, psychological and socio-economic needs of all those directly and indirectly affected, whilst empowering and respecting their dignity and autonomy, is not addressed leading to the disruption of families and adding burden to already suffering communities. There are gaps in the normative, operative and technical framework that would allow Nigerian authorities to effectively, efficiently and safely coping with the full scale of the issue of explosive contamination.

2 - Activities

Activity 1 – Mine Action is effectively, efficiently and safely delivered. Outputs: 1.1) In close coordination with the MRRR, UNMAS leads the Mine Action sub-sector and ensures the mine action contribution to the Protection Sector. 1.2) National Mine Action Standards for Explosive Ordnance Risk Education and Non-Technical Survey (NTS) are submitted to Nigerian authorities. 1.3) The development of a National Mine Action Center is supported. 1.4) Humanitarian Mine Action partners' planning is facilitated and operations are coordinated. Activity 2 - Explosive hazard data is consolidated, analyzed and disseminated to inform an appropriate humanitarian response. Outputs: 2.1) Mine Action Information is standardized through the deployment, operationalization and maintenance of a specific Information Management System (IMSMA). 2.2) Mine Action collection of information is mainstreamed in humanitarian assessments and evaluation questionnaires. 2.3) The flow of information from the communities’ focal points and Mine Action partners' is facilitated. 2.4) Explosive risk data, mapping and analysis is shared to inform humanitarian response. 2.5) Information management training delivered to national counterparts. 6) Victims and survivors from explosive hazards are identified. Activity 3 - Civilians and humanitarian actors in north-east Nigeria adopt safe behavior when in the presence of explosive hazards. Outputs: 3.1) 8 teams are trained, equipped, deployed and mentored to deliver explosive ordnance risk education. 3.2) 100 community Focal Points and members of civil society organizations benefit from a risk education Training of Trainers. 3.3) A minimum of 79,756 conflict-affected people benefit from explosive ordnance risk education. 3.4) A minimum of 80 Focal Points benefit from Conflict Preparedness and Protection (CPP) training. 3.5) Public Information campaigns are delivered in at least five local languages. 3.6) A short awareness movie is produced and broadcasted in local cinemas in the LGAs. 3.7) UN and Humanitarian actors are provided with Explosive Hazards Awareness training, upon request. Activity 4 - Access to safe land and the prevention of accidents is established through the mapping of Explosive Hazards. Outputs: 4.1) Capacity building of national implementing partner through training, management, provision of equipment and mentoring of 10 NTS teams. 4.2) UNMAS-trained Humanitarian Partners operating in temporary and newly accessible and hard to reach areas survey and assess the extent of the contamination, allowing safe access and freedom of movement for the communities in the areas assessed as free from explosives. 4.3) Accessible areas are surveyed through International Mine Action partners. 4.4) Mine Action-related information is shared with UNCT and HCT, civil society and national authorities, allowing safe delivery of humanitarian assistance. Activity 5 -Nigeria's institutional capabilities to efficiently manage the threat of explosive hazards are reinforced. Outputs: 5.1) 60 NEMA and SEMA operators are trained in the delivery of Explosive Ordnance Risk Education. 5.2) EOD training delivered to 20 NPF officers. 5.3) Emergency Trauma Bag training delivered to 30 National Police Force (NPF) Explosive Ordnance Disposal (EOD) officers and 30 Nigeria Security and Civil Defence Corps (NSCDC) personnel. 5.4) Risk awareness training of trainer delivered to 40 NPF and NSCDC personnel.

9.5 - How will your project ensure feedback is collected from the most vulnerable community members?

Vulnerability not only impacts the likelihood of becoming a victim of explosive ordnances, but also structures the ways in which individuals access medical care, reintegrate into society after being injured, and access explosive ordnance risk education. Therefore the project targets the most vulnerable. Furthermore, in order to design and develop a project that will reach all those at risk, one needs to know who is affected and why. The project has a pre and post questionnaire where approximately two percent is interviewed. The sessions, conducted in the local language, are divided, addressing men, women, and children separately. Focus Groups Discussion and working closely with community members and Focal Points allow for honest feedback. Furthermore, the project will follow the Inter-Agency Emergency Standard Operating Procedures for Prevention of and Response to Gender-Based Violence and Violence, for Case Management and Referrals to protection services (The Inter-Agency Cooperation in Community-Based Complaint Mechanisms, endorsed by the IASC Principals in June 2016, revised: Endorsed by the OHCT: 15th October, 2019) and a Community Based Complaints Mechanism (CBCM), a system by which anyone (community member or staff) can bring a SEA allegation to any complaint and feedback mechanism and the complaint will reach the concerned agency while the complainant receives services that they need. When operating with a third party through grant or contract, UNMAS will ensure the same level of feedback collection and accountability is delivered by the operating partner.
