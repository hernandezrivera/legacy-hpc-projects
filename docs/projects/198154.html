Project Summary

The people in NW SW have continued to suffer severe consequences of the ongoing-armed conflict and the situation was exacerbated by COVID-19 pandemic where the NW was one of the most affected with 18/20 Health Districts affected as at November 2020 (OCHA 2020). About 4.7m/n people are affected with 975.8k IDPs of which 600.3k are due to the NWSW crisis, 2.8m/n are targeted for assistance out of which 2.3m/n are children, 2.4m/n are in food crisis and 548.2k are Returnees (Humanitarian Response Plan of 2023). As is the case with any crisis, the crisis in the NWSW has resulted to food insecurity and malnutrition. Food insecurity is due to displacements and COVID 19 pandemic which have led to many people losing their means of livelihood, a reduction in farming as people are displaced away from their farm lands; resulting to vulnerable families running out of food stock quickly, high food prices which they cannot afford leading to low dietary diversity of both fruits and vegetables consequently there is a reduction in coping strategies. This exacerbates malnutrition due to inadequate quality of food, mothers spending less time to care for children as they search for support, compromised access to healthcare, overcrowding in host communities, broken down social support systems and increase susceptibility to opportunistic infections.
This explains the observed rise in cases of malnutrition and under-nutrition in the NWSW. From April to October 2022 the CBCHS in partnership with UNICEF screened 32000 children for malnutrition in the Southwest and 962 was SAM. From November 1-14, 2022 the CBCHS took part in a mass screening organized by the Nutrition where we screened 30,000 children for malnutrition, 254 were SAM and 724 MAM cases. In the course of this exercise communities reported they fear the situation of malnutrition will only get worse as a result of the high cost of fertilizers in the country. Children U5, adolescent girls, and pregnant/lactating women are most affected with the situation worst in hard-to-reach areas, among IDPs and households/communities hosting IDPs. With malnutrition being a serious public health issue because of the adverse consequences on children U5, women of reproductive age and their birth outcomes, a response aimed at improving the nutrition needs of these groups is mandatory. To respond to these observed critical needs, this project seeks to improve the access of children U5, boys and girls in middle childhood (5-9years), people living with HIV/AIDs, adolescent girls and boys, pregnant and lactating women to preventive and curative nutrition packages through outreaches, mobile clinics and facility base services in compliant with COVID 19. Activities include:
- Boys and girls U5 will benefit from diet practices and services that prevent stunting, wasting, micronutrient deficiencies and overweight in the humanitarian context.
- Active mass screening of children 6-59 months for SAM using MUAC
- Community management of uncomplicated SAM as per national protocol
- Referral of complicated SAM for treatment.
- Vitamin A supplementation.
- IYCF-E counselling/awareness to mothers/caregivers,
- Hygiene promotion through community sensitization
- Weekly Iron/folic supplementation for adolescent girls/pregnant women
- Nutrition assessments, counselling and support (NACS) to PLWHA

1 - Besoins / Needs

Reports on nutrition show that prior to the crisis, the NW and SW had high rates of stunting and poor indicators of infant and young child feeding practices. Complementary feeding was reportedly compromised as only 30% of children 6-23 months were reported to consume the minimum acceptable diet with over 40% of children, aged 6-59 months reported to have anaemia. In 2019, nutrition screenings integrated in EFSA revealed a precarious situation with a proxy GAM of 5.1% and 7.1% in NW and SW regions respectively. Furthermore, analysis from MUAC screening data showed the NW to be more affected with a GAM prevalence of 5.0%. Nutrition reports from CBCHS facilities show a steady increase in the number of children with malnutrition with a reported increase in the number of complicated cases brought to the facilities for management than was the case before the crisis. During the Rapid Response Mechanism (RRM) project supported by UNICEF and implemented by CBCHS in hard-to-reach areas in Kumbo West and East health districts between July 2019 and June 2020, 7,078 children were screened for malnutrition using MUAC and 142 SAM (2%) and 437 MAM cases were identified. Of this number screened, 1,954 were IDPs with 91 cases of SAM giving a prevalence of 4.66% SAM among the IDP population and 5,124 with 51 cases of SAM giving an approximate prevalence 1% SAM among the host population. Between September and December 2020, the CBCHS in another project supported by UNICEF in over 10 health districts of the NW and SW mostly in urban or peri-urban areas screened 9,796 children 6-59months for malnutrition using MUAC and had 107 SAM cases giving a percentage of 1.1% of SAM. Majority of the cases reported were among the IDP population. With the populations of the two regions still suffering from the consequences of a six year long armed conflict and the COVID 19 pandemic, this situation is most likely only going to worsen as all of 2022 has seen an increase in the drivers of malnutrition vis-a-vis high food insecurity, lack of access to health services, poor sanitary conditions, morbidity (malaria, respiratory infections, and cholera), poor sanitary conditions and repeated internal displacements. This fact can be corroborated with screening figures obtained during the implementation of the CBCHS-UNICEF Scaling up the Emergency Nutrition and WASH response in IDP sites and host communities of the South West region between April to October 2022. During this period, 32,258 children were screened 962 SAM (2.98%) cases identified and managed. Also, between November 1-14, 2022 the CBCHS participated in a mass screening organized by the nutrition cluster where 30.181 children were screened and 254 SAM were diagnosed and 725 MAM cases. During the screening exercise, members of the communities reported they feared the situation of malnutrition will only get worse as their harvest was poor due to the high cost of fertilizers which they could not afford. The areas selected for intervention are based on the results afore mentioned and also the fact that IPDs are more prone to malnutrition and food insecurity in host communities.
