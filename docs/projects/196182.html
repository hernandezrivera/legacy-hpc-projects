Project Summary

DRC will provide treated bamboo to other humanitarian partners to increase the lifespan of humanitarian shelters by strengthening structural components. Bamboo treatment plant (BTP) operations will provide cash-for-work opportunities to the local community, with individuals prioritized according to DRC’s vulnerability scoring system while ensuring safe and culturally appropriate inclusion of women, the elderly and people with disabilities. In addition, since the BTP is in a secured private compound, wherever possible survivors from DRC’s Protection programme will be referred for cash-for-work opportunities. People with specific needs will be assigned suitable tasks and provided a work environment tailored to their needs such as shaded areas with seating and gender-segregated workspaces. Meetings with the local community will be held to explain the cash-for-work selection process, gain acceptance, and ensure that BTP workers are not exposed to safety risks.
DRC will offer training on the bamboo treatment process to interested local actors and build capacity for a local actor to take over the BTP at the end of the project. To ensure that operations are financially sustainable for the local actor and introduce construction materials with lower environmental impact into the local market, DRC will also conduct a market assessment for treated bamboo products and pilot three new products identified by the assessment. Piloted products will undergo testing for susceptibility to fire, resistance to extreme weather conditions, environmental impact, and cultural acceptance within the camps and the host community.
DRC will recommend BTP cash-for-work participants to local actors undertaking bamboo treatment and will also provide training certification to participants so that they can access other livelihood opportunities. To support the transition of BTP services from donor-funded to pay-per-use, DRC will arrange meetings between the local actor and community stakeholders, as well as potential and existing partners to lend it legitimacy and promote its services.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

According to a study done by UNHCR, over 24 million pieces of bamboo have been used by humanitarian actors for construction from 2017 to 2019, placing significant strain on Bangladesh’s bamboo reserves. Untreated bamboo rapidly deteriorates from exposure to extreme camp conditions including flooding, high winds, landslides, and strong rains, thereby requiring constant replacement. 76% of shelters assessed in the 2022 Shelter Performance Standard Assessment showed signs of insect infestation. With persisting Bangladesh government restrictions against use of more robust materials in the camps, bamboo is anticipated to continue being the primary material for humanitarian construction. The 2023-2025 Shelter/NFI Sector Strategy projects that 150,823 shelters will need reinforcement, reconstruction, repair and maintenance.Structurally compromised shelters primarily impact women, the elderly and people with disabilities who spend most of their time at home due to cultural constraints and movement barriers in the camps. In the REACH Age and Disability Inclusion Needs Assessment (May 2021), 54% of women with disabilities and 44% of women without disabilities reported not having left their shelter in the week prior to data collection. 41% of people with disabilities reported not having left their shelter the week prior to data collection. The IOM/ACAPS “Our Thoughts” study (April 2021) points out that damaged or inadequate shelters force women and girls to “make the difficult decision of whether to leave for their health and comfort or whether to remain inside to maintain their dignity”.Treatment decreases bamboo’s susceptibility to rot, decay, and insect infestations, preserving shelters’ structural integrity and reducing the need for frequent bamboo replacement. DRC is currently one of three humanitarian actors producing treated bamboo in Cox’s Bazar, and while the Shelter/NFI Sector is encouraging widespread use of it to upgrade camp shelters, there is insufficient treated bamboo to cover humanitarian needs. Private actors are interested in producing treated bamboo but do not have access to training opportunities.

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

DRC will not be directly implementing in any camps so there is no risk of duplicated activities. The proposed activities will provide material support to other shelter partners. There is no risk of duplicated bamboo treatment since partners will be delivering their own raw bamboo to the BTP and picking them up after they have been treated. DRC will collaborate with the Sector to coordinate with shelter partners and maintain a dashboard for tracking bamboo delivered to and released from the BTP.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection

DRC will mitigate protection risks to BTP cash-for-work participants by explaining worker prioritization criteria to the community before selecting workers, ensuring that there is informed consent before capturing personal data, and adhering to the DRC data protection protocol. BTP workers will be assigned culturally appropriate tasks and divided into gender-segregated work teams. All DRC staff and cash-for-work participants undergo annual code of conduct and PSEA training. DRC has a PSEA focal point assigned to each of its implementation areas and maintains a code of conduct response mechanism.

3.2 - Gender

Gender disaggregated data is captured in DRC’s cash-for-work participant database, which is connected to an interactive dashboard for easy analysis. DRC aims to engage a safe and culturally appropriate number of participants of different genders, and will assign them workspaces and tasks that are culturally acceptable and in line with their capacity. Work conditions will be explained to the community before recruiting cash-for-work participants to encourage women to apply. Gender is a criterion in DRC’s vulnerability scoring system for prioritizing cash-for-work selection. Where possible, DRC will conduct gender segregated BTP trainings with trainers of the corresponding gender.

3.3 - Age including child safeguarding

Worker ages are captured in DRC’s cash-for-work participant database, and age is a criterion in DRC’s vulnerability scoring system for prioritizing worker selection. No children are permitted in the BTP and DRC will use identification documents to verify that only adults (18 years or older) are engaged in cash-for-work. Workplace accommodations will be made for elderly workers, such as shaded areas with seating, more frequent breaks, and less physically demanding tasks.

3.4 - Diversity

Worker disability is captured in DRC’s cash-for-work participant database, and Washington Group Short Set questions are integrated into the DRC vulnerability scoring system for prioritizing worker selection. Most of the BTP grounds are accessible, and should there be workers with physical mobility challenges, removable ramps will be made available to enable access to the BTP office and storage rooms. DRC will tailor work conditions to workers with disabilities, such as assigning tasks that require less movement around the facility and more frequent breaks.

3.5 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

DRC MEAL conducts an annual survey with DRC cash-for-work participants to understand if cash incentives enable participants to meet their financial needs, if work conditions were adequate, if there was adequate training, and if workers were satisfied with management. All workers are informed about DRC’s Code of Conduct Response Mechanism as part of their induction. All DRC staff and cash-for-work participants undergo annual code of conduct and PSEA training. DRC has a PSEA focal point assigned to each of its implementation areas and maintains a code of conduct response mechanism.

3.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Bamboo treatment solution chemicals are not environmentally harmful, and they are filtered and reused to reduce need for new inputs. Solution tank sediment consists of organic matter, which is used as compost in the BTP community garden. The BTP office is solar-powered and the facility received an environmental clearance certificate from the Department of Environment in 2019. Bamboo treatment mitigates strain on bamboo forests from intensive harvesting for shelter reconstruction and repair by protecting it from insect infestation, rot and decay. The chemicals used are also shown to slow fire spread and reduce fire intensity, protecting camps from largescale incidents that cause environmental damage. Carbon emissions from bamboo transport, and resources used for disposal of damaged bamboo are also reduced.
