Project Summary

The proposed Project will be implemented in Uror and Twic East counties of Jonglei state and Aweil East and Centre of Northern Bar el Ghazal state. There are currently 67,344 IDPs in Northern Bahr el Ghazal NBeG), primarily because it provides a safe haven for those fleeing conflict in neighboring states ,while 50% of communities in Uror are hosting IDPs . Uror and Twic East are predicted to remain at Crisis and Emergency levels (IPC 3 and 4) in January –March 2019, and would be likely one phase worse without humanitarian intervention. Aweil East county is predicted to slip into Emergency levels (IPC 4) in the January –March 2019 period. Poor food security in all locations, coupled with poor WASH practices, is leading to high rates of global acute malnutrition, over WHO’s critical level of 15%. The strong relationship between poor WASH infrastructure and practices, food insecurity and malnutrition is perpetuating high SAM and MAM rates. Use of unsafe drinking water and poor hygiene and sanitation practices are contributing to the high incidence of diarrhea and water-borne diseases in vulnerable communities, sustaining the malnutrition-diarrhea cycle and reducing the nutrient uptake in infants and young children. Tearfund will (1) Improve water supply infrastructure to increase access to safe drinking water and (2) Facilitate hygiene promotion throughout the targeted communities to encourage behavior change relating to hygiene and sanitation practices. The proposed action will enable Tearfund to continue providing critical services to the most vulnerable members in, including IDPs and host communities. Tearfund has a static presence throughout Jonglei and Northern Bar El Ghazal, with bases in Aweil East and Centre, Uror, Pochalla and Twic East and a sub-base in Yuai. The proposed project is part of Tearfund’s wider programme, which integrates WASH, Nutrition and Food Security.

Needs

Access to adequate water is limited in all the project sites with only 27.7% and 37.5% of households in Uror and Twic East respectively collecting water that meets the SPHERE standard of 15L/person/day (Tearfund Post-Harvest SMART surveys, 2017/2018). In Aweil East, the situation is slightly better at 44%, however the situation is still concerning with boreholes highly stressed as communities continue to be displaced. Regular breakdowns in boreholes due to pressure on WASH infrastructure is compounding the problem with an average of 812 people sharing one borehole in Uror according to the Tearfund Borehole Assessment Report (July 2016). The additional stress on already inadequate WASH infrastructure created by the ongoing influx of IDPs into the target areas remains a critical problem. The March 2017 inter-agency assessment found most of the water containers seen at the water points were in poor condition and in need proper cleaning and there were also reports of open defecation particularly in the communities hosting IDPs. This has largely been as a result of the movement of people within the counties, including returnees, who have not been exposed to hygiene promotion activities.Uror County: According to Tearfund’s 2018 SMART Survey, 90.7% of surveyed households have access to an improved source of drinking water, such as a borehole, public taps, protected dug wells and springs. However, 64.7% of households reported not treating water prior to drinking. While communities in Uror have improved access to boreholes through Tearfund’s rehabilitation of water supply infrastructure, access still falls below SPHERE standards as 41.2% of households travel for 30 minutes or longer on a round trip to collect drinking water and 27.7% of sampled households consume 15L water/person/day. This has been the result of regular breakdowns in boreholes due to pressure on the WASH infrastructure. The January 2018 KAP survey found 92.31% of respondents have never used or do not know how to use water treatment tablets, while 94.23% of those surveyed do not have water treatment tablets in their home at the time of the survey. Twic East County: The December 2017 SMART and KAP surveys revealed that although there has been improved borehole coverage in the county (99.8% of households have access to a borehole or hand pump), a significant proportion of the population (28%) continue to collect water from unsafe sources, including surface water (river/pond/canal). In addition, 62.5% of households regularly collect less than the SPHERE standard of 15L/person/day and 36.7% of households have to spend over 30 minutes conducting a round trip to fetch drinking water. Of those using unsafe water sources, 79% do not treat water before drinking. Knowledge on water treatment continues to be low as 62% of respondents have never used or do not know how to use water treatment tablets, and 80% of respondents did not have water treatments tablets at the time of the survey. While 49% of those surveyed had a trained pump mechanic in their village, approximately 72% of respondents reported that their local hand pump had broken down in the last six months, and 88% mentioned that it can take between 1-3 weeks for the hand pump repaired.Aweil East and Centre: Aweil East has been identified by the WASH cluster as one of the counties with the highest level of need in South Sudan, with 223,309 people in need of WASH humanitarian assistance . Borehole coverage remains low and open defecation continues to be prevalent in both Aweil East and Centre. In Aweil East, a WASH Cluster joint assessment was conducted in July 2018, surveyed payams had between 23 and 50% of their boreholes completely nonfunctional.

Activities

The proposed project will carry out the following activities:Provide timely, equitable access to safe and sufficient water to vulnerable and affected populations:The project will repair and rehabilitate 30 community based handpumps across the two states. Water quality testing will be carried out before commissioning for use by the community. Water treatment tablets will be distributed to communities with no safe drinking water source. Increase knowledge of appropriate hygiene practices among vulnerable and affected populations, to prevent and mitigate WASH related diseases: The project will undertake hygiene promotion across the two states (45 villages), particularly targeting women and children at feeding centres, children in schools (including MHM) and community based mass and individual hygiene promotion. Enable vulnerable and affected populations to practice safe, sanitary and Hygienic living through secure, dignified and gender appropriate. sanitation services: The project will implement the PHAST approach across the two states (45 villages), as well as undertaking construction of institutional latrines in Twic East (2x 2 stance latrines and 1x 4 stance latrines). Gender appropriate response starting from a risk assessment, for secure access, location and design of water points, toilets and showers A gender and protection ‘audit’ will be undertaken at the start of the project (and refreshed on a quarterly basis) to assess the impact that activities are having on the lives of women and girls. All latrines will be constructed to include protection features including locks and solar lamps. Water points will be placed in areas most accessible and convenient for women (informed by assessments). Gender appropriate learning methodologies will be applied throughout the hygiene promotion. Provide safe sanitation and hygiene facilities to women and girls with after appropriate risk analysis 10 x 2-stance latrines will be constructed in schools and feeding centres across Uror. The facilities will be fitted with protection features and will be gender segregated. This is aimed at reaching women accessing feeding centres as well as boys and girls attending schools. MHM will be built into the sanitation component in the schools. The project is part of Tearfund’s wider programme, which integrates WASH, Nutrition and Food Security. As such, locations with feeding centres and schools will be prioritized for the provision of WASH hardware service such as boreholes and latrines. These community structures will also provide a critical point of contact to the community and a platform for understanding the role of WASH as an underlying driver of malnutrition. In Uror and Twic East, Tearfund is already present in every village through nutrition activities, while in Northern Bar El Ghazal, Tearfund will continue to work through the relevant partners. Tearfund will use data from feeding centres about rates of diarrhoea from specific villages, to prioritize the provision of critical WASH NFIs and WASH hardware. The concept of food hygiene will also be emphasized during the hygiene promotion component. This will be run alongside Tearfund’s food security activities, to ensure that communities are equipped with adequate knowledge on food handling/storage.

Does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

Yes

How does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

At the beginning of each intervention, Tearfund provides the whole community with information about the project, including beneficiary targeting, locations, feedback mechanisms and expectations of Tearfund staff and volunteers. This is frequently re-iterated through community discussions and focus group discussions. Targeted communities are also made aware of the timing of key project activities and the locations. Feedback on these decisions is welcomed at the project design stage and throughout. The information is further disseminated by Tearfund's network of trained community volunteers, who are embedded within each village.

Are messages context-specific or/and tailored?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Culturally appropriate learning methodologies such as drama and dance are applied throughout the hygiene promotion component. The targeted community will be consulted on the design of hardware activities such as latrines, to ensure they are culturally appropriate. All messages will be translated into the local language before being disseminated.

Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse

Yes

Briefly describe how

Tearfund mainstreams protection throughout the wider programme and all Tearfund staff are required to adhere to PSEA policies. With regards to programming, Tearfund adheres to SPHERE standards with regards to the distances communities are walking to access water, ensuring that WASH hardware such as latrines ensure protection features and taking into account the risks beneficiaries (particularly women and girls) are exposed to when participating in and accessing project activities.

Nature of the consultations

Yes

Briefly describe the nature of the consultations

Tearfund is committed to ensuring the affected populations actively participate in project activities. Tearfund has found that in the past, marginalized groups may fear being involved in community discussions. Therefore Tearfund actively encourages and insists on the involvement of all sectors of the community , including IDPs and returnees. Separate consultations are conducted for men and women, in consideration of cultural or social pressures that women may face when trying to express themselves. Tearfund frequently conducts focus group discussions and key informant interviews, in addition to periodic monitoring assessments.

Feedback /complaint mechanisms?

Yes

Briefly describe the mechanisms

Feedback mechanisms are already established in all project locations, and include reporting to community volunteers, Tearfund staff, quarterly focus group discussions, meetings with local leaders, and feedback boxes at water points and distributions. The feedback mechanisms have been shown to be accessible, appropriate to the context and preferred by the community during other Tearfund projects. Tearfund will ensure that targeted communities are aware of all feedback and complaints mechanisms and the locations of feedback boxes during community sensitisation meetings, and at the beginning of feeding sessions. Tearfund staff are trained on how to handle feedback ensuring anonymity and confidentiality where appropriate, and pass on any concerns to relevant staff members. All feedback is recorded and responded to, after which it is incorporated into the current and future project designs. Any feedback regarding critical or sensitive protection issues will be referred directly to Tearfund Executive Team in Juba, investigated appropriately and responded to according to Tearfund’s Safeguarding Policy.

Community Engagement?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Tearfund aims to be representative in all monitoring and evaluation activities. Project designs actively take into account feedback received from the community during needs assessments and previous interventions including on programme quality and effectiveness, timing of project activities and safety of beneficiaries (including cultural issues that may arise). Furthermore, Tearfund frequently conducts community consultations to assess the effectiveness of the wider programme.

Protection Risk Analysis?

Yes

Briefly describe how

The entire community is informed about the feedback and complaints systems in place including contacting community volunteers (who will provide a direct link to Tearfund staff), directly contacting Tearfund staff, and using the feedback/complaints box. Tearfund staff are trained on how to handle feedback from community members who may want to remain anonymous, and pass on any concerns to relevant staff members. Furthermore, the community volunteers are aware of how to refer community members if concerns related to other sectors being implemented should arise. Feedback is recorded is incorporated into the current and future project designs. Tearfund follows SPHERE standards when selecting boreholes to be rehabilitated to ensure that communities, in addition to accessing adequate amounts of potable drinking water, are also within close walking distance of a water point. As women are often tasked with fetching water, this reduces the associated protection risks. Furthermore, Tearfund actively promotes the participation of women in Water Management Committees to further ensure that women’s view points are taken into account. All committee members are trained to prioritize vulnerable groups including persons with disabilities, during water collection times. Both a mass awareness campaign as well as house to house visits are included in the hygiene promotion component, with the latter aimed at ensuring vulnerable people can effectively be reached through the intervention. All assessments and surveys aim to have a representative sample of all groups within the community (IDPs, Host communities, women, elderly etc). This ensures that programming is responsive to the needs of all members of the community.

Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

The project has identified the cultural, social and physical barriers that targeted communities can face in relation to the project. This includes social stigmas related to bringing malnourished children out of homes (addressed through house to house visits), as well as dangers faced when trying to cross between government and IO lines in the community (addressed through parallel programming across lines).

Addresses inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Previous experience in the project areas has shown that women's access to decision making platforms is severely limited in the project areas. In addition to setting quotas for group membership (eg Water Management Committees), Tearfund will employ its network of community volunteers to work to change the negative attitudes and beliefs that are held within the community about the role of women. This will be done through the 'Transforming Masculinity' approach.

Does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Access is a key issue which in built into all Tearfund monitoring activities. This includes identifying specific cultural, social and economic barriers that communities face in relation to the project. This is done through frequent focus group discussions. In consideration of the sensitivity of the subject, Tearfund undertakes separate discussions for groups such as IDPs as well as women, and ensures that all groups are aware of individual feedback mechanisms.

Does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Safety (both physical and otherwise) is measured through individual and group community discussions as well as frequent field visits and field reports detailing the safety and security situation in the operational areas. Beneficiaries will frequently be asked to provide individual feedback, which will remain confidential. Reports will be generated which separate feedback related to safety and protection, and all incidents will be logged and reported. Appropriate actions will be taken and this will inform future interventions.
