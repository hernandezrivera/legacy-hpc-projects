Project Summary

SC's programming combines immediate relief with the restoration of basic social support services through the delivery of multi-sector responses that integrate child protection (family tracing and reunification, case management and resilience to the impact of conflict and crisis), and MHPSS support to affected populations. Protection concerns are caused and compounded by crisis and displacement, and their acute impact on safety and wellbeing of children is at the core of Save the Children’s commitment and response. Furthermore, ongoing security challenges inside the camps increase psychosocial insecurities of a population which has already resorted to multiple negative coping mechanisms.
Amidst such significant humanitarian need, Save the Children is prioritizing the continuation of its child-centered, gender-sensitive integrated protection and MHPSS interventions responding to the absence of safe spaces to play and express themselves and the acute individual risks faced by the most vulnerable children, particularly UASC and child survivors. For caregivers across Syria, there are almost no opportunities to acknowledge and gain support for the aggregate stressors of displacement, poverty and persistent insecurity, and the ways in which this affects their individual coping capacity and the ways in which they care for their children. The proposed activities draw upon Save the Children's global technical expertise, the evidence base and minimum standards for child protection in emergencies, deep local insights and commitment from a team of experienced frontline staff, and learning from previous projects inside Syria.
Case management and care of UASC: Child protection case management (CPCM) will be delivered by a set of trained and supervised case management staff, in line with the inter-agency CPCM SOPs, and supported by best practice and tailored guidance from SC’s Steps to Protect Common Approach to case management. SC will continue its leadership in the CPIMS+, which went live in late 2020 and strengthens quality service provision and coordination. Where appropriate, SC will explore to deliver cash for protection activities to improve the wellbeing of vulnerable children. The modality and CVA approach will be adapted to the local contexts, and the specific vulnerabilities the programme aims to address.
Community awareness raising and community-based child protection: Parents and caregivers will participate in group-based parenting skills and peer support sessions, drawing upon SC’s Parenting without Violence Common Approach. This will include targeted sessions for mothers of babies and toddlers to foster early attachment and build knowledge on early childhood development and nutrition. Children and adolescents will also have the opportunity to participate in creative and recreational activities in SC’s Child Friendly Spaces (CFSs). Participation will be further supported through child and youth clubs on topics and themes selected by the children and adolescents themselves. SC will continue to collaborate with and learn from community-level child protection actors and structures, including the child and youth clubs mentioned above, Child Protection Committees (CPCs), as well as other existing groups. SC will provide capacity building in the safe identification and referral of vulnerable children, psychological first aid, and child protection in emergencies.
GBV: Where possible, child survivors of SGBV will be able access case management services delivered by child protection case management staff. SC will scale up capacity building on GBV mainstreaming to all staff to mitigate and more effectively respond to GBV, including safe referrals and case management for child survivors.
In accordance with SC’s minimum standards, a gender, age and disability sensitive approach will be used across program components so that the different needs of girls and boys of all ages and abilities are effectively addressed, especially CLwDs.

4 - Needs

In its tenth year, the crisis in Syria continues to devastate the lives of children and their families. Complex and increasingly protracted protection risks—several of which require international cooperation to sustainably resolve—have been further compounded by the global and local financial crisis and COVID-19 outbreak, which has eroded family coping strategies and denied girls and boys even their most basic rights. Across Syria, 13.1 million people remain in need of protection assistance, 6 million of them children below the age of 17 and 3.2 million of them living with some form of disability. 60% of this population is in extreme or catastrophic severity of need (HNO 2021).The communities in camps face a particularly dire and inhumane set of conditions, characterized by inadequate shelter and WASH facilities, critical service gaps especially for children and adolescents, and a growing set of protection and mental health and psychosocial concerns that impact girls, boys, women and men in specific ways. For women and girls, unequal gender norms and threats of GBV impact freedom of movement and increase the risk of early marriage. For boys, economic pressures push them to child labor (including the worst forms) and adolescent boys are caught in a wider security context that favors their removal from the camp—and separation their caregivers—and placement in detention facilities. Bullying by peers and harassment are reported by children and caregivers as daily threats, only exacerbated by feelings of stress, fear and disappointment linked with COVID-19 (SC Barriers Analysis, 2020; SC Global COVID-19 Survey, 2020).Both in NES and NWS, SC will work primarily in camp settings and also in the neighboring local communities.

5 - Implementation of activities

A monitoring plan is developed during the inception phase of the project in coordination with the project team to ensure that the plan is both feasible and aligned with the implementation plan. Data will be collected on a monthly basis by the field teams on activity reporting and monitoring (e.g. training reports, activity reports, attendance lists), with support from the SC MEAL team for follow up with beneficiaries (e.g. focus group discussions, child participation activities). SC will collect data through KoBo or other online databases, to ensure a higher quality of collection and analysis of data; paper-based data collection will be used only if most appropriate for the context. All data is input into the IPTT to track progress vis-a-vis targets throughout the life of the project. FDGs will be held regularly with children and caregivers taking part in child protection activities to ensure that children’s voices feed into programming. Participants will be a mix of women and men, girls and boys. In addition, qualitative data will be routinely collected through monitoring visits. Project quality will be monitored through quality benchmarks. A range of mechanisms will be applied to ensure accountability to the affected population. Information about the project will be made available to target communities through appropriate media (verbally and written in local languages). SC will also create opportunities for communities, including children, to participate in programming decisions and provide feedback throughout the project cycle.SC monitors partner implementation through: 1. Monthly partnership review meetings; 2. Quarterly partnership and management meetings; 3.Quarterly performance evaluation.

7.1 - Please list any risks related to sexual exploitation and abuse that have been identified during the project design phase and any risk mitigation and prevention measures that have been adopted and built into the project

SC is invested in the Centrality of Protection and is developing an action plan that focuses on capacity building of staff and partners, embedding of conflict sensitivity and protection analysis, strengthening safe programming and child safeguarding approaches, improving risk management and integrating protection into advocacy work. In addition, SC integrates protection mainstreaming across its programs, and these principles guide the implementation team to ensure safe and quality programming. Building on the foundation of all staff members being trained on Child Safeguarding, protection against sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA) and Safe Programming, SC is also expanding capacity building of staff in conflict sensitivity, protection analysis and participatory approaches. A child safeguarding risk assessment, gender and GBV audit, and conflict sensitivity analysis are conducted for each program. These identify gaps, inform the project design, and mitigate protection risks for children and their families while accessing SC facilities and using SC services. In addition, SC has prioritized more sustained capacity-building for staff related to disability and disability inclusion, as well as on gender mainstreaming and GBV.

8 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Unstable security situation: conflict escalation, poor acceptance from local stakeholders and/or change in lines of control can prevent access to different program areas, either temporarily or permanently. Inappropriate project design can exacerbate tensions between communities and lead to unintended negative or harmful outcomes during implementation including suspension of activities. In order to manage and mitigate this risk, SC regularly monitors the local context, and SC employs security focal points. SC staff complete mandatory security trainings. Context-specific Safety and Security Management Plans are regularly reviewed and updated.Failure of money transfer mechanisms or change in banking regulation restricts movement of funds; and currency fluctuations: SC may be unable to deliver program activities due to lack of funds or program staff may be put at risk due to a lack of personal funds to hibernate or due to deterioration in local acceptance as a result of unpaid balances to other staff, suppliers etc. SC mitigates this risk by using multiple money transfer channels and agents who have strong reputations and have been able to deliver funds in almost all circumstances inside Syria. All SC agreements are denominated in donor currency.Risks due to the COVID-19 pandemic: The situation may result in risks to children and their caregivers due to lack of access to programming sites; further exposure to protection concerns, including violence; and health risks to beneficiaries and SC staff members. Relevant programme adaptations have been developed and are currently in place. SC has also introduced Standard Operation Procedures for staff working remotely and to identify COVID-19 focal points in each programme area.
