Project Summary

The project is a multi-sectorial protection and education intervention targeting vulnerable children and their families in 10 locations across 5 governorates. Geographical Areas targeted are Rafah governorate (Tal al Sultan) this will be done though Friends of child Association (Partner CBO for CTCCM); Khanyounis governorate (Khanyounis Camp, Al Batn al Sameen, Japanese neighborhood); Middle area governorate (Nusairat, Buraij), Gaza governorate (Al Sabra, Beach camp), Gaza North governorate (Jabalia, Beit Hanoun)
The project will provide
1) Holistic and multi-layered MHPSS services to vulnerable children and caregivers, including group-based PSS interventions and Child Protection prevention and awareness activities; and focused PSS and Child Protection interventions such as case management and trauma recovery intervention to help children manage trauma symptoms, and provide individual counseling for children and caregivers.
2) Provision of Child Protection case management services to most vulnerable children according to Gaza SOP guidelines and referral pathways for Child Protection case management. Some cash will be allocated for covering urgent needs for children/ women benefiting from case management services, such as medical care, transportation, education needs, etc.
3) Referral to specialized mental health services, remedial education and learning for 1st to 4th grade children affected by the conflict.
The project will be implemented by War Child and 3 local partners (Culture and Free Thought Association, Community Training Center and Crisis Management, and Teacher Creative Center), and will build upon the partners' strong expertise and complementarities: War Child will utilize its expertise in MHPSS, CP and established network of CBOs and CBOs volunteers in Gaza, as well as its coordination with relevant clusters (Education, CP/ MHPSS), while CFTA, CTCCM and TCC will further capitalize on their Child Protection, MHPSS, GBV and education experience and reach in Gaza and on their direct and strong relationship with the relevant Ministries.
The project will be implemented in coordination with MOSD and with active members of CPAOR, specifically ones providing CP and will align with Standards SOP for CPCM.
As part of the Case Management task force, War Child will be working with the partners to agree on referral pathways as well as information sharing protocol to ensure complementarity between our CP Case Management and other interventions in the similar areas in the NWB. Reporting on cases will be also ensure through on-going reporting to the CP CM task force, CP AoR that War Child is co-leading and OCHA online 5Ws system to report on cases detected and managed as part of this project under Protection for the case management cases and those benefitted from the project services.

1 - Needs

PROTECTION: protection remains one of the largest needs in Gaza with the escalations of violence that exacerbating an already severe crisis, which directly affects the wellbeing and mental health of the affected population. Children in the Gaza Strip are regularly exposed to devastating violence during the recurrent escalations; Israeli airstrikes and bombings during these escalations lead to a high number of casualties and injuries (including permanent disabilities), including among children. In August 2022, the Gaza Strip was under heavy bombardment resulted in the deaths of 18 children and a further 151 injured. Caregivers still have safety and security concerns for their children; about a quarter of households report safety and security concerns for their children, such as verbal harassment (14.2% for girls and 8.5% for boys), exposure to wildlife and stray animals (7.9% for boys and girls), bullying (4.7% for girls and 6.7% for boys), and sexual harassment/violence (6.5% for girls) as well as physical violence (5.2% for boys). Moreover, the extremely precarious situation in the Gaza Strip directly affects caregivers, who resort to negative coping mechanisms, including violence, against their children; 28.5% of the children are subjected to severe physical violence at home from their caregivers. Protection against GBV also remains a pressing need in the Gaza Strip; in a recent survey, 11.4% of households reported areas in their locations where girls and women felt unsafe. The most frequently reported locations in such cases were on the way to school (38.8%), followed by markets (23.1%) and social/community areas (21.6%). Women reported feeling unsafe in these areas predominantly for fear of verbal harassment (50.4%), but also for fear of sexual harassment/violence (35%), according to the latest available PCBS survey on violence, which predates the COVID-19 pandemic, 37.5% of married or previously married women between the ages of 18 and 64 in the Gaza Strip experienced violence in the 12 months preceding the survey. The issue is worse for women and girls with disabilities, as they encounter additional barriers that limit their access to specialized protection services: 58% of PwDs in the Gaza Strip lack information about SGBV prevention and response mechanisms. MHPSS: the recurrent wars, 15-year-long blockade and the extreme poverty and financial hardships it has engendered (unemployment in Q2 2022 is at a staggering 44.1%) have taken a toll on the mental health of the population of the Gaza Strip, notably children, women and PwDs. Reports and surveys all indicate an increasing number of children, and other vulnerable population, including women, IDPs, and PwDs/CwDs in urgent need of psychosocial services. The recurrent wars directly affect children’s mental health, as they fear for their lives, lose loved ones, are at risk of physical injury, and witness wide scale destruction. In this context, children are particularly vulnerable to experiencing high rates of mental distress, including significant risks of PTSD, depression, anxiety, behavioural problems, etc. The psychosocial wellbeing of children, young people and their caregivers has dramatically declined in recent years in the Gaza Strip: 80% of surveyed children (which would represent about 750,000 children) are experiencing emotional distress (SCI 2022). This mental health crisis has led to a dramatic increase in suicide rates, notably among adolescent girls. The same study indicates that 96% of surveyed caregivers (representing about one million caregivers) are feeling unhappy and anxious. As highlighted above, the poor mental health of caregivers directly affects their children, with caregivers struggling or unable to provide a positive environment for their children, and instead resorting to negative coping mechanisms, such as violence or emotional withdrawal. According to the study, more than half of Gaza’s children are estimated to need child protection and MHPSS services. Mental health and specialized psychosocial support services available in the Strip are insufficient given the severity of the crisis, and shortages in specialized personnel and medication. A quarter (25%) of households surveyed reported that psychosocial services were not available at their children’s school. EDUCATION: According to Education HNO 392,684 children in the Gaza Strip will need humanitarian assistance to access education (81.2% of refugee households and 76.7 % of non-refugee households). The dire economic situation in the Gaza Strip poses challenges for education in the Gaza Strip; nearly one-third (31%) of households report having difficulties to meet essential education expenses (mostly tuition fees and schoolbooks). According to MSNA, 7.1% of surveyed households had school-aged children not attending school; the main reason cited for non-attendance related to the household’s inability to cover school expenses (35.6%). Financial challenges was also the main reason for school children that dropped out in the 2021-2022 school year; of these, households cited an inability to cover school costs (56.5 % for girls and 66.2 % for boys), as well as the need for their children to participate in the work force (67.7 % of boys that dropped out to participate in labour). Children with disabilities (CwD) face even greater challenges; over two-thirds (71%) of surveyed households with at least one CwD reported that their child with a disability faced challenges in accessing education services. A quarter (25%) of households surveyed having at least one child with a disability reported having at least one child not attending school. For the rest of CwDs enrolled in education, they face a higher risk of dropout, due to gaps in adapted facilities and assistive devices at schools, inadequate transportation and staff capacity to accommodate their needs.

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

War Child has an overall Integrity Framework in place at a global level, consisting of: Child Safeguarding Policy, Code of Conduct, and an Anti-Fraud and Anti-Corruption Policy. PSEA is embedded in our Safeguarding Policy, which refers to the UN bulletin on PSEA and commitment to respond to its breach. War Child is committed to the PSEA and incorporates the concepts to prevent any potential violations perpetrated by staff, suppliers, vendors, or community members. War Child is an active member in the PSEA network set by UNICEF and endorsed the Standard Operating Procedures for the Community-Based Complaint Mechanism in oPt. War Child assures that the community members are oriented on the AAP procedures, including FCRM and PSEA, which is planned at early stage of each project through designing, printing and distributing relevant materials and employing the different project interventions to promote the relevant channels employed. The feedback received through the FCRM are recorded on War Child’s FCRM system, categorized and responded to immediately, or after referring the case to relevant staff within the organization. The system provide timely report visualization using PowerBI application. The FCRM thematic analysis help the project team in employing adaptive management and generate learnings. In any case that require handling beneficiaries’ data, War Child ensures adherence to Data Protection Protocols that uphold the privacy and confidentiality in all cases. Access to beneficiaries data is kept limited to the professionals working on the specific case. When the case is closed, War Child the replace any identical information with codes and archives the case data. Those mechanisms are key part of generating organizational learning, as it helps capture the participants’ feedback to reflect on the quality of the processes and make informed decisions and changes to the programs.

9.1b - If 'Other', please specify :

In-line with War Child’s Strategy 2025, particularly for Strategic Objective P4: Improved AAP and Community Participation, the organization programs are devoted to maintain people-centered approach where members of communities, including children, PWDs and marginalized groups, have a clear and visible input into the projects design and influence the implementation of interventions carried by War Child and partners. War Child employ an advanced computerized FCRM system to assure the implementation of APP, effective implementation of the Integrity Framework and full compliance with the CHS. War Child is aligned to the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child as a guideline, and to the Child’s Participation and Protection Rights. These underlying principles and standards, alongside the commitment to do-no-harm and quality programming, are our impetus to have functioning accountability and feedback mechanisms everywhere we operate. War Child prioritize the capacity building and training of all staff and partners on the FCRM SoPs, including the reporting mechanisms, promotion of FCRM channels, usage of the standard forms, and monitoring the quality implementation of the employed feedback mechanisms. Furthermore, War Child is committed to improve the community participation along with the different stages of the projects, including the design, planning, implementation and after action reviews.

9.1d - Explain how you have ensured that mechanism are accessible to all population groups?

The FCRM SOPs suggest that all projects employ several feedback channels that include but not limited to: Hotline, SMS, WhatsApp, Helpdesk and Child-friendly complaint boxes. The project team works with the community stakeholders and general community to verify the relevance of the employed channels and decide on adding more channels in case the basic set of available channels are not sufficient, this include defining new channels that better fit the preference of PWD or marginalized groups needs. The validity of the FCRM channels consider the following aspects: community location, age groups, gender, age, literacy, disability and physical and social barriers to guarantee equal access to FCRM channels. FCRM is properly designed to assure the access of different vulnerable groups including PWDs, elderly, children and females. For example, when the project is targeting individuals with mental- related disability, additional mechanisms with skilled experts are employed to meet the beneficiaries in-person to collect their feedback regularly.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

Accountability to Affected Population, Protection, Conflict Sensitivity, Do No Harm and GBV are mainstreamed into War Child’s project implementation through:1) accountability measures in line with War Child Integrity Framework, including community-based and child-friendly complaints and feedback mechanisms for all services, and participants’ satisfaction assessments using WCH tools and FGD with women, men, girls and boys. 2) Identifying vulnerable groups across communities to foster social stability, avoid exacerbating tensions within the community and promote the equitable distribution of services; 3) Analyzing existing community power dynamics to ensure the needs of individuals and groups at risk of marginalization are voiced (also through the feedback mechanism); 4) Do No Harm principles included within CP and child safeguarding competency development of partners and communities; including ensuring the 4 eye principles are always maintained in war Child’s PSS and CP activities implemented in the CBOs as to always ensure having 2 or more staff members/facilitators leading the children’s activities, gathering consents from the parents and children to participate in War Child’s activities, having zero tolerance to abuse, and ill treatment caused by any staff members and service provides towards children and adult participants in War Child activities and follows the guidelines for Child Safeguarding policy for reporting and following up procedures on reported incidents of abuse. 5) Commitment to child safeguarding and confidentiality by ensuring the need-to-know basis principle by case management staff and the other PSS staff working in their projects, ensuring secure and safe storage of data.6) Timely referral to specialized MHPSS, CP, and GBV professionals; 7) Promoting participation and empowerment of boys and girls, and men and women;9) applying a gender age marker in the design and monitoring of activities.8) Using culturally contextualized and evidence-based manuals and interventions for PSS group activities with Children and caregivers.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

The project enables equal and impartial access to assistance and services and targets vulnerable groups by 1) working in the locations most affected by the crisis; 2) including specialized services for vulnerable children and caregivers survivors of/at risk of CP or SGBV whom will be identified through Case Management; 3) ensuring meaningful access and participation of vulnerable children and caregivers (including people with disabilities) through coordination with MoSD, CBOs and outreach. Selection of participants will be based on vulnerability and following humanitarian standards (such as being acutely affected by air strikes, family or community violence/abuse/neglect, disabilities, child labor, COVID-19, child-headed households, UASC). Activities and interventions will be adapted to meet the specific needs and all team members and field facilitators will receive a refresher/ orientation on inclusion by War Child CP team.
