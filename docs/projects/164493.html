Project Summary

According to the 2019 Humanitarian Needs Overview, 6.07 million school-age children (5-17 years) are in need of education assistance. The highest numbers of children in need in Aleppo (1.1m), Rural Damascus (0.91m) and Idleb (0.83m).
The severity of needs was calculated for the education sector in each community in Syria, based on variables in the three subjects of education access, quality of education and education systems strengthening, together with inter-sector indicators for severity of need based on access to and intensity of conflict in each community. Access indicators include enrolment rate, attendance rate, proportion of school-age children who are internally displaced persons and/or returnees, availability of learning facilities, and availability of teaching and learning materials; quality of education indicators include the availability of teachers, and percentage of teachers receiving a salary/incentives; the education system strengthening indicator includes professional development for education actors. the greatest severity of education needs (severity 4-6), in terms of number of people in need (children and education personnel) are Idleb (0.89m) and Rural Damascus (0.68m) and Aleppo (0.63m).
SC will target 85% subdistricts with a 4, 5 or 6 severity. The areas for implementation in Northwest Syria (NWS) were selected based on numbers of out-of-school children and the capacity of the hosting communities to accommodate them within existing schools. Vulnerability criteria also prioritizes newly displaced children, unaccompanied and separated children (UASC) and children with disabilities. The locations selected, based on the 2019 HNO education severity scale, averaged a 5 of 6 severity. The number of people in need for protection services is 342,078 newly displaced individuals as of November 2019, and 2,425,649 total IDP’s in the NW, which includes many of our target areas . This number continues to grow as there has been a sharp increase in displacements in the NW of Syria. In Northeast Syria (NES), SC’s Education team has worked in five camps - Newroz, Roj, Al hole, Areesha, Washokhani These camps include very different groups of populations such as Iraqi refugees including Arabs and Yezedians, Syrian IDPs and foreign families).
SC recognizes the need for having a gender balanced recruitment and training strategy to ensure that dialogue with communities, implementation of activities, and coordination with stakeholders take the needs of both genders into consideration. An estimated 50%+ of the targeted children are girls, but girls will still be explicitly targeted and prioritized for inclusion. Though retention rates for boys can exceed girls by secondary schooling, initial enrolment rates reflects girls less often reached. SC teams identify barriers to participation and inclusion and implement measures to mitigate these, for example by separating parent/community group meetings, where cultural norms dictate to facilitate female participation; or by ensuring males are also recipients of our key messaging on education as they are usually the gatekeeper in household level decisions. In addition, diversity must be considered very carefully, especially regarding religion, ethnicity or nationality. The contents of education activities must be neutral and suitable for all children in the learning space.
This action is in line with SC Whole of Syria strategic plan. In NWS SC is co-leading the Education Cluster together with UNICEF and in NES SC established and has led the Education Working Group (EWG) for the education sector coordination since 2017.

4 - Needs

Syria remains the largest and longest running humanitarian crisis in the world. An estimated 400,000 civilians have been killed or injured, while over half of the population, approximately 13.1 million people, have been displaced. Approximately one million people have migrated to Europe, over 5.6 million are living as refugees in Syria’s neighbouring countries and approximately 6.3 million remain internally displaced within Syria’s borders, including 2.8 million children. People have often been displaced multiple times, with continuous disruption to education for children. Security concerns often suspend school for extended periods of time, and some children cannot access learning spaces due to long distance and heightened risks on the way to school, with girls disproportionately impacted. Families who have been displaced lack access to learning materials and books at home, resulting in a deficiency in home-based learning support for children, crucial to basic skill development, especially at the youngest ages. The PSS and survival needs of households often cause caregivers' priority towards a child's education to diminish. Formal education system is in need of school rehabilitation and learning materials and the Ministry of Education needs support in the capacity building of their staff. Many families have neglected education as they have had to prioritise work and livelihood, or they are continually on the move, resulting in many children requiring assistance to continue learning from home. Early childhood care and development (ECCD) children have been always neglected in Syria as there are very few qualified staff in this area and there is no specific curriculum used for children of this age. In NES, the Education team conducted a rapid needs assessment in the camps in September 2019. This revealed key needs including additional learning spaces; students’ education materials, teacher trainings, psychosocial support (PSS) and social and emotional learning (SEL).

5 - Implementation of activities

SC’s education approach includes: Access and Quality Learning. The Access objective focuses on increased, immediate access for the most marginalized student populations to responsive, protective, learning environments - Temporary Learning space(TLS) providing ECCD (including Early Literacy and Math), basic education and recreational activities - Mobile education using SLP materials - Child participation and safe access supported through school level safety planning activities and the new Improving Learning Environments Together in Emergencies programming in NES - For the quality objective, teacher trainings ensure better quality of education provision, including the Inter-Agency Network for Education (INEE), TICC Teacher In Crisis Context, hygiene promotion and the new TiCC Peer Coaching and Mentoring packages - Facilitators and camp management personnel trained on Education in Emergency, TICC, positive discipline, case management, classroom management and SC core policies - Learning outcome results will feed into re-designing of approaches and curriculum consistently throughout implementation.This objective focuses on improved capacities and resilience for communities to support learning and protection at home and in schools. - SEL-F programme consisting of a scripted SEL curriculum that is age and stage appropriate for Grades 1-9 - International Social Emotional Learning Assessment (ISELA) to assess the impact of SEL programming on children’s wellbeing – Monthly participation activities such as TLCs (Teaching Learning Cycles), PTAs (Parent-Teacher Associations) and community committee meetings - Using SC’s Community Action toolkit to strengthen community participation in learning Child Friendly Spaces (CFS) established within schools to provide children with structured psychosocial support - Peer-to-peer support groups for adolescents - Community Coordination Centres (CCCs) to support children in at-home learning and to maximize their learning capabilities.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk: Community tension due to service delivery locations or types of services offered if one community is served and a neighboring community not served/cannot access or if information offered is not consistent between communities.Mitigating Measures: Continuously ensure service mapping, consultation with community on program interventions, regular communication with different communities on availability of services including host communities and returnee populations. Expansion of services to non-camp locations where needs are present, to avoid concentration of services in camps. Monitoring Mechanism: Regular consultations with communities, FGDs, feedback boxes Risk: Resistance from local authorities to accessing and implementing programming. Mitigating measures: SC has established relationships with relevant local authorities and stakeholders, such as local council, education directorate, ministries, community gatekeepers and religious leaders. The stakeholder will be, involved throughout program design and implementation. Monitoring Mechanism: Regular meetings with relevant authorities. Risk: Barriers to access for marginalized groupsMitigating measures: Specific measures are taken to ensure input is received from marginalized groups such as women and girls, CwD and the elderly. Feedback is used to inform planning, implementation, monitoring and evaluating activities. Mitigation measures are brainstormed with the community to ensure that girls and boys (incl. children with disabilities) have equal access to activities.Monitoring Mechanism: FGDs, feedback mechanismRisk: SCI or partner staff violating SCI CSG rules harms children; damages the reputation of the organisationMitigating measures: All SC staff, volunteers and representatives sign the CSG Policy and Code of Conduct. Risk: Negative coping mechanisms keep boys and girls from enrollment -- SC conducts outreach and awareness messaging regularly to promote the benefits of education enrollment

7 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Safety & Security (S&S) - All staff movements must be pre-approved and refresher security trainings are regularly conducted. The Hostile Environment Awareness Training is mandatory for NES deployments; local staff have been trained on Advanced Personal S&S. SC’s partners engage with relevant stakeholders and receive timely updates. SC works on a common response to unacceptable conditions. Airstrikes: SC supports partners to immediately report security incidents for analysis and implement comprehensive safety plans and advocates for humanitarian sites and public services (schools, hospitals, etc.) to serve as safe spaces in conflict. Loss of access: SC and partners conduct thorough security assessments and maintain updated contingency plans. If necessary, SC will suspend programs. If not viable, SC would assess possibilities for relocation. Only in a worst-case scenario would project termination be considered. Diversion of resources to terrorist/ proscribed groups. In 2018, interviews were conducted to identify areas for increased risk mitigation; an action plan was developed. SC launched an initiative with OCHA to provide external training on international humanitarian law and negotiation with armed actors. Partners have their own systems of anti-terrorism vetting checks for all contracted suppliers and SC’s Syria office vets against the third-party database for procurements $750+. Disruption to cash transfer operations: In NES, SC review of Hawala delivery to identify mitigation measures/additional suppliers to resolve issues. Further restrictions on humanitarian access through Syria-Iraq border based on pending cross-border resolutions. SC has spoken with both current and new donors to ensure continuity of services. SC also employs a strong national staff and local partner-base who, in the case of loss of access, can aptly continue program activities with remote SC management.
