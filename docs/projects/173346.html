Project Summary

To ensure these communities' access to emergency care, RI will strengthen its mobile medical teams (MMTs) to support with primary care provision during disasters, including through enhanced rosters, training and stock prepositioning. At the same time, RI will enhance communities' access to comprehensive, secondary emergency neonatal and maternal referral care packages with the opening of a new Comprehensive Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (CEMONC) facility. RI will renew its focus on gender-responsive health care through targeted community outreach including gender-sensitive monitoring, SRH messaging and screening of common ante and post natal complications. The program will embed regular feedback from program participants, with particular focus on women and girls, through community outreach and facility-based surveys, to enable program adaptation and ensure continued relevancy of its interventions. To this end, RI will disaggregate key indicators (1.1, 1.3, 2.2, and 3.1) per SADD methodology.

1 - Identified Needs

The sheer magnitude of the Rohingya refugee influx has put massive pressure on all health services while cramped living conditions present significant public health risks. Limited by lack of financial resources and movement restrictions, most Rohingya are completely dependent on health services provided by the Government of Bangladesh and relief agencies including for primary health care, trauma care, reproductive, maternal, neonatal and child health including Basic Emergency Obstetric And Newborn Care (BEmONC) and Comprehensive Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (CEmONC) services, mental health services and psychosocial support (MHPSS). Even three years after the initial crisis in 2020, less than half of refugees in Bangladesh (57%) reported they can safely access health facilities (In the Shadows of the Pandemic, October 2020). The primary driver of insecurity in this period was increased criminal activity as a livelihoods coping mechanism, including the wide-spread gang violence in September-October. Health facilities were already limited, made worse during the pandemic. The government closed down some facilities as a COVID-19 precaution, further shrinking healthcare options. From April-June 2020, CHWs were not able to conduct community case finding and referral reports which led to backlogs in the number of health cases identified and treated throughout the camps and surrounding host communities (Health Sector, September 2020). Access to healthcare was especially limited from April – June 2020 due to COVID-19 related movement restrictions and fear of community disease transmission at overcrowded facilities, resulting in fluctuating consultation rates. Once movement restrictions lifted, patients flooded to a more limited number of facilities than in the pre-pandemic period. As a result, patients overcrowd existing facilities. RI facilities have reported a 150-200% increase in patients between 2019 and 2020, overwhelming current capacity and resources. This presents a catch-22 as fear of overcrowding disincentivized community members’ health-seeking behaviour which in turn created more backlogs in health needs, which ultimately has resulted in even higher overcrowding. Immunizations are a key, unaddressed health need of vulnerable populations, needed to reduce mortality or morbidity stemming from preventable diseases. Data suggests that vaccination coverage may have been further limited during the COVID-19 period when health care systems prioritized acute needs (Health Sector, September 2020). Thus, to prevent concurrent waves of preventable disease outbreaks which can further overwhelm a healthcare system already stretched thin in the pandemic, immunization campaigns are a key need for 2021. Other priority needs remain in reproductive health services, ensuring referrals to health facilities, prevention of cholera/acute watery diarrhoea, and trauma care. Currently, there are limited BEmONC or CEmONC facilities in the camps even though the needs for SRH and emergency obstetric care are high. In September 2020, 70% of RI’s referred cases were for emergency obstetric care. Following the COVID-19 lockdown period, the Gender Hub found that disrupted service provision has only contributed to more barriers to women and girls’ access to sexual and reproductive health (In the Shadows of the Pandemic, October 2020). Early warning and surveillance systems have been established as part of preparedness to mitigate the risk of a communicable disease outbreak. This allows the health response to be adaptive as new health needs and priority interventions are identified at the community-level. With the build-up of non-COVID related cases across the camps during the acute COVID-19 lockdown period (March-July 2020), expanded investment in this surveillance system will be key to minimize health risks of men, women, children, and the elderly in catchment areas and precautions are taken to diffuse build-up of any disease outbreak.
