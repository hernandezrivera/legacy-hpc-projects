Needs

Mental health (MH) conditions as a result of conflict continues to be a significant and urgent need amongst IDPs and conflict-affected communities in all governorates. From the estimates of the World Health Organization, “after an acute onset of major emergency, on average about 1 in 6 people (10–15%) will suffer a mild to moderate mental disorder and about 1 in 30 people (3–4%) will have a mental disorder that is so severe that it undermines their ability to function and survive in a chaotic emergency environment.” For Iraq, WHO estimates that “prevalence of MH disorders remains high (12-month prevalence is estimated to be 11%) while service provision remains low (the treatment gap for management of MH disorders is estimated at 94%).” There are significant gaps in the capacity to provide MHPSS support in the overall Iraq response and a lack of updated MHPSS assessments from the Health cluster and individual organizations. Exposure to violence including bombings and killings, loss of loved ones and possessions, multiple relocations, harsh and uncertain living conditions among others, have greatly impacted the wellbeing of returnees, IDPs and host communities. At the family level, these are the manifested symptoms and negative coping mechanisms: secondary trauma; chronic aggression; domestic violence; intergenerational conflict; dysfunctional family structures leading to disturbed development of children and adolescents; transmission of destructive and negative behavior and role models to children. PSS activities support positive coping of stressful situations and promote wellbeing and resilience. ‘Referral disease’ or over-referrals for specialized psychiatric services which can be addressed at community level through MH and PSS has been an issue among MHPSS actors in the KRG-Iraq area.. It is imperative that MHPSS and wider protection awareness and dialogues happen at the community level. Through these trainings, communities will be taught that only cases of severe MH disorders will be referred for MH consultations and mild symptoms will be referred for community-based PSS services. This will prevent referral to psychiatrists of cases that could be addressed at the community level and which deplete psychiatrists’ capacity to address the more severe cases. With the training, communities will also be better prepared in the setup of PSS structures and activities.or do not feel able to due to access barriers such as stigma or having to be escorted by a male relative.

Protection Risk Analysis

Risk of social exclusion and/or conflict: Tearfund is aware of protection concerns relating to households that are perceived to be affiliated to IS. There have been complaints raised to community and governmental authorities that assistance has been going to these households; there has then been pressure on Tearfund to share beneficiary lists. All civilians are impartially entitled to receive humanitarian assistance. There are concerns that this will continue to be an issue, and that community leaders will be under pressure to exclude households. Previously-displaced persons may be more vulnerable in terms of needs, but those that did not flee may face increased vulnerable situation as a result of perceived connection with the suspected perpetrators. These issues can influence project activities when selecting beneficiaries for projects. In addition, vulnerable groups may not have social integration and acceptance in the community to apply for the project at all, e.g. IDPs in host communities. As previously mentioned, the project target locations are very diverse with different ethnic and religious groups, balance among these communities in proportion and size may have shifted significantly due to displacement or/and impact of the conflict. Negative drivers of the conflict, favouritism and stigmatization are expected issues that can as well interfere with the willingness of beneficiaries to attend. Gender and age considerations: Female-headed households are being recognized as highly vulnerable and between 10-25% of the households in Ninewa Plains are assessed to be female headed (REACH, MCNA, 2018) and 17.7% across 8 villages in Hawiga district (TF assessments, April-Sept 2018). There are cases of female headed households who would struggle to attend an assessment or distribution due to the perceived cultural appropriateness of being present in a public place. Access for the elderly particularly those who have no support from family can be challenging, and often the elderly risk being missed during the assessment and distribution process.

Mitigation Measures

Protection Mainstreaming: Following the key criteria 1. Prioritize safety & dignity, and avoid causing harm; 2. Meaningful access; 3. Accountability; 4. Confidentiality and 5. Compliance with humanitarian principles. Tearfund ensures that all staff and casuals complete e-learning modules covering safeguarding (children and adults), fraud awareness, whistleblowing, corruption and bribery; which give staff clear guidelines on how to conduct themselves. Tearfund develops community acceptance through regular meetings with community leaders, establishing community committees, and listening to the community concerns through key informants. Solutions including access for women and the elderly are discussed, and solutions for the safe delivery of cash modalities resolved. In line with best practice and the recommendations of the CWG, TF mainstreams protection throughout all its projects. Protection needs are considered in the selection of beneficiaries. Vulnerability criteria include: individuals missing documents (which can affect their ability to receive government assistance and return to their area of origin); child headed HHs; widowed or divorced women; and families demonstrating one or more negative coping mechanisms. TF has internal data protection guidelines and trains staff on them. TF is committed to ensuring robust data sharing agreements are in place with actors when coordination requires data sharing. Gender and age participation: Proportionate participation by men and women will be guaranteed during staff recruitment, FDGs, beneficiary selection and creation of community female feedback groups to advise how to increase female participation and ensure gender sensitive approach trainings. Community committees will include representation from both men and women. TF trains staff on gender-sensitive service delivery. At distributions a beneficiary accountability desk is composed of female and male staff ensuring feedback and issues can be discussed with men and women separately so each gender is able to speak freely. TF has mixed gender teams at assessment, implementation, monitoring and beneficiary accountability level, ensuring that issues can be discussed with men and women separately so each gender is able to speak freely. Assessments have shown that men and women have often had differing priorities and preferences of modality of distribution. During distributions TF works with community leadership, community committees, Mokhtar's and camp management to ensure that individuals with specialised needs are identified and steps taken to ensure that they can still access distributions and have equal access to the assistance provided. This is particularly relevant for elderly individuals or those with disabilities.

How is your project accountable to affected population

Understanding the local context: In the initial assessment and analysis of community’s needs, FDGs are carried out with women, youth and different ethnic/religious groups to get a better understanding of their vulnerabilities and abilities. Key Informant Interviews are used to better understand the impact of the crisis on their activities and their ability to meet basic needs for their families and to identify bottlenecks for successful project activities. Local leaders must have a voice in identifying referral pathways, development of referral or helpful contact cards, and being consulted on content and approach of MHPSS related activities. Supporting capacity building of leadership through training will strengthen community resilience and enhance reintegration and recovery of conflict affected communities. Communities will have more capacity to advocate for their needs. It will also generate more community acceptance and ownership of the project and that will promote sustainability. Beneficiary accountability officer: A dedicated beneficiary accountability officer will carry out spot checks and provide input to the project teams to ensure inclusion of beneficiary feedback into project design details and decisions. Beneficiary communities and individuals can contact the free hotline number to report issues and complaints related to the project activities; with each call reviewed and responded with recommendations to improve the project systems or to indicate any action to be taken. Monitoring Evaluation Accountability and Learning (MEAL): As part of the project cycle the teams will be supported by a system of monitoring, evaluation, accountability and learning through the MEAL team who operates independently to the project team. The MEAL team will take responsibility to manage beneficiary database to cross-check transparency and accountability of vulnerability assessments and registration process. The MEAL team will facilitate a midterm lessons learned workshop will be conducted with both Partner’s project to reflect and learn from the first project cycle. Relevant findings, learnings and/or success cases of will be shared with communities. Additionally, an external evaluation will be conducted for the project to assess the impact of the project. Whistleblowing: TF as an organization has whistleblowing mechanisms that can be used by staff to report any identified behaviours or actions by other staff members that compromise both the values of the organizations and put beneficiaries, host communities and key project relationships in a vulnerable position or at risk. All staff complete online or other training to understand the Whistleblowing structure and process for their organization. Exit strategy:Developed with the beneficiaries and service providers for ongoing support if required post distribution/project period. Community meeting for closure of project targets and feedback; hand over to those community networks for ongoing monitoring and support. Referral information provided to beneficiaries about accessing service providers as needed on issues they may face in the future. Ongoing access to the Tearfund beneficiary accountability hotline
