Project Summary

The FSL I Programme started on 1 January 2018 and has been reported under the HRP 2018. The project end date has been extended until 31 March 2019. This submission is for the period of 01 January – 31 March 2019.
The FSL I programme aims to contribute to increased food security for vulnerable displaced and host community households in Idleb and Aleppo Governorates.
FSL I comprises of two components: Household Food Security and Bakery Support.
The Household Food Security component targets an average of 250,000 food-insecure individuals per month with in-kind food assistance (food kits) and cash modality (food restricted vouchers).
The Bakery Support component provides an approximate 600,000 individuals living in supported bakeries’ catchment area with bread at a reduced and stabilised price. This is achieved through subsiding up to 50% input (flour and yeast) of bread production thus supporting local bakeries to produce a stable quantity of bread that meets local needs. This support is conditioned upon the selling of this bread in the local markets at a reduced and stabilised price agreed with and monitored by NGO X’s Cash and Markets, and Monitoring Teams.
This project is implemented in partnership with several other INGOs whose names have been concealed for security reasons. One INGO (whose name has been provided via email to the FSL Cluster Coordination Team) will submit their planned activities under this project separately based on a prior agreement between the two agencies. The two agencies have coordinated carefully the programme targets and figures to avoid duplication.

Needs

Of the overall 13.1 million people in need in Syria, 10.5 million are food insecure, and 6.5 million, or 54% of the population, are facing acute food insecurity. This is in large part due to the depletion of assets with 9.8 million people being in need of livelihood support, while the average price of the food basket is now 800% higher to pre-crisis levels. According to the data from the mobile Vulnerability Analysis and Mapping report, one in four of the surveyed Syrian households (HH) had inadequate food consumption, reflecting a diet of low or medium diversity – mainly consisting of bread, rice, sugar and oil. The same survey found that the most affected by food insecurity include displaced HH, HH headed by women, large HH and HH living in rented accommodation. Idleb, Aleppo and Hama Governorates continues to host the second largest number of displaced persons in Syria (c. 1.2m IDPs of a population of 2.45m ). Approximately 56% of communities surveyed by REACH in May 2018 reported that humanitarian assistance was the main source of food for them. In 63% of surveyed communities in August 2018, REACH found that some HH reported resorting to negative coping strategies to meet their food needs in the previous month. Aleppo has the highest food insecure population, followed by Rural Damascus and Idleb. The Food Security Sector estimates that Idleb, hosting over two million people, has nearly 65% of its people in need. Of them, 35% are acutely food insecure and 30% are at risk of food insecurity (HNO 2018). One of the main drivers of food insecurity in Idleb remains the high concentration of IDPs (44%) and their increasing vulnerability. At household level, the Food Consumption Score (FCS) has shown worrisome trends in the last years, with the highest number of households categorized as having poor food consumption being in Rural Damascus (50.8%), Homs (24.8%), Idleb (21.6%) and Aleppo (20.0%). The Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS) has also shown worrying trends, with significant decrease among the general population compared to the previous years. Idleb scored medium and poor HDDS among 55% of its population, while Aleppo has 58.5% of its population scoring medium to low HDDS, with higher percentages among IDPs. These results, in turn, lead to the population altering their food consumption by using various strategies to adapt to reduced access to food. The food consumption-based reduced Coping Strategies Index (rCSI) has been found at 9 in Idleb and 11 in Aleppo with Hama scoring the highest rCSI (26) among all governorates. The most frequently cited coping strategies are: relying on less preferred or less expensive food; borrowing food or relying on help from relatives; reducing the number or meals per day; reducing portion sizes; and reducing consumption by adults in order for small children to eat.

Food Kit/Voucher distributions will take place at designated distribution sites. Sites are identified by program field teams based on a set of criteria, that include travel distance (<1 km), security, and protection and gender considerations. In extenuating circumstances, the team will distribute directly to beneficiaries with restricted mobility, e.g. elderly or disabled head of HHs. In accordance NGO X’s general eligibility and vulnerability criteria. In cases where a beneficiary is uncomfortable attending the distribution centres, distribution is brought directly to the beneficiary’s location. This measure is particularly aimed at making it easier for female headed HHs and vulnerable groups, who may not be in a position to attend the distribution points themselves. Where appropriate and possible, NGO X insures that there are separate female and male lines at distribution points. Distributions sites are subject to distribution monitoring conducted by the MEAL Team. This includes monitoring distributions against quality checklists and gathering beneficiary feedback on the distribution process and assistance they are receiving. Results are compiled and communicated weekly to the Food Security Team, the Assistant Country Director Programs, and the MEAL Coordinator who follow up with corrective actions and amend the program accordingly. At the end of a distribution, remaining food assistance will be returned to the NGO X warehouse. Damaged items are returned to warehouses for replacement and an investigation is conducted into the cause.

Protection Risk Analysis

This project has been implemented since January 2018 and is based on the findings from routine assessments, monitoring activities and the Vulnerability Assessment carried out by NGO X in mid-2018 to identify the protection risks and enhanced vulnerabilities requiring adjusted assistance. Following findings from these exercises, NGO X have revised the organisation’s Food Distributions Standard Operating Procedures to include measures for households with restricted mobility or enhanced vulnerability / protection risk. Such measures include door-to-door distribution measures (‘home deliveries’) in addition to other measures taken by NGO X’s distribution, monitoring and safeguarding teams. Other measures are advised by NGO X’s in-country and Turkey-based security teams who thoroughly and regularly monitor the security situation in NGO X’s operational areas. Distribution teams also take caution in deciding on the place and times of distribution taking into account security teams advice and inform beneficiaries on these adjustments. Social Workers recruited by NGO X accompany distribution and monitoring team to identify vulnerable households that need assistance beyond NGO X’s mandate or capacity and refer them to community services in line with the Protection Cluster referral and case management guidance. For this purpose, a service mapping exercise has been carried out by NGO X with support from the Protection Cluster to inform referral pathways. A Special Needs Fund is being currently established with a view to provide additional material or service / referral assistance to the most vulnerable households. NGO X’s Safeguarding Reporting Framework is now in place with trained Safeguarding Focal Points being in place and ready to identify or record reports on any protection concerns against NGO X beneficiaries committed by NGO X or partner staff or other programme stakeholders. These cases are recorded and investigated with due seriousness and the investigation outcomes are reported to NGO X management who then decides on the appropriate actions.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Assumptions:- Continued access to targeted areas and vulnerable populations remains permissive, supported by continued adherence to NGO X's acceptance strategy and the JOPs; - Security conditions remain sufficiently stable to allow NGO X and partner staff to carry out distributions and monitoring activities; - Sufficient and timely access to required inputs through procurement, reliable supply and available transportation continues to be achieved; - Border crossing remains open for delivery of supplies- Food price inflation does not outpace overall inflation and household economic gains; - Food inflation and currency exchange rates remain relatively stable and predictable;- Cash transfer channels remain open and sufficient to handle the volume of payments; - Local markets continue to function with an adequate supply and diversity of food items. Risks:1. Access restrictions to geo area due to active fighting and/or shifting of frontlines would prevent the humanitarian actors to provide timely emergency relief to the affected population.2. Closure of the border for commercial shipments or not renewal of SC would have a very heavy negative impact on the cross-border activities for both NGOs and UN agencies as it would prevent the transhipment of the NFIs across the border from Turkey into Syria.3. Tension between host community and IDPs due to lack of context/needs understanding or poor communication and/or tension between included and excluded IDPs might lead to conflict between target groups and complaints against the humanitarian organizations.
