Project Summary

The already high needs of critical child protection services in South Sudan has been exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic, protracted displacement, and limited access to basic services which continues to significantly affect the physical, mental, and social well-being of children, adolescents and caregivers. This situation has been worsened by the limited availability of mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) services and the low level of core child protection services, including family tracing and reunification, case management, and reintegration services for children affected by armed forces and groups, leaving many children untreated and unable to access the needed services. Climate shocks, severe drought, massive flooding, and conflicts have contributed to the increase in GBV risks in the country which requires to strengthen GBV case management, psychosocial support, legal service, livelihood, economic support, GBV referral pathways and to integrate GBV into other sectoral responses. The high contamination with landmines and explosive remnants of war (ERW), especially in the Greater Equatoria region, along major supply routes and areas for returnees from Uganda, and the increase in expected returns require to prevent, mitigate and respond to mine risk issues through enhanced preparedness and resilience.
This proposed integrated CP, GBV and EORE project is in line with the Protection Cluster Objectives and will ensure the provision of critical protection-related assistance and specialized services and the prevention and mitigation of Protection risks through enhanced preparedness. The project targets to reach an average of 60,000 (20,000 women, 20,000 men, 10,000 girls, 10,000 men) (CP), 50,000 (20,000 women, 10,000 men, 10,000 girls, 10,000 boys) (GBV) and 20,000 (5000 men, 5000 women, 5000 girls, 5000 boys) (MA) including people with disabilities.
Using the 2023 Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP), World Vision International South Sudan (WVI-SS) aims to reach the most vulnerable populations in 11 counties in Warrap, Upper Nile, Western Equatoria and Central Equatoria States with improved access to quality lifesaving child protection for vulnerable girls, boys, men and women including children with disabilities who are at risk of mortality and morbidity and protection incidents/threats. Through the provision of specialized / lifesaving child protection and multi sectoral services WVI-SS seeks to reduce the suffering of boys, girls, women and men including children with disabilities who are at risk or experience violence, abuse, exploitation and neglect including GBV.
The proposed project will reduce the suffering of GBV survivors including women, men, girls, boys and people with disabilities by providing multi-sectoral specialized GBV response services. Furthermore, WVI-SS seeks to ensure that vulnerable women, men, girls, boys including people with disabilities have improved access to the provision of GBV prevention and risk mitigation interventions. Through protection monitoring, advocacy, awareness-raising and prevention and response services the project aims to reduce the vulnerability of women, men, girls and boys including people with disabilities at increased risk of mortality and morbidity and protection incidents/threats in priority areas.
In line with the Core Humanitarian Standards, WVI-SS will maintain its commitment to engage with affected communities at all phases of the project cycle, through focus group discussions with women, men, boys and girls. Establishing/strengthening local structures such as Project Management Committee is one example of how WVI-SS engages the community in a sustainable and accountable manner to determine and support appropriate need-based responses. WVI's Quality Assurance framework ensures that each project implemented is carried out effectively and continually reviewed in line with community needs and in line with other humanitarian frameworks.

1 - Needs

More than 3.5 mio people (2.9 million children and adolescents), including those with disabilities, are at heightened risk of violence, abuse, and exploitation. CP needs are high in severity in nine counties including Tambura and Ezo (severity 4). This situation significantly affects the well-being of children, adolescents and caregivers. According to the CPIMS+, the number of documented CP cases including UASC increased from 26,009 (12,128g, 13,881b) in 2020 to 30,441 (14,185g, 16,256b) by the end of 2021, with Upper Nile as one of the States having the highest number of documented CP cases. An assessment (Apr-Aug 2021) in Western Equatoria State (WES) shows that in 69%/43% of the assessed settlements early marriage was the main protection concern for girls/boys. An UNMISS HRD's investigation in 2021 documented that in Tambura increased violence saw over 440 people killed (48 children). A protection assessment (Aug 2021) show that family separation was reported as main protection concern in 21-40%/1-20% of assessed settlements in Tonj East (severity 3)/Juba (severity 3). The Country Task Force on Monitoring and Reporting on grave child rights violations verified 196 grave violations including the recruitment/use of 129 children (5g) in 2021, compared to 62 children (1g) in 2020. In addition, according to the GBVIMS, of the 6,237 GBV incidents reported between Jan-Sept 2021, 28% of the cases are child survivors under the age of 18. An estimated 2.6 mio people are at risk facing GBV in 2022, which represents a 25% increase compared to 2021. GBV needs are highest in 13 counties including Tonj East and Twic (severity 5). Tonj North, Tambura, Ezo, Yambio, Fashoda, Melut and Renk are classified with severity 4 and Juba with severity 3. 48% of HHs surveyed expressed fear/concern about GBV with IDPs (57%) expressed greater concern/fear about GBV, specifically rape and forced marriage. An assessment (Apr-Aug 2021) in WES indicates that in 54%/15% of the assessed settlements SGBV was the main protection concern for women/girls. In Tambura women, men, girls and boys are at risk of conflict related GBV as an UNMISS report (Jan-Dec 2021) indicates that at least 64 individuals (53 w, 7 m, 4 g) were subjected to sexual violence, including rape and sexual slavery and at least 74 civilians (52 m, 9 w, 13 children) were abducted; men and boys were forcibly recruited to take part in the hostilities. A protection assessment (Aug 2021) shows that KIs in 61-80%/41-60%/21-40% of assessed settlements in Juba/Yambio and Ezo/Nzara reported SGBV as main protection concern for women and/or girls. An assessment (Apr-Aug 2021) in Central Equatoria State (CES) suggest that conflict-related SGBV remained as main safety concern reported in 33%/13% of assessed settlements for women/girls. The 2021 analysis of GBV survivors revealed that 97% of survivors were women/girls. The FSMNS+HNOi survey reveals that only 6% of the community members are aware of GBV response services available. There is need to strengthen GBV response by multi-sectoral service providers in the referral pathway. Over 18 mio m² of land is suspected of being contaminated with landmines and explosive remnants of war (ERW). The highest levels of contamination are located within Central Equatoria State with Juba classified as catastrophic (severity 5). Fashoda and Melut in Upper Nile State are classified with severity 3. Explosive hazards inhibit civilians from collecting water or firewood, cultivating land, attending school, and receiving health care. In 2022, more than 650,000 people are estimated to be at risk of injury or death from landmines and ERW. An estimated 327,000 children are affected by MA challenges and 215,000 IDPs and Returnees are among MA people in need. The high contamination requires clearance of landmines and ERW to ensure the release of land and other infrastructure in order to reduce tensions around natural resources and free up land for cultivation.

2 - Project activities

For Child Protection the project will focus on the core Child Protection Area of Responsibility (CPAoR) interventions that include; CP Awareness raising; Establishment of CP referral pathways; Roll out of structured MHPSS to child survivors and vulnerable children; Referrals for specialized MHPSS services through established CP case management referral pathways with service providers in the area of operation; Adapting programs per evolving situation to support caregivers, life skills training and recreational activities, Integration of CP with other sectors to optimize child protection outcome and response through coordinated activities; Strong collaboration and coordination with GBV AoR to address increasing needs of child survivors of SGBV; Conduct awareness raising on prevalent child protection concerns in the communities; door to door visits; campaigns and dialogue engagements through Community Based Child Protection Structures; Establishment and operation of integrated Child Help Desks (CHDs) at service points such as schools or health centers, situation and response monitoring; and the use of different communication platforms and IEC materials to maintain the information among the masses to promote resilience and reduce negative coping strategies; Strengthening Community-based child protection, institutional capacity of national NGOs, frontline child protection workers, and community volunteers to expand coverage in under-served areas and improve the quality-of-service provision; Support to UASC by facilitating Family Tracing and Renunciation for Unaccompanied and Separated Children. With the high prevalence of GBV cases as a result of rape, physical assault, sexual abuse, forced and early marriage and risks from routine activities for women and girls as well as killings, abductions, and forced recruitment of men and boys, WV will implement a comprehensive GBV response and focus on key GBV AoR interventions which include: Community engagement through awareness-raising activities on risk factors to GBV, available GBV response services, and seeking of GBV response services within 72 hours of the incidents happening; Integration of GBV risk mitigation and survivor support across different all sectors in World Vision’s projects; Distribution of dignity kits to girls and women of reproductive age; Conducting safety audits and providing livelihood and economic support to vulnerable women and girls; Providing psychosocial support, and GBV case management services to survivors; Establishing and strengthening GBV referral systems with service providers in the area of operation; Implementing Women and Girls Friendly Space programming; Providing capacity building training to frontline service providers are the other response priorities; Providing legal services, livelihood, and economic support, including Cash and Voucher Assistance. The project will prevent, mitigate and respond to Mine Risk issues through enhanced preparedness and resilience. Therefore, WVI-SS will focus on the following key activities for Explosive Ordnance Risk Education (EORE) as stipulated in EORE AOR: Provision of Explosive Ordnance Risk Education; Training and accreditation of EORE Teams; Support integration of EORE into schools and CFS; Printing and/or Use and/or Distribution of EORE Materials; Reporting and referral - Quality Monitoring System; Advocacy for EORE in alignment with the MASC & South Sudan National Mine Action Strategy. The project takes note of the differences in needs amongthe different groups, for instance, physical protection and privacy are important for GBV, CP and MA prevention meaningful access is critical for disabled persons. Informationregarding specific needs for the different groups of people will be obtained through needs analysis conducted using focus group discussions and key informant interviews.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

The project will assess whether those at risk of GBV can safely access markets to spend their cash transfers or vouchers.It will also prioritize advocating for free access to GBV-related services like health or legal services; if this is not possible, CVA to promote access to these markets can be considered.The seasonality of products available in the market as well as climate change like flooding that interfere with access to market is well articulated in the disaster risk contingency plan.

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

Cash transfers are proven to meet humanitarian objectives for a range of outcomes and it is in line with the commitments made by OCHA and its partners regarding the systematic consideration of CVA in responses and use of Multipurpose Cash (MPC) for a multi-sector response including under the Grand Bargain and the DFID Payment by Results (PbR) framework.Cash and voucher is effective in a way that provides choice and flexibility which empowers survivors and vulnerable people affected by crisis.Cash transfers are generally more cost efficient to deliver than in-kind and every cash distributed will be spent in the local markets.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

WVI-SS will ensure that all staff are involved in the implementation of this project and will involve and inform the beneficiaries and other key stakeholders at all stages of the project management cycle starting from the initial assessment, design, implementation and close out stages. The project participants will also be sensitized on their rights to launch complaints, express grievances, and give other forms of feedback about the implementation process, quantity and quality of services they are getting from the project. Communities are asked which complaints and feedback mechanisms they prefer, with efforts to ensure that unique needs of vulnerable populations are met (for example, considering child- and gender-friendly methods of collecting feedback, accounting for literacy or individuals’ safety, protection, and access to mechanisms, etc.)Project information will reach community through holding meetings with local leaders, volunteers and local committee members. Community sensitization meetings will be carried as at the project launch and throughout in order for beneficiaries to understand their entitlements and roles to play.Establishment and operation of integrated Child Help Desks (CHDs) at service points such as schools, distribution points or health centers, situation and response monitoring; and the use of different communication platforms and IEC materials to maintain the information among the masses to promote resilience and reduce negative coping strategies.Project monitoring visits carried by WVI-SS team will enhance relationships with communities and the levels of interactions and community meetings will also offer opportunity for sharing information with beneficiaries and as well as responding to the complaints and feedback raised by the community.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

Complaints regarding any protection-related issue are handled by a Protection Officer to ensure confidentiality and to be able to determine an appropriate response to the feedback received, particularly in the event that such responses require CP or GBV intervention from the case management team. Any complaint involving sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) will be immediately escalated to the human resources team in Juba. All WVI-SS staff are trained on SEA and all have signed WVI ’s code of conduct. Cases of SEA will be investigated by a team led by WVI South Sudan’s human resources staff in collaboration with the Safeguarding Manager in a safe and confidential manner.Information about sexual exploitation and abuse will be shared in community meetings, community trainings and awareness sessions. They will be made to understand the vice and how to report.All frontline workers will be trained on safeguarding and behavior protocols of World Vision and commit to protect all children and adult beneficiaries from PSEA.The project will use contextualized IEC materials translated in culturally accepted terms in local languages and use visual translation for children and those that cannot read and write to understand the concept of PSEA.Feedback and response desk will be set up at every service point with contact details for reporting of PSEA.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Project participant feedback is compiled and shared with the project teams on a weekly basis. The feedback is then verified and responded to by the relevant teams. To accurately respond to feedback and complaints, an investigation is conducted on each feedback item submitted. Thus, after the team verifies the concerns raised, corrective measures are taken and incorporated into project implementation.The feedback gathered from beneficiaries will be included when conducting surveys, monitoring visits and post distribution monitoring to better understand how project can be improved.Feedback will be discussed at the project level as well as with the community and appropriate action agreed upon will be implemented to improve the project’s service delivery. This will entail either feedback good practice to be continued or adopted in other areas or a complaint on something that requires to stop or be improved.The feedback can also be used to improve internal policy of the organization through development and amendment of SOPs and conducts that protect the rights of beneficiaries.
