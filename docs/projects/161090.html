1 - Needs

Since the outbreak of conflict the December 2013, the Republic of South Sudan faces considerable humanitarian problems, which have increased substantially since the outbreak of violence across the country in December 2013. Among the counties affected include Magwi County of Eastern Equatoria State. In addition, a major crisis erupted in July 2016 and intensified in the Greater Equatoria region which further aggravated the humanitarian situation, producing secondary or third displacement with populations under stress lacking access to basic services and constraining humanitarian access.IPC acute food insecurity and acute malnutrition analysis August 2019 to April 2020 Issued: 11 September 2019 reported that an estimated 6.35 million people (54% of the population) are classified in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity, among whom an estimated 1.7 million people are facing Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity and 10,000 people are in Catastrophe (IPC phase 5). Compared to the same period in 2018, there is a slight reduction in the proportion of people facing Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity by an estimated 5%. However, high levels of acute food insecurity still persist in the country. acute food insecurity persists, driven by localized conflicts, climatic shocks, pests and diseases, slow recovery from prolonged asset depletion, poor macro-economic conditions and sub-optimal market functionality. The study showed that Nationally, 17.9 percent of children under age five are stunted, and 5.2 percent of children are severely stunted and Eastern Equatoria (25.1 percent). According to IPC Analysis August 2019 reports that Magwi county 74000 individuals are in stressed phase, 62000 in Crisis Phase and 12000 in emergency phase. As well the FSNMS Round 24 reports that in Magwi County House Hold Dietary Diverity stood at 51.41 (Medium)% and Food consumption 25.23% (poor). The IHO July 2018 assessment reported that 52% health facilities had no nutrition services, 40% had no staff trained in nutrition, 76% needed new latrines. Only 30% of the schools treated helminth infections among children. Only 2 schools screened children for malnutrition. One school reported visits from nearby health facilities to provide nutrition and health services like deworming. Only 3 schools had a kitchen garden and 3 schools provided meals to students. Only one school had least one teacher trained to implement school/nutrition programs, 60% of school had no latrine. Only 3 out of 5 functional clubs had its members trained on health, WASH and Nutrition issues. The FSNMS survey July 2019 reported children <5 years were malnourished for main causes of food insecurity at the household level include insecurity, short and erratic rainfall which results in crop failure, lack of food in the markets, lack of capacity to produce food or earn income to purchase food, lack of nutritional knowledge. Persistent insecurity and armed conflict disrupted livelihood activities, affected market functionality and limited physical access to markets. The current economic crisis has resulted in high food prices, local currency devaluation and hyperinflation, which in turn eroded household purchasing power. In addition, an anticipated earlier-than-normal start of the lean season in Magwi County will further hamper people’s food security, threatening the very survival of the most vulnerable. In order to prevent food insecurity and acute malnutrition from escalating to catastrophe levels in Magwi, IHO will ensure the availability of nutrition services to 21,196 Individuals (2021 SAM Children under Five (Boys 990, Girls 1031,), 9761 MAM children Under Five ( Boys 2305, Girls 2399), and 4704 MAM PLW and MIYCN (Male 1318 Female 1371) in Magwi through OTP and TSPF activities within twelve months of the year 2020.

2 - Activities

IHO will align the project in line the nutrition cluster 2020 and these will be as follows: To increase equitable access and utilization of quality preventative nutrition specific service delivery for children under five years’ adolescents and women IHO will:• Provide specialized food obtained through UNICEF and WFP to prevention of malnutrition • Prevent malnutrition through behavior change communication • Prevent micro nutrient deficienciesTo increase equitable access and utilization of quality lifesaving nutrition services for early detection and treatment of acute malnutrition for girls and boys under five years of age and pregnant and lactating women affected by acute malnutrition IHO will; • Conduct early detection and treatment of girls and boys under five years of age • Promote community participation for a responsive nutrition service delivery. In order to increase equitable access to nutrition sensitive interventions from health, WASH, Protection, FSL, Education and Protection sectors IHO will undertake. HEALTH• Prevention, testing and treating for malaria among children under five years SAM• Referral of SAM children with complication to SC for admission.• Establishment of kitchen gardens per nutrition sites which will act as demonstration to promote dietary diversity by using locally available foods.WASH• Provision of WASH kits to children with SAM in nutrition sites • Educate mothers and caregivers on proper sanitation and hygiene EDUCATION • Establish and Train Nutrition clubs in schools and train club Members• Undertake outreach sessions in schoolsECD• Establish safe spaces at Nutrition site for children 0-5 years, their primary caregivers and pregnant/lactating women• Train nutrition/health staff on Care for Child Development and similar types of packages• Provide developmentally appropriate play materials for health/nutrition spaces (i.e. ECD kit or similar) and relevant health/nutrition materials.• Integrate early stimulation and play in nutrition/health spaces where children and parents can use developmentally appropriate toys.GBV• Assess the level of participation and leadership of women, adolescent girls and other at-risk groups in all aspects of nutrition programming • Assess community perceptions, norms and practices linked to nutrition that may contribute to GBV (e.g. gender dynamics in food consumption; obstacles to nutritional assistance for at-risk groups; etc.)• Assess awareness of nutrition staff on basic issues related to gender, GBV, women’s/human rights, social exclusion and sexuality (including knowledge of where survivors can report risk and access care; linkages between nutrition programming and GBV risk reduction; etc.)• Prepare and provide training for nutrition staff and community nutrition groups on the safe design and implementation of nutrition programmes that mitigate the risk of GBV• Implement strategies that increase the safety, availability and accessibility of nutrition services for women, girls and other at-risk groups (e.g. locate services in safe areas; establish supplemental feeding schedules in collaboration with women, girls and other at-risk groups; consider the need to bring feeding supplements to GBV survivors and their children in safe shelters; etc.) • Implement proactive strategies to meet the GBV-related needs of those accessing nutrition services (e.g. consider including a GBV caseworker as part of the nutrition staff; organize informal support groups for women at feeding centres; etc.)• Undertake GBV awareness-raising including information on survivor rights (including to confidentiality at the service delivery and community levels), where to report risk and how to access care for GBV.• Organize informal peer empowerment and support groups for women and adolescent girls participating in nutrition programmes about issues of concern to them (e.g. childcare, reproductive health, domestic concerns, women’s/human rights, etc.).
