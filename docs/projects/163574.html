Project Summary

The project aims to create an environment in Libya where women and girls of all backgrounds are able to enjoy full access to their human rights, through preventing instances of GBV and improving immediate and longer-term service provision for survivors in Benghazi. In addition to contributing to the 2020 HRP priorities 1 and 2, the proposed action contributes to the GBV sub-sector Objectives 1 (Improve access to safe, timely, confidential and coordinated GBV services, provided according to a survivor-centered approach) and Objective 2 (Strengthen capacities of and increase coordination among service providers and local institutions in GBV response, prevention and risk mitigation).
Drawing upon its extensive experience in protection and support to civil society, ACTED will take a holistic approach to address the structural issues contributing to instances of GBV and contribute to a comprehensive humanitarian response for survivors in the East of Libya. Through providing trainings to local GBV actors on advocacy and service provision for survivors of GBV, establishing a local network of civil society organizations (CSOs) working on GBV, and implementing an advocacy campaign on GBV and discrimination against women and girls through its partner Amazzonat targeting the local community, businesses, and government, local actors will have strengthened capacities to prevent and respond to instances of GBV (Expected Result 1). Secondly, through establishing and supporting inclusive Women-and-Girl Friendly Spaces (WGFS) with integrated service packages in Benghazi, the access of survivors of GBV in Libya to immediate specialized support services is expected to improve (Expected Result 2). Finally, through establishing community-led socio-economic empowerment opportunities for women and girls, the proposed project will enhance the access of survivors to long-term socio-economic support and rehabilitation services (Expected Result 3).
This action offers the opportunity to complement and expand existing programmes and initiatives where ACTED has a demonstrated protection technical capacity and proven on-the-ground experience in protection in Benghazi. Notably, through ACTED’s protection programming in Benghazi supported by UNICEF, UNHCR, SDC, ACTED has established processes and tools in place in relation to the majority of proposed activities, notably protection monitoring, referrals, awareness-raising activities, tailored cash and NFI assistance to female headed households, including survivors of IPV and specialized trainings. Notably, synergies will be sought between this project and ACTED’s existing protection referral pathway in Benghazi. Furthermore, the activity aimed at establishing community-led women and girls’ socio-economic empowerment opportunities will build off of ACTED’s extensive experience fostering inclusion and sustainable growth in Libya.
ACTED is also an active member of the GBV Sub-Sector Working Group and the Child Protection Working Group, as well as a member of the Protection Sector Strategic Advisory Group. Through its ongoing protection programming, ACTED has established strong linkages with other local CSOs in Benghazi, and supports them on an ongoing basis to gain more traction within the humanitarian coordination system in Libya. ACTED will build on existing GBV sector initiatives such as the GBV pocket guide, and will draw upon sectoral tools and guidance to implement trainings to ensure a harmonized approach with other GBV partners.

1 - Needs

The ongoing-armed conflict and general insecurity across Libya puts populations at high risk of gender-based violence (GBV). Approximately 40% of respondents in a 2018 assessment in Libya indicated GBV was ‘very common’, with women, girls and boys identified as the populations most at risk (UNFPA, 2018). According to a 2017 UNFPA study, the most common types of violence apparent in Libyan society include physical assault (31%), forced marriage (31%), denial of opportunities (27%), and sexual violence (14%) (UNFPA, 2017). The Libyan legal framework fails to provide adequate protection for women. Loopholes prevail which effectively remove the minimum age threshold for legal marriage (through the permission of a guardian), facilitating early and forced marriage. Furthermore, Libya’s ‘Zina legislation’ which criminalizes all extra-marital sexual relations, encourages the marriage between perpetrators of rape and female victims to protect the victims from social stigma and marginalization (CEDAW, 2009).Domestic violence also remains a key issue, with a lack of laws in Libya that specifically criminalize domestic violence. Although a large majority of both men (75%) and women (85%) believe that a man should be punished for making his wife engage in unwanted sexual activity with him, no respondents mentioned that the local authorities should prosecute the perpetrator of violence.Survivors of sexual and gender-based violence face significant difficulties in accessing adequate GBV services, reporting crimes, and accessing justice (NRC, 2019). Few essential services exist for survivors of GBV across the country, with service gaps in terms of geographic coverage and scope including the broad lack of referral pathways for the clinical management of rape, mental health and psychosocial support services, case management, community outreach, legal aid, and livelihood and rehabilitation opportunities for survivors of GBV in Libya. Despite the emerging civil society in Libya and the commitment of many international and local organizations to ending sexual and gender-based violence, the response to GBV remains limited. Underreporting of GBV incidents is also widespread, due to a wide range of factors, including social barriers. In an UNFPA GBV situational analysis in Libya in 2017, all FGD respondents state that survivors of sexual violence would usually not report it to anyone for fear of how society would view them. This culture of impunity and silence raises stigmatization of survivors and discourages them to come forward out of cultural, social, or even legal reprisals. Failure to report GBV further stems from the mandatory reporting of sexual violence cases by medical practioners to authorities prior to the provision of medical services, going against survivors’ wishes and rights (GBV Sub-Sector Mid-Year Update, 2019). Survivors risk being prosecuted for engaging in sexual relations outside marriage, discouraging them from seeking recourse through formal or informal justice mechanisms in the first place. Notably, in the UNFPA study, girls’ fear of being blamed and the stigma attached with lost virginity was mentioned as the largest cause of underreporting for this population group. Survivors even fear that reporting instances of GBV will lead to retaliation such as threats, kidnapping, physical assaults, or even death by honor killings (with penal leniency for men who kill females in their families who are suspected of engaging in extra-marital sexual relations receiving reduced sentences).Displaced populations, including IDPs, migrant, refugee, and asylum seeker girls and women face additional challenges and risks. Child marriage has already been reported for girls as a negative coping mechanism, specifically for IDP families who are in vulnerable and fragile economic situations (NRC, 2019).

2 - Activities

Firstly, the project will reinforce the capacities of local CSOs and service providers to prevent and respond to instances of GBV. The local CSOs and GBV service providers in the East of Libya will be provided with capacity-building trainings in both advocacy as well as GBV service provision and referrals. Following capacity-building trainings, as well as the establishment of the local CSO network in Benghazi, ACTED will implement a year-long advocacy campaign through its local partner Amazzonat and the established network of local actors. Advocacy topics identified by a range of women’s rights activists and service providers in ACTED’s GBV conference held in Benghazi in December 2018 as well as ACTED’s protection team on the ground, include: sexual violence, Intimate Partner Violence (IPV), prevention of Violence Against Children (VAC) in schools, early and forced marriage, emotional abuse, sexual harassment, gender-sensitive parenting, sexual and reproductive health, positive coping mechanisms, and legal rights for women and girls. The target population for the advocacy campaigns will be the broader community in Libya.Secondly, the project will also focus on improving the provision of immediate specialized support services for survivors of GBV in the East of Libya. A second inclusive Women-and-Girl Friendly Spaces (WGFS) with integrated service packages in Benghazi will be established. This comfortable and safe community center, with trained social workers managing the space, will provide a critical opportunity to reach the most vulnerable groups in Benghazi. Finally, community-led women and girls’ socio-economic empowerment opportunities will be put in place within the WGFS: a) the supervision of vocational training programs for women run by local CSOs and experts; b) start-up grants for women to start their own income-generating enterprises or social initiative; c) life skills sessions with women and adolescent girls, including survivors of GBV.

4 - Protection risk analysis

Risk 1: The Security Situation in Libya Prevents Access to Beneficiaries and Endangers Field Staff: As the security situation remains unpredictable in Libya, a sudden escalation of the conflict could limit the access to areas of implementation. Should the situation escalate, it is possible that direct implementation would no longer be possible without risk to staff or beneficiaries. Risk 2: Risk of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (SEA). The involvement of humanitarian workers in acts of SEA is a serious violation of the ‘do no harm’ principle, and humanitarian organizations’ obligation to protect populations affected by crises. Risk 3: Data Protection: Given the that these populations are already vulnerable as a result of GBV they faced, it is essential that ACTED put in place mechanisms of information management to ensure confidentiality. This applies even more to the populations that are in situation of migration, who might face a double stigma, as well as the burden of their status legality. Risk 4. ‘Do No Harm’ and the Well-Being of Survivors: Working with vulnerable populations implies that consent should be sought at each step of the case management process – from initial assessment through to service provision.Risk 5: Limited engagement by potential beneficiaries: GBV survivors might be afraid to participate in the activities for fear of reprisal if their participation (and status as a GBV survivor) was known within the local communities.

5 - Mitigation measures

R1. ACTED dedicated security staff continuously monitor conflict developments in order to anticipate access concerns and modify procedures in advance. ACTED has in place standard operating procedures designed to minimize the exposure of staff and beneficiaries to security threats. Should changes in conflict dynamics cause a deterioration of the security situation, threatening the safety of staff or beneficiaries, ACTED will potentially adapt the program.R2. ACTED’s CRM provides a mechanism for beneficiaries or community members to report any instances of SEA on the part of ACTED staff or partners. ACTED will ensure that the protection activities in WGFS are carried by an all-female staff. ACTED also has a Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse policy that all staff have to abide by, and any violation of this policy results in immediate termination of staff. R3. The project requires robust data protection. Data will be password-protected and all data shared will have all personal information redacted in order to ensure the data protection of complaints. The linkage with the referral system will consider full respect for safe reporting systems, guaranteed confidentiality and safe storage of records. Training will be provided to staff on steps to follow when receiving disclosures in group settings or doing public activities. R4. ACTED will utilize survivor consent form for all GBV cases that it manages. Should ACTED wish to interview any GBV survivors to evaluate the impact of the trainings and network on service provision, consent will be sought from all individuals. The potential impact of each activity will be carefully assessed to ensure the application of the “do not harm” principle in each phase of the project implementation.R5: Through awareness raising sessions on GBV services, ACTED will inform women and girls of its data protection and confidentiality policies to ensure that they feel comfortable accessing services.

6.1 - Other accountability mechanisms

ACTED’s Complaints Response Mechanism (CRM) will be a key means of ensuring accountability towards beneficiaries and external stakeholders. The CRM is managed by AME staff to ensure independence from program teams and for which confidentiality is a fundamental principle. Complaints are received via a dedicated CRM phoneline, WhatsApp, Viber and email. Complaints are also received in the field by AME and program staff to be recorded and transferred to the CRM confidentially. All complaints are registered and recorded by the CRM Focal Point in a password-protected database and categorized based on the level of sensitivity. The CRM Focal Point leads to resolve complaints raised, with the involvement of program, AME or senior management staff, depending on the case and its severity. Information is shared on a strictly need-to-know basis and restricted as much as possible. The CRM Focal Point responds to all individuals who have made a complaint to inform them of the outcome of their complaint, within 15 days or sooner. ACTED ensured that all beneficiaries are made aware of the CRM process and that they could raise any issues and activities are planned to disseminate the CRM information to all project beneficiaries and participants. Within this project, ACTED will actively work to ensure that the CRM is able to be accessed by any community member regardless of their level of literacy, to ensure that marginalized groups can access it—including men, women, boys and girls of all ages and backgrounds. This served as an active line of communication for ACTED to receive any grievances from community members regarding the implementation of the project activities or any suggestions for changes. These calls therefore directly translate into recommendations to programs for improvement of programming, and the resolution of such complaints communicated directly back to the beneficiaries.
