Project Summary

Since the crisis escalation in late 2017, the North-West and South-West regions (NWSW) have experienced a drastic deterioration of living conditions, putting children and women at high risk of various forms of abuses, exploitation and violence.
Girls and boys are the primary victims of the current crisis. This translates into serious violations of their fundamental rights and constant insecurity, causing both visible and invisible trauma in the short and long-term, separation from their families and limited access to basic social services such as education, for the past three years. Being direct or indirect witnesses of violence linked to the conflict, their wellbeing, both physical and psychological, is fundamentally at stake.
As of September 2020, the crisis in the NWSW has caused the displacement of an estimated 200,000 children within those two regions, and an additional 155,794 children are estimated to be displaced in West, Adamaoua, Littoral and Centre regions. Adolescent boys and girls, children with specific needs and unaccompanied and separated children (UASC) remain the primary categories at risk among all affected groups of IDPs, returnees and affected host communities, especially in rural areas and hard-to-reach areas. Lack of access to basic social services including child protection services remains a key risk factor especially for children in rural areas, hard to reach areas and most remote localities such as the ones along the Nigerian border where access and insecurity prevent protection actors from implementing any program.
In order to protect children affected by armed conflict from violence, abuse and exploitation, as a priority and main intervention, UNICEF will work on the offer of services and provide psychosocial support to 340,000 children and caregivers. Psychosocial support will be provided to caregivers as well when necessary and to the extent possible so that they are better equipped to protect their children.
As a direct corollary, based on previous years trends, an estimated 10,200 unaccompanied and separated children (UASC) are foreseen to be identified during the implementation of the project. All the 10,200 children will benefit from case management, appropriate alternative care arrangements, support and individual follow-up while due to the difficult access and insecurity UNICEF plans to reunify a minimum of 990 unaccompanied or separated children with their family.
UNICEF with its implementing partners will run sensitization campaigns with the aim to reach out to 169,000 persons, with specific attention to parents to pass on messages on child protection in emergencies, trauma early warning signs for children affected by armed conflict, effects of family separation, GBV in emergencies and other types of violence children are at risk of at the community level so that parents and community members can play an active role in child protection in emergencies.
UNICEF and its partners will also provide support to an estimated 1,200 conflict-affected children who do not have a birth certificate, as they are particularly vulnerable to various forms of discrimination and abuse because of the lack of birth certificates.
In urban and peri-urban areas, both in NWSW and outside where IDP children and families from NW/SW are present, support will be provided to 2,700 families identified as at-risk to protect children from child labour and exploitation. Support will be provided to children already engaged in child labour (including WFCL, such as sexual exploitation and recruitment by armed groups and armed forces) with case management and referral to multisectoral services.
UNICEF as lead of the Child Protection Area of Responsibility will continue to consolidate the coordination, build capacities of members and ensure alignment and complementarity of interventions in the field of child protection.

1 - Besoins / Needs

As of September 2020, the crisis in the North West and South West regions has caused the displacement of an estimated 200,000 children within those two regions. An additional 230,246 children are estimated to be displaced in West, Littoral and Centre regions (MSNA, August 2020). Adolescent boys and girls, children with specific needs and unaccompanied and separated children (UASC) remain the primary categories at risk among all affected groups of IDPs, returnees and affected host communities, especially in rural areas and hard-to-reach areas. Lack of access to basic social services including CP services remains a key risk factors especially for children in rural areas and most remote localities such as the ones along the Nigerian border where access and insecurity prevent protection actors to implement any program. Physical violence and killing of children are reported. Most importantly, trauma and the psychological impact of the violence is directly affecting their emotional and cognitive development and remains one of the highest priority needs in 2020. Specific data pertaining to children living with disability is not available, but they remain one of the most vulnerable categories at risk due to the disruption of basic social services.Among displaced children, a growing number of UASC has been reported in 2020 (5,816 unaccompanied children and 16,240 separated children recorded against 2,500 UASC identified in 2019) with little alternative care solutions in place. As a consequence some of these children end up being involved in some of the worst forms of child labor or survival sex to have access to food and shelter. The prolonged closure of the vast majority of education facilities has had dramatic consequences on children’s well-being, sometimes resulting in exploitation, drug abuse, physical abuse and, at times, reported use and recruitment of children by NSAGs. Lack/loss of birth certificates as a result of displacement and destruction of homes is another key limiting factor for children to attend education facilities when security situation makes it possible and also constitutes an obstacle to benefitting from the protection services they are entitled to as minors. Children leaving NSAGs receive no support for their reintegration, are stigmatized and risk being arbitrarily killed. Adolescent boys are also more likely to be victims of arbitrary arrests, forced recruitment and extrajudicial executions and are at greater risk of injury or death directly related to the crisis. As a result, some families limit their sons' movements to eliminate the risk of their children being killed or forcibly recruited. The phenomenon of recruitment and use of children by NSAGs is difficult to measure but rather than forced recruitment, the reported growing interest of young boys (and girls to a lower extent) in joining NSAGs as they continue to be deprived of learning opportunities, is worrisome. Children are exposed to SGBV as a direct consequence of the violence but also as a negative coping mechanism from families forcing their children (especially girls) into child marriage. The pre-existing discriminatory gender norms and practices disadvantaging girls; combined with the weakening of family and community support networks, leave girls and teenage mothers in a vulnerable situation. Girls living with disability are five times more exposed to sexual violence in the ongoing crisis. Due to lack of documentation, fear of forced recruitment, harassment and arrest of boys and young men, parents often send girls to collect humanitarian assistance or to fulfill their traditional roles by collecting basic items for the household, exposing them to further risk of sexual violence as they are on the move. Absence of functioning health facilities in rural areas, lack of sexual and reproductive health programs and challenges in sharing efficiently information on existing services and referral pathways are aggravating factors.
