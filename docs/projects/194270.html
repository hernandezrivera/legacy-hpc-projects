Project Summary

Food for the Hungry (FH), has been working on community development and humanitarian response projects and programs in South Sudan since 2001. We established a field office in Malakal in 2004 to support our work in the Upper Nile and northern Jonglei States. FH is proposing to implement a education program focusing on education and water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) needs in Longichuk, Ulang, Penyikang, Cana/Piji, and Fangak Counties. The activities will leverage our presence in Upper Nile and Jonglei states implementing similar activities like Girls Education South Sudan (GESS) supporting IRNA and strengthening linkages between the state and county governments.
In this Sector, FH will focus on the following activities:
• Establish five and renovate ten inclusive temporary learning spaces (TLS) that are accessible to children with disabilities;
• Improve the quality of education by building the capacity of 150 teachers (100M/50F) and 195 (150M/45F) school managers;
• Build the resilience of the communities by training education managers (County Education Directors _CEDs, Payam Education Supervisors (PES), Schools Management Committee (SMC) members and Parents Teachers Associations (PTA members) on school governance and management. This will include modules on monitoring the enrollment and attendance, especially disadvantaged groups like girls and children with disabilities as well as building their capacity to identify, support, and refer children who need protection, psychosocial support, and trauma healing;
For critical inclusion of WASH, into the project, these activities will be implemented:
• Improve inclusive WASH facilities in 15 schools that takes care of the needs of CWDs;
• Build the resilience of the communities by training education managers (by establishing/renovating gender-segregated latrines in 15 schools;
• Initiate schools hygiene clubs that will sensitize schools and the school communities) on proper hygiene practices and COVID 19 protocols;
• Through the school hygiene clubs in the target Counties, conduct outreach activities to the communities on proper hygiene practices and COVID 19 protocol.
• To monitor protection cases, FH will establish Child Help Desks in each of targeted schools and will work closely with the Protection and Gender-Based Violence (GBV) Clusters for any referral of cases that need
• In Upper Nile State, FH will provide coordination support to the Education partners including, creating linkages between the Sub-Cluster, State Ministry of Education, National Cluster and the partners, organizing partners to participate in the IRNA as well as organizing and leading Education Sub-Cluster meetings.

1 - Needs

According to South Sudan Key Context Updates 2-8 March 2022, the number of people in need of humanitarian and protection assistance is 7 percent more in 2022 compared to yea. Partners including FH conducted multi-sectoral Initial rapid Needs assessments (IRNA) from January 2021 to December 2021 to ascertain the impact of the flooding and determine priority needs in the greatly affected Counties of Upper Nile and Jonglei States. a). 135,163 people were affected with 91% of them displaced. 27 schools suffered physical infrastructure damage, including the destruction of both teaching and learning materials, and learning spaces for 6,034 children (56% of them girls). The Girls Education South Sudan WASH Facilities Assessment of Schools in South Sudan 2021 indicated that only 60 percent of schools assessed have toilets and of those assessed only 33 percent have functional hand washing facilities and 19 percent of the girl's toilets have access to menstrual hygiene facilities. Education Needs Assessment South Sudan 2021 found that from March 2020 to May 2021, the most commonly reported reasons for schools' closure were flooding, lack of support /systems in place to support reopening of schools after COVID 19 and looting by armed groups. Teachers fleeing, students fleeing and insecurity around the school account for some of the main reasons for schools' closure in the remaining months of 2021. The flooding and insecurity have caused massive displacement of households and schoolchildren. An IRNA conducted in 4 out of 8 Payam of Ulang County in November 2022 established that 22 school building including the WASH facilities have been submerged, school materials were destroyed and schools in dry areas were occupied by floods affected IDPs. As a result, 8,500 leaners in Ulang were reported out of school unless Temporary learning spaces or tents are established, and school materials are provided to the affected schools. The conflict in Panyikang County in August through September 2022 led to displacement of the Panyikang community to Malakal PoC. As a result, 6 schools in the Malakal PoC are occupied by the IDPs. Data collected by education cluster members in Malakal PoC through school visits and taking note of enrolment figures indicate that there are 24 out of 34 schools from Panyikang with a total enrolment of 7,054 (3957M 3097F) have reopened in the Malakal PoC operating triple shifts (Morning, Afternoon and Evening) in a crowded classis. Ther e is therefore urgent need to establish more learning spaces, training teachers on EiE methodology and Mental Health and Psychosocial support and support the teachers with incentives since the government does not paid salaries for teachers in the PoC. The conflict in Panyikang reached Pigi/Canal and Fanagak but no INRA has been conducted in the area, but people were displaced even though they have started coming back following the relative calm. As a result, the education system is faced with; Inadequate learning spaces and WASH facilities for IDP children in areas where schools were destroyed/damaged by flooding and or armed conflicts, Inadequate teaching and learning materials and hygiene supplies to facilitate quality and safe learning and learning is supported by demotivated volunteer teachers who have limited or no teaching skills and skills to appropriately deal with children experiencing psychological stress. Children with disabilities suffer double as they struggle to access learning spaces. Unpublished pregnancy's mapping assessment report by GESS2 indicated that a number of schoolgirls got married especially during the COVID 19 period.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

After that, a series of community mobilization and sensitization meetings will be conducted at the start of the project with the community to ensure everyone has enough information about the project including, objectives, targeted population, caseloads, and the role of different community actors in the project. FH has already identified organizations working with people with disabilities and organizations of people with disabilities in the State. FH will work closely with these organizations in the identification of vulnerable children like children with disabilities and out of school children, designing the implementation process and monitoring the implementation of activities. At the school level, FH will hold quarterly meetings with the SMCs members to plan, implement and monitor each school’s activities. FH has an established complaint and feedback mechanism (CFM), which is part of its accountability system that ensures target populations have access to safe and reliable reporting mechanisms. The CFM includes establishing a properly constituted help desk at every community meeting, suggestion boxes at each school. Reporting details are displayed during SMC meetings and schools across the counties. FH also encourages the community to speak with FH staff on the ground or senior management staff when they visit project sites regarding any complaints they might have towards project staff or the community. When senior project staff visit the project areas they have confidential focused groups discussion (FGDs) with separate groups of school children including girls and boys, men, women, the elderly and CWD and get any complaints and feedback directly. All complaints are treated with confidence and all FH staff must act.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Inception meetings will be held at the Boma, Payam and county levels with key stakeholders like government and community representatives, traditional leadership, village heads, children, youth, elderly, women and PWD representatives. After that, a series of community mobilization and sensitization meetings will be conducted at the start of the project with the community to ensure everyone has enough information about the project including, objectives, targeted population, caseloads, and the role of different community actors in the project. During project activities like training, FH will display banners showing beneficiary entitlements. At the school level, FH will hold quarterly meetings with the School Management Committee (SMC) members to plan implement and monitor each school’s activities.FH has an established complaint and feedback mechanism (CFM), which is part of its accountability system that ensures target populations have access to safe and reliable reporting mechanisms. The CFM includes establishing a properly constituted help desk at every community meeting, suggestion boxes at strategic locations in the county identified together with the community, anonymous complaints email option, an international toll-free number, and a local reverse charged mobile number with a WhatsApp option that is managed by the Human Resources Manager at Juba Level. A local accountability committee (LAC) with representation of women, elderly, PWD, Host, IDP and Returnees, will be established to continuously interact with the project and capture their feedback. The project cycle details will be simplified and communicated clearly to the LAC along with timelines. There will be periodic meetings held with the LAC to discuss the implementation process, challenges/gaps and possible solutions. These reporting details are displayed during all community meetings and at public places and schools across the counties. FH also encourages the community to speak with FH staff on the ground or senior management staff when they visit project sites regarding any complaints they might have towards project staff or the community. When senior project staff visit the project areas they will have confidential Focused Groups Discussion (FGDs) with separate groups of schoolchildren including girls and boys, men, women, the elderly and PWD and get any complaints and feedback directly. All complaints are treated with confidence and all FH staff must act accordingly with due diligence on any complaint raised to them by the community. In the school, children will be trained on how to report suspected child protection cases anonymously through the suggestion boxes placed in each of the 15 schools.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

FH has a robust policy on protection against sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA) to protect target communities by raising awareness on the policy and how it applies and providing reporting mechanisms within the target communities. FH will take time to explain its PSEA policy, emphasizing the right of the community to report any SEA cases and the available reporting mechanisms during any project related gatherings. FH will also produce banners in English and in local languages, with basic information on its PSEA policy and available reporting options. The banners will be displaced at all community meetings, at the gate of the respective FH field offices and in public spaces and schools. FH will strengthen existing or encourage schools to establish child protection clubs in each of the targeted schools. Through this proposed project, FH will reinforce training on reporting of suspected SEA cases through the toll free phone numbers and the suggestions boxes. The SMC members will receive a refresher training on PSEA and the available referral pathways. FH will work closely with protection clusters/partners to provide support and referral of PSEA victims.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

All complaints/feedback received will be logged on to a complaints/feedback log sheet. If the complaint/feedback is something that can be addressed by the supervisor at the field level, he/she has an obligation to adapt activities based on the feedback. At the school level, the complaints/feedback raised by learners will be discussed during monthly SMC meetings and addressed. In case the SMC body is not able to resolve the issues, they will be escalated to the Payam education supervisors and the PTA members for further advice. If complaints cannot be dealt with at the School level, they are escalated to the Payam Education Supervisor's level and then to the County Education Office level. The feedback loop is only closed when it signed off by a senior member of FH staff as having been satisfactorily resolved.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

Risk of interference from external stakeholder. FH will hold inception meetings with all stakeholders to ensure all are aware of project objectives, target and targeting criteria. FH will use a community participatory process to refine targeting criteria to be context specific and acceptable to all stakeholders. FH will ensure continuous awareness raising of its Community Feedback Mechanism (CFM) and will react promptly to feedback raised through the CFM. Risk that marginalized groups, will not participate in project: FH will: • Conduct an induction training for all project staff, SMCs, PTAs, County and Payam Education authorities on inclusiveness especially for vulnerable groups like women, the elderly and people with disabilities. • Identify groups of PWD, organizations for PWDs, the elderly, youth, women and girls to inform them about the project, identify those interested, and collect data of PWD interested using the WGQ (for PWD); • Budget more time for activities to accommodate PWD and the elderly who may be slow in accomplishing tasks; Ensure activities begin with confidence building for PWD who may lack self-assurance due to prolonged stigma and exclusion;• Ensure the schools and latrines' for CWD and the elderly to access without risk of injury of fatigue; Arrange for school and family member support where needed to ensure the CWD can be supported during the activity in school and at home; • In consultation with organizations of PWD and the elderly sensitize school communities on how to care for vulnerable children on basic adaptation of the home environment to enable them to work at home; simple modifications may include raising garden beds; creating more space between garden beds to enable wheelchair movement; siting gardens close to water source and closer home; creating sitting/resting places around the garden.• Link CWD with organisations that provide appropriate assistive devices; School authorities trained on disability and inclusion will act as advocates to ensure schools’ infrastructure is inclusive for CWDs, teachers and family members.
