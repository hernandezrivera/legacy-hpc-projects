Project Summary

In line with the Protection Cluster Objectives 1 and 2 (ensure provision of critical protection-related assistance and specialized services; and prevent and mitigate protection risks through enhanced preparedness), the project seeks to (1) enhance access to comprehensive case management services for vulnerable girls and boys with protection concerns, including UASC, (2) provide vulnerable girls and boys, their caregivers and families, with quality age and gender-appropriate PSS, following community-based approaches and best practices, and (3) strengthen capacity of girls and boys, their caregiver, families, and communities, to prevent and respond to violence through systems and community-strengthening mechanisms – reflecting the Child Protection Sub Cluster’s overall strategy and objectives.
The project targets locations in Tier 1 (Juba) and Tier 2 (Yambio, Tambura, Melut, Fashoda, Renk, Gogrial West, Tonj North, Twic). A total of 60,000 children will benefit from individual, group, and specialized PSS in the project, including: 25,000 boys and 35,000 girls. More specifically, 250 children (125 girls, 125 boys) will receive FTR services. To support community structures and via awareness raising activities, 100,000 individuals will be engaged (30,000 boys and girls, each, and 20,000 men and women, each).
The project will include the following key interventions in all locations: comprehensive CP case management (identification & documentation of children with protection concerns, case management capacity building, FTR of UASC and placement in alternative care within framework of comprehensive case management, referrals to specialized services); provision of focused PSS for CAAFAG and other vulnerable children with protection concerns; capacity building of CP staff, frontline workers, teachers, government/local authorities in CPiE; strengthening of CBCPCS; Provision of structured community based PSS through CFS, peer support for adolescents and other relevant modalities; community awareness raising on CPiE / children’s rights, engagement of adolescents and youth, positive parenting, messaging and campaigns. Interventions will be informed by safety audits and safety perception mapping, to be conducted on an ongoing basis (and particularly as the changing context dictates); such tools will not be exclusive to protection (child protection and GBV) interventions, but mainstreamed across World Vision's interventions, particularly in Food Security and Livelihoods, Shelter / NFI, Health and Nutrition.
The project reflects the unique needs of people in need, committing a gender marker that sees significant contributions to gender equality (including across age groups), prioritizing the needs of disabled populations (reflective of strong protection mainstreaming), and accountability to targeted communities (engaging stakeholders and conducting broad consultations to inform interventions and ongoing monitoring. The project design prioritizes a Do No Harm approach with an explicit intention to address protection issues experienced by women, girls, boys and men, recognizing that respect for human rights and increasing gender equality are enablers of the South Sudan humanitarian response strategy.
Finally, World Vision maintains zero tolerance for sexual exploitation and abuse, and has a robust Child and Adult Safeguarding Policy and approach to realize this at a national level across South Sudan, and globally.

1 - Needs

The protection situation in South Sudan remains dire. The various risks and rights violations faced by girls and boys, include: family separation/UASC, CAAFAG, SGBV, psychosocial distress, child, early and forced marriage, cattle-related conflict and violence, abuse, exploitation, and denial of humanitarian access. Since 2013, the UN’s South Sudan CTFMR has received over 4,000 allegations of grave violations; between 2014 and 2018, 3,440 incidents have been verified, affecting 93,633 children (39,000 girls). Recruitment and use of children continues to be documented. While the UN HRC’s South Sudan report (March 2019) indicates that the recruitment and use of children in conflict has been on the decline, thousands remain (nearly 1,000 of the estimated 19,000 children have been released in 2018). In Western Equatoria state, rehabilitation and reintegration of former CAAFAG remains a challenge due to shortage of services and partners, lack of psychosocial resources, social and economic reintegration, and lack of education and employment opportunities. WV has a robust CAAFAG reintegration program in Yambio, supporting 752 children, with more children expected as releases continue. Girls in particular are at risk of sexual violence, early marriage, and early pregnancy (including as a result of recruitment). Per available 2019 GBVIMS data, nearly 20% of survivors of GBV are under the age of 18 (consistent with 2018). The UN’s HRC highlights that 52% of girls in South Sudan were married by the age of 18, particularly as economic and physical insecurity is common, and caregivers resort to this negative coping practice to ‘protect’ girls and keep them safe from harm. Early marriage is an obstacle working against the realization of women and girls’ equal rights, and has long lasting negative effects, including: early pregnancy (and related health complications & death), deterrence from education, and an increasing likelihood that she will experience GBV as she grows older; this is common across Western and Central Equatorias, Upper Nile, and Warrap, evident in WV’s existing case load across these areas. In Western Equatoria, WV has been working with child survivors of GBV, many of whom are young mothers, encouraging them to return to school, providing opportunity for peer support and social reintegration. Children’s psychosocial wellbeing and mental health are severely affected after witnessing violence, loss of family and friends, and difficulties in accessing basic services (food, water, health, etc.). Approximately one million children are in need of PSS as increasingly negative coping mechanisms are often observed among children who have been displaced for several years and continue to encounter the same or similar situations. WV’s experience with children in Upper Nile highlight many specific reasons why girls and boys experience psychosocial distress, including: lack of learning around MHM and sex education, lack of female teachers leading to fear among some girls, adolescent girls who enrol late in primary school are bullied, school dropout due to pregnancy is common, etc. WV assessments conducted in Melut (Sept 2019) indicate that greater efforts are required to engage with youth, particularly adolescent girls, who continue to be reserved and less active in centre activities. A focus on intergenerational activities and group PSS that strengthens self-esteem, confidence, and social cohesions among age groups and across sex is necessary. Children are at-risk of being separated from families, caregivers, and communities; there are over 8,226 active cases of UASC due to ongoing displacement and conflict. (CPSC 2019-2021 Strategy) As this is common across Western and Central Equatorias, Upper Nile, and Warrap, per WV’s existing caseload, the project intervention prioritizes a systems approach to CP prevention and response that strengthens individual capacity, families, and community supports.

2 - Activities

World Vision supports a systematic approach to CP prevention and response, strengthening systems and services for survivors of CP violations, at-risk and vulnerable groups (eg. CAAFAG, child survivors of GBV, UASC, those affected by psychosocial distress). Prevention, response and mitigation measures will be taken, working across the socio-ecological model of at-risk girls and boys, including: individual interventions (comprehensive case management, individual counselling and PSS), family/relational interventions (household and group PSS, training in parenting skills), and community interventions (CFS, community-based protection mechanisms, awareness raising). CPSC OBJ1 [Comprehensive Case Management, Capacity Building, Referral Pathways, link to PO1, SO1] Case Workers will provide individual case management services using a holistic, age-appropriate, gender-sensitive approach, that is reflective of the needs and actions determined by the child’s vulnerability (eg. CAAFAG, UASC, Survivor of GBV, Psychosocial Distress). Individual, group or family-based structured PSS will be provided from CFS, home or alternative location, as per the individual case plan. Case management, including UASC requiring FTR, will follow South Sudan’s SOPs. Training on comprehensive case management, including incidence of GBV, will be provided, in line with South Sudan’s National CP Case Management Toolkit and SOPs, and new Social Reintegration Guide for Female CAAFAG (UNICEF 2019). A focus on specific topics (eg. FTR, responding to child survivors of GBV, coaching and supervision for case supervisors) will be included. Service mapping and strengthening of referral pathways (including awareness raising that is age- and literacy-sensitive) will be completed, so survivors of CP violations and at-risk girls and boys have access to: PSS, medical, safety, and other basic services, done in the best interest of the child. CPSC OBJ2 [PSS, CFS, Community, link to PO1 and PO2, SO1] World Vision will provide structured and unstructured PSS activities at CFS. CFS will be supported by community animators (realizing gender parity) to ensure cultural sensitivities, acceptance, and community strengthening is prioritized. CFS will operate in line with CPSC SOPs, UNICEF PSS Manual, and the new Alliance CPMS (latter two released 2019). Further training and capacity building to support PSS activities will include: child-friendly approaches, child and human rights, PFA, and understanding of referral pathways. Level 1, 2, and 3 PSS services, and referrals to Level 4 will be provided. The project will ensure age-appropriate activities for children, adolescents and youth (6-10 years, 11-13 years, 14-17 years) for them to enjoy safe play, express and discuss their fears, emotions and ideas for the future, and develop positive social interaction skills with their peers. CPSC OBJ3 [Prevention, Community PSS, CFS, Community, link to PO2, SO2 and SO3] To support prevention and mitigation efforts, community-based solutions will be identified. Child Help Desks will be established, managed by CFS animators and members of the CBCPC. This will enable children who have feedback to celebrate or improve World Vision’s service delivery, or who have a protection concern and are looking for further services to communicate with centre staff and community representatives. CBCPCs will be made up of key stakeholders (eg. community leaders, PTA and School Mgmt. Committee representatives, child, youth, and women’s group representatives). Groups will be trained to: increase basic knowledge of child rights and protection issues; improve awareness of GBV; increase knowledge of CP and GBV reporting, referral pathways and services available; and, build their capacity to act as advocates for improved CP reporting and monitoring. Community-led awareness raising campaigns will be conducted. Various modalities will be used in awareness raising (pictures, drama, event, IEC materials, radio).

6.6 - Will beneficiaries' rights be legally recognised?

Training and awareness raising will include topics in child rights, specifically the Child Act 2008 and UN Convention on the Rights of the Child. The thoughtful dissemination, training, and capacity building on national law, policies, and procedures, will empower girls and boys, their families, caregivers, and communities with information, and to report where violations are observed / experienced.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

Safety Audits and Safety Perception Mapping will be conducted on an ongoing basis (using both observational and interview / focus group discussion tools to ensure that the voices and needs of communities are felt). Complaint, feedback, and response mechanisms are standard in all World Vision projects, and include mixed approaches, taking into account, age, gender, ability, literacy, etc., so all people can safely provide feedback and share perspectives. Specific to Child Protection, Child Help Desks will be established to ensure that girls and boys can provide feedback on services, but also self-refer / report should they need response services. Child Help Desks will be managed by CFS animators and CBCPC representatives who are trained in child-friendly interactions.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

Project works to strengthen and establish (where none exist) community-based child protection committees, that encompass representatives from various stakeholders and influential leaders (including, for example, community leaders, faith leader, women's rights organizations, and youth representatives, parent teacher associations, school management committees).

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

Project is aligned with the Ministry of Gender, Child, and Social Welfare's National Action Plan (2015-2020) on Resolution 1325 (Women, Peace, and Security), and specifically Objective 1: provide protection for women and girls, including those disabilities, against any form of SGBV, and restore the respect for human rights, human dignity, and equality in South Sudan. In addition, project is aligned to MoGCSW Strategy, which includes specifically a goal to "protect children from all forms of violence, exploitation, discrimination, and abuse", with related thematic areas in: gender quality, protection of children and their right.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

World Vision has a broad footprint in South Sudan, and is proposing specifically to maintain CP operations in existing areas and new areas where growth is possible (pending resource gathering), to grow CP operations in areas where World Vision has established presence, so that girls and boys in need (including their families, caregivers, and communities) have a comprehensive package of multi-sectoral life-saving interventions. As a community-based organization, World Vision has established community acceptance in the areas proposed, and applies ongoing context monitoring and crisis modification to respond to changes in the environment that would require hibernation or scaling back of activities, prioritizing the safety and protection of World Vision staff, volunteers, and communities.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

World Vision seeks to work with existing protection committees and structures, not duplicating any community-owned initiatives, but further strengthening what exists, where possible. Women's rights organizations, youth groups, and protection committees more broadly are fundamental stakeholders to support sustainable action. World Vision relies on the contextual knowledge and expertise of local community members with experience working with children, particularly when engaging with them in a volunteer / CFS animator capacity. This ensures that children are safe, understood, and well-cared for, but also, engages key community members and stakeholders as 'change agents' and influential leaders championing the rights and needs of children, contributing to overall sustainability. Sustainability is possible as project locations and interventions have been identified together with communities, for example, locations of CFS may be near schools or places of worship, where there is community buy-in and interest to see an intervention for children succeed and continue once World Vision transitions out. Community protection committees (made up for various local leaders and stakeholders) will be responsible for ongoing management, and supported throughout the project to understand how best to maintain the space.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

Community consultations are broad and include representatives across various age, sex, and vulnerabilities (ensuring gender sensitivity, safe child participation, disability inclusion, and various literacy modalities). Consultations with women’s group leaders, existing CPCBCs (male/female all ages), children (at CFS, through school clubs), vulnerable children participating in child protection services being provided by World Vision are prioritized. Examples: Use of Household Survey and Focus Group Discussions (mixed sex and age, disaggregated groups). Child participation techniques are explicitly used to garner feedback from children from age groups 0-6, 7-10, 11-14, 15-17 years, in formats that seek feedback separately from girls and boys, as well as together. While Key Informant Interviews and feedback is collected from local authorities, community leaders and faith leaders, the majority of influencing data and feedback is sourced from women, men, girls and boys within the community broadly. Information is provided to adult and child beneficiaries in age appropriate methods at the project start, through community engagement & routine monitoring, and at project end to discuss outcomes. Child Help Desks and Information Boards are used at Child Friendly Spaces / schools / health facilities.
