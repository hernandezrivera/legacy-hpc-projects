1 - Needs

Lack of individual refugee registration and documentation remains a key driver of refugee vulnerability in Sudan, especially for refugees living in out-of-camp settlements, as those without documentation are unable to access socio-economic and protection services where available, access formal and stable livelihoods opportunities, and face heighented protection risks such as arrest, detention and refoulment. Education needs remain high with about 67 per cent of primary-school aged refugee children remaining out-of-school and estimates of above 90 per cent being out-of-school in more remote refugee-hosting areas outside of camps among South Sudanese refugee children. According to the BANVA 2021, refugees in camp/camp-like situations reported higher health vulnerability than out-of-camp refugees. Refugees reported higher healthcare needs in the household compared to their host communities except for refugees in Khartoum and Central Darfur. On the same breath, refugees in Kassala and Khartoum spend a larger proportion of their monthly income on healthcare compared to host communities while both refugees and host communities in East Darfur allocate a large proportion of their household income to healthcare. According to BaNVA, only 22% of refugees live in shelters of acceptable condition, while the majority, 36%, live in substandard shelters, followed by 28% of refugees that live in shelters which are unsafe, and finally 14% of refugees that live in shelters that don't provide protection from the elements. Almost half of the refugee population resides in emergency/makeshift shelter typologies, followed by 39% of refugees that live in semi-permanent structures while only 12% of refugees live in permanent shelters. 66% of refugees reported having no documents that guarantee security of tenure. Amongst the most pressing NFI needs we have solar lamps that 75% of refugee population is missing and mosquito nets that 69% of refugees do not possess. In addition to the unfavourable external conditions, refugees continue to face additional challenges to access work permits, despite improvements on refugee eligibility to work. According to WFP's Food Security Monitoring System report for Q3 2021, 51 percent of refugee households were found to be food insecure with the highest prevalence of food insecurity affecting refugee households in Darfurs. Access to livelihood opportunities remains extremely limited for refugees in Sudan and in addition to the economic crisis, the economic vulnerability of the refugee households is the main factor contributing to their food insecurity. Based on the Basic Needs and Vulnerability Assessment for Refugees in Sudan, high WASH vulnerability is an issue for both refugees and host communities across all surveyed states, namely Gedaref, Sennar, Khartoum, North Darfur, Central Darfur and North Kordofan. While water supply quantities average 17 litres per person per day for South Sudanese refugees across the response, some areas remain well below response standards with just 8 litres per person per day, especially in Gedeid and Gereid in South Kordofan where refugees walk for over 5km in search of water, especially during the dry season. In Dimso, South Darfur, CAR refugees are living in settlements in Maramosa and Karakada towns where water distribution networks need to be rehabilitated to accommodate the needs of both refugees and host communities. For new Ethiopain refugee influx water distribution networks in Um Rakuba, Tunaydbah, Village 8 and Hamdayet need to be expanded to phase out from water trucking and accommodate the needs of both refugees and host communities. 52% per cent of in-camp and large camp-like settlement refugee households across all state hosting refugee still do not have access to household latrines while access to communal latrines is low and are poorly managed and dirty most of the time.

2 - Activities/Outputs

otection response will focus on supporting the Government of Sudan in implementation of the Comprehensive Refugee Response Framework and the soluition-oriented approach through alignment of the relevant national legislation with the international refugee and human rights law and inclusion of refugees in national systems, that would result in enhanced opportunities for local integration as well as improved protection services for refugees and asylum seekers, with particular focus on strengthening awareness of their rights and obligations under the relevant national laws, referral pathways, capacity building, mainstreaming an age, gender, diversity approach. Improved child protection case management with prioritization of UASC. In order to support self-reliance of refugees and subsequently their local integration in Sudan, enhanced efforts will be made to advocate with GOS through different forums to amend the current policy and legislative framework, particuarly in relation to the GOS's encampment policy, which imposes movement restirictions on refugees who wish to seek employment/livelihoods, education, health and other opportunities and services otuside of their designated areas of residence within the country. Resettlement remains to be a valuable protection tool addressing the specific needs of refugees but is available only to an extremely limited number of refugees who meet very specific criteria as per the quotas provided by resettlement countries, which are far below the current estimated needs. The overarching objective is to support GoS achieve their GRF pledge to integrate refugee education into national systems in a gradual manner and ensure that all refugees, IDPs and other Persons of Concern to UNHCR in Sudan have access to quality basic, secondary, and tertiary education. In addition to this, the Education Management Information System was rolled out across the country with the inclusion of a refugee questionnaire to collect data on refugee children. For refugee children scattered within host communities, UNICEF will take the leading role in coordinating with partners on community-based assistance in order to increase the enrolment of refugee children in host community schools. To mainstream refugee education, partners will advocate for the absorption of these and other refugee community schools into the national system so that provision of education across Sudan is by the GoS and staffing and oversight is undertaken by the MoE. Many schools suffer from severe shortage of teachers. For the 2022 CRP, the refugee health and nutrition sector focus on the following objectives: Comprehensive essential life-saving primary, secondary and tertiary health care services are provided to refugees. Effective prevention of undernutrition and micronutrient deficiencies including anaemia and stunting will be prioritized by gradually integrating refugees into the national nutrition strategy and thus ensure that refugees have access to food fortification, deworming, immunization, and vitamin A/micro-nutrient supplementations. The response plan will focus also on the reforestation of degraded forests surrounding the refugee settlements/camps, since the refugees continue depending on wood for cooking. Refugees receive a well balanced, culturally acceptable, basic food ration The first objective aims to food assistance to vulnerable refugees including new arrivals, children and pregnant and lactating women. Refugees in Sudan continue to face challenges to access work permits and support to refugees to access work permits remains a key priority. The refugee WASH sector will work closely with the refugee education sector in the areas of hygiene promotion and awareness as well as provision of WASH facilities at schools.

6.2 - Explain how your project aims to ensure protection mainstreaming during its design and implementation

UNHCR aims to strengthen protection, gender mainstreaming and accountability toaffected populations as they link with the principle of “do no harm”. The particularneeds of such individuals will be reflected in NFI assessments, programme design,implementation and monitoring and evaluation. In these areas, the sector will ensurethe following elements are taken into account: (1) safety, security, and dignity; (2)equitable access; (3) accountability through community-based feedback mechanisms,with information collected based on on-site and post-distribution monitoring that willfeed into the sector’s program cycle; and (4) sharing information, consultation,participation and community empowerment. The sector will work with partners tostrengthen the implementation of the prevention of sexual exploitation and abusethrough awareness-raising for both partners and suppliers. UNHCR within its capacityas Shelter/NFI Sector lead will advocate to draw attention to shelter issues such ashousing, land and property rights, particularly for returnees and/or integrated IDPs.HLP due diligence tools will be set in place with cooperation of the HLP WG toharmonize the shelter responses among partners ensuring that before building durableshelter partners will ensure in cooperation with the communities that beneficiaries’land if free of claim and refer those conflictive cases to protection partners providingICLA support through a referral pathway. This is to reduce the risk of further harm andmainstream protection on the shelter construction programs.
