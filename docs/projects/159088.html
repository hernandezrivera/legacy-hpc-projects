Project Summary

The WHO trauma and emergency care programme is an extensive series of interventions that aim to improve the management of traumatic injuries, from the post of injury, at the prehospital level all the way to hospital care, including emergency departments and surgery, to rehabilitation and recovery. By investing systematically in the health system, this approach aims to strengthen the system by building in resilience to be able to deal with future shocks and disasters as well. The broader set of interventions involve an array of humanitarian development nexus activities, but in this proposal, only the humanitarian elements have been presented. For 2020 HRP, WHO aims to (1) strengthen prehospital care by upgrading the centralised ambulance dispatch centres and cadre of paramedics to allow for a timely response to all acute cases in the opt through training of over 250 paramedics from PRCS and MoH (2) continue to provide some support to the 10 trauma stabilisation points through simulation exercises and small scale provision of supplies (top up supplies) in Gaza (3) Enhance the level of care at six key emergency departments in Gaza and up to four emergency departments in the West Bank through clinical, practical and theoretical coaching on mass casualty management (4) provide essential training to surgeons on acute surgery, with a particular attention to damage control surgery (5) provide essential services for specialised services such as limb reconstruction in Gaza; to be able to treat approximately 500 complex limb reconstruction patients per year (6) continue coordination efforts in the trauma working group in Gaza providing support to over 20 agencies. Finally, the project will also aim to enhance the level of preapredness in case of mass casualty events through prepositioning essential life saving supplies at the prehospital and hospital level.

1 - Needs

Violent injury contributes a substantial disease burden in Palestine, particularly since the disproportionate use of force by the Israeli’s against the Great March of Return in Gaza, which began in March 2018. Meanwhile, in the West Bank, clashes with isolated communities living in Hebron, Area C or seam zone have resulted in injuries and death. Numbers from a one-year analysis in Gaza revealed that there were almost 30,000 casualties from violent- trauma (Gaza Trauma Response Publication WHO 2019). From the total injured, there were almost 7000 gunshot wounds, accounting for 25% of the total casualty caseload and over 40% of the caseload managed by hospitals. Gunshot wounds was the highest cause of death amongst the casualties; the anatomical distribution of gunshot wounds reveals that the majority of fatalities were caused by gunshots to the chest (38.6%) and head and neck (37.1%). Furthermore, the increasing rates of trauma as an indirect consequence of the conflict also adds to the burden; for example, increased crowding in highly urbanised areas in Gaza, particularly in areas of IDPs, result in accidents and burns injuries. In the West Bank, although the numbers are comparatively less, the likelihood of clashes, and increasing settler violence and attacks is high (OCHA dashboard). The burden of trauma in Gaza and West Bank as a result of the conflict has a particular impact on young people and people in their prime working years. For example, in Gaza, over 70% of those severely injured were aged between 18-39 years, and 90% were male. In an assessment conducted by the WHO in 2019, results showed that although the health system in Gaza is accustomed to managing a high numbers of injuries, prehospital care is decentralised with paramedics ranging in quality of care, and even access to basic supplies was an obstacle. Furthermore, the emergency departments required training, improvement of patient flow and better crowd control,and damage control surgery remains a major obstacle with ‘quick patch up’ efforts and premature discharge, often leading to infections and further complications. WHO’s assessment also revealed the need for essential tertiary care services for restoring lives, with over 17,00 in need of limb reconstruction surgery. The tertiary hospitals are overloaded, there is very limited post-operative and rehabilitation capacity and only residual capacity for limb reconstruction. In fact, the current limb reconstruction specialists face major challenges in conducting the work due to limited OT access, supplies and a lack of dedicated outpatient clinics.

2 - Activities

High incidence of violent injury requires sophisticated and well-resourced response mechanisms, placing enormous strains on already under-resourced and over-burdened health systems. But traditionally, only a very small number of humanitarian agencies have developed capacities for providing trauma care in conflict settings. Most humanitarian health agencies have not prioritized injury care in conflict because of mandate issues, security concerns and capacity and resource gaps; when non-specialized agencies do engage, it is too often uncoordinated and inconsistent with international standards. But the growing burden of trauma in current-day oPt can no longer be ignored. Lessons learnt from WHO’s recent experience in occupied Palestinian territory (oPt) demonstrate that strong technical leadership and coordination can substantially augment the capacities of local health staff and partners to manage trauma - and to measurably save lives. In Gaza (oPt), it is estimated that the enhanced trauma response saved between 435-1,227 lives over a period of one year - and the numbers are likely to increase as the event is ongoing. The impact on morbidity, including long-term disability, has not been calculated but can be assumed to be substantial. Based on previous lessons learnt, WHO will work across three pillars of care, which includes the prehospital, hospital and post-hospital level of care, by improving both the clinical capacity and managerial and organizational ability of the local health system. At the prehospital level, WHO will support the PRCS in centralizing all ambulance movements through the development of an ambulance dispatch centre, taking lessons learnt from West Bank to Gaza. This will be supplemented by upscaling the capacity of PRCS and MoH paramedics through training and clinical follow-up coaching of 250 frontline workers and paramedics. For surge support during acute emergencies, WHO will continue to provide simulation excerises and small supplies to the 10 trauma stabilsiation points in Gaza. At the hospital level, WHO will develop the capacity of six trauma hospitals in Gaza and four in the West Bank. The purpose is to provide a range of interventions, from improved infrastructure, provision of supplies, training and clinical coaching for all doctors and nurses working at the emergency departments. This will be extended to include training for surgeons on acute surgery to improve damage control surgery through basic principles of control of hemorrhage, prevention of contamination and protection from further injury. By investing in training on essential skills, alongside infrastructure improvements, WHO also aims to build a local network of knowledgement management. This will over time reduce the dependency for quality care from international organisations for frontline services as the expertise will exist locally. Finally, at the post-hospital level, WHO will focus primarily on tertiary care, which in 2020 will be the dedicated limb reconstruction unit. The creation of a unified limb reconstruction unit, would bring together under one ceiling. This important step would centralize knowledge while rationalizing human resources, time, equipment and costs. Visiting foreign medical teams would also be able to focus their interventions in a centralized structure, maximizing training opportunities in particular for the younger resident staff. In 2020, WHO will focus on providing clinical coaching for those working in the unit, alongside provision of supplies. Across all three levels of care, WHO will enhance coordination and information. WHO will continue to, as the cluster lead agency and to coordination the Trauma Working Group in Gaza. Finally, WHO's efforts will also extend to enhance the local capacity of auxillary or otherwise known as 'support services' for emergency care.
