Project Summary

The protracted drought, compounded by the economic crisis and socio-economic Impact of COVID19, will continue to expose most vulnerable women and girls, men and boys, to exacerbated risks of GBV and harmful practices in 2021.
Between March and October 2020, the National GBV hotline has recorded an average increase in GBV calls of 60%, with 94% being females and 90% of cases being IPV. As a result of the multi-hazard crisis and its socio-economic impact, more than 1,700,000 women and girls and 560,000 men and boys will face increased risks of exposure to GBV in 2021.
GBV vulnerability assessments conducted between March and October 2020 reported that drought is contributing to modify daily routine patterns (e.g. water fetching and firewood collecting) which forces women and girls to walk long distance and spend long hours away from home, generating tension on the homestead, and leading to increased IPV. In the eastern provinces, where Apostolic faith sectarianism is predominant, girls are increasingly exposed to early marriage, as lobola (bride price) is often the only alternative household income source. SRHR consequences include increased teenage pregnancy rates, and increased risks of ante and post-natal complications, which can be fatal.
Bordering towns also represent hotspots for GBV, as women and girls are exposed to trafficking and illegal migration, while female headed households become the most frequent target for SEA. IDPs in the eastern provinces that were affected by Cyclone Idai in 2019 continue to face increased GBV risks as they continue to be hosted at temporary camps with limited GBV risk mitigation measures. Women and girls with disabilities are also among the most vulnerable to GBV risks, and face further challenges of mobility to reach essential services.
The protracted drought, compounded by the economic hardship, hyperinflation and related basic goods price Increase, and the constrained access to cash, further contributes to reducing the capacity of survivors to sustain transport fees to reach services. The gradually decreasing availability of basic health services further contributes to the pre-existing critically low access to life-saving essential GBV services. According to the ZIMVAC rural livelihood assessment (October 2020), 68% of Individuals have no access to GBV services. When available, GBV multi-sectoral services remain fragmented, and mostly available at provincial and district level, often resulting out of reach for those living in most remote areas. De-prioritization of GBV services Is a documented result of unavailability of services, especially in remote areas. The proliferation of traditional healing systems deriving from the health system constraints, also results in an increased SEA risks.
In order to respond to the heightened GBV concerns, UNFPA with support from
international and local NGOs will scale up GBV mitigation and response interventions
targeting a total of 337,472 individuals (74% women and girls, 26% men and boys, 7% PWDs) in 26 districts.
The proposed interventions aim at:
1) Enhancing effectiveness of community-based GBV risk mitigation mechanisms;
2) Strengthening the availability and accessibility of life saving multi-sectoral survivor-centered
GBV services for GBV survivors in the most hard to reach areas
3) Improving the capacity of GBV specialized and non-specialized humanitarian actors to
integrate GBV and PSEA interventions across programs, to avoid unintended GBV
consequences and ensure DO-NO Harm.

1 - Activities

1. Establish community-based mechanisms for GBV risk mitigation and protection from SEA, including: - Set up and operationalize 52 safe spaces (2 in each of the 26 targeted districts), targeting a total of 30,000 most vulnerable women and girls (578 reached in each safe space with PSS, livelihood activities, integrated GBViE and SRHRiE information and FP commodities) - Conduct Community-based GBV risk mitigation outreach, targeting 337,472 women and girls, boys and men in hard to reach areas, including 2% women and girls with disability - Distribute NFIs (25,000 Dignity kits, MHM kits, mama kits) to most vulnerable women and girls 2. Scale up availability and accessibility of GBV life-saving multi-sectoral services (Health, PSS, Safety and legal), through: - Establish 26 roving mobile one stop centres in most hard to reach areas of the 26 targeted districts - Provide multi-sectoral services at mobile OSCs for 20,000 GBV survivors, including 2% survivors with disabilities - Enhance capacity of GBV national hotlines with support to 10 additional lines/counsellors - Providing dedicated MHPSS support to 1,000 GBV service providers Including counsellors, nurses, VFU officers and paralegal officers ( 70% female, 30 male) 3. Strengthen referral mechanisms for multisectorial GBV services, including: - Train 520 (20 x district) Community volunteers on GBV risk mitigation, GBV surveillance, and the referral pathway, including sign language, PFA, PSEA and integrated SRHRiE- Disseminate life-saving information on the referral pathway, through IEC materials, community radios, roadshows and other community mobilization tools. 4. Scale up GBV risk mitigation and PSEA across all humanitarian response sectors, including: - Train 1,220 humanitarian aid workers, including 600 specialized and 620 non specializedservice providers on integration of GBV mitigation and PSEA into humanitarian action.

8 - Accountability to Affected People
8.1 - Describe how your project was designed in consultation with communities? How will you incorporate communities in project implementation and what feedback mechanisms do you have in place?

The project interventions were tailored on the basis of context-specific needs of the targeted districts, and refined on the basis of the information received through FGDs and community members’ engagement. Key community stakeholders were engaged throughout the definition of the strategic interventions, including Traditional Chiefs and village headmen, community members and volunteers, to ensure that activities within safe spaces, mobile OSCs, NFIs distribution schedules and other community-based programs are in line with locally acceptable socialization patterns and routines, and as a way to ensure community buy in towards transformative results. Mobile GBV services will be provided through coordinated efforts of the multi-sectoral teams on the ground, in close consultation with community volunteers and cadres (Behavior change facilitators, Ward coordinators, Village health workers) in order to improve mobilization of community members and enhance service uptake in the areas that are hardest to reach. The engagement of local communities will continue to play a critical role during implementation, as district specific interventions will be designed through a co-creation model, which entails design of activities at safe spaces by the clients themselves, as opposed to a one size fits all approach. The existing community GBV surveillance system will be utilized as a project monitoring mechanism in order to ensure constant feedback from the recipient community on appropriateness of interventions and needs for adjustments. A survivor-centered approach will be applied throughout the project implementation in order to ensure vulnerabilities of most in need are not heightened as a result of the project.

9 - Environmental considerations
9.1 - How does your project impact on the environment, is there mitigation measures or recommended actions such as environment assessments that are planned for?

The project will have no impact on the environment, as most of the planned interventions do not include infrastructure or utilization of natural resources. Procurement of NFIs will be sensitive on minimizing the use of plastic wrapping versus reusable and sustainable materials.
