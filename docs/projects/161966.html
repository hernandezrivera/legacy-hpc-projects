Project Summary

The project aims to contribute to enhancing empowerment and resilience of Rohingya women and adolescent girls in the refugee camps through development and delivery of a learning programme. UN Women's 5 existing Multi-Purpose Women’s Centres (MPWCs) in the refugee camps will provide safe spaces for women and adolescent girls to access learning and skill building opportunities, basic services and information and are a key component of this project. While targeting, women and adolescent girls and other vulnerables like widows, elderly women, GBV survivors, people with disabilitties, etc would be prioritised and would be also supported with special provision like household-level sessions, if needed.
The learning programme would contribute to educational knowledge of women and adolescent girls through basic literacy (in Burmese and/or English) and numeracy, lessons on life-skills such as sexual and reproductive health and menstrual hygiene management, financial literacy, digital literacy and awareness raising sessions on gender-based violence, negative consequences of early and child marriage, and trafficking, disaster preparation, disability sensitisations or any other topic based on a learning needs assessment to be conducted. For this a curriculum would be based as per the guidelines of Education sector, and in liasion with the sector. Initially, course content can be accessed from the global UN Women SCE (second-chance education) online learning platform that is being developed in 2019. This course would be adapted to the local context, and harmonized with the learning framework for the Rohingya adolescents that are currently under the discussion between the Education sector and Bangladeshi Government. In doing so, the partners' capacity will be built.
Given the activities are conducted in tandem with other multi-sectoral activities being provided in UN Women MPWCs, space utilisation would be of otpimal. All of UN Women's MPWCs are multi-purpose facilities and one of the MPWC is also a two-storey structure in line with site development facility rationalization planning, hence aligning with space maximisation . Also, close coordiantion will be done with several sectors for effective multi-services provision (WASH, livelihoods, health, GBV), in addition to close coordination at HoSO, ISCG, GiHA level.
DRR and environmental considerations: The constructs of the existing MPWCs in the camps were reinforced by WFP DRR unit in order to be more resilient during the Cyclones and monsoons season. Furthermore, information sessions on cyclone and monsoon preparedness will be conducted in the MPWCs in line with agreed messaging by the EPWG and the CwC WG as well as linking women in the centres to the CPP volunteers. The project is not expected to have negative impact on the environment, including increasing vulnerability to disasters. Locations of new MPWCs will be determined based on dialogue with the DCO and relevant local government authorities with due consideration of not contributing to further deforestation. In terms of making positive contributions to environmentally sustainable development, UN Women has been partnering with FAO and WFP to engage women to plant trees inside and around the MPWCs to contribute to greening the camps and decrease the risk from landslide. The green gardens at the available land will be built, intensifying climate smart kitchen gardening. For environmental protection as well as to reduce GBV risk, women at the UN Women MPWCs are provided with information on LPGs for cooking in close collaboration with WFP.

Needs

Pre-existing gender inequalities, gender-based violence (GBV) and discrimination are often reinforced, perpetuated and exacerbated during crisis and this is disproportionately affecting women, girls and the most vulnerable and marginalized Rohingya refugee population groups. Gendered isolation and restricted mobility of women and girls according to traditional norms of Rohingya are limiting their access to life-saving assistance, services and information as well as their participation in decision making. The Rohingya community is highly conservative in their gender norms and practices. Practices such as purdah are restricting the mobility of women and their access to lifesaving assistance, services and information. Adolescent girls, young women, and women heads of households are some of the most at-risk in this context. Adolescent girls experience severe restrictions on their freedom of movement, limiting their access to services and resources. It also denies them the opportunity to develop support networks and friendships – leaving them with little they can do to lift their spirits and help them cope with the current situation. Cultural factors and security concerns often confine older girls in particular to their households. Rohingya women and girls are not merely vulnerable victims. They play a key role in increasing the resilience of families and communities. Family structures have changed, and all persons in the household now face new duties and must engage in new activities and this is giving space for new gender roles to emerge. There are Rohingya women leaders, including survivors of conflict related sexual and gender-based violence, that are self-organizing and forming their own groups in the camps to advocate for their rights and call for justice. Humanitarian responses often miss opportunities to transform socio-cultural gender norms and relations through the leadership and empowerment of women and girls, as well as by promoting positive forms of masculinities — notwithstanding the fact that these are key to a right-based and effective response and to communities’ longer-term resilience and social cohesion. Leveraging women’s participation and leadership capacities is not only a way to ensure humanitarian efforts respond to the specific needs and vulnerabilities of affected communities – whether women, men, girls or boys- but also a strategic investment in whole community resilience. A narrow asset base, gendered burden of domestic work, limited access to economic resources combined with the lack of alternate livelihoods mean that recovery from shocks for women takes longer than for men. Furthermore, disrupted social relations and gender norms especially put women and girls at increased risk of gender-based violence, and they are bearing the brunt of the negative consequences and coping mechanisms from breakdown of protection mechanisms and lack of livelihoods options. There is a significant gap in addressing adolescents’ educational and learning needs, which puts adolescent girls and young women even more at risk and is a key barrier to their empowerment (See: Plan International ‘ Adolescent girls in crisis: voices of the Rohingya’ April 2019, https://plan-international.org/emergencies/Rohingya-girls). Taking into consideration the very high level of illiteracy among Rohingya women – particularly adolescents who did not have access to education in Myanmar -, repressive gender roles and customs being reinforced because of insecurity, and harmful societal norms.

Activities or outputs

The project will be carried out against the following outputs and objectives, as part of broader UN Women programme for gender equality and empowerment of women and girls through multi-sectoral services including livelihoods skills for Rohingya women and adolescent girls: 1. Conduct learning needs assessment of Rohingya community adolescent girls and women 2. Develop curriculum for the ‘second chance education’ for adolescent girls and women, in line with UN Women's 'Second-Chance education' global course and education sector guidelines. 3. Identify selection criteria of Rohingya community adolescent girls and women 4. Develop enrollment plan 5. Sensitisations at community level, community consultations with the families of the participants and community stakeholders for regular community engagement and accountability to affected population6. Provide information on post-course completion options for participation of livelihood and income generations activities as well as leadership trainings 7. Deliver ‘Second chance education’ learning for adolescent girls and young women’ sessions at the centers, and at household levels, if needed to participants with special needs8. Conduct mentoring system where participants will be identifed who could enact as role models, support other participants, and eventually build their capacities in being tutors for the community.9. Evaluate relevant and impact of the Second Chance Education learning
