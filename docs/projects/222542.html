Project Summary

Conflict, subnational violence and economic shocks are escalating humanitarian needs and undermining fragile development gains in South Sudan. The climate crisis is a risk multiplier threatening lives, eroding already fragile food systems and ecosystems, and heightening social tensions and conflicts over depleted resources. As of 2024, the ongoing conflict in neighboring Sudan is dramatically exacerbating a dire situation. Humanitarian needs remain alarmingly high, yet funding needed to respond to all needs is not keeping pace. An estimated 9 million people require humanitarian assistance, with over 7 million (56 percent of South Sudan’s population) acutely food insecure and 79,000 at catastrophic food insecurity levels in 2024.
About 80 percent of the estimated 13.7 million population lives below the international poverty line of $1.90 per day. Over 74 percent of the population is under 30 years of age. Young people face high rates of unemployment and precarious employment. Almost half of school-aged children (around 3 million) are out of school. Social protection coverage remains very low with only 2.4 million people covered by social protection programmes in 2019. Pervasive inequities in education and employment opportunities combined with lack of access to essential services contribute to trapping women and girls in a vicious cycle of vulnerability, amplifying hunger and exclusion.
The most recently IPC analysis released November 2023 reported that in NBEG, an estimated between September and November 2023, 893,000 people (46% of the State’s population) are IPC Phase 3 or worse acute food insecurity. Of this population, 310,000 people are in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity, and 583,000 people are in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) acute food insecurity. The drivers of food insecurity are prolonged dry spells, floods, inflations, closure of trade routes between Sudan and South Sudan that have affected market functionality and led to high food prices, low household income and purchasing power, and the influx of returnees and refugees from Sudan. Some of the mitigating factors during this period include availability of wild foods and wild game.
During the lean-season projection period from April and July 2024, the food security situation deteriorated further in the State, with an estimated 1.13 million people (59% of the State’s population) likely to have faced Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity, of which 425,000 people are likely to be in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity, and 661,000 people are likely to be in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) acute food insecurity. The key drivers of this deterioration were the seasonal depletion of harvests, high food prices in markets that are sub-optimally operational because of the closure of trade routes between Sudan and South Sudan and the disruption of supply markets because of the conflict in Sudan, and the likelihood of continued influx of returnees and refugees from Sudan.
The level of malnutrition will persist with an estimated 1.65 million children under the age of five in South Sudan likely to suffer from acute malnutrition in 2025. Overall, the project will deliver lifesaving humanitarian interventions that meet both short and medium term hunger gaps while helping households build longer-term food security and resilience against future shocks and stresses, it will contribute to Food Security and Livelihoods cluster objectives - CO1, CO2, & CO3.
The program will target vulnerable populations, especially women, girls, elderly, disabled, PLW, children U5, HIV & the chronically sick amongst IDPs, refugee, returnees or host communities. FLDA is well placed to implement the above program as it has been operating in the location for the last 9 years, FLDA is an active member of coordination mechanisms. Complementarity and synergies will be on WFP’s ACL (3, 853 HH) and FAO’s RALP (5,620 HH) and FAO’s RALP additional financing (5,000 HH) in Aweil South.

1. Needs

Overall, NBEG State is affected by the climate change that includes droughts and floods which influences food availability. NBEG normally experiences a unimodal rainfall pattern with 800-1100mm annual rainfall. However, over the recent years, the shift in rainfall patterns extended the dry season by 5-6 months. As a result, traditional water sources that used to support a three-to-four-month dry season have dried up, increasing livestock movement to permanent rivers and water sources across counties. Most parts of NBEG are flood prone as well as exposed to dry spell that may contribute to delay in the planting seasons or prevent farmers from accessing fields altogether, disrupting the agricultural calendar critical for maximizing yields; lead to deterioration of soil fertility through erosion by losing essential soil nutrients, leading to long-term agricultural challenges; lead to displacement of communities since most communities are covered by floods and it destroys shelters and the available means of settlements. From the livestock point of view, the effect of climate change also increases livestock diseases, tsetse flies, HS, BLACK -QUARTER/LEG, ANTHRAX, CBPP (endemic). Early movement and extended stay (Jan-June) of nomadic Arabs; Fulani and other internal seasonal migration/movement from the north with millions of livestock competing for scarce water and pasture resources. There are a few storage facilities, but they are not well maintained, leading to post-harvest losses due to pests and disease. The availability of different pesticide formulations has increased, but there are concerns about their safety and environmental impact. Integrated Pest Management strategies are being promoted as alternatives that reduce reliance on chemical pesticides. In many areas, seed banks and agricultural extension services play an important role in providing access to these seeds. However, the cost of agricultural inputs is very high and there is a lack of appropriate agricultural tools such as ox ploughs (communities continue to use hand tools), inadequate veterinary services and few trained animal health workers (CAHWs). FLDA’s humanitarian response plan targets both Aweil South and Aweil Centre counties of Northern Bahr El Ghazel State. Aweil South County has official 8 Payams (Tier Aliet, Tarweng, Nyieth, Panthou, Wathmuok, Ayai, Nyac Awany and Gakrol). Aweil South County population according to 2008 Census put the county population to 73,806 whereas 2021 National Bureau of Statistic PES Population estimates put the County population to 239, 810 Respectively. The county falls under the western flood plains sorghum and cattle livelihood zone with grassland, swampy areas with papyrus reed, and pockets of forest being found in this area. The is most prone to flooding in the state. Flooding in the county has historically occurred around October/November as a result of riverbanks overflowing, at the time when most people have harvested most of their farm produce. However, changing weather patterns have meant that earlier onset of rains has increasingly flooded fields and destroyed crops before they are harvested. The prolonged dry spell equally affects the seasonal calendar. Aweil Centre County borders WBEG (Raja, Wau and Jur River Counties) to the west and south. The northern part of the county falls under the western flood plains sorghum and cattle livelihoods zone, whereas the southern parts of the county fall under the western plains groundnuts, sesame and sorghum livelihoods zone. The area around Aroyo town falls under the western iron stone plateau (USAID 2007). The county features a relatively low population density compared to other counties such as Aweil East. The foreign nomads come with their grazing animals and destroy farms and buy food staff on large scale resulting severe shortages in the markets and local surroundings. Besides raising competition in grazing and water reservoirs.
