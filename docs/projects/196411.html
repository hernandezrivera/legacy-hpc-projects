Project Summary

Despite the signing of the Juba Peace Agreement (JPA), armed clashes have continued across Sudan, the severe economic crisis continued to negatively impact on the lives of the people in Sudan. These situations have been exacerbated by the recent armed conflict between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF); this fighting has further destabilized the protection mechanisms for children, consequently exposing children and their caregivers to furth violence, abuse, exploitation and neglect. The overall protection needs of the children have increased considerably. According to the updated Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP) 2023 for Sudan (May 2023), the revised People in Need (PIN) is 24.7 million, 13.6 of whom are children. UNICEF Sudan plans to reach 11.9 million people, 9.4 million of them are children2. This includes newly displaced people, host communities and those trapped in conflict areas without access to basic services. Given UNICEF’s sector leadership in Education (co-led by Save the Children), Nutrition, WASH and the Child Protection Area of Responsibility, as well as last resort role, UNICEF aims to cover 50-80 per cent of each sector’s need. Actual targets may differ, based on the capacity, specific intervention and other partners’ coverage. International support towards the transitional government has not yet translated into substantive financial and policy support to contribute to a progressive reversal of the situation. As a result, humanitarian needs continued to grow, driven by underlying factors including the economic crisis, food insecurity, flooding, disease outbreaks and intercommunal violence. All these factors have resulted in prolonged displacement in addition to recent fighting in Jebel Marra between different factions of armed groups and intercommunal conflict exacerbated by the deteriorating economic conditions and other political factors. The country has also continued to experience rains and floods emergencies in 2022, affecting people across the 18 states of the country. Moreover, endemic diseases such as Malaria, cholera, dengue fever and chikungunya, other novel diseases are also emerging including some which have not been reported in over ten years.

The people in need in Sudan will be the highest over the past decade. Protection of children from violence, exploitation, abuse, and neglect within such complex emergencies is challenged by many factors that child protection partners are working to address. Children affected by conflict, displacement, disease outbreaks and other emergencies are more prone to all types of violations to their rights including their rights to basic services. Many have been separated from their families due to sudden displacement and become vulnerable to further violations including recruitment and association with armed forces/groups. Others who witnessed violence and atrocities during conflict are psychologically affected and need psychosocial support services to avoid further deterioration to trauma and mental health problems that needs specialized services, which are not available in almost all conflict-affected areas. Displaced children and adolescents, especially adolescents’ girls are at risk of gender-based violence (GBV) because of the breakdown of the protective family and community environment during displacement and conflict. Many children in the states affected by armed conflict are victims of unexploded ordnances (UXOs) and some of them lost their lives especially in the newly-accessible areas in Blue Nile, South Kordofan and Jebel Marra in Darfur. The six grave violations against children by armed forces/groups are widespread in the conflict-affected areas. 1 - Needs

People in the Sudan remain extremely vulnerable to emergencies. The recent armed conflict that started on April 15 has further plunged the entire country into crisis with an increase in the number of internally displaced persons (IDPs) in all of the states There is an enormous breakdown of protection system and safety nets for children; lapses in the rule of law are allowing ethnic tensions to erupt into violence; flooding and disease outbreaks are destroying lives and livelihoods; and education interruptions in 2019, 2020, 2021, 2022 have left more 14million children out of school. COVID- 19 and related mitigation measures are further complicating these existing challenges. The closure of health services due to the lack of lifesaving supplies and the displacement of services providers have further decelerated the health situation for children. These hardships are threatening people's lives, denying children – particularly girls – access to an education, creating serious protection risks and giving rise to violations and abuse, including gender-based violence. Past conflicts in the Sudan and neighboring countries have left thousands of people internally displaced are going without basic support, in or out of camps. Fighting between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF) began on 15 April 2023 in Khartoum and swiftly expanded to neighboring cities and states, especially in Darfur, and some other parts of Sudan thus causing mass displacement of citizens some of whom were previously displaced from other crisis. With the recent development, the humanitarian needs of the population have risen to another level. The updated HRP 2023 for Sudan (May 2023) revised the PIN to 24.7 million, 13.6 of whom are children. UNICEF Sudan plans to reach 11.9 million people, 9.4 million of them are children. In addition to the violence, flooding and disease outbreaks affect the population of Sudan, as well as the economic turmoil that has undermined livelihoods and increased vulnerabilities across the country. The Sudan’s food and nutritional security are eroding, deepening a crisis already affecting 14 per cent of the population. As a result, there are huge needs to ensure that affected children and their families will receive the needed mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) to avoid further mental health problems. The sudden outbreaks of fighting have resulted in many children being separated from their families and they are in need of family tracing and reunification and alternative care arrangements. The displacement and breakdown of family and community protective environment have led to many GBV cases reported among the displaced population, including children and adolescents. Many other children and adolescents’ victims of grave child rights violations are also reported such as abduction recruitment and risks of UXOs/explosive ordinance that have increased significantly as a result of the recent fighting. Children released form armed forces/groups and other vulnerable children have little opportunities and are at risks of further violations to their rights unless their needs for reintegration with their families and communities and civilian life is addressed.

2 - Activities/Outputs

The activities include provision of MHPSS services to children and their families, provision of FTR and alternative care services, prevention, and response to GBV, explosive ordinance risk education, prevention and response to recruitment of children and reintegration of children released from armed forces/groups and establishment of community-based child protection networks (CBCPNs) to engage with communities on prevention and response to grave child rights violation. UNICEF has long experience in working with the government and civil society partners on all the emergency issues faced by Sudan. UNICEF's response depends mainly on these partnerships and the child protection system that have established and supported during the past 15 years. So, FTR is already established with clear standard operating procedures (SOPs) that identify the roles and responsibilities of different partners. Social workers in all the states affected by conflict are well trained on FTR issues and the alternative care procedures. For MHPSS, UNICEF in collaboration with the National Council for Child Welfare (NCCW) has been working during the past years on the design and production of a PSS manual for children and adolescents affected by conflict. This manual has been approved and used widely in providing PSS to children and adolescents by trained social workers in all the states affected by conflict. UNICEF has also long experience in reintegration of children released from armed forces/groups. In collaboration with DDRC, a training guide for social workers on working with children recruited or associated with armed forces/groups was designed and used in the reintegration of more than 2500 children after the signing of the CPA. The activities on mine risk education are it mentioned as part of the Humanitarian Action for Children (HAC) appeal indicators, but given that is it one of the dangers for children, and lifesaving, UNICEF has blended it with the indicator on violence against children. Both targets and budget are combined. Building on the critical mass of experience and lessons learned, UNICEF together with UNITAMS is engaged with SAF, RSF and Juba Peace Agreement signatories and other non-signatory parties on the way to implement a national strategy developed for the release, the interim care, family tracing and reunification as well as the sustainable reintegration of children associated with armed forces and armed groups across the different states across Sudan. For this purpose, road maps have been adopted and a technical level committee and high-level committees have been established to oversee the sustainable reintegration of the children in close collaboration with the UN agencies and other organizations. Furthermore, an effective case management system is being strengthening under the case management working group of the Child Protection subsector. The same applies to activities around mine risk education where a community-based EORE manual has been designed and currently used by community volunteers and social workers to provide risk education to affected communities.

6.2 - Explain how your project aims to ensure protection mainstreaming during its design and implementation

The project addresses child protection across sectors through the child protection subsector coordination to ensure that child protection issues are properly addressed and mainstreamed in the health, Social Protection, WASH and Education sectors. For instance, GBV and MHPSS in schools and health facilities will be mainstreamed through training of teachers and health workers and linking them to a referral system with social workers from the ministries of social welfare. Wash facilities will ensure the safety and dignity of children and adolescents specially girls to, at, and from WASH facilities.
