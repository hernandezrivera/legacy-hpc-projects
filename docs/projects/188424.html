Project Summary

A decade-long crisis in Syria has reversed human development, impoverished the lives and livelihoods of Syrian families, and eroded the resilience of vulnerable communities. Syria has also been experiencing an unprecedented economic downturn since 2019, with soaring inflation and a collapse in the value of the Syrian pound (SYP).
The Syrian crisis is estimated to have resulted in physical capital destruction, including productive assets, and communal infrastructure. The result has been a near-total collapse of food systems in many rural and urban areas, leading to food insecurity and food assistance dependency. The HNO estimates that more than 13 million people are in need of humanitarian assistance.
In response and to reduce the high levels of dependency from food assistance, WFP implements a range of projects designed to create livelihoods, and reconstitute the resilience of households, communities, and food systems. These efforts contribute to Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 2 (Zero Hunger), in addition to having significant positive multiplier effects towards SDGs 1 (no poverty), 4 (quality education), and 5 (gender equality).
In line with the HRP Strategic Outcomes 1, 2 and 3 and in coordination with the FSA and ERL sectors, WFP identifies the need of enhancing resilience and early recovery to play a significant role in addressing the protracted crisis and food insecurity situation in Syria. In 2022-23 WFP will be scaling up the activities in SO 02 and 03 of the HRP. In fact, resilience-building is a crucial aspect to help vulnerable smallholder farmers and livestock keepers more self-reliant through the improvement of livelihoods assets, rehabilitation of irrigation systems and other community assets, restoration and enhancement of the national bread value chain.
The WFP Livelihoods Strategy focuses on early recovery interventions as necessary tools to relieve the pressure from emergency food interventions and prevent households and communities currently at risk to fall further into food insecurity. The WFP Strategy has 4 pillars: 1: Food Assistance for Assets (FFA) through, a) Provision of household and group-based productive assets; and b) Rehabilitation of agrarian community assets. 2: Food Assistance for Training (FFT) through human capital development. 3: Market Access Support via Retail Sector Rehabilitation, with a specific focus on the wheat-to-bread value chain. 4: Social Transfer/Basic Income via Financial Inclusion.
WFP plan is to support 530,000 beneficiaries (106,000 households) in 2022, with livelihoods and resilience activities. 80% of the beneficiaries are expected to be the users of the rehabilitated assets, while the remaining caseload will be assisted with households’ level complementary activities, including Food Assistance for Assets (FFA), Food Assistance for Training (FFT), both done through food vouchers and vouchers, as well as other livelihoods and income-generating activities. In 2023, WFP plan to continue expanding the caseload reaching 700,000 people, or 140,000 households.
The long-term overarching goal is to enhance the food security of Syrian communities and enable planning a progressive reduction of food assistance needs in the country. In the rollout of this approach, WFP will closely coordinate with other UN organizations such as FAO and UNDP, as well as with the FAS and ERL sectors. In particular, despite directly reporting to FAS, WFP will foster complementarities with ERL.
WFP plans its activities using the Three-Pronged Approach (3PA) to define appropriate interventions, accurate targeting, and geographical coherence; the tools are the Integrated Context Analysis (ICA), Seasonal Livelihoods Planning (SLP), and; the Community Based Participatory Programming (CBPP). The tools are also important to minimize exclusion errors and to reduce the risk of exacerbating existing conflicts, by ensuring extensive dialogue with communities and other stakeholders.

4 - Needs

The food security crisis in Syria is a "protracted crisis”, and remains characterized by very complex drivers and contributing factors that require humanitarian actors to go beyond short-term responses but to also focus on scaling up early recovery and livelihoods, Including work on food systems. The Food Security Sector (FSA) provided record levels of assistance in Syria in 2021, increasing general food assistance in average from 5 million to 6 million people assisted each month. The FSA estimates 55.3% of the population is food insecure in Syria. In parallel significant challenges continue to impact local food production in Syria and this pillar remains under-funded. Scaling up local food production, improved food systems, and agriculture-based livelihoods, especially in rural areas, will be fundamental to sustainably reducing food insecurity and vulnerability levels in Syria. Additionally, water scarcity, damaged irrigation infrastructure, and the low rainfall level have forced most farmers, especially in Rural Damascus and Rural Aleppo, to rely on surface wells to cover part of their irrigation needs. The low production of wheat and other crops during the 2021 harvest season has resulted in a significant gap in supply and demand, mainly for wheat and other crops such as legumes. Agro-climatic conditions, particularly the erratic rainfall distribution and amount, are projected to be worse in the 2021 – 2022 agricultural season, especially in northern and southern Syria.

5 - Implementation of activities

WFP plans to implement the following activities in 2022 – 2023, in line with SO 01, 02 and 03 of the HNO. The area-based approach is designed to ensure the interventions are interconnected and complementary to each other. Pillar 1: Communal Assets Rehabilitation a) Food Assistance for Assets (FFA) - to enhance the community resilience to shocks and promote restoration of lost livelihoods;b) Rehabilitation of agrarian assets - such as large irrigation systems; farmers and livestock markets, etc. Pillar 2: Food Assistance for Training (FFT) to enhance human capital through a soft conditional transfer modality. Pillar 3: Market Access Support via Retail Sector Rehabilitation, with a specific focus on the wheat-to-bread value chain. This includes the soft rehabilitation of public bakeries; public mills; silos. Pillar 4: Social Transfer/Basic Income via Financial Inclusion to support the transition to social welfare for the people most in need. Under this framework of activities, WFP is and will coordinate with other stakeholders at the national and regional levels, such as: FAO and the FSA sector for the agricultural and irrigation components and with UNDP and the ERL sector for early recovery and infrastructural activities. In the implementation of 2022 activities, WFP expects to reach 106,000 direct beneficiaries through various transfer modalities, and 424,000 people as users of the communal assets rehabilitated along the farm-to-bread value chain.

9 - Protection Risk Analysis

The Syrian crisis continues to expose the affected population to a multitude of protection risks, including violence against civilians, sexual and gender-based violence, movement/access restrictions, lack of documentation, amongst others. With an awareness that these factors can exacerbate food insecurity, restrict access and compromise the impact of resilience and livelihoods activities, ensuring safety, dignity and integrity of its beneficiaries is a key priority. WFP conducts protection risk assessments on an ongoing basis. Risks identified included fear of violence, explosive remnants, movement restrictions, lack of personal documentation, constraints related to safe, consistent, access to the rehabilitated assets. WFP will continue to support partners in applying protection principles, including raising awareness among staff and partners on the inclusion of people living with disabilities, PSEA protocols and AAP. WFP has Complaint and Feedback Mechanism so that protection concerns can be updated and addressed. WFP Helpline mechanism will be further strengthened in 2022 and 2023. The line is open to all communities that are reached through WFP activities and will be expanded to resilience and livelihoods activities.


10 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

A key aspect in the prevention and reporting of SEA is awareness. WFP employees and partners are expected to be able to define, recognize and respond to SEA, including understanding their obligation to report. All reports are confidential and can be made anonymously to WFP’s internal investigations unit, the Office of the Inspector General - OIG, or PSEA Focal Points (who refer matters to OIG). WFP takes a victim-centered approach to its prevention and protection measures against sexual exploitation and abuse. We seek to ensure that victims are provided with support, as needed, at the same time as reports are investigated. We rely on protection partners and strong local referral networks for direct assistance for victims of sexual exploitation and abuse. In 2022 and 2023 WFP will continue its work with other UN agencies participating in the PSEA network and its partners to ensure that the in-place PSEA policies and protocols are fully adopted and implemented including capacity building and establishing mechanisms. All the Field Offices have an active PSEA focal point who has been trained on how to safely identify and report cases. Focal Points also raise awareness among staff about SEA related issues. All WFP partners have been assessed within the PSEA Interagency Network on capacities and partners with low capacities and without clear policies to prevent and respond to SEA cases, will be trained in 2022 and supported in the development of strategies and policies.


11 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

The main risks foreseen by WFP are related to the lack of stability in the country and the potential disruption of the livelihoods and resilience programming in some of the areas of implementation. Additionally, as presented in previous sections, the risks of underfunding are concrete and could negatively affect the potential and expected impact of the 2022 – 23 plans. Moreover, WFP sees as a risk the possibility that the interventions might exacerbate existing community level and political tensions, as well as the risk of exacerbating inequalities through the implementation of its projects in areas where access is still a challenge. To mitigate the above risks, WFP is investing in building risk preparedness within its partners; is active in the development of free tool helpline to enhance accountability to the affected populations and protection; and is mainstreaming gender and protection policies to ensure that programmes are gender-sensitive and beneficiaries have appropriate and safe CFM mechanisms to use.
