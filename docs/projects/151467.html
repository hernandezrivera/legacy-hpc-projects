Project Summary

Malnutrition is one of the most persistent humanitarian challenges in Somalia despite concerted efforts to address it. Acute malnutrition in children between 6-59 months is a direct outcome indicator of recent changes in nutritional status which could be attributed to changes in food security, WASH and health situations among others. The significant deterioration of malnutrition situation seen among the IDPs can be attributed to high morbidity (disease incidence e.g. AWD, measles …etc), low humanitarian support, poor child feeding and caring practices. Similarly, partners on the ground generally felt that food insecurity, limited health service availability, like poor EPI coverage, and increased morbidity, poor health seeking behaviour, and difficulty of accessing clean water supply are driving factors for the current situation.CARE is currently implementing integrated nutrition, health and wash services in parts of Bari, Sanaag, Sool and Lower Juba regions across three states (Puntland, Somaliland, Jubaland). We are targeting IDPs in Bosaso and Kismayo as well as rural pastoralists from livelihoods of Northern Inland Pastoral (Sool and Sanaag regions) that are considered by FSNAU as malnutrition hotspots requiring urgent lifesaving nutrition and health interventions. The most recent FSNAU post GU assessment found critical level of Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) of 15.5% in most of Sanag and Sool regions; 17.1% GAM in Bosaso IDPs and a serious nutrition situation with GAM of 14.4% Kismayo IDP. Whats most worrying is the critical high level of SAM found in these regions that are ranging from 2-3.3%.
High HH food insecurity is most likely to further exacerbate the malnutrition rate into 2019. Childhood morbidity in Bari, Lower Juba, Sool and Sanaag is also high ad ranges between 14.3% to 29.1% and this predisposes children to malnutrition through a cycle of repeated episodes of illnesses leading to rapid weight loss. According to most recent IYCF KAP study conducted by CARE in 5 districts of Sanaag, and Sool regions in September 2018 found sub-optimal IYCF practices, including low rate of exclusive breastfeeding (69%), poor dietary diversity (4.6%) and generally low IYCF awareness. Only 56.8% of children continued to breastfed to one year and 69.8% of the children had the minimum meal frequency (2-3 meals a day). This predisposes children to reduced immunity to diseases and faltering growth. In addition, inappropriate and poor feeding practices, poor hygiene and unsafe water exacerbate malnutrition. The rate of continuation of breastfeeding up to 2 years is also very low at 28%. The extremely poor dietary diversity leads to micronutrient deficiencies. Most families were unable to purchase and consume a balanced diet due to their low purchasing power. In addition, the rate of bottle feeding was 46.8% which is very high and carries an increased cost burden of buying formula milk or cow milk as a substitute to breastfeeding children under 6 months of age making these households prone to food shocks when food prices rise.
Therefore there is a huge need to scale up IYCF-E services to improve practices, prevent further deterioration and reduce child deaths. The drought has also had negative impacts on the consumption of milk in children, pregnant and lactating women (PLW) especially in pastoralist HH contributing to increased malnutrition. PLW are facing multiple challenges that hinders optimal child care practices.They are engaged in petty jobs to earn a living restricting exclusive breastfeeding and the same time trek for a long distance to fetch water. This is coupled with low income levels resulting in poor dietary diversification. This compromises IYCF, including exclusive breastfeeding for children less than 6 months. Malnutrition in mothers, especially those who are pregnant or breastfeeding, can set up a cycle of deprivation that increases the likelihood of low birth weight, child mortality, serious disease, poor classroom performance and low work productivity. This is because women and children are particularly vulnerable to malnutrition due to their physiological needs for both growth and they extra needs during pregnancy and lactation. In Somalia women and girls are often marginalised and excluded from employment and education opportunities, this makes women and girls very vulnerable to fall into the traps of poverty. Vulnerable women, especially those in female-headed households, frequently have limited access to nutrition information and the resources they need to improve food security, such as income, land, equipment, financial services and training. They also require 2.5% more dietary iron than men, and women need more protein than usual when pregnant and lactating. In many societies including Somalia men and boys are given priority when meals are served. This can contribute to under nutrition in women and girls and due to the apparent vulnerability of PLWs and children aged 6-59 months will be targeted.
HEALTH
The fragility in Somalia in the past two and a half decades has resulted in the collapse of the health sector and a subsequent focus on emergency response interventions to recurrent crises characterized by high levels of population displacement, widespread vulnerability, insufficient and weak health service delivery. Somalis’ overall health status is poor due to multiple factors including fragmented service delivery; poor community participation, low behavior change; inadequate funding and human resource allocation; and inadequate health systems strengthening. The U5MR is 137 per 1,000 live births with one in seven Somali children dying before they reach their fifth birthday-unacceptably high rates despite them declining. The leading causes of under-five mortality are acute respiratory illnesses such as pneumonia (24%), diarrhea (19%), neonatal disorders (17%), and measles (12%) . Immunization coverage rates remain low in Somalia, with an estimated under 50% coverage for measles and DPT3 in 2014.
Somalia has one of the highest maternal mortality rates due to limited antenatal care (ANC) being only accessible to a quarter of women and only 1/3 of births attended by skilled medical personal. Despite the implementation of the Essential Package of Health Services (EPHS), use of health services in Somalia remains very low. Over a Somali woman’s lifetime, she will face a 1-in-18 chance of dying from causes related to pregnancy or childbirth. The maternal mortality ratio (MMR) in Somalia is 732 per 100,000 live births; among the world’s highest. Somalia is ranked as the worst place in the world to be a mother in Save the Children’s 2016 State of the World’s Mothers report. Although the MMR is down from 1210 in 1990. The average fertility rate is 6.6 children per woman with unmet need for birth spacing. Access to maternal health services is low with 44 and 38% of births in Somaliland and Puntland being attended by skilled birth attendants. Only 25% of women receive professional ANC with only 6% attending more than the recommended 4 visits.
Across all sub-sectors, Sool and Sanaag regions continue to be highlighted as key gap regions by the Health Cluster as well as in CARE’s rapid assessment in February, 2018 in which there was poor access to basic health services, with low coverage of HFs and services, inadequate health partner coverage and limited MOH capacity Morbidity in the regions is high, especially with respiratory illness and diarrheal diseases. The community indicates the greatest need is improved access to health services, especially basic MCH services, as the rural areas have few health posts with vast distances between HFs and overall limited service availability. A rapid needs assessment conducted in March 2018 by American Refugee Committee indicated that over 50% of all assessed HFs in Sool and Sanaag lacked basic infrastructure, essential supplies, and human resource incentives support . Furthermore, none of the MCHs surveyed had readily available, equipped ambulances resulting in delayed access to comprehensive emergency obstetric care.
Therefore in light of all of the increasing humanitarian situation forcasted in 2019 the situation calls for sustained humanitarian interventions in the form of integrated nutrition, wash, health services and in order to prevent further deterioration in the nutrition and health situation. CARE will implement an integrated package of health, nutrition and wash curative and preventative services targeting children aged 6-59 months and pregnant and lactating women. The intervention will provide access to basic health, wash and nutrition services through both mobile and fixed health facilities. Protection will be mainstreamed by considering separate waiting areas for males and females, providing PSEA sensitisation training to health care professional working in the health facilities, having a proportionate number of female and male health staff; employing health staff with skills and experiences working with children. We will also consult with the community at the design, response and monitoring stages of the projects to understand better how power dynamics in the community can affect the patient’s response and attitude towards seeking nutrition and health services.
As we are targeting children under 5 and pregnant and lactating mothers for nutrition and health services, we will conduct community meeting sessions with caretakers of children under 5, fathers and PLW separately to gauge get an understanding of the nutritional status of girls and boys, women and men, including older people and those with chronic illness and how they seek help for nutrition problems. We will do this by:
• The targeting of the project’s intervention is informed by an analysis of how girls, boys, women and men are distinctly affected by under nutrition, malnutrition and micronutrient deficiency diseases
• The design of nutrition services is determined through consultation of men and women, including pregnant and lactating adolescent girls, in order to ensure equitable access and
• Special arrangements are in place to ensure that those with limited access to services, due to mobility issues, to risks of violence or to domestic and care work, receive assistance

Activities

1. Identification, referral and treatment of children and PLW suffering from severe and moderate acute malnutrition through community, facility based approaches. Mother-led MUAC approach will be used to identifying and diagnosing malnutrition by mothers. CHWs will be trained to screen and refer malnourished children and PLW. CARE will provide OTP, TSFP and SC services to children in our project area. Survivors of gender based violence (GBV) identified at the nutrition sites will be referred to multi-sectoral services. 2. Prevention services will include deworming for children 12-59 months and pregnant women in the 2nd and 3rd trimester, Vitamin A Supplementation, micronutrient supplementation, EPI immunization, counselling and promotion on infant and young child feeding. 3. Provision of nutrition, health, and hygiene (NHHP) promotions to caregivers/PLW Caregivers with children in the health and nutrition sites will receive education sessions to promote hand washing at critical times and safe water handling amongst other key messages. Caregivers will also receive soaps and hygiene kits. 4. Treatment of common childhood illnesses in children in the nutrition sites. Children presenting with uncomplicated cases of common childhood illnesses in fixed health centers and through mobile teams will be treated on site using the IMCI guideline. Provision of reproductive health, maternal and nutrition services (RHMNCH) services to caregivers attending health facilities. This will include: focused antenatal care, assisted deliveries, nutritional supplements, nutrition education as well as establishment of delivery plan including referrals when appropriate. They will also receive PNC services i.e. advice on new-born care, birth spacing and IYCF counselling following delivery. All normal pregnancies will be targeted for assisted delivery at the MCHs whilst the complicated pregnancies will be referred to nearest hospital for CEMOC services.

Protection risk analysis & Mitigating measures
Preventing risks of exclusion based on displacement

The Protection and Return Monitoring Network reported that some 151,097 people have been displaced in Sool and Sanaag between January and November 2017, of these 150,540 cited drought as the main reason of their displacement. Many new IDPs have relocated from rural to urban areas of the country, resulting in overcrowding at IDP settlements. SGBV remains one of the critical protection concerns in these settlements. Internally displaced women and girls are at particular risk of domestic violence, rape, and sexual abuse violence by armed men, including government soldiers and militia members, and civilians . Women and girls who are displaced or from marginalized groups suffer the most due to insecurity, inadequate protection mechanisms, lack of or limited access to available formal and informal justice mechanisms and weak clan protection. According to UNICEF, more than 76% of recorded GBV survivors are reported to be from IDP communities . Insecurity in IDP camps, particularly at night, poses among the gravest risks of sexual violence for women and girls. Displaced women and girls are also vulnerable when they leave their IDP settlements to gather firewood or water.

Preventing risks of exclusion based on gender and age

CARE will mainstream protection by ensuring the nutrition and health centres do not pose a security risk to women and the office hours are convenient for them. health care staff at the fixed facilities will receive GBV training including sensitisation PSEA and how to identify and refer cases for specialised services.

Mitigating the impact of armed conflict and violence

CARE has a strong security surveillance system which advices staff on the precautions needed to be taken in their project area. As Sool and Sanag regions are contested areas it is likely that tension between Somaliland and Puntland troops may occur in 2019 as well. This will have major security implication of the provision of nutrition and health services. The situation will always be monitored and stakeholders will be engaged in assessing the risk and who is most likely to be affected. Appropriate measures will then be taken to ensure the welfare of our beneficiaries and staff
