Project Summary

The project is planned to support education services of IDPs, returnees and host community children in South Sudan. The main goal of the project is to ensure girls and boys aged 3-18 years (40 percent girls), adolescents/ youth affected by conflict have improved access to safe and protective education services. Train teachers (male and female) and PTA/SMC members (male and female) for improved capacity and quality of education. The project will also ensure community engagement through social mobilization and sensitization to support enrollment and school environment as well as contribute to the Education Cluster objectives.
Since the outbreak of the second conflict in July 2016 , South Sudan has been confronted with many challenges. These include large population displacements, skyrocketing hyperinflation, protracted civil conflict and severe food insecurity that made the country volatile. The humanitarian crisis, displacement, protracted conflict emergency, poverty, and livelihood insecurity are the main drivers leading to interruption of education (HRP 2020 Education Cluster Narrative). The already fragile education system drastically deteriorated at both the systemic and service delivery levels. It is characterized by high numbers of out-of-school children and adolescents /youth, particularly those affected by conflict and other emergencies estimated to be 2.2 million (UNESCO 2018, Global Initiative on Out-of-school children: South Sudan Country Study).
Through this project of frontline services, children (boys and girls), particularly the most vulnerable and those affected by conflict will have increased access to quality education services in safe and protective environments.

1 - Needs

Since the second outbreak of the conflict in July 2016, South Sudan has been confronted with many challenges. These include large population displacements, skyrocketing hyperinflation, protracted civil conflict, and serious food insecurity. The already fragile education system characterized by high numbers of out-of-school children (boys and girls), particularly those affected by conflict and other emergencies and poor educational outcomes drastically deteriorated at both the system and service-delivery levels. In South Sudan, continuity of access to education drops off significantly as children move through the system. While 99 per cent of schools provide Grade 1 education, only 27 per cent offer up to Grade 8 education. The protracted conflict also led to the closure, damage or occupation of schools. Some families that have fled conflict continue to reside in areas without access to safe and protective learning spaces or in host communities where education resources are overstretched. Other communities have voluntarily returned to their places of origin where provision of education services is highly needed. The insecurity in the country, coupled with hyperinflation, food insecurity, low and delayed payment of teachers’ salaries have also led to higher rates of teacher absenteeism as many of them deserted the profession and opted for jobs with NGOs. The involvement of volunteer teachers has partially filled this gap, but only 30 per cent of South Sudan’s primary-level teachers are qualified, putting the provision of teacher training high on the needs scale. Also, the scarcity of gender and age appropriate teaching and learning materials is particularly worrying given that so many teachers are untrained. All these supply-side challenges are magnified in the nascent early childhood education system, responsible for preparing children to succeed in primary school. On the demand side, particularly for girls and other marginalized groups such as pastoralist children (boys and girls), many parents prefer child marriage for female children and marginal gainful employment for male children as more prudent life choices. These perceptions and attitudes need to be more fully addressed, especially in the light of a limited national education budget and an increasing reliance on short-term donor funding rather than local solutions to improve education service delivery. School coverage rates in South Sudan remain well below the regional average with gross enrollment rates (GERs) of 10 per cent at pre-primary level and 72 per cent at primary level. The current trend in female enrollment is particularly disconcerting with the Gender Parity Index (GPI) ranging from 0.92 in pre-primary to 0.68 in primary and, ultimately, 0.46 in secondary level. Overall, school life expectancy, or years of education completion for a South Sudanese child is five years; again, well below the regional average. Any modest gains in enrollment are immediately undercut by dropout rates of over 10 per cent. Thus, the number of out-of-school children in South Sudan is likely to remain the highest in the world at 72 per cent if access to quality education services is not increased.

2 - Activities

To address the main educational needs and priorities described in the needs analysis, the project aims to ensure boys and girls aged 3 -18 years, particularly the most vulnerable and those affected by conflict have increased and equitable access to quality education services. The project will carry out the following major activities:1. Provide appropriate learning opportunities for 709,002 (40 percent girls) children and youth aged 3 - 18 years affected by humanitarian situations to quality education services in safe and protective environment. Learning opportunities will range from pre-primary/early childhood education (ECE) to basic education and will cut across both the formal and non-formal sectors to reach the greatest number of vulnerable young children and youth (boys and girls). 2. Improve learning outcomes to enable learners (boys and girls) reach their full intellectual and emotional potential. Emphasis on the foundational skills of literacy and numeracy will increase academic success, while life skills and psycho-social support that improve communication, interaction with others, critical thinking and readiness to learn will contribute to emotional and social maturity. Specifically, the project will:a) Conduct social mobilization and community sensitization through Back to Learning campaigns to enroll out of school children;b) Establish/rehabilitate 360 safe and protective learning spaces, including gender-sensitive WASH facilities for pre-primary and primary education.;c) Conduct capacity building of 5,648 (30 percent female) teachers and volunteer teachers through orientation and induction training on EiE, pedagogy, conflict sensitive programming, life skills and peacebuilding;d) Provide monthly incentives to teachers and volunteer teachers (post-IMPACT);e) Conduct capacity building of 5,592 (30 percent female) PTA/SMC members on codes of conduct, hygiene promotion, and school-related gender-based violence; andf) Engage adolescents and youth for peace building and conflict resolution through life skills promotion activities.

6.6 - Will beneficiaries' rights be legally recognised?

Working with the "Do No Harm" principle, the project will ensure that the rights of the beneficiaries are recognized and safeguarded. Teachers will be trained on the Teachers' Code of Conduct to mitigate encroachment of the rights of the beneficiaries.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

The project will ensure that displacement-affected communities (IDPs, returnees and host) access/benefit from the interventions. Perspectives of the direct beneficiaries will be taken into consideration during the implementation of the project.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

The project is linked to government/community systems. Long-term interventions for sustainability will require parents and communities to take on greater responsibility for initiating and sustaining their children’s education. Parents will need to understand that education is a better life choice for their children than child marriage or marginally skilled employment. Furthermore, prioritizing education for their children requires their commitment to engage with their children’s learning and create a culture of education in their community. Through social mobilization and community sensitization, the project will ensure that education is understood at the family and community levels as a critical pathway to peace and in the best long-term interests of children, family, and community.

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

Through the project, support to national and sub-national platforms and mechanisms will encourage the necessary dialogue, partnerships, and local solutions around which education stakeholders will coalesce and maintain conditions for sustainable education. Where these entities already exist, they will be strengthened, and where they do not, they will be created.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

Children (boys and girls) in areas with military operations and inter-communal conflict will continue to be marginalized, denied their right to education and risk recruitment as child soldiers or children associated with armed forces and groups (CAAFAGs). The project will mainstream peacebuilding activities, including within other sectors beyond education, and continue illuminating the plight of the children and their right to education. The project will ensure that Parents-Teachers Associations and School Management Committees are established and the members trained on school management and development. Teachers will also be trained on learner centered pedagogy, education in emergencies, psycho-social support, and peace-building.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

With the active involvement of the Parents and Teachers Associations in the management of the schools to ensure that they are safe and protective, the community will have ownership of the project.

8.18 - Please indicate how the risks selected above will be mitigated.

Attacks on school is one of the grave violations against children's rights. In conflict situations, schools are often occupied by armed forces for shelter and military operation base. Schools previously occupied by armed forces become exposed to the risk of contamination with explosive hazards. This can be mitigated by advocacy for military to vacate occupied school and refrain from use of schools for military purposes. Vacated schools should be cleared by mine action groups before their re-use for education services. On the military use of schools, the Security Council in its resolution urged parties to conflict to refrain from actions that impede children’s access to education, and specifically requested the Secretary-General the continuation of monitoring and reporting on the military use of schools. The use of schools for military purposes puts children at risk of attack and hampers children’s right to education, resulting in reduced enrollment and high drop out rates, especially among girls and may also may lead to schools being considered targets for attack (Children and Armed Conflict: Working Paper No. 1). Interference of external stakeholders in impartial and needs-based selection of beneficiaries can be mitigated by the use of needs-based community participatory approaches to identify project sites. To abate interference by individual leaders, the community members will play a key role in identification of the beneficiaries.
