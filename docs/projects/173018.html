Project Summary

Migrants living in Libya face significant protection risks for a wide range of reasons, including violence, exploitation, hazardous living conditions, and abuse at the hands of smugglers and traffickers. IOM will work to ensure that migrants requiring specialized assistance as well as mental health and psychosocial support are appropriately supported while alleviating suffering and maintaining human dignity. The project’s main line of work is related to service provision to vulnerable individuals on the one hand, and capacity building of duty bearers and contributing to an enabling environment for migrants on the other hand. In addition, in depth analysis underpinned by accurate data is crucial and IOM will produce evidence base that can serve to inform programming and advocacy purposes as well.

1 - Needs

According to the Libya Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO), in 2021, an estimated 102,000 migrants face protection risks and violations and need assistance. Continued insecurities, violence and the COVID-19 pandemic have augmented pre-existing risks and vulnerabilities of migrants in Libya. According to the Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM), unemployment and lack of access to livelihoods remain major risk factors affecting migrants in Libya. This has led to multidimensional vulnerabilities with cross-sectoral implications such as increased food insecurity, reduced access to social services, and overall reduction positive coping capacities and resilience. COVID-19 prevention measures, such as mobility restrictions, have deepened socio-economic vulnerabilities for many migrants, including limiting their access to livelihood generating activities, impeding mobility, and amplifying psycho-social stressors.The lack of access to safe spaces is associated with an array of secondary protection risks for migrants, including exposure to violence, arbitrary arrest, physical abuse, robbery, abduction, human trafficking, and psychological distress. The lack of clear legal status in Libya and formal documentation impacts migrants’ ability to meet their basic needs, as well as accessing specialized protection assistance. Migrants are also at risk of being arrested and detained by law enforcement and militias at any time. They are arbitrarily arrested, kidnapped, ransomed, and trafficked for forced labor and sexual exploitation by militias. The recent sporadic periods of confinement due to the evolving impacts of COVID-19 and associated government mitigation measures have greatly impacted people’s mental health and psycho-social wellbeing. It has also increased the risk of exposure to Gender Based Violence (GBV) and other forms of abuse, particularly for migrants and IDPs. These concerns are highly gendered, with women and girls from migrant and internally displaced persons (IDP) communities more at risk of experiencing GBV. Providing psycho-social assistance to migrants and displaced persons in educational, cultural, community, religious settings may reduce psychosocial vulnerabilities, whose stagnation might otherwise result in social pathologies, community risks and individual mental disorders. Migrant and displaced populations include individuals with pre-existing psychiatric, psychological, and psychosocial vulnerabilities, whose effects may be enhanced by migration, due to inaccessibility of adequate services, stigma, language barriers.According to HNO 2021, around 271,000 children and caregivers, including 44,000 migrants, are at risk of violence, exploitation, and abuse, requiring sustained child protection services. Unaccompanied and separated children are at heightened risk of human trafficking, forced labor or sexual exploitation and face arbitrary detention in centers that operate under inhumane conditions. They face greater risks of child labor, economic deprivation, and poverty.The need for child protection services, such as case management, community-based support, family reunification, MHPSS support is necessary for both children and their caregivers. Migrant children in Libya have specific vulnerabilities linked to their status and, in some cases, nationality and race. As a result, migrant children face challenges in accessing basic needs like formal education or/and primary healthcare. The general insecurity in Libya, combined with their poor living conditions, family separation, inability to access safe spaces and limited recreational activities has had an impact on their mental health and psychosocial wellbeing. Children and adolescent are likely to adopt negative coping mechanisms. Reinforcing existing child protection community mechanisms and enhancing the capacities of parents/caregivers and psychosocial workers will be critical to mitigate protection risks experienced by children.

2 - Activities

This project will support a total of 18,100 migrants and 135 persons including humanitarian workers, CSO members, local/national authorities, community committee leaders for a total budget of USD 3,440,800 through the following activities: 1.Support collation of data, research and monitoring modalities on protection risks faced by migrants in Libya to inform advocacy and programming. Through the support of internal in depth studies, conducting comprehensive research and analyzing protection data. 2.Build capacity of duty bearers, partners and community members on protection and MHPSS through trainings, workshops, roundtables and study visits. 3.Provide specialized services for vulnerable migrants in Libya.This includes conducting protection assessments, provision of tailored assistance like shelter, family linking calls, family tracing and reunification, best interests assessment (BIA), best interests determination (BID) and referrals to other specialized services. IOM will also provide multipurpose one-time cash assistance for extremely vulnerable cases. 4.Provide direct MHPSS services such as basic counselling, individual/group support, awareness raising, psychological first aid, art based psychosocial interventions, psychosocial assessment and referrals of the most vulnerable migrants at disembarkation points/detention centres and urban locations to specialized mental health services or other services, remote MHPSS support through MHPSS helpline. 5.Establish safe spaces for vulnerable migrants. These shelters represent safe places where vulnerable migrants will be supported with tailored assistance and a safe return is ensured. 6.Expand community-based placement programmes for the most vulnerable migrants. IOM has established a network of families from migrant communities who host vulnerable migrants including UMCs, victims of exploitation and abuse, survivors of GBV. 7.Raise awareness on prevention strategies relating to protection risks.

5 - Mitigation measures
5.1 - For each risk mentioned under 4 above, please describe the ways you plan to mitigate, prevent and/or address these.

4.1: IOM Hotline managed by staff trained on how to respond to disclosure of protection incidents; Common Feedback Mechanism; Regular FGDs and outreach activities. 4.2: Assistance provision is based on need determined by assessment; Transparency ensured through IOM hotlines where migrants can provide feedback; Participation of the community through consultation;; Mainstreaming protection and MHPSS considerations in service provision; 4.3: All interventions are framed according to humanitarian principles of impartiality; 4.4:switching to telephonic case management services to avoid large gathering and minimizing in-person interactions. provision of phones to a limited number of cases. Adopting COVID-prevention measures to allow a continuity of services. MHPSS remote services are provided through the helpline, and tailoring MHPSS group activities to smaller groups with all COVID19 preventive measures in place. 4.5: IOM’s General Procurement Principles and Processes stipulates non-exploitation of child labor as a prerequisite for vendors. 4.6: Since some of the assessments occur at the detention center there are concerns to the migrants’ safety if privacy is not adhered to. However, all IOM protection and MHPSS staff are trained and adhere to IOM’s data protection measures and are aware of the necessary minimum requirement before proceeding with assessments. 4.7: IOM follows the existing guidance on humanitarian interventions in detention such as the mitigation measures agreed under the HRDDP and the Principled framework on interventions in detention centers. 4.9:IOM maintains its independence in all decision making and operates in locations with the understanding that there will be no interference; 4.10: IOM adopts age, gender, and diversity considerations in all areas of programming to ensure equal access of services. IOM will integrate measures to ensure that the principles of do no harm, safety and non-discrimination guide any activity and are adhered to throughout all interventions, in line with its Protection Mainstreaming guidance and Framework for Addressing GBV in Crises. 4.11:Protection is mainstreamed in all areas of programming to ensure meaningful access to services being provided by all migrants, and barriers in accessing to services for persons with disabilities are removed. MHPSS mainstreaming planned as well. 4.12:MHPSS mainstreaming and awareness on mental health issues to reduce stigmatization related to mental health disorders. 4.13:Gender is mainstreamed in all areas of programming to ensure meaningful access to services being provided by all migrants. Programming is tailored based on feedback provided by the target population, prioritizing safety, security and dignity and avoiding causing harm. 4.14:Gender and GBV risk mitigation considerations are integrated in all areas of programming to ensure safe and meaningful access to services being provided to all migrants. All IOM staff complete mandatory PSEA training, and internal monitoring and reporting mechanisms are in place.4.15:IOM staff and affiliated workforce are trained on PSEA on how to safely report. Regular induction sessions on PSEA, posters and flyers dissemination. IOM also has a global online complaint platform. 4.18: IOM’s assistance is not contingent on providing identity documents but based on identified needs. IOM provides assistance to access consular services and the issuing of identity documents. 4.19:IOM provides assistance to access consular services and refers to the embassy on certain issues referrals to partners providing legal assistance. 4.22:IOMprovides protection counselling and refers to other protection actors if needed. IOM chairs the MHPSS TWG, mapping of MHPSS actors is being conducted to develop MHPSS referral mechanism linking with protection, GBV and CP actors. 4.23:IOM provides protection counselling to explain possible assistance, limitation and duration to avoid creating a situation of dependency.

6.2 - Other accountability mechanisms

Capacity building of staff on protection core concepts, risk identification and mitigation, and responding to a disclosure. IOM currently operates a hotline that allows migrants to reach out to inquire on the status of their assistance, as well as provide any feedback on the services they have received or any issues that they might have. IOM is also a part of the Common Feedback Mechanism to ensure that beneficiaries are also able to go through independent complaint mechanisms. IOM’s ensures all staff are trained on protection core concepts as part of its approach to implementing and mainstreaming accountability throughout its work and to ensure quality, safe and responsive programming. IOM also has a zero tolerance against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (SEA) and other forms of misconduct. IOM ensures the participation of the beneficiaries through focus group discussions and outreach activities, throughout the program cycle in order to take into consideration the evolving needs of affected populations and communities. IOM employs a variety of monitoring and evaluation tools, such as post distribution monitoring and program monitoring.
