Project Summary

The project aims at responding to the protracted needs of refugees through strengthening the community based mechanisms and responses. An instance of this is protection whereby ADRA will capitalize on the existing systems and mechanisms in the community, further enhancing their understanding and ability to address protection concerns through capacity building. Furthermore, ADRA will ensure an integrated approach in assistance. This is one of the main reasons that ADRA has prioritized specific locations. Access to water supply especially in the new extensions in Al-Jamya and El-Keshafa are important to counter both COVID-19 and other waterborne diseases. An assessment by ADRA shows that nearly 78% lack access to clean water in those camps. Assessment also indicated that nearly 91% of SSR prefer household latrines as they are more private and easier to clean and maintain.
To address the emerging Ethiopian Crisis, ADRA will provide emergency water supply, and NFI assistance in the camps in Gedarif and Wad Almahi. ADRA will also compliment this with Emergency NFI Shelter assistance.

1 - Needs

As of November 2020, Sudan hosts over 1 million refugees from South Sudan, Eritrea, Central African Republic, Ethiopia and other countries, including over 133,000 refugees, mainly Eritrean and Ethiopian, living in nine camps and urban areas in East Sudan. Participatory Assessment findings for refugee communities across all States indicate that manyrefugees are concerned about the prevalence of sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV) in theircommunities. SGBV risk is aggravated by inadequate lighting in camps/settlements, limited access toenergy and water supply gaps that require women and girls to travel long distances to collect water andfirewood, and lack of toilets. The lack of livelihoods opportunities, the deteriorating economic situationand COVID-19 pandemic further undermines the safety and well-being of refugee women, girls and boys.Early marriage and survival sex are among negative coping mechanism practiced across differentnationalities. Young men and women, boys and girls are often physically and sexually assaulted bytraffickers/smugglers. Refugee survivors of SGBV are treated within traditional justice system as they havevery limited access to formal justice and legal aid, and there remain significant gaps in medical and othersupport services. FSL:The economic downturn, currency depreciation as well as the consistently high inflation provide a difficultenvironment to improve refugee’s livelihoods in Sudan. In addition to the unfavourable externalconditions, refugees continue to face challenges to access work permits, despite improvements onrefugee eligibility to work. The available livelihoods opportunities for refugees are largely in the informalsector with high uncertainty of daily labour accompanied by high risks of exploitation, sexual harassmentand abuse. Consequently, the lack of income also undermines refugees’ housing security, with manyreporting being unable to cover increasing costs for services and food. In camp refugees depend on assistance, and whatever meagre labor costs they are able to get in the harvest season through working in the field. In White Nile and according to an assessment conducted by ADRA, 87% of the households undertake harmful coping strategies such as skipping meals (especially for Pregnant and lactating women) and collecting and selling firewood to secure money for food. Many of the refugees have not been able to achieve dietary diversity and malnutrition in the camps remain high. In urban areas, and due to the limited skill set of many of the refugee households, securing paid jobs is difficult and so households resort to negative coping strategies. Food needs are a compounded need that is often also associated with protection risks. WASH:Wash has been identified as Education:Education needs in Sudan remain high with about 67 per cent of primary-school aged refugee childrenremaining out-of-school and estimates of above 90 per cent being out-of-school in more remote refugee hosting areas outside of camps among South Sudanese refugee children. In addition, over 90 per cent ofrefugee children eligible for secondary school are out-of-school. Refugee children in East Sudan, Darfurand in Khartoum also lack access to inclusive and equitable education. Especially outside of camps lack ofaccess to livelihoods and household income is a key barrier to refugee children attending school as familiesstruggle to pay for school fees, uniforms and supplies. Additionally, it is also common for children to haveto work and provide additional income for their families, preventing them from attending school andaccessing feeding programmes in refugee-hosting schools. There is also a need to mainstream refugeeeducation access within the national education system as much as possible to support equitable access toquality education for refugees living outside of camps in urban and out-of-camp areas.

2 - Activities/Outputs

FSL:1- Refugee population (urban and camp) receive non-conditional multi-purpose Cash assistance. ADRA will provide a minimum expenditure basket. 2- Providing vocational training and community saving schemes for urban refugees. Literacy and Education:1- Rehabilitation of schools, and provision of school supplies to in camp and out of camp refugee populations. 2- Literacy circles for urban refugee females. WASH:1- Drilling of boar holes. 2- Provision of clean and safe water to refugee populations through rehabilitation of hand pumps and tap stands in White Nile and Central Darfur. 3- Installation of household level latrines. 4- Cash for work sanitation activities for cleaning of existing shared sanitation facilities. 5- Provision of hygiene information. 6- Provision of hygiene NFI. Shelter:1- Emergency Shelter for incoming refugees. 2- NFI assistance to refugees. Protection:1- Community based protection groups for referral and community based PSS. 2- PSS counselling.
