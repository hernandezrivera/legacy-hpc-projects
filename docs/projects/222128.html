Project Summary

In response to food insecurity and malnutrition driven by recurrent and overlapping sub-national violent conflict and climate-related shocks, including extreme flooding and severe drought, CRS will meet life-saving needs through humanitarian interventions to protect or restore food production and livelihoods in Duk, Ayod, and Akobo counties of Jonglei state, and the Greater Pibor Administrative Area (GPAA).
CRS’ interventions will complement emergency food assistance from WFP (which is implemented by CRS in most of these counties) or other actors, supporting crisis-affected households to restore and maintain food production through the end of 2025, helping to ensure they access adequate nutritious food. This will contribute to preventing hunger and malnutrition, as well as preventing further deterioration of food security and livelihoods for target HHs until the harvest season of 2025. CRS will provide farmers with key inputs, training, or support they need to: 1) ensure a strong harvest of wet-season vegetables and field crops in late 2025, 2) produce nutritious vegetables and/or utilize small livestock during the dry season to access a diverse and nutritious diet in interim months before harvest, especially during lean season when food security conditions will be most severe. 3) In areas where the risk of flooding is high and with the potential to disrupt the livelihood of agro-pastoralists, CRS will provide participants with fishing kits and training to engage in fishing and help meet their immediate food and nutrition needs. These interventions are primarily aimed at ensuring food access to meet nutrition needs in order to save lives, reduce suffering, and prevent harm to vulnerable communities and families. These interventions will also contribute to protecting resilience gains from previous projects, and encourage early recovery of livelihoods through restoration of productive assets and income.
CRS will build on learning and success of its recent/ongoing WFP, BHA, and EU funded projects, leveraging existing farmer-producer groups and fishing groups and trained Field Extension Agents (FEAs) in the project areas. The project will benefit from CRS’ strong relationships with key stakeholders, established presence in the target communities, and strong understanding of each local context. CRS will coordinate to complement emergency food and livelihood assistance interventions programmed by World Food Program (WFP) and FAO.
The project will adhere to a “Do No Harm” approach while considering the unique vulnerabilities and capacities of men, women, boys, and girls. CRS centralizes protection and conflict sensitivity, while integrating robust PSEA measures, including: 1) CRS consults men, women, boys and girls from diverse groups and conducts risk assessments which apply gender and conflict sensitivity lenses, including considering risks around GBV and intra-household conflict/violence. 2) Regular monitoring activities actively gather information about the experience of project participants and other community members during programming, providing the project team with data which is triangulated with field monitoring visits and feedback & community response mechanisms (FCRMs) to understand unintended consequences and adapt programming. 3) CRS interventions target women per vulnerability criteria, and provide services, interventions, and assets that are culturally appropriate for women and girls to utilize, own or govern. 4) All CRS staff as well as local authorities and community leaders are trained on reporting and referral systems in place for GBV, alongside the code of conduct, the free assistance to expect, the targeting/inclusion method. 5) Service points are located to ensure vulnerable beneficiaries can access them with safety and dignity, and without traveling long distances. 6) Beneficiaries and other community members are sensitized, particularly during distributions, on available FRMs and how to report concerns.

1. Needs

The humanitarian situation in Duk, Ayod, Akobo East, and the Greater Pibor Administrative Area (GPAA) remains severe, with food security crisis and emergency conditions driven by large numbers of IDPs and returnees, persistent inter-communal conflict, macro-economic challenges depleting purchasing power, four consecutive years of flooding, and limited agricultural production. Families affected by this emergency are in many cases completely dependent on humanitarian food assistance. Vulnerable populations, especially those that have been displaced or are isolated by conflict or floodwaters face not only hunger but also dramatically elevated risks of malnutrition and communicable diseases; children, vulnerable adults such as pregnant or lactating women, and persons with disabilities are disproportionately affected. IPC Projections for lean season 2025 (April-June 2025) indicate that Duk, Ayod, Akobo, and Pibor counties will experience emergency food security (IPC4) conditions. (IPC predictions released 18 Nov 2024). Given the high likelihood of conflict, flooding, drought, returns/displacement to impact food security in 2025, the severe degradation of livelihoods due to recurrent shocks over multiple years, and the poor macro-economic conditions (including a steady trend of inflation of food prices and devaluation of the South Sudanese Pound), there is every reason to anticipate severe emergency again in 2025. Per the Intersectoral Prioritization shared in Nov 2024, all target counties are ranked as severity class 4 (extreme) and are Priority 1 (Pibor) or Priority 2 for response.Food insecurity is driven by complex factors: in the greater Jonglei area, 5 successive years of severe flooding and persistent localized violent conflict have repeatedly displaced households, disrupted livelihoods, and resulted in the loss of productive assets, while also hindering the delivery of humanitarian assistance. Localized long dry spells in multiple successive years, coupled with cattle raiding and other incidents of violent communal conflict have led to poor harvests of staple crops and depletion of pasture and water resources critical for livestock. The detrimental effects of conflict and disaster related shocks continue to hinder the gains made by previous resilience and development activities. Successive severe shocks have resulted in poor agricultural production, driving immediate food insecurity, interrupting livelihoods, and undermining the ability to absorb and adapt to these shocks. Additional challenges include poor agronomic, farming, and pastoral practices including poor crop storage techniques; lack of extension services and skills for crop production; lack of access to key inputs due to lack of purchasing power, lack of local availability, and challenges to accessing markets. These are underlying drivers of poor agricultural production, reducing communities’ capacity to meet their own food and nutrition needs, and leaving them highly vulnerable to the climate and conflict-driven shocks that disrupt their livelihoods and exacerbate food insecurity. Based on recent Seed System Security Assessment conducted by CRS in Jonglei, CRS feels that provision of seeds is critical to ensure productivity among farmers affected by climate and conflict-driven shocks for multiple seasons, and is a cornerstone intervention to improve food security and nutrition for vulnerable households and communities. As an emergency intervention, distributing seed from the FAO pipeline can be effective to save lives through improved food security, as long as the right seeds are available and are delivered within the needed timeframe. A more ideal solution is to facilitate local farmers to produce high-quality seed; where CRS has already developed producer groups who are effectively implementing seed multiplication systems with quality improved seed varieties, CRS can procure from these local sources or facilitate seed fairs for local seed distribution.

2. Project activities

CRS will provide livelihood support for 10,000 HHs (60,000 individuals to meet their food and nutrition needs). This will include supporting 8,000 HHs to produce adequate nutritious food throughout 2025; supporting 1,000 HHs to have improved production of small ruminants; and supporting 2,000 HHs for improved fisheries production. 1) Distribution of Seeds and Tools for cultivation of wet-season field crops: A wet season crop production kit accompanied with sound agronomic training and mentoring will enable HHs to grow and harvest enough nutritious food and meet their basic needs for at least seven months after harvesting. CRS will carefully assess risk of flooding to ensure strong chance of crop maturity and successful harvest. 2) Distribution of dry-season vegetable farming kits: Vegetable farming kits and training will help ensure early-maturing, nutrient dense crops to promote dietary diversity and availability of food during lean season. CRS follows SOPs for local seed procurement and quality assurance developed under P2R and approved by BHA. To empower farmers to sustainably produce local seed, CRS will include training on seed production/storage to FPGs and provide seed multiplication training to selected groups. Increased income among farmers will stimulate demand for seed in turn, strengthening the market for local seeds. 3) Field Extension Support: CRS will work with seconded Field Extension Agents (FEAs) and livestock LFEAs who will be coordinated by CRS and supported by the State-level Ministries of Agriculture to improve climate smart agricultural and livestock production practices through training and mentoring. 4) Training Farmer Producer Groups: CRS will work with existing Farmer Producer Groups (FGPs), and support training these FPGs by FEAs on climate-smart and nutrition-sensitive agriculture topics. CRS may organize farmer field schools. CRS will emphasize improved Post-Production and Storage Techniques, promoting the use of hermetic Purdue Improved Crop Storage (PICS) bags.5) Small ruminant restocking through livestock fairs: CRS will re-stock small ruminants to households affected by conflict and flooding, primarily female-headed vulnerable HHs. CRS will utilize voucher-based livestock fairs to support the local livestock market, give participants more agency, and allow CRS to respond quickly. 6) Training Livestock Producer Groups: CRS will support existing Livestock Producer groups (LPGs) or organize new ones. The LPGs will be trained on key practices, and in some case CRS will organize pastoral field schools to promote climate smart livestock production practices. LPGs will be trained by the LFEAs who will be trained and directly supported and accompanied by CRS staff based in the field.7) Distribution of Fishing Kits: CRS will distribute fishing kits to conflict/flood affected HHs (nets, hooks of approved standard by the line ministry) to kick start fishing activities where feasible. The immediate benefit will be improved food and nutrition status, with potential to positively impact the whole community, as the sale and trading of fish will contribute to the functioning of local markets.8) Training of Fisher Producer Groups: CRS will organize fisherfolk into Fisherfolk-based Producer Groups (FBPGs) to sustain their current productive activities which are essential to food security in these communities, as well as to continue to build their nascent capacity. CRS will provide basic training on improved fishing methods as well as fish handling, processing, and storage, including fish preservation skills training Reducing loss and waste will have a greater impact on food security, and may also increase revenue from excess fish, to only to meet basic food needs but also to restore other productive assets.
