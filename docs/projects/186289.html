Project Summary

Street Child will continue to build on previous humanitarian support interventions with a holistic livelihood support and recovery package aimed at promoting resilience and early recovery in northeast Nigeria’s BAY States. 10,000 direct beneficiaries (59% women) will be identified from within vulnerable households – for instance female and disability headed households, or households with children who are vulnerable to extreme protection risks – and supported with holistic livelihood support initiatives including cash for work and IGA grants/training and mentorship schemes, building resilience in areas of return and displacement and allowing beneficiaries to rebuild economic capacity and sustainability. 3,000 beneficiaries will in addition be supported in formation of Village Saving and Loan Associations (VSLA), ensuring that the wider communities also have increased access to financial and saving services and promoting cultures of savings that contribute to enhanced financial resilience.
Considering cross-sectoral outcomes, the proposed project will also contribute to child protection outcomes outlined in the HRP by increasing economic resilience of families and reducing risk of survival strategies that breach child protection principles, such as recruitment or exploitation. This cash- and capacity-based support will contribute to additional aspects of the response strategy, targeting extremely vulnerable households including those headed by women, IDP households, households hosting non-birth children, and people living with disabilities.
Within the scope of this project, the following LGAs have been identified for intervention:
Adamawa LGAs: Hong, Mahia, Michika, Mubi North, Mubi South
Borno LGAs: MMC, Jere, Bama, Hawul.
Yobe LGAs: Bade, Damaturu, Gujba, Yunusari.

1 - Needs

The combined impacts of COVID-19 and repeated cycles of violence and displacement, compounded by pre-insurgency poverty and subsistence livelihood, have all contributed to a livelihood crisis in northeast Nigeria. As people return to communities from where they were previously displaced, accelerated by government return policies, there will be an increased acute need for livelihood support that facilitates early recovery and durable solutions that facilitate genuine social inclusion – both for returnees and host communities – in order to prevent any renewed displacement. At present, the increased hardship brought on by COVID and displacement are straining inter-communal tensions between IDPs, host communities and returnees and any intervention in livelihood will be required to take these social dynamics into account as well as taking steps to mitigate and overcome them. The 2021 Nigeria Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP) identifies 2.1 million people in need of livelihood and recovery support, of which 0.3 million have been targeted for intervention. Children face considerable risk of safeguarding and Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC) breaches including SGBV, early and forced marriage, child labour, recruitment by armed actors, and other forms of exploitation. These risks are magnified in households affected by poverty and loss of livelihood or income, particularly households displaced by armed violence, as children are forced into income-generating activities that are often exploitative to support the family unit. In addition to utilizing children as sources of income generation, other negative coping mechanisms employed by households in financial distress include sale of productive assets, purchasing of food on credit, and borrowing of money from informal sources. Cash support, particularly when accompanied by IGA grants and training, address the most urgent needs identified by community members in recent surveys that their most urgent need is financial support to boost liquidity and allow them to rebuild inventories and repurchase assets.

2 - Activities

The proposed project identifies three key activities that are harmonized and complimentary in order to build resilient economic networks within target communities. 3,064 households (66% female-headed households) will receive an NGN 50,000 cash grant to either start a micro business or help to rehabilitate a pre-existing business. The grant will be divided into a NGN 40,000 conditional payment to be injected directly into the business, while a “top-up” 10,000 is provided unconditionally so that families have liquidity in the first month of the project to address urgent household expenses. This split payment helps to ensure that business grants are fully saved or invested within the business, improving resilience. Trained livelihoods officers will carry out assessments of each beneficiary household’s income and baseline business capacity, before developing a tailored and individualised business plan for how to utilise the cash transfer most effectively. To further support beneficiaries and ensure maximum longevity and resilience, two cycles of four-day training will be held utilising Street Child’s proven "Family Business Scheme (FBS)" model, building beneficiary capacity in business administration and strategy such as bookkeeping, saving, investment and business strategy. All business support training will be informed by a market analysis, held before training takes place, that identifies key areas for training relevant to the current market needs of the community where intervention is taking place. A cash for work scheme, supporting approximately 3,898 beneficiaries (49% women) will employ unskilled and semi-skilled workers in target communities to rehabilitate damaged infrastructure – for instance drainage and irrigation systems, roads and paths, and public spaces such as markets. This rehabilitation will facilitate access to markets and income for small businesses also targeted under the intervention, for example by providing common spaces for community members to trade and sell produce, particularly with people from outside the direct community. Finally, development and support of Village Savings and Loan Associations (VSLAs) in the target communities will further strengthen the community's economic resilience, with access to credit and communal financial services. Beneficiary households will benefit from increased wellbeing with improved ability to budget and save for out-of-pocket expenses, and reduced risks of negative economic coping strategies involving children. VSLAs will be formed by a total of 3,038 beneficiaries (65% women), who will receive resources and materials as well as training on how to effectively run the VSLA.

8.6 - Please describe briefly the modalities your project will put in place to ensure protection mainstreaming (particularly referring to the above questions).

Protection concerns will be mainstreamed throughout all project activities, regardless of implementer, to ensure the safety and dignity of all beneficiaries and community members associated with the project. Any unintended consequences of our work will be closely monitored to be able to avoid or prevent harm to the beneficiaries or communities where we work. A comprehensive risk assessment will be carried out by SC's Security Advisor to identify possible risks, and to develop necessary precautions for smooth project management. For example, a protection assessment will be conducted with all consortium partners and specific SOPs followed prior to any distributions of items to ensure the safety of beneficiaries and communities. This will include special consideration to particularly vulnerable groups, including people with disabilities, and will include specific consideration of the varied needs of and risks facing different groups of children and protection against the spread of COVID-19. We will include participatory assessments in the early stage of the project to facilitate diverse community voices.SC's clinical psychologists and MHPSS department will provide specialised support for GBV survivors or referral to specialised actors as necessary. All staff involved will go through regular training on safeguarding, PSEA and understand the code of conduct to work with children and with accountability towards affected population.Street Child is also a member of the PSEA Working Group and the focal person representing the organisation is also the safeguarding focal person and is involved in this project. Any incident report will be also shared with the WG who request regular reports.Moreover, equal and impartial access to services will be ensured by putting in place clear processes and procedures for selecting beneficiaries and providing support solely on the basis of need and predetermined vulnerability criteria. These criteria will be developed by the communities, and information on relevant services and feedback mechanisms disseminated in each community. Assistance will be provided to both host community and IDP community members to avoid discrimination. SC will continue to use the approved Child Protection Information Management System (CPIMS) and Gender Based Violence Information Management System (GBVIMS) for case management to protect the data, privacy and security of at-risk women children. More generally, SC has policies in place to safeguard the confidentiality of beneficiaries and protect them from potential protection risks such as discrimination or stigma.

9.5 - How will your project ensure feedback is collected from the most vulnerable community members?

Proven feedback mechanisms, which are already widely used and understood by stakeholders and beneficiaries in northeast Nigeria, will be utilised at all stages of the project cycle. At the planning stage, a needs assessment will be conducted in targeted communities and utilise focus group discussions (FGDs) to collect direct feedback from a diverse range of stakeholder constituencies, including gender, age, disability, and other group markers. FGDs will take place at both baseline and endline assessment, for community members to give feedback on the project life cycle and to allow SC to measure community satisfaction. In all target communities, a dedicated complaints mechanism officer (CRM) will be identified and made responsible for collection of feedback and coordinating the sharing and actioning of feedback between Street Child, partners, and community members. The CRM officer will hold weekly helpdesk sessions in each community for any beneficiaries who are unable or unwilling to use other feedback mechanisms. A complaints register, featuring details of complaint and actions taken, will be maintained. Outside of individual feedback, Street Child or project partner staff will attend weekly coordination meetings with community coalitions and structures, VSLAs being made up of women and men including with disabilities. For community members who want to give feedback anonymously, anonymous complaint boxes will be placed in each community and checked weekly. A toll-free line will also be advertised where community members can call at any time to provide feedback or share urgent concerns, for instance acute safeguarding concerns. All feedback received will be fed into review sessions and management decisions, allowing activities to be adapted to respond where appropriate in line with Street Child's commitment to accountability and evidence-based learning.
