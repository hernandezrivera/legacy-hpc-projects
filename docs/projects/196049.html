Project Summary

The project aims to 1) improve the quality, accessibility, and inclusion of existing response services for GBV survivors, especially linkages between case management, PSS, medical care, self-reliance, educational activities, and legal services for the most vulnerable groups 2) mitigate the risk of GBV and SEA through multi-level and cross-sectoral strategies, including capacity building and community-led initiatives and 3) prevent GBV by motivating significant changes in social norms undergirding violence, systematic discrimination, and gender and age inequalities through structured and evidence-based programs. Highly vulnerable and underserved populations will be targeted, such as PWD and gender-diverse populations. UNHCR and partners work based on community-based and survivor-centered approaches, engaging refugees as active agents and emerging leaders within their communities and during individual case management. We will increase women and girls’ participation in decision-making and leadership by building their confidence and capacities with skills-building and vocational training sessions, engaging female refugees of all ages in our CBP work, and involving men and boys as allies in promoting gender equality. Key components of the 2023 GBV programming include improving the quality of safe spaces, delivering life, vocational skills, and continuous MHPSS, enhancing community volunteers’ capacities on how to work with people with disabilities and GD population, EMAP, encouraging women-led initiatives, and delivering women’s leadership training to make them empowered and safe to make their own life decisions. UNHCR and partners continue to provide case management and specialized PSS services for survivors of GBV, with particular attention to strengthening the survivor-centered care practices within the multi-sectoral referral network and increasing accessibility for and capacity to serve target populations, gender, and age wide. Also, a more integrated approach to GBV response services is prioritized, including within health centers, legal service centers, learning centers, and drop-in centers for the LGBTIQA+ population and sex workers. This approach will streamline our services and improve free access to key populations. Capacity-building components include caseworkers’ minimum training package on case management, confidential referrals, comprehensive follow-up, supervision, diversity, and safeguarding GBV cases. Training sessions are also prioritized to sensitize health, legal, site management, law enforcement service providers, and community members on how to identify and refer GBV and SEA cases safely. GBV risks will be mitigated through a three-tiered strategy: working with the community; UNHCR multi-functional; and inter-agency levels. Community-level work will include GBV risk mapping and the generation of risk mitigation measures by refugee women and girls, with community GBV risk mitigation actions. UNHCR’s multi-functional work will develop GBV risk mitigation action plans across functional units, using safety audit tools to assess and measure GBV potential risks. Finally, the inter-agency work will be done in collaboration with other agencies to mainstream GBV risk mitigation across all eight sectors in the Rohingya humanitarian response and an initiative for PSEA. UNHCR and its partners will implement structured and evidence-based GBV prevention activities, both those that take a whole-of-society approach (SASA! Together) and those that target specific groups (Girl Shine, EMAP, Male Role Models). The multi-year SASA! program is closely coordinated with UNFPA and its partners to avoid operational overlaps. Promoting the centrality of protection and AGD approach, UNHCR and partners will invest in AAP with community-based complaints and feedback mechanisms, SEA risk mitigation through capacity-building and protection mainstreaming across sectors, and targeting highly vulnerable populations.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

Rohingya refugees who fled Myanmar in 2017 were escaping violence that included widespread gender-based violence and sexual exploitation and abuse against females and males, which inflicted lasting traumas on the population. These and more recent arrivals joined an existing Rohingya refugee community in Bangladesh, which shares social norms that maintain and reinforce gender inequalities and systematic discrimination that enable and perpetuate gender-based violence, especially against women and girls within their communities. According to the main findings of community consultations conducted in 2022, the risk of rape and sexual abuse was the top concern among girls 18 years old and under, followed by child marriage and human trafficking. Although the risk of rape and sexual abuse remains high across all age groups, statistics on intimate partner violence (IPV) increase as a concern with age. The threat of trafficking is also most significant for young women and adolescent girls. Assessments over consecutive years (MSNAs, RGAs, FGDs, GBVIMS) have revealed a grim protection picture. Refugees feel unsafe both within the community and inside their shelters, particularly women and girls. During community consultations, women have reported violence in the home and violence in the community as their top two perceived risks. According to the gender-based violence information management system (GBVIMS), intimate partner violence is the most reported form of GBV. Chronic underreporting means that we cannot assume that what is reported is an accurate reflection of reality. From January to September 2022, new cases were recorded and assessed in GBV facilities funded by UNHCR. From the total, physical assault accounted for over 55% of reported GBV incidents, followed by 24% of emotional violence and 15% of denial of resources cases. From the total, 119 cases of sexual violence were recorded, 99% of female survivors. Social norms restricting females’ public movement create barriers to services and reduce the number of GBV reports. The camp design presents other risks, and access to functional latrines and public lighting remains an important GBV risk.The reduced number of trained humanitarian actors and the lack of coordination among GBV actors correlate with an increased violent crime within the camps, heightening fear and a sense of general insecurity. Parents fear for the safety of their adolescent daughters – including the learning centers and restrict their movements to prevent them from being sexually harassed. Facing closed schools and failing self-reliance activities, parents see marriage as a protective measure for girls under 18. Men in the camps are opportunistically practicing polygamy, while men in neighboring countries are arranging the smuggling of young brides, despite the journey’s grave risks. Human smuggling and trafficking in the region have become ‘feminized,’ with more women and girls forced into dangerous journeys. Pre-existing risks have been further exacerbated for critical populations in host and refugee communities, including gender-diverse populations and individuals engaged in sex work. The discrimination, risks, and lack of services for these populations are identified. Male survivors of sexual violence are similarly underserved, but the GBVIMS has captured an increasing trend of male survivors seeking services. Despite protective legislation, access to justice for GBV survivors—both refugee and host—is limited by justice system inefficacy and distrust. Rohingya refugees are reluctant to seek remedies in the formal legal system. Most households reported that they would refer a friend who was sexually assaulted to a Majhi or a CiC. Informal, gender-biased resolution mechanisms further entrench unequal gender norms and disempower women. Success in women’s empowerment has also been threatened by backlashes targeting female leaders and volunteers.

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

The proposal is aligned with the rationalization principles and process. UNHCR was actively involved in all discussions led by the GBV sub-sector and thematical working groups around this process. To actively contribute to the rationalization process, UNHCR staff conducted a massive mapping of GBV facilities operating in all refugee camps with UNHCR presence. As a result, every camp facility conducting GBV activities was identified, and their block coverage, prominent donors, and location. Considering facilities operating under UNHCR funds, comprehensive data on case management and awareness activities were requested. In addition, recent statistics were analyzed to understand better the impact of activities on the community's well-being, and potential gaps or overlapping interventions. By analyzing the case management data, it was possible to observe the specificities of each facility and how community members - especially girls and women, are engaged in the proposed activities. Also, UNHCR monitored all GBV facilities to follow if partners met basic requirements and if activities were being implemented aligned with global Policies and GBV Guidelines. Based on these results, UNHCR decided to close some facilities and merge services to adequate our programming to guarantee a more integrated approach. All UNHCR facilities are open to receive GBV survivors, including LGBTI clients, men survivors, and people with disabilities, following a mainstreamed protection strategy. UNHCR makes all efforts to respect the needed rationalization process without jeopardizing the quality of the service delivered to the survivors. Activities managed by UNHCR and its IPs are inclusive and transparent. Statistics are uploaded monthly on GVBIMS and shared with other stakeholders, members of different working groups, and task forces. Coordination can guarantee good communication among IPs, greater coherence with the Government, and better continuity of services to affected refugees.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection

UNHCR works from a protection, gender, and disability mainstreaming perspective. All activities implemented by partners and by UNHCR are oriented to identify potential protection risks in a confidential manner and make safe referrals to local service providers. All Implementing Partners, as well as UNHCR staff members, works based on the four principles of GBV programming: (i) Safety and Security, (ii) Confidentiality, (iii) Dignity and Respect, and (iv) Non-discrimination. Activities are led by the Do not Harm principle and survivor and rights-centered approaches, and all partnerships are signed based on a previous and clear assessment of the organizational capabilities to prevent and combat SEA/SH during the execution of their contract, in line with the zero-tolerance policy advocated by the UN system.

3.2 - Gender

UNHCR programming is defined based on the 2020 Global GBV Policy, which determines three working pillars: (i) Prevention, (ii) Risk Mitigation, and (iii) Response. Under the Risk Mitigation pillar, UNHCR is working with all operational units to create an objective work plan that considers all potential GBV risks those operations may create or increase to mitigate them timely. As a mitigation measure, UNHCR is enhancing the socio-economical response to GBV incidents by creating self-reliance opportunities for women and adolescent girls. Livelihood activities and vocational training can improve women’s autonomy and technical knowledge, reducing the notable gender power imbalance among Rohingya refugees. Gender is tackled as a cross-cutting area, present in all UNHCR activities, and as a tool to fight against unequal social norms and systematic discrimination.

3.3 - Age including child safeguarding

UNHCR acts to protect children by responding to their specific needs and any risks they may face before, during, or after displacement. In the Rohingya context, child marriage is the main GBV issue identified among girls and female adolescents. Significant efforts are dedicated to coordinating and creating, some campaigns and awareness activities focused on mitigating the risks of child marriage and supporting survivors. As girls and adolescents are highly exposed to different forms of violence and exploitation, UNHCR also works to ensure immediate access to appropriate services and provides durable solutions in the child's best interest. Two priorities are to adapt the GBV case management to a child-friendly model and reinforce our prevention activities. Since September 2021, UNHCR and its partners has implemented the Girl Shine curriculum to offer an alternative life perspective to adolescent girls and their caregivers to develop their skills and confront gender norms.

3.4 - Diversity

UNHCR activities, aligned with its AGD Policy, ensure that beneficiaries are at the center of programs. It undertakes core actions in engagement area: AGD-Inclusive Programming; Participation and Inclusion; Communication and Transparency; Feedback and Response; Organizational Learning and Adaptation and Gender Equality and Commitments to Women and Girls. To promote further empowerment of beneficiaries, UNHCR works based on an inclusive approach to promote gender equality and protect gender-diverse population and PWD, exploitation and abuse. Most vulnerable beneficiaries face barriers to access basic services and are often excluded from education/livelihood opportunities. UNHCR activities are focused on mitigate GBV risks and strengthen mechanisms to ensure AAP and its GBV facilities are child and disable-friendly with trained staff to provide services and case management to survivors, based on an integrated approach without discrimination and respecting confidentiality.

3.5 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

Effective and accountable humanitarian responses require: continuous, meaningful engagement with beneficiaries; understanding their needs and protection risks; building on their capacities; and pursuing protection, assistance, and solutions that consider their perspectives and priorities. UNHCR's responses include continuous and meaningful engagement with beneficiaries by assessing their needs through community consultations and FGDs, and building their capacities. UNHCR has multiple accessible complaints and reporting mechanisms at facilities to support the strengthening of AAP, including PSEA. All UNHCR staff is trained on CoC and PSEA annually, and internal focal points are established in the operation with PSEA Network focal points. To ensure the implementation of a zero-tolerance policy on SEA, humanitarian actors continue to show leadership and build capacity to prevent, mitigate, and respond to risks and SEA through a survivor-centered approach in coordination with the network.

3.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

UNHCR remains a central player within interagency disaster preparedness efforts for the monsoon and cyclone responses and trains community members on how to mitigate risks and quickly respond to climate concerns. When disasters occur, it targets protection services to respond to the needs of those affected, focusing on women, children, and persons with specific needs (PSNs). Its facilities include consideration of environmental and climate concerns and DRR. Protection Emergency Response Units supported by the Protection Sector, continue to engage with the community to disseminate key protection messages, identify urgent cases and referrals, and monitor the situation of PSNs. During emergencies, PERU teams ensure that protection mainstreaming and strengthening of community involvement in all sectors is met, and the response on the ground is in line with Protection principles and sensitive to AGD concerns.
