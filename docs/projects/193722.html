Project Summary

This project aims to support the well-being of social and health service providers, and to provide MHPSS specialized services for women , girls, boys , men and people with disabilities, at risk of GBV and referred cases.
The main outcomes of the project are:
Care for carers (service providers) by providing MHPSS support such as stress management by using WHO self Help protocol and advanced technical supervision.
Enhanced capacity of service providers, social and health workers in shelters, safe spaces, Primary Health Care and emergency departments in general hospitals, in WHO mental health gap action programme (mhGAP) intervention guideline v. 2.0 and GBV medical management guideline.
Enhanced Mental and psychosocial health for people in need through benefiting from specialized mental health services, mainly couples and their families.
The project is built based on the affected community needs and feedbacks, considering the vulnerable groups and women and girls at risk, in addition to UNFPA and WHO partners' experiences and updates from the feld, the established safe spaces, and home visits. Moreover, this project builds by robust experience and evidence the importance of MHPSS mainly after the repeated escalations in Gaza and the unstable political situation in West Bank. Also, both UNFPA and WHO produced many assessments and studies regarding the current MHPSS conditions and status of affected populations in Gaza Strip and West Bank.

1 - Needs

According to HNO 2022, the occupied Palestinian territory (oPt) remains a protracted protection crisis, where too many Palestinians struggle to meet their most basic needs and live in dignity. The crisis is characterized by more than 54 years of Israeli military occupation, lack of respect for international humanitarian and human rights law, internal Palestinian political divisions, and recurrent escalations of hostilities between Israel and Palestinian armed groups. COVID-19 has exacerbated the crises in the past two years. This year has witnessed further deterioration in the humanitarian situation in the oPt due to: 1- The most serious escalation of hostilities between Palestinian armed factions in the Gaza Strip and Israel since 2014. 2- Movement restrictions imposed by Israel, citing security concerns, which impede the access of people and goods into and out of Gaza, hamper the implementation of infrastructure projects and delay economic recovery. 3- An increase in conflict-related Palestinian casualties in the West Bank, including East Jerusalem, and in settler-related violence against Palestinians and their communities. 4- The restrictive and discriminatory planning regime applied by Israel in Area C and in East Jerusalem, which continues to prevent Palestinians from addressing basic housing, livelihood and basic service needs. 5-The intra-Palestinian divide between Hamas and the Fatah-led Palestinian Authority (PA), which remains unresolved, deepening territorial fragmentation and reducing the capacity of local institutions in Gaza to deliver basic services to the population. 6- Intensification of the COVID-19 pandemic, with Gaza experiencing its third and the West Bank its fourth wave in 2021, overburdening the already-stretched healthcare system and aggravating socioeconomic conditions across the oPt.The 2022 People In Need (PIN) was calculated based on the first-ever household-level Multi-Sector Needs Assessment (MSNA), rather than on a harmonized dataset generated from the various cluster needs assessments, as was the practice in past years. Total of 64 per cent of people in need live in Gaza, and 36 per cent of people in need live in the West Bank. Within Gaza, 63 per cent of residents need humanitarian assistance, while 21 per cent of West Bank residents need humanitarian assistance. Mental health and psychosocial (MHPSS) issues also constitute one of the largest, least acknowledged health problems in the oPt. According to WHO World Mental Health Report 2022, COVID-19 has sparked and amplified serious mental health problems. According to WHO brief in March 2022, Covid-19 pandemic triggers 25% increase in prevalence of anxiety and depression, which includes estimates from the latest Global Burden of Disease study, that shows the pandemic has affected the mental health of young people and that they are disproportionally at risk of suicidal and self-harming behaviors. It also indicates that women have been more severely impacted than men and that people with pre-existing physical health conditions, such as asthma, cancer and heart disease, were more likely to develop symptoms of mental disorders. In oPt, Mental health services providers report spikes in calls to hotlines and for phone counselling from people stressed and anxious and threatening self-harm. (according to SAWA, which runs Mental Health and Psychosocial (MHPSS) and Gender-based violence (GBV) helplines across the oPt reported a 30-35 per cent increase in calls in June-July compared to May 2020). As well an increase of substance abuse has been observed by the mental health service providers in MOH.According to AIDA &MDM 2022 study, the context of violence in which Palestinians are living has an impact on the psychosocial well-being and the mental health of the population, even when they are not directly involved in critical incidents. Populations exposed to conflicts are well known to be at risk of increased incidence of poor mental health outcomes. What is sometimes mistaken for resilience is the necessary adaptation to a stressful environment, in which encountering violence is part of the “daily routine” and where acute stress is the norm. Parents and caregivers struggle to ensure the physical and emotional security of their children, when they themselves feel stressed, anxious and depressed about the situation. There is an overwhelming feeling of powerlessness within these communities. Indeed, in the Palestinian context, the collective trauma caused by the military occupation and repression has led to higher levels of distress. Palestinians can suffer from anxiety, sleep disorders or regressions for children, impacting academic progress or developing social skills for instance. Since incidents are happening continuously, the communities find it extremely difficult to have emotional balance and stability. By experiencing mental and physical coercion when they are being forced off their land, it is Palestinians’ relationship to their community and home that unravels and with it, any sense of safety and security essential to a human being.MHPSS is an essential component of a multisectoral response to GBV, and MHPSS outcomes should be integrated into health, justice, education, social services, and other sectoral initiatives that address GBV. There is also increasing evidence around the role that MHPSS can play in preventing violence. While poor mental health and psychosocial well-being are not causes of GBV, promoting protective factors at individual, relationship, and community levels, such as empathy, communication skills, coping, emotional regulation, and nurturing family environments has been shown to be effective in reducing forms of GBV, such as intimate partner violence. When driven by the needs and priorities of survivors and those at risk of GBV, MHPSS can complement and bolster gender transformative prevention approaches that address root causes of violence as gender inequality and power imbalances.

2 - Activities

Output 1 and 2 : Care for carers and service providers, including capacity building and life-skills.Activities: - Provision group supportive supervision sessions for 150 (100 G, 50WB)(75 F, 75 M) of care Caregivers (medical staffs, professionals, etc.) in Gaza Strip and West Bank: - Conduct of (150) 2-hours monthly group supervision sessions for 150 persons formed into 15 groups (10 persons/group). The group supervision sessions will be conducted out of the workplaces. - helping the helpers in MOH for 100 health workers in Gaza 50 (25 F and 25 M) and WB 50 ( 25 F and 25 M); conduct 2 hours monthly on the job support for 5 per session for 10 groups. - building the capacity of MOH health workers 100 ( 50F, 50M) in Gaza and 100( 50F and50M) in WB on WHO mental health gap action programme (mhGAP) , GBV medical management. Output 3: specialized mental health services for cases in need.Provision mental health services and couple specialized therapy sessions for cases in need.- Provide specialized couple therapy sessions for 40 couples in Gaza Strip and WB (25 G, 15 WB). Those couples are cases of the implementing partners, or referred from some UNFPA safe spaces in Gaza Strip and West Bank. Each couple will receive monthly sessions according to the clinical procedures and individual therapeutic plan. The sessions include psychological assessment, psychotherapy sessions and psychosocial support.40 couples (80 individuals): (40 F: 40 M) - Provide an integrated individual treatment plan for cases that includes psychological assessment, psychotherapy sessions, psychosocial support, home visits and medications. They will be referred to by project partners.170 cases (100 G, 70 WB): (60% adults, 40% children) - Build capacity of 80 youths of UNFPA Y-Peer (40 F: 40 M) formed into 5 groups (16-17 persons/ group) on mental health topics, stress management and self-care techniques. Each group will receive five 5-hours daily sessions. Totally 25 hours/ group.Those youth will disseminate the skills wider for children in schools and community summer camps. Each member of the 80 Y-peer and youth groups will target at least 10 boys and girls from schools and vulnerable communities, in coordination with the implementing partners and UNFPA youth partners in other projects. 400 boys and 400 girls (400 G, 400 WB). The project activities inside refugee camps are coordinated with UNRWA, as UNRWA is part of the GBV Sub-cluster and the service directory and referral pathways in Gaza Strip and West Bank. There is robust coordination between UNRWA and UNFPA and other service providers.UNRWA will refer the cases to the implementing partners based on the regular coordination meetings between UNFPA and UNRWA.The refugees will be targeted in coordination with UNRWA and Women Programs Centers under the supervision of UNRWA in the camps in Gaza Strip and West Bank. All the targeted beneficiaries in this project will be aware of the PSEA reporting channels and will receive awareness materials.Also, the relevant communities and indirect beneficiaries will be targeted as well through the awareness about PSEA.UNFPA/WHO will continue raising the awareness about PSEA safe reporting channels through its ongoing projects, the GBV sub-cluster meetings, and other events.

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

Feedback from services’ recipients is valued as essential to improving accountability. For UNFPA, the affected communities are integrated in the working process of the organization as the main and final clients or partners of our services and interventions. Their opinion and feedback is, therefore, essential in achieving success in our work. UNFPA& WHO have MEAL specialists that guide the staff and partners to proper guidance and feedback for the complaints mechanisms. UNFPA& WHO use the CM for ensuring the proper implementation for the activities on monthly basis and each quarter. UNFPA& WHO ensure the accessibility for all targets groups mainly women and girls with special needs such as disability, chronic illnesses and elderly. UNFPA& WHO and IPs are keen and committed to resolving issues, through conversation, sharing information or taking action on the spot. UNFPA& WHO and IPs ensure the outreach of the wider communities and vulnerable groups by adapting and mainstreaming. changing modalities if needed and when required. building trust and transparency between UNFPA& WHO and IPs and the affected population. ensure proper coverage for the locations, especially restricted areas. build on the beneficiaries' feedbacks for upcoming needs assessments, planning and designing other projects. ensure accessibility for beneficiaries in case of emergency situations such as escalations or lock downs with alternatives, such as remote service delivery, updating information of beneficiaries in each partner's database, refer to other service providers in case of not having the service, inform beneficiaries of diverse mechanisms for service delivery. increase field visits when there are no restrictions to meet beneficiaries and directly notice their attitudes, behavior, knowledge, satisfaction and benefit. invest more in service providers' capacity and remote services' infrastructure and equipment, to ensure providing alternatives when needed urgently.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

“Do No harm” is one of the main GBV / MHPSS guiding principles and approaches according to UNFPA / WHO minimum standards and all partners of GBV service providers know how to apply these principles in their work with GBV survivors and MHPSS cases. GBV service providers and GBV SC members received in September 2022 a case management session with UNFPA consultant about the global standards of case management, to protect the identity, personal information, and safety of GBV survivors by not disclosing any personal information about the survivor or the incident to anyone without the survivor’s explicit permission and consent. Also, GBV service providers have robust experience in ensuring GBV survivors and MHPSS cases are protected from harm and in providing psychological and emotional safety to survivors who may be at high risk from the perpetrators or from their family members due to notions of family honor. Additionally, targeting men and boys in most of the interventions will ensure the balance the male to female dynamics in the family and community, and to solve any risky challenges through the male counselling units and the couple therapy. UNFPA conducted different capacity building trainings to GBV SC and other clusters’ members on PSEA, and establish safe and confidential pathways such as SAWA hotline that covers Gaza and West Bank, where we inform women and girls and all target groups to use if any incident of SEA occurred or was suspected.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

The project is designed to provide safe and equal access to adequate MHPSS services for women, men, boys, and girls and for the ones with disabilities through UNFPA and WHO implementing partners of GBV and MHPSS service providers, who are specialized in providing comprehensive services for MHPSS and GBV survivors and the staff received multiple trainings on manuals that focuses on dealing and including people with disabilities, in addition to adapting their safe spaces to be accessible for Women and Girls With Disabilities. Additionally, the service directory for GBV service is available in Braille and sign languages. Also, the project focuses on reaching the vulnerable beneficiaries from different areas of Gaza Strip and West Bank, Additionally, the project will target young youth to provide them with special stress management services to deal with the hard situations and to disseminate these skills to others in their communities, in addition to some special needs and treatment. Moreover, based on UNFPA study about violence on elderly, the project will target elderly people mainly women to ensure their equitable access to MHPSS services due to their specific needs and psychological conditions. On the other hand, the awareness, outreach initiatives, advocacy campaigns and the social media materials will cover wide areas to ensure all targeted communities have a better knowledge about MHPSS, GBV, PSEA, rights of PWDs and other important topics.
