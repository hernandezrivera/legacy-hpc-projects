Project Summary

The continued economic decline in Syria and uptick in security incidents have drastically increased out-of-school (OOS) rates in NES. In 2023, for example, the OOS rate in Ar-Raqqa increased from 22% to 48%; 470 schools are in need of rehabilitation/repairs (HNO, 2024). In 2023, we surveyed 4,306 children (2,305f, 2,001m) in 11 locations in NES, and 3,954 of these – 91.8% (92.1%f, 91.5%m) – were OOS, the most-cited reasons for which being cost (28.8%), displacement (20.0%) and poor quality of education - teaching/curriculum (14.7%).
This project targets populations in Severity 4+ displacement-affected informal settlements/disadvantaged areas. Locations are chosen based on the number of OOSC determined in collaboration with the local Education Committees (ECs) and the Education Working Group, triangulated by NGO's own door-to-door assessments. It will cover two of the governorates with the highest OOS rates in Syria: Ar-Raqqa and Aleppo (38%). The 2023 NES Joint Education Needs Assessment showed that the greatest needs in most categories were in Aleppo and Deir-ez-Zor, which will also be targeted.
The project provides accessible, quality non-formal education facilitating the successful transfer of NFE students to formal schools. This is combined with rehabilitation of formal schools and the provision of WASH facilities. The project tackles barriers to gender and inclusion, and provides an integrated response, offering basic literacy and numeracy (BLN) and social and emotional learning (SEL), and referral to protection projects under the same organisational response for vulnerable students and families. It thereby contributes to the Education Sector’s HRP objectives of increasing education enrollment, inclusion, equity and attainment.
The project equips OOSC with the skills, knowledge and confidence to (re)join formal education successfully. It: (i) provides NFE through accelerated education (ALP), adolescent groups (AG) and homework support (HWS); (ii) rehabilitates formal schools to accommodate NFE graduates; (iii) provides gender-segregated, accessible WASH facilities in schools to remove enrollment barriers.
ALP: Illiterate OSSC aged 8-13 attend at least 6 months of BLN and SEL in NFE centres. We use IRC's Safe Healing and Learning Spaces curricula and our own PSS manuals.
AG: Illiterate OOSC aged 14-17 attend 9 months of BLN/SEL in NFE centres, using SHLS + SLP to support reintegration in higher grades.
HWS: In-school students aged 8-17 at risk of dropout, especially NFE graduates, attend after-school remedial classes in formal schools and NFE centres.
Caregivers of NFE students attend parenting sessions designed to foster safe, caring and predictable home environments conducive to improved children’s well-being.
The project will also lightly rehabilitate two formal schools and install resilient temporary learning spaces in two more. As well as accommodating NFE graduates, this will address gaps in school infrastructure and the safety risks posed by damaged, non-climate-proofed buildings. Schools/TLSs will be equipped with all necessary equipment/materials to make them functional, including furniture. To address gaps in sanitation facilities and clean water, existing WASH facilities will be fixed and additional, gender-segregated and accessible facilities will be added adhering to SPHERE standards in a further 12 schools. Hygiene promotion will be undertaken in all schools.
The project will be implemented by the NGO along with two local NGO partners who will bring local expertise and the ability to reach hard-to-access areas.
BLN and SEL outcomes are monitored through monthly ASER tests and evaluated using EGRA/EGMA and SERAIS baseline and endlines. Caregiver sessions are evaluated by surveys undertaken with caregivers at the beginning and end of each NFE cycle that assess changes in their level of engagement in activities that support home learning. The results are triangulated by similar surveys with children.

0 - Project Summary (extended)

Protection risks include PSEAH, GBV and bullying on the way to and in the NFE centre; perceived discrimination against non-IDPs; non-reporting of protection incidents due to perceived lack of follow-up or risk of stigma/repercussions; bullying, especially of CWDs, in formal schools; older girls being excluded for cultural reasons; child labour and unsafe infrastructure. Mitigation measures include: leverage facilitator-caregiver committees and student councils to identify areas of risk and help design responses, and have staff accompany children from home to NFE centre; include the host community in the programme; promote the complaints/response mechanism (participants directly provide feedback or complaints about programmes, staff and systems via WhatsApp, email, telephone and complaints boxes); involve PWDs in the design of accessible infrastructure and hold awareness-raising sessions in formal schools about the rights of CWDs when rehabilitated classes are handed over; have girls-only AG classes; integrate programming with cash assistance; and consult children, caregivers and teachers on the rehabilitation plans for schools and WASH facilities. Protection risk monitoring is undertaken on a continual basis, and reported monthly.Our Code of Conduct and Associated policies are consistent with the six core principles of the IASC Task Force on PSEA in Humanitarian Crises. The principal PSEAH risks include the solicitation of favours to access the programme, and sexual harassment in the NFE centre or on the way to it. Our strategy for PSEAH includes: train all staff on our Code of Conduct; appoint PSEA focal points in all project sites and offices, establish focal points for referrals and PSEAH in the education team to raise awareness and refresh staff training on prevention/response; use PSEA Network-developed field observation checklists to identify, prevent and mitigate SEA; have equal numbers of male and female facilitators and education assistants so a trusted role model/confidante of the same gender is available. Risks include: conflict escalation, ISIS resurgence, authority-community or inter-community tension jeopardising access; inflation and supply shortages caused by currency devaluation, border closures or conflict; drought increasing hunger and negative coping strategies, including child labour, early marriage and recruitment by armed groups. Assumptions include: continued access to beneficiaries; no significant shifts in control over key locations; markets continue to function; internal and regional borders remain open; goods available locally. Mitigation includes: strengthen operational resilience by partnering with LNGOs able to access conflict-affected areas; shift operations in increased insecurity to nearest unaffected base of operations; full engagement in continuity of services plans to minimise the potential humanitarian consequences of any disruption in services to target area.
