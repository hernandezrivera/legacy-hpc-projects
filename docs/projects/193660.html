Project Summary

CARE in partnership with Palestinian Medical Relief Society (PMRS)/Local Health NGO will respond to this situation with a comprehensive intervention that combines elements of direct health service delivery to the identified vulnerable communities in Area C, East Jerusalem and H2, with a special focus on women, children, elderly, adolescents and people with disabilities (PWD), those in need of reporting and referral of S/GBV, PSEA cases, and the injured as a result of the Israeli settler and military force violence, and it also responds to the ongoing COVID-19 Pandemic through increased community awareness and full coordination with Ministry of Health offices (MoH) in order to support their vaccination process as well as the logistics processes, if and when needed. CARE will continue working with trained community representatives (focal points) and project staff to report/refer these violations using the agreed upon mechanism.
The proposed intervention will be targeting 50 vulnerable communities in Area C, East Jerusalem, H2 and North Jordan valley in the West Bank (Jenin, East Jerusalem and north Jordan valley, Hebron, and south Hebron hills, Masafer Yatta) that were identified by CARE/Partners/ MoH/ and the HNC during the recently conducted HRP 2023 workshops and via mobile clinics working group. All identified locations have serious access issues and lack of any type of health services. The identified locations will be targeted through 4 mobile health clinics 9 health teams that will visiting these communities regularly to provide the needed health services as per the agreed upon program/plan with the Health and Nutrition Cluster (HNC) and MoH.
The 50 locations are distributed as follows: 16 locations in Hebron Area mainly in Masafer Yatta, 13 locations in Jenin areas mainly inside the wall as Barta’a enclave., and 9 locations outside the wall area C, 12 Bedouin communities in East Jerusalem and middle Jordan valley area.
CARE/partners, in close coordination with MoH centrally and at the governorates’ level,
will provide life-saving services in targeted communities in the WB using mobile clinics/teams that offer basic curative and preventive Primary Health Care services (PHC), including women’s healthcare/ SRH (ante and post-natal care, obstetric care, adolescent health, elderly, and post-menopausal syndromes. Midwives added to health teams to enhance health awareness activities and quality of services and referral mechanisms, etc.), general practice care (GP), health awareness with focus on communities’ needs and priorities, including COVID-19 control, prevention and referrals, basic lab tests, home visits, children health and psychosocial support. The team will also be provided with emergency kits that will enable them to respond to emergency incidents in these communities while working in the clinics to ensure best case management and lifesaving before transferring to the hospitals. Additionally, the teams will train selected community focal points (mainly females) on First Aid and provide them with First Aid kits to better respond to emergencies in their vulnerable and isolated communities. The package of services was designed jointly with UNFPA, HNC, the communities and MOH to synergize and unify the services all while building on the long operational and programmatic experience in working with the targeted locations.
The project is designed fully in coordination with other actors, mainly MoH, with which CARE/partners conducted several coordination meetings during August and September 2022 at MoH offices around the West Bank, and along with other INGOs (UNRWA, UNFPA, MAP), community leaders, focal points, beneficiaries, and community representatives, and women groups to capture women and girls’ special needs including PWD.
CARE/partners ensured that the existing data are updated, as well as indicators, that the stakeholders’ feedback is reflected in the proposal, activities that meet community needs and no overlapping.

1 - Needs

TESTThe design of this project has been guided by the assessments of the needs of target communities, based on several key sources, including the data and mapping exercise updated by the Health and Nutrition Cluster for most vulnerable Area C locations that lack any type of health services. CARE and PMRS were among the small group who worked with the HNC to develop and update the matrix of all locations in the West Bank. CARE also has conducted a separate needs assessment that included several meetings and discussions with community members and the MoH district offices in the targeted governorates to obtain the latest data about the situation on the ground. CARE has rigorously reviewed different and relevant documents/studies; such as oPt 2022 Response Plan/HRP 2023 and MSNA 2022. According to all assessments and reports, access to primary health care in the WB continues to be problematic, largely due to Israel’s continued violations of human rights, including settlement expansion activities / colonization of land and fragmentation of communities, evictions and forcible transfers, demolitions and seizures of Palestinian-owned structures, and the alarming increase of settler violence and Israeli security operations, restrictions to movement and access to public services, permits regime, stop work orders, etc., further entrenching the occupation and destabilizing the situation across the entire oPt.”. Moreover, almost two years of responding to the COVID-19 pandemic has had a significant impact on the availability and delivery of other essential healthcare services mainly PHC.According to the MSNA 2022, “In the 3 months prior to the MSNA data collection, 62.6% of West Bank households reported a member of their household having a healthcare need requiring care - with 97.8% of these households reporting a barrier to care as follow Area C 99.9%, H2 (Hebron100.0%), East Jerusalem 100.0%. 42.6% of households reported that at least one member of their household had a chronic illness, and 13.3% of households had a pregnant or lactating household member at the time of the MSNA data collection”. These communities face severe restrictions in accessing primary healthcare, particularly women, children, the elderly and people with disabilities. Without the humanitarian intervention of the mobile health clinics, no alternative PHC services are accessible. In June 2022, CARE reviewed its own findings of the mapping exercise identifying the availability of health services through direct focus group discussions and meetings with stakeholders, including MoH, health care providers, mobile clinics working group and local community representatives taking into consideration the needs between different gender, age and other diverse groups in the identified WB communities. The mapping exercise revealed that approximately 50 communities are lacking any type of health services. Within these communities, poor health outcomes are a result of the compound effect of lack of quality care, shortages in medical supplies and lack of health education and awareness. For instance, the spread of infectious diseases and complications in chronic diseases such as diabetes, has been noted, as well as complications related to pregnancies and post deliveries. Difficult access to PHC, combined with traditional cultural inequities within communities often limit or block women and PWDs’ access to health services.Inadequate care and treatment of reproductive health (RH) problems is common, often leading to complications. The incidents of GBV tend to be under-reported even under the best of circumstances, and in these communities, the prevalent culture often excuses violence against women and discourages survivors from reporting. Also, the communities in Area C are largely excluded from services for survivors of violence. As the provision of health care is often a primary entry point for GBV survivors, the offering of essential primary health care for these communities will offer at risk groups and survivors a vital means to obtain basic health services, information on available GBV hotlines and support, and obtain appropriate referral services. CARE identified the targeted communities, and this was unanimously agreed upon among the Mobile Clinic Working Group members, including CARE, PMRS, UNFPA, MAP UK, UNRWA. The incidents of GBV, SGBV, PSEA, settler violence, demolitions attacks, forced Immigration and imminent threat of eviction, violations of IHL, IRHL and the resulting lack of security, in addition to the di cult access to the health services, are major causes for our interventions that have prompted great needs upon which the proposed project design is based.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

CARE’s ‘Do No Harm’ principles are embedded in all approaches and interventions by CARE, from the ideation process of a project, throughout its duration and after the project is finished. CARE has identified risks, their potential impact and mitigation strategies, and all activities proposed are designed based on numerous discussions with community members, partners and other stakeholders, as well as on needs analyses (i.e. rapid / gender needs assessment) that are sensitive to the complexities of the local context to ensure that people are not exposed to additional risks through our action. CARE is committed to protection and ensuring safety and security of participants, dignity and accessibility of services; CARE’s activities are inclusive, and designed with the community members’ feedback, and the selection criteria for activities are transparent; the data collection processes are sensitive to the context, power dynamics in communities, and also the management of sensitive information is conducted with caution and care. The intervention provides equitable access for women and men to the health services, and members from different vulnerable groups within the targeted communities, such as people with disabilities, elderly, and children under 18. CARE’s reports from previous projects showed that girls and women composed 71% of the total beneficiaries, thus illustrating commitment to equality and the prioritization of safety and dignity as key protection standard where CARE delivers interventions in a manner that ensures that its actions do not cause unintended harm or result in reduced protection of beneficiaries, communities or staff. CARE will address concerns that arise through ‘do-no-harm’ approach and will ensure that its assistance and service delivery is carried out in accordance with internationally agreed standards and humanitarian principles and its own protection standards. Furthermore, regular monitoring activities will measure levels of CARE/partner’s compliance with these standards and take necessary corrective action. The design of the project is built on the previous experiences in working on GBV including identification and case management, provision of services like Psychosocial support and referral as needed in line with the national referral mechanism. The project teams include psychosocial workers that will follow up on all identified GBV cases. The activities also include development and dissemination of PSEA and GBV awareness, training including information on rights and safe complaint channels available to the beneficiaries and the awareness raising targets are developed for all project sites. Moreover, as explained earlier, the Project will adopt a safe FAM that will be shared with the beneficiaries and accessible to different groups in the communities. As part of CARE FAM, the project has a protocol and clear process for receipt and referral of complaints of PSEA and all project staff will be trained on PSEA and other trainings. Moreover, all project’s related contracts include standard clause on PSEA.
