Project Summary

According to FSNMS+ round 27, a total of 8.4 million people (60 per cent of the total population) will be at risk of severe food insecurity in 2022, while only 47.1 per cent of households reported having access to an improved water source in under 30 minutes without facing any protection concerns. The morbidity rate is high among under-five children with 56 per cent of children reported sick from one or more illness (diarrhoea 16.9 per cent, fever 44.2 per cent and cough 28.0 per cent). In 2022, an estimated 2 million people in South Sudan will be at risk of acute malnutrition, including 302,078 children (148,018 boys and 154,060 girls) with SAM, 1,039,214 children (509,215 boys and 529,999 girls) with MAM, and 675,548 pregnant and lactating women with acute malnutrition (AM-PLW). In addition, 50,638 (15.4 per cent) will need treatment for acute malnutrition, including 8,388 SAM children, 26,092 MAM children and 16,158 AM-PLW. According to the FSNMS+ round 27, 26 counties in Jonglei, Unity, Warrap, Upper Nile, Northern Bahr el Ghazal, and Eastern Equatoria) have Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) prevalence above the emergency threshold of 15 percent (very high acute malnutrition level- WHO classification). The barriers to accessing nutrition services included insecurity, flooding, distance and poor road conditions with combination of other factors, including high morbidity, low immunization and vitamin-A supplementation, and poor care practices. The prevalence of acute malnutrition was significantly higher as compared to the same period in 2018 (P<0.0001). The prevalence of malnutrition (GAM) among boys and girls was not significantly different with a WHZ mean of -0.59 z-score for female and -0.64 for male. According to FEWS NET February to September 2020, acute malnutrition has remained at ‘Serious’ (GAM WHZ 10-14.9 percent) or ‘Critical’ (GAM WHZ 15.0-29.9 percent) levels across the country during the post-harvest period, indicating that urgent nutrition assistance is needed, 20 counties in Upper Nile, Unity, Warrap, Eastern Equatoria, and Northern Bahr el Ghazal states are classified as ‘Critical’ (GAM WHZ 15.0-29.9 percent) and 28 counties are classified as ‘Serious’ (GAM WHZ 10-14.9 percent). The proposed activities will improve equitable access to quality lifesaving curative nutrition services through systemic identification, referral, and treatment of acute malnourished boys, girls and pregnant and lactating women among vulnerable & marginalized community and families with malnourished children. It will also provide holistic life-saving and life sustaining nutrition intervention, NRDC has been implementing nutrition intervention in Fashoda and Panyikang Counties through the support from DCA, therefore NRDC intends to continue its nutrition intervention in Fashoda and Panyikang Counties in Upper Nile State through outpatient therapeutic program sites hence provide active community cascading, screening, admission and treatment of severe acute malnutrition to boys and girl. NRDC shall enhance referral services to the effective and most complicated cases to the nearest stabilization centre to enable provide adequate attention to the severely malnourished boys, girls among the internally displaced person, With the organization has strong experience working with most vulnerable community and marginalized community, NRDC shall also provide routine immunization, deworming and vitamin A supplementation services to most vulnerable community. NRDC has strong coordination with organizations operational in West Bank of Upper Nile State and will continually coordinate for better integrated services to vulnerable community looped in poverty and difficult. NRDC with therefore, ensure closer coordination with nutrition national and state cluster as well as Ministry of health, NRDC shall also ensure greater coordination with community while it endeavours inclusion of disabled and gender appropriate approach in the project implementation.

1 - Needs

According to HNO February 2022, an estimated 2 million people in South Sudan will be at risk of acute malnutrition will need treatment for acute malnutrition, including 302,078 children (148,018 boys and 154,060 girls) with SAM, 1,039,214 children (509,215 boys and 529,999 girls) with MAM, and 675,548 pregnant and lactating women with acute malnutrition (AM-PLW). There is increase of 5.2% in 2022 compared to needed treatment for acute malnutrition in 2021. According to the FSNMS+ round 27 results, 26 counties in six states (Jonglei, Unity, Warrap, Upper Nile, Northern Bahr el Ghazal, and Eastern Equatoria) have Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) prevalence above the emergency threshold of 15 percent (very high acute malnutrition level- WHO classification). In 2021, the high level of food insecurity and malnutrition are compounded by new shocks including COVID-19, localized conflicts and floods that affect large parts of the country especially in Jonglei, Warrap, Unity, Upper Nile and Western Equatoria states. SMART 2021, it is estimated that 2,017,344 people (host and refugees) will need treatment for acute malnutrition in 2022. 302,162 children (148,059 boys and 154,103 girls) suffering from severe acute malnutrition, 1,039,472 children (509,341 boys and 530,131 girls) from moderate acute malnutrition, and 675,548 pregnant and lactating women from acute malnutrition. Fashoda and Panyikang Counties was hit by flooding and conflicts Panyikang and Fashoda Counties are among Upper Nile State’s 13 Counties according to CSRF South Sudan has suffered from intermittent conflict attributed to multiple factors. CSRF-has pointed to “5,514 IDPs and 12,614 returnees (Q1 2020),” while HNO 2021 identifies 49,000 out of the 65,300 (=75%) as the people in need (and 19,000 at” Severe” phase). the ravaging effects of the COVID-19 pandemic have now compounded the situation. Consultations between NRDC and local authorities in two Counties between November and December 2021 revealed significant gaps in health service delivery with inadequate preparedness to outbreak response; only 3 functional Health facilities out of original 15 with average walking distance of about 10 hours which is below standard ratio. This is among the factors behind the poor ANC attendance observed in 2021 January to December in Fashoda and Panyikang Counties data review. Even the existing health facilities are greatly improvised with limited staffing and luck basic medical equipment for quality health service delivery. Ongoing Flooding are making health facility access even more difficult coupled with the vast geographical landscape. Limited availability of community based health activities in two counties, in some areas such services have virtually ceased due to lack of funding, affecting vaccination coverage with less than 15% of children under 12 months provided with Penta3 which is below target. NRDC activities and addressing the deteriorating nutrition situation by supporting stabilization Centres, Supplementary Feeding Programs, Outpatient Therapeutic Programs, MIYCF and community outreach activities including vitamin A supplementation and deworming in two counties using both static and outreach strategies. This project will significantly contribute towards the reduction in nutrition related mortality and morbidity improving access to high quality multi-sectoral life-saving nutrition interventions for the most vulnerable populations notably U5 children and pregnant and lactating women. The project will support treatment of acute malnutrition (SAM and MAM) for children below 5 years and PLWs as one of the key need for saving lives of most at risk. Program approaches will include community education, active case detection and treatment, MIYCF in emergencies, deworming campaigns, and mass screening targeting host communities will be complemented with preventive responses like BSFP and MIYCF targeting at risk PLW and children U 5 living in 2 Counties.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

NRDC very well understands and appreciates that Feedback mechanisms are valued by beneficiaries as avenues through which they can exercise voice, and indeed, influence the quality of responses! For this reason, therefore, and as standard practice for NRDC, we endeavor to the extent possible to consult beneficiaries at the start of the project design period…including through/during needs assessments, stakeholder workshops and consultative meetings with their representatives including local authorities. For this reason, therefore, and following from this exercise, we ensure the original project design as envisaged is adapted based on the initial feedback. Such consultations and feedback adaptation practices ensure that projects then address need that are relevant for the beneficiaries, for stakeholders and for the context! The Project Steering Committee (PSC), the feedback and complaints mechanisms, community volunteers…..shall all be important structures for collating beneficiaries’ feedback, documenting the same and ensuring it is shared with the Project Team for purposes of adapting project assistance. Through the regular reports filed during the implementation process (weekly, monthly, quarterly….), we shall discuss and agree on modalities for adaptation of beneficiaries feedback. NRDC also often uses Case Studies as part of the reporting process and will, in doing so, also track vital beneficiaries feedback that shall then be ‘ploughed back’ in the continuing implementation process as a means to improve on accountability and quality of the response. Owing to the COVID-19 pandemic, we shall endeavor to be a little creative and will deploy technology, including platforms like social media, to gather feedback and adapt the same for project assistance. NRDC shall continue to deploy established practices and standards, including for example, the SPHERE standards in ensuring beneficiaries’ feedback become adapted to project assistance. By capturing beneficiaries’ feedback through an ongoing process, NRDC shall promptly respond to emerging beneficiaries’ issues in real time and act upon it before the project ends. Due to the fragile and dynamic humanitarian context in which the response happens, there may be some instances that may require us to fine-tune or make bigger changes to the project mid-stream. Some examples of these instances include:- i) Changes in types of interventions for the context we operate in; ii) Changes in the quality of the services provided; iii) Changes related to the needs of beneficiaries that were not previously identified. It is important to mention that (also in line with counsel from the HNO 2021), “…....the new reality of COVID-19, risk communication and community engagement requires continuous efforts to ensure a two-way dialogue with refugee and host communities and inclusion of COVID-19 messaging through various platforms and feedback mechanisms, including rumour tracking mechanisms and addressing misinformation.”

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

NRDC has the requisite PSEA Policy, complemented with a Gender Policy and a Staff Code of Conduct as a well as an established, fully fledged Protection Department. NRDC develops and or adapts context-specific PSEA outreach and communication materials targeting communities during project implementation, such as the following:  Awareness-raising materials (including print materials) that explain core concepts, such as what can constitute “sexual exploitation and abuse’; the obligations of all actors to prevent and address such acts; protect witnesses, victims and survivors and clarify that protection, assistance and access to solutions are never conditional on sexual favours or relationships;  Materials (including print, video or audio) that share information on where to safely report and seek help, in line with established complaints and feedback mechanisms (CFMs) and referral pathways;  Community engagement tools, such as outlines and facilitators guides to lead community dialogues, role plays or focus group discussions that increase awareness on SEA risks, and the rights of communities and people affected by crises to receive protection and assistance free from sexual exploitation and abuse; and  Other appropriate PSEA information, education and communication (IEC) materials, tools and approaches, as relevant to the local operational and cultural context. NRDC also ensures that activities are planned to ensure that the products and approaches developed are suitable for communicating with women, men, girls, and boys of diverse backgrounds, through means that are appropriate and accessible to all groups in a community. NRDC emphasizes some specific standards of behaviour - and at all times - amongst the organization’s staff, volunteers, community committee members (such as Project Committee Members..), consultants, service providers that NRDC deals with, interns…..; this includes both within working hours as well as outside of standard working hours. NRDC abhors sexually exploitative relationships on account of humanitarian assistance and vigorously disseminates information on the same! It is also the case that NRDC makes it clear to all and sundry (during meetings, staff dispatches /memos, public gatherings….) that any violation of the set standards constitutes gross misconduct. Failure to adhere to these standards or failure to report sexual exploitation or abuse is enough reasons for administrative disciplinary action! As standard practice, NRDC also ensures that PSEA materials developed under the various humanitarian responses become “open source” and publicly available for the benefit of other humanitarian actors and communities (for example, through depositing at various strategic locations like food distribution centres, stakeholder workshops and training venues..). We are also well alive to the fact that the project acknowledges that different groups (the disabled, IDPs, returnees, the chronically ill….) within the same community will have different communication needs and capacities. It is important to point out that for purposes of ensuring adherence, NRDC has established a PSEA Policy Steering Committee (under the stewardship of the Protection Program Manager) charged with monitoring and regularly reporting on progress to implement and observe the PSEA Policy; the report becomes available to NRDC’s Board of Directors and through the Executive Director! In summary, NRDC envisages fulfilling its commitment to protection from sexual exploitation and abuse through some strategic approaches as follows: Awareness, Prevention, Reporting, Response and Promoting a culture of openness, sensitivity and support.
