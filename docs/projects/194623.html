Project Summary

Southern Sudan Healthcare Organization (SSHCO) plans to provide essential healthcare services in two areas: Maar Primary Health Care Center (PHCC) in Twic East County, Jonglei State (JS) and, Mobile Clinics in Mongalla Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs) Camp in Juba County, Central Equatoria State (CES).
In Maar, SSHCO will continue to provide essential healthcare services to the people of Twic East county by operating out of the Maar PHCC. In 2022, the total population for Twic East County is an estimated 118,975. In the previous census, the Pakeer Payam population was estimated to be over 16,000. For our Maar, PHCC, we plan to target 3,130 IDP people to provide essential primary health services targeting:
• IDPs - Targeted male children under 5 =200
• IDPs - Targeted female children under 5 =300
• IDPs - Targeted male children aged 5 - 17 years =500
• IDPs - Targeted female children aged 5 - 17 years=650
• IDPs - Targeted male adults aged 18 – 60 =500
• IDPs - Targeted female adults aged 18 – 60 =800
• IDPs - Targeted male adults aged over 60=80
• IDPs - Targeted female adults aged over 60=100.
SSHCO has been working in South Sudan since 2011, beginning with the construction of a PHCC in Maar, Pakeer Payam, Twic East County, JS. In 2012, the PHCC was finally equipped and open to the public for health care services. The potential catchment area is far higher as several patients travel to the Maar PHCC from remote areas which do not have any health services. In Maar PHCC, past data shows an average of 125-175 patients seen each week.
In Mongalla, SSHCO seeks to provide primary healthcare services to the IDP population of 17,000 people who live there since the inception of the flooding in 2020. The IDP population arrived from Twic East and other flooded regions of JS during 2021, changing the health care picture for this region. Currently, the SSHCO sees an average of 258 patients a week (1,070 patients a month) in the mobile clinics in Mongalla. We will disaggregate the data by targeting the following population in Mongalla:
• Number of Male IDP Children <5 = 1,500
• Number of Female IDP Children <5 =1,500
• IDPs - Targeted male children aged 5 - 17 years=3,000
• IDPs - Targeted female children aged 5 - 17 years =3,500
• IDPs - Targeted male adults aged 18 – 60 =3,000
• IDPs - Targeted female adults aged 18 – 60=3,500
• IDPs - Targeted male adults aged over 60=500
• IDPs - Targeted female adults aged over 60=500
There is a focus on maternal and child health at the Maar PHCC with a female midwife facilitating safe births and clinical officers providing antenatal care over the course of 4 visits. SSHCO hired a female midwife after receiving feedback from the women in the community that they were more comfortable with a female providing maternal services than a male midwife. SSHCO plans to include infant formula and child food items to combat the growing threat of severe acute malnutrition (SAM) in both Maar and Mangalla, as outlined in the Humanitarian Needs Overview. The SSHCO listens to the needs of the elderly and disabled through their representatives or directly. Care is tailored to our most vulnerable population to ensure their needs are met, and they are consulted. Hospital beds are given priority to older and disabled patients, and one bed is designated for maternal care when needed. To meet the challenge of Coronavirus (COVID-19), the SSHCO has been utilizing basic Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) provided by the health cluster according to World Health Organization's (WHO) guidelines. Additionally the SSHCO clinical staff held community health training to educate the local populations in Maar and Mangalla on proper safety protocols when receiving healthcare.

1 - Needs

SSHCO plans to meet the need for essential primary healthcare services in Maar and Mongalla. Twic East County, (JS) and Juba county, (CES) are both listed as ‘in extreme need’ based on the Humanitarian Needs Overview. Based on previous data collected by the SSHCO, the SSHCO sees around 1200 patients a month in Maar PHCC. Additionally, each month, the SSHCO sees an average of 388 patients under the age of 5 and an average of 706 patients aged 5 and older, in Mangalla. These numbers show the number of patients who rely on the healthcare services of SSHCO each month for essential life-saving services. South Sudan has had an increase in over 600,000 people since 2021, making the needs even greater for the area.Beyond healthcare, there is an extreme risk of severe acute malnutrition (SAM) in CES and Twic East County in JS according to the Humanitarian Needs Overview. There has been a great deal of flooding, causing increased issues for people with regards to gaining access to food or WASH, which is essential for living. The Humanitarian Needs Overview lists food security as a major priority this year, with over a million more people in the region labeled as food insecure with more increases to come. According to the Humanitarian Needs Overview, Jonglei State was listed to be one of the States in the area that is above the 15% emergency threshold for global acute malnutrition (GAM).Additionally, South Sudan has some of the highest mortality rates for under 5 populations anywhere in the world. Some of the major causes of mortality and morbidity are malaria, acute watery diarrhea, and acute respiratory infection, which all are set to increase over 2022. Maternal health in South Sudan has also become an increasing issue, with rising rates of mortality and morbidity. Though efforts have been made to improve maternal health through the Boma Health Initiative, there remains an extremely high maternal death rate and high infant mortality with a high number of births taking place outside of health centers (90.7 deaths/1,000 live births). There is a lack of healthcare workers for addressing the maternal and infant health issues, causing increased morbidity and mortality for preventable issues. There are also increasing issues for gender-based violence (GBV) in the Humanitarian Needs Overview, as with increased flooding there is less land to inhabit and more safety concerns for women, children, and persons with disabilities.

2 - Project activities

Medical: The SSHCO will provide primary healthcare services in Mongalla and Maar. This organization intends for Maar to be the primary care health care center, as a static center, serving people with essential healthcare. As well, the project intends to have mobile health units weekly to travel to the areas of Wuchung, Dhiam Dhiam, and Jonglei island along the Nile to help those who cannot travel to Maar. These services are provided by clinical officers, nurses, a midwife, community health workers, and project managers. The main medical care is administered by clinical officers. The staff treat a variety of diseases, including but not limited to malaria, acute respiratory infection, acute watery diarrhea, and acute bloody diarrhea. SSHCO plans to increase its staffing to better collect and report on diseases occurring in order to better prevent and treat them. Currently, there is one midwife who will be in charge of providing maternal care and birthing support. We have also added a delivery coach and traditional birth attendants to assist in the delivery process; this also better integrates people in the area who have experience with this over the last two decades. We hope to increase the number of midwives and traditional birth attendants to better provide prenatal, birthing assistance, and postnatal care within the communities. As well we have brought in state-of-the-art equipment to assist in the birthing process. We also hope to utilize this even more and the additional staff to assist in mental health support for women who experience gender-based violence (GBV). Moreover, SSHCO plans to distribute clinical management of rape (CMR) kits for the women who have undergone GBV to integrate clinical GBV health services not offered to the people anywhere else. SSHCO is also working to reduce morbidity and mortality for infants through the provision of basic immunizations at birth. As well, iron sources and supplements will be given to women suffering from severe anemia and/or breastfeeding mothers to support their needs. This organization plans to better identify and treat the most vulnerable populations, with a high emphasis on those with disabilities, to reduce the impact of physical and emotional stresses on their lives. With a stronger process for feedback, the organization plans to strengthen itself in the community and become a more integral part of each person’s life. SSHCO plans to create more sustainable solutions for the people in this area with a stronger health care system, allowing the people to be more resilient to their environment.Non-medical: Beyond the provision of medical care, the staff assists in distributing both food items and non-food items (NFI). With the growing need for nutritional assistance, SSHCO seeks to meet by providing infant and child food items in order to combat the SAM outlined clearly in the Humanitarian Needs Overview. SSHCO’s efficacy in distributing materials was demonstrated when the SSHCO distributed malaria protection, canoes, motorboats, NFIs, and other items related to assistance with the flood including food. The staff will distribute key vitamins and minerals noted in the WHO Care for SAM guidelines as most essential, to children and infants. SSHCO will also promote safe infant practices and work through community engagement to reduce the spread of seasonal infections to create a more resilient population from the stresses they are undergoing with recent floods. The SSHCO will continue to hold training for the staff for the prevention of COVID-19 and other communicable diseases. As well, the SSHCO clinical staff also intends to hold training on protection against sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA) to better protect the most vulnerable population in the area. Further training on various topics will be held throughout the year with flexibility for topics that arise (The Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI), landmines, etc.).

3 - Project indicators

This project will utilize five indicators to focus on monitoring, reporting, and treating the case fatality rates for malaria by age and gender for each area in order to reduce mortality rates. An additional indicator will focus on the percentage of alerts, rumors, and outbreaks investigated within 48 hours of notification, as we are working to improve response times for outbreaks to practice better prevention in both regions. A third indicator we are focusing on is in the vaccination coverage for children under the age of one for PENTA3 and all essential vaccinations since SSHCO is working to provide these for infants with our vaccination team in place. As well, SSHCO’s fourth indicator centralizes around adding a greater number of community health workers per 1,000 people to better reach the individuals of this area. This indicator will have a particular emphasis on women and children, as their mortality rates are increasingly high. The fifth indicator for this is in the number of consultations per person per year for SSHCO and working to better collect and report data disaggregating by sex, age, and each disability so the population can be better assisted and understood going forward.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

Our staff can hold a seminar for the local population in Mongalla, about topics specifically related to PSEA, to distribute information orally. Through the monthly meetings in Maar and a planned seminar in Mongalla, the SSHCO will have the platform to disseminate information on topics related to PSEA. They can also hand out information for beneficiaries to read, depending on funding. To be more personal and confidential, a female staff member could be designated as the responsible staff member to be contacted about PSEA, and report it. For example, PSEA topics could be incorporated into the women’s group meetings. These meetings in the past have been very successful. Additionally, we hope to increase staff and provide mental health support to women and children who receive PSEA so they may be more resilient to the shock incurred and empowered. Also, with increased staff, it would allow for more one-on-one interactions to support these individuals. The SSHCO has a written explicit policy regarding PSEA that we hold all of our staff to and enforce in the community as well.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The project will maintain its method of being open to suggestions and concerns of the local community, which as previously demonstrated has been very successful. The feedback will follow the line of communication from the clinical staff to the administrative staff. The feedback given by the beneficiaries will be reviewed by the administrative staff and given warranted concern. The monthly meetings in Maar and the planned seminars in Mangalla will allow the staff to hear feedback on a consistent basis. The SSHCO will also rely on the information disseminated by the health cluster and the UNHCR, UN OCHA, and WHO representatives to inform our work.
