Project Summary

In South Sudan 6.35 Million people (52.6% of the population), 5.82 million (48.3% of the population) and 7.24 million people (60% of the population) were projected in October to November 2020, December 2020 to March 2021 and April to July 2021 respectively to faced crisis (IPC phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity. An estimated 24,000 people were classified in Catastrophe (IPC phase 5) acute food insecurity, of which 89,820 people in Tonj North County of Warrap state are in IPC phase 5 (IPC South Sudan Oct 2020 - July 2021). Almost half of Tonj North County have a convergence of high needs related to food insecurity, protection and WASH due to inter-communal conflicts. Livelihood needs in Kajo-Keji, Terekeka and Kapoeta East are dire given that they are classified as IPC 4. The proposed intervention aims to saves lives, enhance early recoveries and build community resilience by addressing the main drivers of vulnerabilities because apparently unresolved conflicts leaves these areas extremely fragile. With the advent of COVID-19 with lockdown the already ailing economy drastically went down making the already vulnerable population lose their livelihoods and ability to feed their families. It is widely appealed that in order to save lives and avert total collapse of livelihoods in the affected counties, particularly those with the populations in catastrophe IPC Phase 5 and Emergency IPC Phase 4, there is an urgent need for immediate scale-up humanitarian assistance.
The intervention will seek to support 41,850 vulnerable people with emphasis on access to food emergency livelihoods deprived during conflict and floods. IRW will provide food items, unconditional and conditional cash transfers in all areas of intervention except Tonj North where delivery of food items to the beneficiaries will be through Voucher. To further strengthen resilient livelihoods income diversification the project will provide pathways for communities to recover and adapt to climate variability. Activities will provision of inputs for crop and vegetable production, distribution of livelihood kits with farming tools (hoes, moloda, rakes) crop seeds (groundnut, maize, sorghum, cowpeas), and vegetable seeds (tomatoes, onion, eggplant, Okra, kale, kudra, Amaranth). IRW will also conduct basic training in agronomic- and post-harvest practices, and emerging threats such as trans-boundary crop diseases.
Enhancing household resilience will be through capacity building including trainings on modern livestock management and milk handling and processing to increase production, Community Animal health Workers (CAHWs) will be trained and provided start up kits for management of livestock diseases. The CAHWs are specifically work on livestock and poultry. Trainings will be done by facilitators contracted from the department of Livestock at state level because they use local languages understandable by beneficiaries. Some households will benefit from Re-stocking of livestock program to replace the animals which were killed or died during the crisis as registered and confirmed by the local beneficiary community themselves during open meetings; Distribution of fishing kits and training on fish preservation and marketing during fishing seasons in river areas. Training of Community Agricultural Extension Workers (CAEW) who specifically deal with crops by providing extension services to farmers benefiting from the in order to increase agriculture production.
The intervention is expected to reinforce and strengthen protection of the various groups affected by the conflict. Livelihood roles are expected to change to include women as decision makers in household income generation hence reducing the risks of SEA accosted with poverty. IRSS will conduct FSL based protection risk analysis to mitigate all risks to various groups women, girls and children and the marginalized of the affected group. Awareness on GBV, sexual exploitation and child abuse and exploitation.

1 - Needs

Humanitarian situation in south Sudan remain dire, across the country Some 8.3 million people in South Sudan are estimated to be in need of humanitarian assistance in 2021. These include 8,000,000 South Sudanese women, men, girls, boys, 310,000 refugees, and asylum seekers. This is an 800,000 increase in absolute numbers from the 7.5 million people in need in 2020. An estimated 7.7 million people are expected to experience acute food insecurity and worse (IPC 3, 4 and 5) in 2021. A total of 72 counties face extreme needs while five are in severe need(HNO 2021).These needs are closely link to the conflict (internal displacement), the economic down turn and still exert further strain on already limited FSL services. The magnitude and severity of food insecurity is anticipated to increase and worsen respectively in 2021 compared to 2020. The direct and indirect impact of COVID-19 and related pressure put on markets and the movement of goods; an upsurge in sub-national violence, especially in Jonglei; desert locust invasions in the east of the country; recurring flooding in areas still recovering from the 2019 floods; a protracted macro-economic crisis; higher food prices; and a devaluating local currency are all threatening the food security of people. Other factors that will influence the food security situation include the seasonality of livelihoods, grain stocks, prices, climate, movement of livestock, nutrition status of people, and ongoing conflict. The impacts of flooding affected over 1 million people in 2020. Some 25,000 people fled to Juba due to sub-national violence in Terekeka County, Central Equatoria. In Lainya County, Central Equatoria, some 3,500 people were displaced. Fighting in Tonj North, Warap displaced some 3,000 people and led to civilian casualties. IRW-SS conducted assessments in Tonj North County have great potential for agricultural activities as the land is fertile to produce plenty of food. At the time of assessment, most households were experiencing hunger and needed food assistance from WFP and other partners. 273(86%) households reported experiencing food shortage and only 45(14%) reported not having experienced food shortage over the past 12 months. Rainfall is a key challenge that is affecting crop and livestock production. The rainfall pattern has become unpredictable due to climate change and it is becoming increasingly hard to rely entirely on rain-fed agriculture. Insecurity disrupted agricultural activities, leading to the loss of livestock and other assets, restricted physical movement, and access to markets, and disrupted market functionality. The COVID-19 measures were also reported to have contributed to high food prices because of supply chain disruptions, and traders’ reluctance to extend credit facilities. Many households sold their livestock as coping strategy to reduce shock.The major need include; (1) Food assistance (in kind/ cash & voucher/ hybrid) as part of a holistic multi sector response to IPC Phase 4 & 5 HHs and as many IPC Phase 3 HHs as resources can allow to meet immediate food needs; (2) to maintain food availability and access across farming and agro/ pastoral communities in the medium to longer term requires emergency cropping (cereals, pulses & vegetables), livelihood (fishing kits) and livestock (vaccine, treatment & feed stocks) support; and (3) addressing deeply rooted causes of food insecurity needs strengthening of coping mechanisms and building resilience, while linking with peace building and development responses. Some are harmful to children such as early/forced marriage and child labour. Both men and women report relying on small-scale livelihood activities to mitigate the difficulties they face, including fishing, hunting, farming, harvesting honey, brewing alcohol and selling charcoal. Some of the vulnerable women and girls opt to exploitative survival sex in exchange for basic needs.

The project is designed to facilitate services delivery in areas where markets are functional but are unable to fulfil the needs of people because they do not have income for purchasing the required goods (food and other essential items). Therefore, vouchers shall be appropriate when the required goods (food and other essential items) are not available in markets.

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

In emergencies with significant access challenges for humanitarian actors like South Sudan, the use of Cash Transfer Programming (CTP) has great potential to help provide life-saving support to the most vulnerable people. CTP may not require a heavy and consistent staff presence, is not subject to the same logistical barriers as in-kindassistance, and can often continue during peaks of disruption and displacement. Cash transfer provides beneficiaries with greater choice which promotes the dignity of the affected people. Monitoring and evaluation of CTP have shown that risks of abuse are no greater than in-kind support or provision of essential commodities; risk mitigation strategies are required for emergence and development response interventions.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

IRSS acknowledge that different groups face different kinds of vulnerabilities. Therefore, IRSS will conduct conflict analysis through collecting primary data and triangulating with secondary data to understand the conflict dynamics of the area. IRSS has a policy of Do no Harm, impartiality, neutrality, and identify the conflict sensitivity that could potentially harm our communities, staff and ensure that safety measures are put in place. Staff with conflict sensitive are deployed in their respective places of origin by supporting local recruitment plan to gain community acceptance. Communities in conflict with one another are served separately on the bases of their needs. During project implementation, conflict mitigation, prevention and response approaches such as community conflict awareness, trainings and community dialogues will be integrated into program outcomes. We shall also work with the communities to identify dividers and connectors as strategies to initiate activities that could potentially encourage peaceful co-existence among communities. Moreover, women and girls are in general more exposed to different types of violence such as GBV and abuse; boys are more exposed to forced recruitment; children are denied education; and people with disabilities challenges such as stigma, lack of access to services and general neglect. To mitigate threats as these, and reduce vulnerably and ensure protection and safeguarding, the project will therefore ensure equal participation and full inclusion of all community members with special attention to children, women, elderly and persons with disabilities and their respective needs. To do this, all IRSS staff will be trained on protection and inclusion principles to be able to incorporate all relevant aspect in their work, including in-person PSEA training. The project will also design a protection mainstreaming checklist for each sector with special attention on protection and inclusion of vulnerable groups to ensure that humanitarian inclusion standards are fully applied in each project phase. The Gender, Protection and Inclusion Coordinator (GPIC) will act as Safeguarding Focal Point for this project, responsible for monitoring these aspects across all project components. Safeguarding focal points will also be appointed in each project location whom will be trained on receiving verbal, written, phone and mail complaints and reports, and ensure that all vulnerable groups are familiar with their rights. With regards to S/GBV, which is widespread in the country, IRSS recognizes the need to address and design all activities with the core aim to reduce risks related to it. IRSS aims to hamper the protection risks by conducting community awareness sessions on gender equality and S/GBV, as well as social cohesion through conflict resolution to impact women’s equality, sensitization on GBV issues and prevention of child recruitment to armed conflicts. This will be complemented by also providing additional trainings for 300 men and boys on WASH related protection risks, which hopefully will reduce identified threats and incidents and improve safe use of WASH facilities. With regards to safeguarding, IRW’s PSEA and Safeguarding policies are subject of revision every two years in order to strengthen its accountability and PSEA approach. Report of any SEA incident will be investigated by IRSS’ safeguarding focal point with full respect of the principles of confidentiality during investigation procedures and afterwards.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

IRSS adopted an accountability to the affected population strategy of working with diversified groups of affected populations, the people with specific needs, people with disabilities in assessing needs capacities, threats and coping mechanisms of women, men, girls, boys and those with specific needs such as the elderly, pregnant and lactating mothers and persons with disabilities in line with the CHS for accountability and transparency. In order to guarantee accountability and full participation of the affected population, the community will be consulted for culturally acceptable, safe and accessible mechanisms in terms of diversity. This consultation will be done in forms of rapid needs assessment, community stake holder's meetings and service mapping. Moreover, IRSS will establish various community project management committees to support the intervention: Accountability to Affected Population Committees (AAPCs) will facilitate identification and registration of beneficiaries and receive and address complaints, including supervising provision of livelihood kits, cash transfers and cash-for- work projects; Water Management Committees will encourage community participation and ensuring ownership and sustainability of WASH installations; and Community Health Committees will link up health and nutrition services with the community, and encourage community participation in the implementation of the program. These will be chosen by community members after sensitization and clear selection criteria, making sure they represent various community diversities i.e. women, men, youth and other geographical and cultural diversities. Moreover, to further ensure inclusive participation of all crisis-affected people IRSS will designate two complaint and feedback focal points– one male and one female, in both locations to respond on traditional barriers in communication between women and men, limited access for persons with disabilities and persons with chronic illness, and low literacy among the population in the area of intervention. The feedback collected will be used to improve program quality as well as plan for future improvements in gaps that shall be identified. The feedback is intended for IRSS to respond to sensitive complaint and establish a safe and confidential strategies for safeguarding communities from any forms of exploitation and abuse. The feedback/complaints out of IRSS scope of work shall be used for lobbying and advocacy with partners as well as referrals. IRSS intends to use the already existing community structures in the respective projects in place. IRSS will also ensure cooperation with national and local organizations working with persons with disabilities to have their advice on specific barriers, stigma and discrimination community members face at the target locations. Another channels that will be established includes complaints drop box, Hotlines for feedback, and setting up Help/Complaints Desk, not forgetting community holding feedback meetings will be used as a very good platform for receiving general public feedback. This approach will provide IRSS with specific information and measures to overcome all identified institutional and physical barriers. At the inception meeting with the main stakeholders, IRSS’ representatives will present the purpose of CFM and share information on how it works, why complaints and feedbacks are valuable for IRSS, how communities could influence projects with expressing ideas, needs, remarks and critiques, and how IRSS use their opinion in improving assistance based on beneficiaries’ needs. Based on IRW approach towards complaint and feedbacks, every complaint is registered, and if not anonymous, complainant will receive IRSS’ response in 72 hrs (whenever possible). IRSS will make sure that previous complaints will not be repeated in the upcoming project and/or that strong mitigation procedures are in place.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

IRSS believe that project beneﬁciaries have the right to complain and to receive an appropriate and timely response on food safety and quality. Formal mechanisms for complaints and redress will be an essential component of its accountability and give affected communities some element of control over their lives. A complaint will contain a speciﬁc grievance and will alert IRW to serious misconduct or failures in the response, allowing them to take timely action to improve programme quality. Like all organizations, IRW is susceptible to fraud or abuse of power and a complaints system helps to recognize and respond to malpractice, manipulation and exploitation. To realize this IRW will put in place strategies to ensure: i. Consultations with project beneﬁciaries on the design, implementation and monitoring of complaints-handling processes. ii. Welcoming and accepting complaints, and communicating how the mechanism can be accessed and the scope of issues it can address iii. Managing complaints in a timely, fair and appropriate manner that prioritises the safety of the complainant and those affected at all stages. iii. The complaints-handling process for project beneﬁciaries is documented and in place, covering programming, sexual exploitation and abuse, and other abuses of power. iv. Formation and training of Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) Committees in all project locations during inception.
