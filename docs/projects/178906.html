Project Summary

Provision of information, legal counselling and legal assistance (ICLA) on Civil Documentation (CD), Housing, Land and Property (HLP) and Livelihoods rights to people affected by crisis and displacement inside Syria. NRC will respond to the diverse problems and needs faced by vulnerable populations throughout Syria through the delivery of a multi-faceted response focusing on protection of the rights to legal identity and security of tenure. Through its Capacity Building Programme and ICLA core competency NRC’s work is intended to contribute to HRP Protection Sector Objectives 1 and 2.
NRC will deliver this project through a combination of direct, partner-led and mixed modalities which will include provision of legal awareness sessions as well as individualised counselling and legal assistance. Partner names have not been included in this submission for reasons of confidentiality.
Geographic areas will be targeted based on the severity and scale of needs, inter-agency coordination at hub-level, and access. NRC will provide assistance to vulnerable crisis and displacement-affected populations, including IDPs, refugees, members of host communities, IDP and refugee returnees, and non-displaced households suffering the effects of the crisis. All interventions will seek to prioritize the most vulnerable households and pressing needs. Key needs include the issuance of basic civil documentation such as birth, marriage and death certificates which are necessary in order to access education, health care and humanitarian assistance. Groups with highest needs include children without proof of legal identity and women with missing or deceased spouses who require confirmation of their legal status and that of their children. Other basic needs include protection of tenure security and legal support to prevent arbitrary evictions.
NRC’s intervention forms part of integrated programming inside Syria, in close collaboration with the Shelter & WASH, Livelihoods and Food Security (LFS) and
Education programmes, NRC will aim to meet its overall objective of enabling conflict and displacement-affected populations to meet their basic needs, enjoy their rights, and benefit from pathways to durable solutions. The NRC response in Syria will consist of two components:
a) Increased Knowledge and Capacity to Respond: Legal research and analysis, capacity building, HLP and CD coordination and preparation for durable solutions including return b) Direct Assistance: NRC will assist target groups on issues that link to accessing essential services and CD, as well as improving protection in accessing livelihoods and HLP rights. In 2021, NRC will increase its support for Collaborative Dispute Resolution (CDR) techniques, such as mediation and negotiation. NRC will continue to carry out HLP due diligence to support shelter/WASH, education and livelihood interventions.

4 - Needs

Documents of millions of Syrians were lost, destroyed or confiscated. In GoS areas, procedures for acquiring documents are complex and many civil registries overwhelmed or still closed. Over half of communities surveyed in the 2020 MSNA cited lack of documentation as barrier. In non-GoS areas, Syrians face challenges obtaining official documentation and often cross conflict lines to access GoS civil registries. Only 29% of persons living in areas outside government control in NW Syria can access government registries whilst up to 58% of children are not registered in their family book (NRC, ’Documenting Life and Death’, 2021). 84% of NW Syria respondents cited fear of approaching authorities as the main cause of not applying for documents. Lack of documents is a hindrance to accessing humanitarian aid, education and freedom of movement. Missing, expired or incomplete documentation will affect return and other durable solutions. GoS requires burdensome administrative and security approvals and reviews of HLP transactions, including for lease agreements. Women face additional barriers to HLP rights, especially when divorced or widowed and missing property documentation issued in their names. Population of non-GoS areas needs urgent security of tenure protection: NRC 2021 Eviction Monitoring revealed 27% of beneficiaries were evicted due to inability to pay rent. Syrians require knowledge on employment laws: 50% of the population remains unemployed whilst overall poverty is at 90% (HNO 2021) making it critical for Syrians to be able to safeguard their employment rights, claim unpaid wages and avoid unfair dismissal from work. Needs have worsened with C-19 and economic crisis which increased violations of work rights.

5 - Implementation of activities

NRC will conduct research or studies, on legal identity, HLP, employment laws and return-related topics, and will inform in-depth fact sheets and training on CD and HLP for duty-bearers, humanitarian partners, and community leaders. NRC will continue to lead national and hub-level coordination on HLP and CD, engaging with legal service providers, building capacity and highlighting gaps, collaborating and coordinating with partners and specialised actors. NRC will provide information and assistance on access to essential services, livelihoods and legal identity, HLP and employment, in particular on return and durable solutions for IDPs and through NRC’s regional durable solutions work. There will be more focus on HLP-related coordination in cross border operations, with NRC prioritising HLP due diligence for shelter/WASH, education and livelihoods to protect all parties’ rights. Information and feedback collected will be used to inform programme, advocacy and preparedness, and a detailed M&E plan will track output and outcome indicators. NRC will use expertise from its capacity building programme on labour law to share information on workers’ rights, market facilitation and how to adapt business practices to the new context, to support and protect workers’ rights by preventing illegal dismissals and ensure health and safety. These are critical to protect livelihood rights and help to cover basic needs, including rent. This is pertinent due to socio-economic impact of Covid-19 to reduce families’ risk of destitution, being evicted, or using negative coping mechanisms. Regarding the legal aid ban, NRC will not conduct legal activities in GoS controlled areas but will continue with endorsed capacity building activities.

7.1 - Please list any risks related to sexual exploitation and abuse that have been identified during the project design phase and any risk mitigation and prevention measures that have been adopted and built into the project

All NRC staff and non-staff are bound by NRC’s Code of Conduct, protection from sexual exploitation and abuse, and child safeguarding policies. Several sessions took place in 2020 to raise awareness of PSEA and child safeguarding to staff, in addition in sessions for contractors. All NRC teams have to complete online PSEA and safeguarding essentials e-courses as part of their induction. NRC participates in PSEA network meetings, regularly reports and shares information on good practice and challenges related to PSEA. NRC’s Complaints and Feedback Mechanism (CFM) is open and sensitive to SEA and child safeguarding reports, which are managed by a dedicated team in Head Office with support from regional and country office teams. In addition to inquiring during post-delivery monitoring (e.g. PDMs) whether people were asked to exchange anything for aid. Attempts by non-traditional humanitarian actors (e.g. local councils and authorities) to interfere in beneficiary selection and eligibility are reported and addressed as they come. In order to reduce perceptions of power, NRC asks who recipients of aid think provided the assistance, in order to identify possible areas of concern. Efforts in 2021 to strengthen child-friendly CFM have started and are expected to conclude by mid-2021. Specific Risks to ICLA: Threats to disclose sensitive information provided by beneficiaries if sexual favours aren’t provided, threats to withhold assistance and counselling.

8 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Threats to health, safety and security of staff, partners and beneficiaries linked to conflict or criminal activities: dedicated staff to ensure follow-up and implementation of NRC Security policy, regular risk assessments, updated security and contingency plans, security and safety training for all staff. Limitations by local authorities preventing NRC to access specific areas and to independently select beneficiary or staff: networking with peer organisations to advocate for unified policy positions, regular meeting with local key stakeholders to reinforce humanitarian principles, transparent recruitment policy and criteria for selection of beneficiaries, clear red lines identified, careful liaison work. Border closure: Market analysis for local procurement, Advocacy at UN level for humanitarian exemptions, Stockpiling of basic needs items when possible and relevant. Fraud/Corruption/Bribery: Enforcing NRC’s policy of zero tolerance through strengthening of procurement and operating procedures, promote the “whistle-blowing” policy, corruption risk mapping, ensure partners implement anti-corruption measures. Loss of access in targeted areas due to context developments, legal restrictions or loss of acceptance: good understanding of context, contextual analysis, security plans and procedures, contingency planning, community acceptance and good relationship with local authorities. C-19 Restrictions: SOPs for safe implementation during C-19; adapted implementation modalities.
