1 - Needs

Coordination and health information management are essential as the nature of humanitarian needs across the country has not changed. The year of 2020 will be the year of the shift of coordinated response to “severity scale” prioritization. The health sector has identified 3,970,842 people in need of health assistance. This includes 1,662,586 people in areas ranked 4 on the severity scale, and 122,485 people in areas ranked 5. A total of 1,785,072 people have acute health needs. 54% and 4% of baladiyas and 41% and 18% of muhallas were classified as severity scale 4 and 5 respectively. In 2020 coordination will focus to identify solutions for access: to deliver health supplies; to expand and carry out health services; to carry out assessments and surveys; to monitor and evaluate needs and levels of assistance. Refugees and migrants in the west, east and south listed health as one of their top three priorities (source: MSNA), yet many people lacked regular access to primary and secondary health care services. Severe shortages of health staff, medicines, supplies and equipment have been compounded by years of under-investment in the health system and a lack of transparency and accountability. Between 22 and 25% of public health care facilities are closed. Of those that remain open, 26% offer no services at all and almost three quarters do not provide preventive and curative services for children under five years of age. Only 6% of 78 functioning hospitals offer all essential services. The situation is exacerbated by attacks on health care. As of December 2019, 75 people had been killed and 49 had been injured in 60 separate attacks. Coordination and health information management will continue to ensure response to the following needs: Insufficient number of mobile clinics/teams to areas of acute emergency needs; Disrupted emergency referral system in public health care facilities, preparedness and response levels to improve the management of trauma patients; Lack of medicines, equipment and supplies (including cold chain); Deficient reproductive health services; Ineffective detection and response to communicable diseases; Limited health care for patients with TB, HIV; A potential for increase of vector-borne diseases across the country; Non-continuous and interrupted routine vaccination of children, insufficient number of vaccination teams; Increasing demand for services for patients with injuries: rehabilitation of persons with disability; Limited MHPSS activities and services, including community mental health system; Shortage for diagnosis and treatment of non-communicable diseases; A high number of public health care facilities, especially PHC level, being closed and in need of repair and rehabilitation. The capacity of health authorities in health emergency preparedness, planning and operational readiness needs to be strengthened. There is a strong lack of national NGOs with potential or current health projects. Strong sense of competition for funding: INGOs and UN agencies. Sector has not yet demonstrated the expected flexibility to shift operational response. There are gaps in assessment, supervision, monitoring and evaluation-related activities across the country. Absence of comprehensive monitoring and evaluation plans to systematically evaluate trends, and weak capacity for data analysis and regular reporting. Quality of 4W remains poor. There is no dedicated information management unit or IM officer within WHO Libya as health sector lead. The work of active national health sector coordination group, led and co-lead by MoH needs to be continued as well as activities of 2 sub-national health sector groups in Sabha and Benghazi hubs (led by MoH/NCDC). Five thematic sub-sector working groups, Gender-Based Violence, Mental Health/Psychosocial Service Support, Tuberculosis, Sexual and Reproductive Health, and Migration, will need to be further supported.

2 - Activities

The health sector’s priorities will be guided by the severity of needs. The health sector will aim to overcome the challenges of 2019 related to the lack of operational capacity to adapt operations to meet emerging needs andthrough improved dialogue with the health authorities at all levels. Main activities will include the provision of a minimum package of integrated health care services at primary and secondary levels. The package will include emergency and trauma care, the management of communicable and noncommunicable diseases, maternal, neonatal and child health, mental health and psychosocial support, vaccination, disease surveillance and outbreak response. The sector will develop mechanisms to eliminate duplication of service delivery/activities: Introduction of reporting tools (4Ws); provision and consolidation of bi-weekly inputs. Continuation of implementation of DHIS-2 is imperative with the establishment of M&E Unit in Health Information Center of the MoH. 6 regional teams will be established and supported. Capacity building of M&E teams of health authorities to report on performance indicators will be essential. SARA 2017 dataset will be updated accordingly in selected areas. Monthly national and sub-national health sector meetings, preparation of the minutes and dissemination will be continued. Coordination of mobile medical teams will be in place among public health facilities, IDP camps/settlements, disembarkation points, detention centers, etc. The sector will update earlier developed contingency plans, including joint assessments and Public Health Situation Assessment. Further activities to further identify and promote the use of national and international standards and guidance. The sector will continue updating the list of advocacy issues for Libya on a monthly basis; include in all briefing notes/papers, statements and documents. The sector will monitor and report on the implementation of sector strategy and results.

4 - Protection risk analysis

There are continuous shifting lines of conflict. The situation (political, security) is fluid. There are de-factor two governance structures (GNA and LNA). There is a large degree of uncertainty. Essential health services are not accessible to people. No information is available to population about functioning and supported health services. Health care staff, patients, health facilities are subject of violence with no protection mechanisms in place. Protection related activities are not mainstreamed throughout health programming, coordination fora, training/workshops with health partners. Hard-to-reach areas and areas of higher severity scale are not being prioritized and not being accessed through different modalities of response. No advocacy is in place to register and report on all incidents related to the protection of health. GBV activities are not mainstreamed into health while health services are often the first, and in many occasions the only point of contact for GBV survivors seeking assistance, or coming for health care for GBV related emotional or physical conditions, including injuries, without necessarily disclose the abuse due to shame or fear. On protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse PSEA – there is no working environment forbidding sexual exploitation and abuse, and inclusive to accountability measures. Community based complaints mechanisms may not be in place with no victim assistance being planned. No effective and comprehensive mechanisms established to ensure awareness-raising on SEA amongst personnel. No information is available to communities and people affected by crisis about the organization, the principles it adheres to, how it expects its staff to behave, the programmes it is implementing and what they intend to deliver. There are limitations related to the absence of civil society, grass-root organizations, community based approach in health sector planning and response.

5 - Mitigation measures

The sector will ensure to have a balanced approach between different parties to the conflict. The emphasis of health sector coordination will be to empower health response on the ground, at a community level. The operational framework developed for health sector will enable tracking down humanitarian and development defined activities but with clear reference to “severity scale” approach. A quarterly review of severity scale prioritization will be produced with development of necessary recommendations. There a pressure and perceptions to disconnect emergency and development imperatives/activities while NEXUS (“humanitarian” and “development” priorities) should be equally addressed and funded in health). Rapid response mechanism should be more effectively continued via different modalities, including replenishment of health supplies, support to the referral hospitals, and deployment of mobile health teams. Health sector will emphasize inclusion of vulnerable and minority groups needs throughout the program cycle through gender, age, and disability disaggregated analysis of beneficiaries with identification of risk factors impacting service provision. Addressing the specific needs of vulnerable groups through supporting dedicated health subgroups groups (trauma, physical and mental rehabilitation, sexual and reproductive health, etc.). Regular revision and updating of regular, contingency, and emergency plans with emphasis on Do No Harm principles. Applying the designated de-confliction mechanism for health facilities, and increasing advocacy efforts by the health sector with all parties to the conflict. Continued reporting on attacks against healthcare. Affected population should have access to the reproductive health services of the Minimum Initial Service Package (MISP). 6.1 - Other accountability mechanisms

• To adopt the same commitments of AAP framework. The health sector is to consider accountability to affected populations to be a cornerstone of quality service provision to meet the affected populations evolving needs and to deliver more sustainable outcomes through community engagement. • When and where possible, the best practice approach is to speak directly to the people affected. Through a partner network some formal and informal mechanisms, standards and practices are in place to ensure the meaningful participation of and communication with the affected people at various stages in the programming cycle. The health sector will commit that all activities, measures and mechanisms will respect in principle and practices gender equality in the approaches undertaken. • The health sector will deliver on its commitments by focusing on:o Community consultations at each stage of the humanitarian programme cycle;o Building trusted relationships; o Listening to people and taking their concerns seriously; Information sharing by trained staff, and informed trend analysis, and sensitization/advocacy/capacity building of diverse stakeholders;o Engagement with diverse groups in communities (taking into consideration gender balance);o Engagement with vulnerable groups within communities (including people with special needs, elderly people, etc.)o Complaints and feedback mechanisms and redressal.• Be transparent with the affected populations by providing them with accessible and timely information on selection criteria for targeted assistance, organisational procedures and processes that affect them.• Set up accessible, confidential and well understood feedback mechanisms for suggestions and complaints with a view to improve programming, understand community perceptions, promote beneficiary empowerment and assist in detecting misconduct. Eg., feedback box at health centre, phone lineso Set up mechanisms for submitting feedback that does not require the beneficiary exposing themselves to project staff. o Respond to complaints, regardless of whether corrective measures can/need to be put in place. o Organize awareness raising sessions so that people know how it works. o Consider a joint feedback mechanism with other sectors to minimize confusion. • Health staff and health committees should be representative of all groups within the community (e.g. gender, age, ethnicity, socioeconomic group, persons with disabilities) and all staff and members should receive protection mainstreaming training.o They can play a key role in identifying issues related to exclusion and discrimination and be proactive in ensuring the voice of marginalised groups is represented.• Ensure all health staff, implementing partners and volunteers working with affected populations understand, sign and adhere to a Code of Conduct stating their commitment to respect and foster humanitarian standards and the rights of beneficiaries, including the confidentiality of patients. o The code of conduct should also address respect for other medical staff, in particular female staff.
