Project Summary

This profile responds to the distinct needs and vulnerabilities of food insecure rural populations in Somalia. Within the 2019 HRP, over 1 million rural Somalis are prioritized for food security assistance in the aftermath of severe drought in 2016-17 and the more recent impacts of flooding, cyclones and conflict in 2018. FAO's contribution to assisting the rural population in need centres on: (i) addressing immediate food needs, while (ii) restoring livelihoods, and (iii) protecting food and income sources against likely shocks in 2019. Outside of the HRP, it is critical that acutely food insecure rural households in IPC 2 that are not targeted within the 2019 HRP receive urgent assistance through resilience programmes or other response planning mechanisms.
FAO will target 72 700 rural households (436 200 people) directly with cash and/or livelihood support programmes, prioritizing the most vulnerable in IPC Phases 2-4. In addition, large animal health campaigns and preparedness efforts will indirectly serve around 2.7 million people across Somalia. The main purpose of the assistance is to assure immediate access to food, while protecting and restoring rural people’s own food production and income within the shortest lapse of time. All proposed cash interventions are linked to productive livelihood activities. All livelihood activities tailor to the needs and vulnerabilities of distinct livelihood zones.
Cash+. FAO’s Cash+ programme will deliver 3-months’ of unconditional cash transfers (UCTs) plus emergency livelihood packages to re-engage farmers, agropastoralists, pastoralists and fishers in productive livelihoods, with the aim to stabilize households' own access to food and income in 4-9 months.
Cash-for-work. Rural households will engage in 24 weeks of paid work to rehabilitate community-based infrastructures that support the restoration of rural livelihoods (e.g. water catchments and small irrigation canals), or in other productive works such as the control of prosopis. Highly vulnerable families unable to work will receive UCTs (e.g. pregnant women, elderly, ill or disabled people).
Emergency livestock support. FAO will support pastoral communities to protect their remaining livestock and related food sources through a nationwide animal health campaign and fodder production. The animal health interventions include vaccination of 14.5 million goats against Contagious Caprine Pleuropneumonia (CCPP), treatment of 3.5 million livestock against common illness and disease.
Preparedness. Emergency preparedness interventions are planned to reduce the risk – or possible impacts – of likely climate shocks and food chain threats in 2019, with a particular focus on localized flooding and outbreaks of livestock/crop pests and diseases. Mitigation measures include the repair of broken river embankments, crop pest and disease awareness campaigns and control measures, vector-borne disease control in riverine areas and the pre-positioning of strategic stocks of veterinary vaccines and equipment, plus related trainings (in line with the Global Eradication Strategy of Peste des Petits Ruminants - PPR).

Needs

Famine was averted in Somalia following severe nationwide drought in 2016-17. Drought ended in April 2018, with unanticipated heavy rains and a flood emergency in southern Somalia’s breadbasket. In the same weeks, Cyclone Sagar caused severe damage along the northern pastoral coast. Despite unrelenting shocks, the projected number of acutely food insecure Somalis decreased 25% over the past year (from 6.2 to 4.6 million), comparing Post-Gu assessment findings of 2017 and 2018 (FSNAU). Rural areas experienced the most direct damage and losses from the drought, flood and cyclone emergencies, valued at $1.9 billion from the drought alone (DINA). The food security of rural households is very fragile and precarious due to a progressive loss of livelihood assets, production and savings for 2+ years. This includes consecutive poor harvests, high livestock loss and growing debt to otherwise meet basic needs. Damage to rural infrastructure from recent floods and storms (e.g. embankments, irrigation, water catchments) has weakened livelihood production capacity and increased communities' risk exposure to potential floods/drought conditions in 2019. Cash assistance is critical to address immediate food needs and mitigate further displacement out of rural areas. Cash should be integrated with livelihood support to restore food self-sufficiency within the shortest timespan. Livelihood packages should be designed for rapid gains in household food security (e.g. next harvest, increased milk yield or resumed fishing activity) and deliberately engage women (e.g. veg gardens, milk production, fish processing). Animal health campaigns will be vital to protect surviving livestock and related food sources, and support herd regeneration. Preparedness actions against likely climate & food chain threats in 2019 include the repair of embankments, regional stocks of veterinary vaccine & supplies, and community surveillance of fall armyworm, desert locust and other threats.

Activities

FAO will directly target 436 200 rural people in need (72 700 HH) targeted for FSC assistance, including rural IDPs. A further 2.7 million will benefit from countrywide animal health campaigns. Preparedness actions will safeguard lives, livelihoods & food sources against likely shocks in 2019. (1) CFW (24 weeks paid work) targets 31 600 HH, to increase immediate access to food, while rehabilitating rural infrastructure or undertaking prosopis control. HH unable to work (pregnant, elderly, ill, disabled people, etc) will receive UCTs. (2) Livelihood support plus cash (“Cash+”) targets 38 100 HH with 3 months of UCT plus tailored livelihood packages. All packages are adapted to the local context & may include the following: (i) Cash+ Agriculture for 29 250 HH (cereal, legume, veg seeds, tractor/irrigation hours, fertilizer, training), (ii) Cash+ Livestock for 7 350 HH (feed blocks, deworming, mazzicans, training); and (iii) Cash+ Fisheries 1 500 HH (e.g. shared paddle boat, gear, safety supplies, cooler, handling & cooking kits & training – tailored to diverse coastal areas). New Cash+ models may be developed to meet emerging needs, targeting the same livelihood groups w/ adapted packages. HH without land/livestock assets may receive UCTs alone. (3) Emergency livestock support includes CCPP vaccination (14.5m goats), supportive treatment (3.5m livestock), & support to fodder production, processing & storage (2 000 HH). (4) Preparedness & response to emerging shocks includes (i) embankment repair and irrigation pumps (to mitigate further man-made breakages), (ii) Fall Armyworm awareness campaigns, training & control measures on 30 000 hectares; (iii) regional stocks of PPR vaccine (3m doses), equipment & related training (part of new Global Eradication Strategy), (iv) vector control (2m livestock in riverine areas); animal feed inventory, (v) trainings in Livestock Emergency Guidelines and Standards (LEGS), disease surveillance and veterinary public health.

How is the project linked to durable solutions/resilience?

Protection is mainstreamed throughout the project cycle - from design to community sensitization and use of feedback mechanisms to address problems/complaints as they arise. Grievance mechanisms are communicated by implementing partners to council members, elders and the community, as well as through face-to-face sessions with beneficiaries. FAO Somalia also uses radio campaigns, ad-hoc Call Centre exercises, communication through field monitors and Third Party Monitors, and its Hotline number (displayed on vouchers and sent via SMS). Other valuable feedback and complaints come from the radio Ergo’s Common Feedback Project (CFP) initiated by the Humanitarian Country Team of Somalia. These feedback loops inform programming to ensure that assistance and services both reach and properly engage vulnerable populations. Taking the example of cash-for-work, FAO ensures that highly vulnerable people are not left behind by providing UCTs to households unable to engage in work (e.g. headed by pregnant, elderly, ill or disabled people). Other examples of protection measures include prioritizing works near homesteads for women; allowing flexible working hours; offering payment at work sites where possible to minimize walking distances to collect cash; providing transport to work sites when far from home and to money vendor locations if not at village; &permitting beneficiaries to nominate someone else to work on their behalf but with the cash continuing to be given to the registered beneficiary. The project links to and actively engages government, community & other local networks. This includes, for example, the active participation of local government, community animal health workers (CAHWs) and Livestock Professional Associations (LPAs) in animal health campaigns; as well as the sourcing of quality inputs from established networks of local seed traders and agrovet dealers. These activities and partners link to longer term projects, including for example continuous capacity development & strengthening of CAHWs and LPAs, while the seeds distributed under humanitarian projects are local high performing seed varieties that have been recovered and multiplied through years of development work. To achieve sustainability, technical training is provided for each activity to IPs, and to beneficiaries on how to maximize their use of project inputs, how to manage and sustain rehabilitated infrastructure, among other topics. FAO also assesses the efficacy of training using the Kirkpatrick model, which evaluates: (i) immediate utilization of training, (ii) knowledge gained through testing, (iii) application of knowledge gained, & (iv) wider impact of the training. The proposed activities link to FAO Somalia's wider Resilience Programme and FAO/WFP/UNICEF Joint Resilience Action. FAO will actively seek to sequence, layer and integrate funded activities with those of UN/NGO partners to maximize impact and address the multifaceted needs of affected households.

Protection risk analysis & Mitigating measures
Preventing risks of exclusion based on displacement

FAO's beneficiary target includes rural IDPs, who are especially vulnerable to exclusion as rural livelihoods rely on land use and other natural resources. FAO will adapt cash and/or livelihood packages to rural IDPs' distinct needs and surrounding realities. Other highly vulnerable groups include destitute pastoralists who lost all livestock assets, migrated to coastal areas and want to engage in fisheries livelihood activities. FAO's Cash+ fisheries activities will make provisions to include and introduce interested ex-pastoralists into fisheries livelihoods and related emergency programmes. FAO deliberately targets both IDPs and members of host communities, which contributes to reduce social tensions and build cohesion. Communication messages and community mobilization campaigns in target communities explicitly state the vulnerable groups targeted by the intervention, including IDPs. Recent M&E findings show that livelihood support provided to returnees and host communities helped in achieving social cohesion and harmony, as reported in focus group discussions. A key objective of FAO’s emergency livelihood programme is to prevent displacement in the first place by delivering timely assistance into remote rural areas. Livelihood activities are designed to protect and boost food production through in-kind and technical support, providing rural families with the means and incentive to remain productive on their land and within their community. All cash programmes are linked to productive livelihood activities – whether through cash-for-work on rural infrastructure or Cash+ programmes that pair UCTs with seasonal livelihood inputs.

Preventing risks of exclusion based on gender and age

As part of its Call Centre surveys, FAO Somalia verifies and ensures that beneficiaries identified in the tripartite (NGO, elders and council members) participatory selection process meet the selection criteria (female, male, age, IDP, household heads, etc.). FAO’s beneficiary selection criteria ensures that at least 30 percent of beneficiaries are women-headed households across the programme. Select programmes – such as Cash+ Livestock – are designed to respond to the specific needs and protection issues of pastoral women and other vulnerable groups during peak times of vulnerability: i.e. during seasonal migrations periods when women, children, elderly and other vulnerable family members stay behind with few small animals to meet their needs. All Cash+ Livelihood interventions are designed to enhance the engagement of women in productive livelihood activities (e.g. vegetable gardening, small livestock rearing and fish handling/processing). FAO’s field monitoring process through its own staff or TPM assesses and reports impact by gender.

Mitigating the impact of armed conflict and violence

Insecurity caused by armed groups and clan conflicts pose significant threats to the well-being and food security of affected populations. Insecurity is a main reason why people abandon their livelihoods and migrate in search of safety. In contrast, food security, strong social networks and better livelihood opportunities are key reasons people opt to stay where they are. Insecure and conflict-affected rural areas are largely cut off from aid. FAO has a wide network of local NGO partners and field monitors who are embedded in remote rural areas and able to deliver assistance to people in need. Many operations must and can be managed remotely, including rural infrastructure repairs under cash-for-work, which is facilitated and verified by satellite imagery, remote sensing and other FAO verification tools. FAO's risk management includes robust due diligence verification to prevent diversion of project funds to illegal entities, including Al Shabbab or other armed groups, though the risk remains. Food insecurity can be both a cause and a consequence of violence in rural areas. Conflict-sensitive livelihood programmes contribute to addressing both sides. Interventions to sustain rural livelihoods reduce food insecurity and poverty in conflict affected areas, while also protecting at risk populations (especially youth) from joining extremist groups or illicit activities (e.g. piracy), undergoing informal migration or adopting other harmful coping strategies. Livelihood programmes within this HRP (such as animal health campaigns and the rehabilitation of community based infrastructure) build social cohesion around shared resources and services – bringing together men and women from different villages, clans and sub-clans. Other examples of social networks in rural areas include farmer/pastoral/fisher field and life schools, water user associations and women-led producer groups.
