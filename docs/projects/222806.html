Project Summary

"In 2025, UNICEF will a provide a comprehensive, inclusive, and lifesaving GBV prevention, risk mitigation and response program, reaching 82,805 individuals- including 3657 persons with disabilities, in the Rohingya refugee and Bangladeshi host communities. Steered by the GBV Sub-Sector (GBVSS) and collaboration with BRAC, IRC & CARE, UNICEF will expand its presence to address critical gaps in the following camps: 8E, 11, 12, 17, 20, 21, 25, & 27.
Guided by the IASC Minimum Standards, the project will adopt a survivor-centered approach and uphold the principle of Do No Harm. The project has 4 key result areas: Comprehensive GBV case management services; transformational social behavior change programs; GBV awareness raising & risk mitigation programs; and capacity building on GBV response and prevention, aligned with GBV SS guidelines. These efforts aim to reduce risk, promote resilience, and aid recovery.

Recognizing the intersections between violence against women and violence against children, UNICEF will bring an age-responsive approach that ensures quality outcomes for women and girls. The project will combine facility-based service provision through 12 Safe Spaces for Women & Girls (SSWGs), 2 multi-purpose spaces (MPCs) and community outreach programs. The two MPCs will provide a comprehensive Inclusive GBV services tailored to meet the needs of girls, boys, women, men, and gender diverse population.
UNICEF will adopt a rights-based, public-health approach to its GBV program, ensuring that survivors and those at-risk including girls, women, and gender diverse population, have access to comprehensive services including case management utilizing GBVIMS+, with survivors benefitting from care plans, referrals, and provision of dignity kits. UNICEF will engage girls, women, and their community in GBV awareness raising and risk mitigation activities by rolling out psychosocial support services, disseminating messages on GBV including sexual reproductive health, and support community-based risk identification exercise and empowerment activities. Cognizant of the armed violence in the camps, we will monitor and report on sexual violence and other forms of GBV perpetrated by armed groups.
To address negative norms that drive GBV, UNICEF will support communities through behavior change programs including prevention activities that aim to transform gender norms and promote respectful relationships. These include programs that: support parents and caregivers with positive parenting guidance; engage girls and their caregivers through the Girl Shine program to combat child marriage; nurture soft skills especially amongst adolescent girls through life-skills based program; provide social cohesion program that engages youth as agents of change in line with peace and security campaign; provide access to vocational training opportunities where possible; and engage men and boys to combat negative gender stereotypes and GBV through the EMAP program. We will also advocate for justice for GBV survivors.
To enhance the quality of our GBV program and in alignment with GBV SS guideline, UNICEF will support the GBV organizational capacity development of government counterparts and implementing partners. We will continue to: train and mentor caseworkers, facilitators, and volunteers; collaborate with community to mitigate GBV risk; mainstream GBV across other sectors; and engage both GBV and non-GBV actors to strengthen their knowledge and support for our GBV program.
As a lead agency in the GBV Sub-Sector, UNICEF will coordinate closely with sector stakeholders including the working groups for Case Management/prevention, Gender in Humanitarian Action, PSEA, Age & Disability, and others, to ensure a reliable and high-quality GBV response. UNICEF, our partners, personnel, and affiliates, will uphold the strictest adherence to PSEA and Safeguarding across the project. "

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

"Approximately 994,124 people are estimated to be residing in Rohingya refugee camps, with 52% percent of them being children, according to the GoB/UNHCR Fact Sheet dated August 2024. This population heavily depends on humanitarian assistance. Children and women, especially Unaccompanied and Separated Children, female-headed households, persons with disabilities, and those with alternative gender orientations, are exposed to various forms of violence, including trafficking, child marriage, child labor, sexual violence, and Gender-Based Violence (GBV). These vulnerabilities are exacerbated by restricted movement, protection risks, and natural hazards like monsoons, cyclones, and landslides, which adversely affect the safety and well-being of the population in both the camps and the host community. Negative social norms and harmful practices, including gender discrimination and child marriage, persist as indicated in the JMSNA report from September 2024. Reductions in food rations due to funding cuts and an increase in climate-related disasters continue to contribute to factors that heighten the occurrence of all forms of violence against children and women, child marriages, and GBV incidents. According to the GBVIMS Q2 report for 2024, revealed that sexual violence accounted for 10.4% of reported incidents (including 5.8% rape cases and 4.2% child sexual abuse cases). Intimate Partner Violence accounted for 73.8% of reported GBV incidents, with 98 percent of these incidents occurring in survivors' homes where children and other family members live. The impact of IPV extends beyond survivors, affecting children and adolescents who witness the violence. Child marriage remains a significant concern, although incidents reported through GBViMS are relatively few. The JMSNA in 2024 revealed that child marriage is a coping mechanism for many families struggling to care for their children. Although women and girls are disproportionately affected by GBV, studies have also shown that men and boys are significantly affected by violence, including sexual violence (Women's Refugee Commission (WRC), 2023). Humanitarian access and service delivery in Cox’s Bazar continue to be impacted by the presence of armed groups. Recently, violence in Cox’s Bazar has escalated, with armed groups linked to the Myanmar conflict becoming more active. This increased hostility threatens the area's stability. Child rights violations by groups like the Rohingya Solidarity Organization (RSO) and Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army (ARSA) include killings, sexual violence, injuries, recruitment, and abductions of children. Humanitarian workers and community leaders also face threats and harassment, making families fearful of reporting violations. In August 2024, UNICEF conducted a needs assessment on justice for children. The study involved quantitative surveys with 558 samples (56% girls) across all 31 camps and 60 interviews with key informants, to assess the violence faced by children and the effectiveness of current justice mechanisms. Among the 151 children in conflict with the law interviewed, 34% were involved in theft and 29% in physical violence. Of the 221 children considered victims, 45% had experienced physical violence, 25% had sexual violence, 15% were victims of child marriage, and 13% had been trafficked. Children in Rohingya camps often lack awareness of their rights and available support services, hindering their ability to report crimes and seek justice. In addition to the camps, an estimated 201,554 individuals in the host community are in need of humanitarian assistance. According to the J-MSNA report from September 2024, a substantial percentage of females reported the need for protection services. Due to deeply ingrained patriarchal social norms and human rights violations, women and children remain at a higher risk of exposure to all forms of violence, including sexual exploitation and abuse, child marriage, and GBV. "

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

The Project is founded on protection principles and strives to foster meaningful access, safety, and dignity for children and women in the Rohingya refugee camps and host communities. The project will employ global protection mainstreaming toolkit and SPHERE standards to incorporate protection principles throughout the project cycle, including non-discrimination, meaningful access, safety & dignity, ‘do no harm’, accountability, participation, and empowerment. By leveraging service delivery nodes, especially Safe Spaces for Women and Girls, and by conducting extensive community outreach to reach the most vulnerable, the Project ensures that access to assistance and services is provided without discrimination or barriers. All our activities are led by the Do not Harm principle and survivor and rights-centered approaches. Our interventions aim to enhance rights-based, reliable, and quality humanitarian programming, considering gender, age, sex, disability, specific needs, and cultural appropriateness. Activities are designed with a protection-mainstreaming lens, promoting safety, dignity, meaningful access, participation, and empowerment of beneficiaries across the response. Additionally, the Project's design incorporates an information and feedback mechanism, empowering the affected population to demand accountability for the adequacy of interventions and allowing UNICEF and its implementing partners to effectively address concerns and complaints. UNICEF will also ensure that all partnerships are signed based on a previous and transparent assessment of the organizational capabilities to prevent and respond to SEA/SH during the execution of their contract, in line with the zero-tolerance policy advocated by the UN system.

2.3 - Age & Disability

The Project's primary objective is to prevent and respond to Gender-Based Violence (GBV). It includes age-specific interventions, encompassing psychosocial support activities and adolescent life skills-based learning. All interventions are tailored to ensure that all beneficiaries, particularly children and persons with disabilities, can actively engage with our services and interventions and derive maximum benefit from them.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

"UNICEF will ensure that AAP feedback and response mechanisms are established according to the AAP principles of participation, inclusion, communication, and transparency, at all stages of the programme cycle. UNICEF will ensure all staff, volunteers, children including adolescents, caregivers, and members of the community have access to reliable and timely information on organizational procedures, structures and processes on affected population capturing age, gender and specific groups at risk of discrimination and vulnerable. UNICEF and partners will take measures to reduce barriers so that children of all age groups and those who are at risk of discrimination are participating in planning, implementation and monitoring process while implementing the projects by the partners. UNICEF will support partners to strengthen organizational child safeguarding policy through conduct risk assessment and increase staff capacity to take appropriate measures. Also engage the community and target groups to participate throughout needs assessment, programme planning and implementation using standard assessment tools while inclusion of Children with disabilities and their caregivers. UNICEF will continue its regular technical guidance for partners on AAP to ensure that girls, boys, women and men, including those with disabilities, can participate in decision-making and programming, all partners have multiple feedback and reporting mechanisms in place to introduce feedback and response mechanism into their activities, with follow up actions and assign AAP focal person where needed. Embedded within project design is an information and feedback mechanism through which the affected population can demand accountability for the adequacy of interventions, and for UNICEF and implementing partners to address concerns and complaints."

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

UNICEF upholds stringent standards and protocols related to Child Safeguarding and the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA). UNICEF has zero tolerance for sexual exploitation & abuse (SEA) and sexual harassment (SH) and actively advocates for this principle across the organization, IPs, vendors, service providers & beneficiaries. The organization is also committed to preventing SEA and providing immediate support to victims. Internally, UNICEF champions an organizational culture that promotes a safe, respectful, inclusive, and enabling working environment that is free from sexual exploitation and abuse. UNICEF organizes regular PSEA and child safeguarding training for its staff. UNICEF’s Implementing partners undergo thorough assessments to ensure a high level of adherence to these standards, with the requirement to achieve a low-risk rating. Furthermore, UNICEF plays a pivotal role as a key member of the PSEA Network. UNICEF is committed to developing appropriate Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) materials on PSEA and child safeguarding to raise awareness and sensitize the affected population about the Complaint and Feedback Mechanism. All partners are expected to have multiple feedback and reporting mechanisms in place to introduce feedback and response mechanisms into their activities. These may include tools such as U-report, complaint boxes, hotline numbers for PSEA and child safeguarding, and the assignment of an Accountability of the Affected Population (AAP) focal person where necessary. UNICEF delivers an annual refresher training to its partners and monitors their performance to cascade the training to the national staff, volunteers, community leaders, and members of all communities, focusing on the safe identification and confidential referral of cases. UNICEF also ensures that all its partners are members of the PSEA network and have policies in place on child safeguarding, whistle blowing and PSEA.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Yes. A central objective of the Project is to promote resilience and preparedness of the communities served by UNICEF. Our IPs are actively engaged & part of the Protection Emergency Response Units (PERU), and the emergency response teams coordinated by the sector and mobilized in the event of natural hazards. The active participation of UNICEF IPs in PERU showcases a proactive approach to emergency response, enabling swift and effective interventions in the face of natural disasters to mitigate their impact on the affected populations. UNICEF partner staff including volunteers are also trained to respond to disaster-related emergencies to support community members during acute and post-disaster phase. Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) and climate change are seamlessly integrated into all of UNICEF’s activities and programs. This integration ensures that DRR measures are embedded within community-based protection activities, enhancing the overall resilience and preparedness of the communities served by UNICEF. To strengthen DRR through our programs, UNICEF: conducts GBV assessment and safety audits incorporating disaster risk identification; includes a budget in all our project allocating funds for emergency response-related activities; Provide onsite PSS support to beneficiaries including women and girls during emergencies; train its partners on emergency preparedness and response, including Fire Safety Assessment & Drill; empower the community particularly women and girls to become agents of change, promoting resilience, sustainability, and equitable development in the face of climate change and disaster; prepositions, stores & distributes customized Dignity Kits to reduce vulnerabilities of affected women & girls during acute emergencies; promote eco-friendly practices by encouraging the use of sustainable products in the construction and renovation of all our facilities and using solar panels to helping reduce reliance on fossil fuels.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

"Yes. All activities are guided by UNICEF’s Emergency Preparedness and Response (EPR) plan, which is reviewed and updated annually. This plan includes sector-specific guidance and aligns with existing emergency coordination structures in Cox’s Bazar. Each partnership includes allocated contingency funds as part of the emergency preparedness and response strategy, ensuring the following: Children including adolescents, caregivers, and community members, including at-risk groups, are actively involved in community-level preparedness activities, program design, and emergency response; Quality, coordinated, survivor-centered, and age-appropriate response services are promptly available and accessible during emergencies, including mobile outreach services as needed; prevention activities are implemented to mitigate risks; Child protection projects integrate actions to address and reduce GBV risks; and community groups, along with partner staff and volunteers, are empowered to support the community during any emergency.Partner project documents have a specific budget line with emergency contingency funds as standard, as part of the emergency preparedness and response strategy. Routine assessment ensures these are used effectively; trainings are organized for the staffs and volunteers, awareness among children, parents and care givers, and CBCPCs, on safety and security (for example the location of safe shelter’s in their community). A regular damage tracker for child protection facilities (e.g. MPC) and ensure timely maintenance. Any repairs or reinforcements of facilities to withstand potential hazards carried out in an environmentally sustainable manner, ensuring no harm to the surrounding environment."

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

"Yes. The project will take a socio-ecological approach that recognizes the roles of children, including adolescents and youth, in fostering a protective environment and driving change toward gender equality. Specifically designed to address the needs of children, adolescents, and youth, the project emphasizes gender equity across all aspects. This includes acknowledging the unique protection challenges faced by adolescents and youth, particularly the issue of GBV.To meet the needs of children including adolescents and youth, the project will continue to utilize dedicated Safe Spaces for Women and Girls, adolescent clubs, and multi-purpose centers to provide tailored case management, psychosocial support, and skills development services, delivered by trained professionals. Adolescents will also benefit from life skills-based learning, which includes guidance on Adolescent Sexual and Reproductive Health (ASRH), pre-vocational training, social cohesion, resilience-building, sports, and engaging recreational activities. All activities designed for adolescents will be gender-sensitive and inclusive, providing both support and education. This support will empower adolescents as agents of positive change within their families and communities.In summary, the project will continue to amplify the voices of children and adolescents, ensuring their involvement in all program stages—from design to implementation and monitoring—to empower youth fully. The socio-ecological approach acknowledges the contributions of all adolescents and youth, including LGBTQ individuals, persons with disabilities (PWDs), and other diverse groups, in creating a safe and protective environment."

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

Yes. UNICEF will enhance the self-reliance and resilience of girls and women while promoting their protection from violence, including GBV. This will be achieved through various initiatives, such as: Implementing community-based safety planning; Engaging men, boys, community and religious leaders, and other influencers as champions against GBV and as role models to challenge harmful gender norms; Supporting the economic and social empowerment of women and girls through life skills and vocational training; Addressing underlying negative social norms and root causes of GBV, such as gender inequality, abuse of power, and harmful traditional practices; Upholding the dignity of women and adolescent girls by ensuring access to essential, lifesaving GBV services, including sexual and reproductive health services and dignity kits; and collaborating with other sectors—such as WASH, Health, Education, Nutrition, and Livelihoods—to mitigate GBV risks.

4.2 Do project activities in the host community link to development initiatives, and/or align with development priorities in the Cox’s Bazar district? If yes, briefly describe.If yes, briefly describe.

Yes. UNICEF’s programme in the host community aligns with Bangladesh's National Action Plan to Prevent Violence Against Women and Children (2013–2025). Bangladesh ranks among the top 10 countries globally with the highest prevalence of child marriage, at 51.4%. The 2022 National Child Labour Survey by the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics identified 1.7 million children engaged in child labor out of 3.53 million working children. Furthermore, the MICS report (2019) revealed that 88.8% of children aged 1-14 years experienced physical punishment and/or psychological aggression by caregivers. Addressing issues like child marriage, child labor, and VAWC requires long-term, multi-sectoral collaboration. To support government efforts at the district level, UNICEF continues to work closely with relevant government ministries and departments to strengthen child protection including GBV response systems in alignment with the national plan of action to end violence against women and children.
