Project Summary

UNFPA will continue to reduce maternal and newborn morbidity and mortality; support the EPHS during the crisis, pandemics, and early recovery; provide FP commodities, and provide medical assistance and care to GBV survivors including CMR. In Syria, SRH service gaps remain; access to EmONC services are limited despite increased health needs, including among WRA. This will be achieved by 1) ensuring the availability of and access to integrated quality SRH services and information; 2) building health worker and CHW capacity; 3) increasing access to services in areas where no SRH services exist; 4) ensuring availability of essential medical, PPE, and supplies; 5) ensuring delivery of complementary comprehensive services, including for MHPSS and non-communicable diseases; and 6) strengthening referral mechanisms and improving national/subnational SRH coordination.
UNFPA will follow the Whole of Syria approach, implementing in all Syrian governorates from Damascus and cross-border from Türkiye. Vulnerable populations prioritized include women and girls of reproductive age, IDPs, host communities, returnees, adolescents, and PwDs. Through October 2022, UNFPA reached 845,845 people with SRH services through 97 PHCs, 19 EmONCs, and 99 mobile clinics in government-controlled areas and 118,418 people through 10 EmONC facilities in NWS.
Key 2023 objectives include:
Increasing access to lifesaving and life-sustaining coordinated, equitable health services for the most vulnerable and in need persons.
Increasing access to primary healthcare by providing essential primary health services to cover reproductive, maternal, newborn, and child health needs of vulnerable populations and repairing facilities. Improving access to secondary and tertiary healthcare for high risk pregnancy, malnourished PLW, EmONC cases, and complex cases which cannot access treatment in their area.
Supporting access in areas with service gaps through mobile teams and the community midwifery network in the most vulnerable rural areas.
Building the capacity of health workers and CHWs on SRH and GBV principles, outbreak IPC and treatment, referrals, and any technical gaps.
Supporting essential infrastructure maintenance, equipment repair and supply to health facilities, in particular for areas in acute need.
Strengthening and consolidating medical supply chains, ensuring availability of safe and quality medicines for essential primary and obstetric care, IPC supplies and PPE, infectious diseases including HIV to prevent mother-to-child transmission, and FP methods.
Pre-positioning emergency stocks of RH kits, medicines, and supplies in line with contingency scenarios for reduced access and increased hostilities.
Strengthening health sector coordination and HIS, emphasizing enhancing protection, increasing access to health services, and continuing coordination within and across hubs, including through joint contingency and preparedness planning.
Supporting continuity of care, strengthening community resilience, responding to IDP movements and shifts in control, and beneficiaries’ participation in programming.
Supporting resilience and early recovery through 2 midwifery schools in NWS and 2 in GOS/NES, and supporting the full implementation of DHIS2 in NWS and later at the national level.
Adapting, updating, and adopting national RH guidelines, including on CMR and Health and Nutrition of PLW in 2023, with the MoH, and developing training packages, subject for potential accreditation.
Continuous monitoring of implementation based on the five AAP principles and UNFPA’s global results-based monitoring framework. UNFPA or third party staff and IPs will conduct joint monthly supervision visits, subject to access/approval.
Standardized tools including the supported services and supervision checklist are used to assess service availability/quality. Clinic registration data will be collated monthly. Beneficiary feedback (complaints box, FGDs, exit interviews) will be collected.

3 - Needs

Health needs are exacerbated by access constraints, COVID-19, cholera, facility damage, the economy, insecurity, displacement, and lack of staff, resulting in higher risk of child labour, GBV, early/forced marriage, and other exploitation. Over 40% of health facilities are partially or fully non-functional (2023 HNO). Access to EmONC is limited, particularly in NES, with few hospitals providing free care and funding shortages limiting staff and supplies. Almost half of sub-districts have under 11 clinical health workers per 10,000 people (GHO 2022). CMR services are limited. WRA are particularly vulnerable at 28% of the 15.3m people in need of health assistance, including 2.1m IDPs and 376,337 expected pregnant women (HNO). In some rural areas, health services are over 100 km away, and the fuel crisis reduced transport access. C-section rates are high at up to 25% of births in NWS and 50% in GOS, as they are easier to schedule, quicker, and follow a previous CS. The Nutrition Cluster reported 12% of WRA are malnourished and 56% have anemia. ANC services target 20% of girls below 18 in NWS and 5% in GOS. The 10 MoH nursing schools graduate 300 nurses and 45 midwives annually. Around 4,077 midwives work in the public sector across Syria (CBS 2019), most in central cities, leaving gaps especially in NES and the south. In NWS, each midwife covers about 2,400 WRA. Breast cancer incidence increased from 30% in 2015 to 39% in 2020, with limited screening and treatment.

4 - Implementation of activities

UNFPA prioritizes maternal and newborn health through integrated SRH and GBV services, supporting 24 EmONCs, 95 PHCs, 154 IMTs and partial support to 923 MoH PHCs in GOS and NES, including cross-line; and 9 EmONCs and 2 mobile clinics in NWS. IDPs are reached with mobile clinics/teams and in camps. UNFPA early recovery activities include SRH services; facility rehabilitation, such as transition to solar power; CVA for obstetric emergencies; capacity building; providing essential RH equipment, medicines, and supplies; strengthened referrals; and improved coordination and standardizing SRH. UNFPA builds resilience through community midwifery groups, midwifery schools, updating the national midwifery curriculum, and incorporating RH into DHIS2 in NWS.UNFPA IPs implement the MISP, supporting the EHSP to reduce maternal mortality. UNFPA ensures social inclusion of PwDs and older persons through transport, accessible facilities and mobile clinics. UNFPA builds capacity on lifesaving protocols; C-sections; CMR; cancer screening; MISP; MHM; GBV risk mitigation/referral; MUAC screening; and outbreak response. UNFPA has designated facilities for cholera in NWS and enhances referral throughout Syria. Beneficiaries participate in the program cycle through AAP and UNFPA’s framework. UNFPA collects monthly data and annually assesses impact.UNFPA refers to other facilities, including EmONC and GBV facilities using referral tracking tools and for other services not available through UNFPA.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

Violence against health facilities, health workers, UNFPA, and IP staff:With the health sector, increase advocacy efforts on health protection and respect of international humanitarian law. Consult with UNDSS, health sector, and local communities if violence escalates on how to safely respond, as this may increase targeting of health facilities and displacement, and reduce access to services.Service gaps and monitoring challenges remain due to insecurity and movement restrictions.UNFPA and IP staff have restricted movement and higher risk in insecure areas, especially NES, NWS, and in camps.Female patients face limited access to services due to insecurity, social norms, and economic situation.Gender-Based Violence: Prevention and management of sexual and intimate partner violence is a lifesaving intervention. Affected populations should have access to the MISP SRH services as part of the essential health package. Train specialized health staff to provide first-line support, CMR, identification of GBV, and safe referrals. Utilize safe referral pathways to ensure comprehensive management of GBV cases connecting health services and clinical management with social and other services. Recruit female staff in health facilities to increase access of women and girls to health services.UNFPA provides equitable health services for vulnerable and minority groups, and collects and analyzes gender, age, and disability disaggregated data on beneficiaries to continually improve.


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

UNFPA enhances the prevention and mitigation of protection risks through mainstreaming the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) into all activities, where UNFPA’s partners integrated PSEA policy into the code of conduct for the facilities staff. Risks: - The COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated PSEA risks for displaced populations. - The deteriorating economic situation inside Syria has led to more human rights abuses, including SEA. - Ongoing conflict enables a context where SEA cases are inadequately addressed. Mitigation: - UNFPA organizes specific trainings on PSEA for staff and IPs- UNFPA conducts PSEA assessments of all its IPs and supports them in developing and validating a PSEA policy. - UNFPA has established a hotline to report any SEA related incidents. - UNFPA has a zero tolerance policy with regards to SEA. UNFPA ensures that implementing partners apply the following:1. Embed PSEA into policies, contracts, and code of conduct; in the health facilities UNFPA ensures that all HFs staff are aware and signed the codes of conduct.2. Embed PSEA into MEAL toolkits; UNFPA is committed to integrating the PSEA and protection mainstreaming components into the design of all monitoring tools, which is applied by the TPM.3. Enhance the awareness of both the HF staff and the beneficiaries regarding the prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse through displaying PSEA posters in the supported health facilities.


9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks: Increased targeting of health facilities, increasing displacement and limiting access to services. Program monitoring is difficult due to restricted access and insecurity. Large displacements overburden partners. Weak and scarce partners can require more resources to ensure quality services. Movements are restricted, relying on authorities’ approval. Limited staff from delayed visa/long recruitment processes, increased outward migration and internal displacement. High turnover of staff results in delay of activities. Sanctions make local procurement difficult, and international procurements are long. Fraud/Aid Diversion as the economy deteriorates. COVID-19 and cholera. Lack of acceptance of sexual terminology, as SRH is a taboo subject and is often not prioritized. Uncertainty on the cross-border resolution renewal will affect services in NWS. Reduced donor funding and prioritization of the Syria crisis may reduce UNFPA’s services. The economic crisis, including inflation, unemployment, fuel shortages, and unstable exchange rates, increases operational costs, limits outreach, and affects access to services. UNFPA has strong contingencies for these risks and leads contingency planning within RH/GBV coordination groups, including access changes, through UNFPA staff and third party monitoring. UNFPA has its own internal risk management framework. UNFPA provides remote and mobile services, including through community midwives, and stocks partners on a quarterly basis.
