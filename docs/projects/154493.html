Project Summary

Women and girls have been disproportionately impacted by the Gender Based Violence (GBV) during the conflict in Iraq. Pervasive GBV abuses, including systematic and targeted rape, abductions, sexual slavery, mutilation, executions, torture and forced marriage have been documented as a deliberate tactic by the Islamic State of Iraq and the Levant (ISIL). In displacement settings as well as areas of return, threats and risks of GBV against women and girls persist, particularly sexual violence, intimate partner violence, family violence, sexual exploitation, harassment and early and forced marriage. Many women and girls may struggle with fears for their personal safety, as possible targets of violence. Cases of sexual violence being used by some militias and armed actors as a reprisal for actual or perceived ties with ISIL have been documented by human rights watch groups. Even before the conflict, GBV was widespread in Iraq and fears of harassment and stigmatization, including honour killing, remain major barriers to seeking services and justice for GBV survivors. Exposure to such violence and chronic stressors may often inflict on survivors’ psychosocial well-being and mental health.
The objectives of the proposed project are GBV prevention at community level and multi-sectoral support services for women and children affected by violence and conflict. The project will support the delivery of an integrated package of risk mitigation and prevention of GBV activities, at the community level, and specialized services to survivors in the areas with severe needs. The aim of community-based GBV prevention interventions is to provide immediate support to women and children at risk, mitigate risks of GBV, and shift social norms that underpin GBV by promoting gender equality and behavioural change and working with community-based structures. The project will also strengthen service provision with a strong focus on improving care for child survivors and the most complex cases and the quality of specialized services such as case management and PSS. Complementing the specialized services and PSS, recreational activities supported by the project will help women and children cope with the psychological aftermath of conflict and violence and strengthen their resilience skills, with a strong focus on adolescent girl-focused interventions to empower girls and increase girls’ access to services.
To ensure that quality case management services are available to survivors in some of the worst conflict-affected areas of Iraq, the project will also address capacity strengthening of the critical sectors (PSS, health, legal, security) to provide quality services to survivors of GBV, and will increase access to support and services through women and girls’ centers and targeted GBV legal assistance. This project targets women, girls and boys in IDPs (in and out of camp), returnees and host communities.

Needs

Women and girls have been disproportionately impacted by GBV during the conflict and continue to face risks of multiple and intersecting forms of discrimination and violence in the post-conflict Iraq. Sexual violence, systematic and targeted rape, abductions, sexual slavery, executions, torture and forced marriage, were used as a deliberate tactic by ISIL. As displacement becomes increasingly protracted and the general lack of safety in communities continues to deteriorate, risks of all forms of GBV against women and girls persist, including sexual violence, intimate partner and family violence, sexual exploitation, harassment and forced marriage. Almost 50% of married women have experienced some form of violence by their husband, and 46% of girls aged 10-14 suffered violence at least once by a family member (Ministry of Planning Central Statistical Organization-CSO, “Iraq Women Integrated Social and Health Survey (I-WISH) Summary Report,” March 2012). This violence is legitimized by communities through reference to cultural and religious norms (UNFPA, A report on the GBV Assessment in conflict affected governorates in Iraq, 2016). Children born of rape by ISIL fighters and their mothers face stigma and rejection by communities making it difficult to obtain basic services and legal protection. Grave concerns about SEA have been reported, including women and girls forced to exchange sex for basic needs, and the presence of security actors in camps exacerbates these risks. In some IDP camps, sexual violence being used by militias and security actors as a reprisal for actual or perceived ties with ISIL have been documented (Amnesty International, The Condemned: Women and Children Isolated, Trapped and Exploited in Iraq, 2018.) Many women and girls in Iraq still struggle with fears for their personal safety, as possible targets of violence, and female headed households and adolescent girls are at higher risk of GBV, especially sexual violence. Girls who have been sexually abused are at higher risk of experiencing intimate partner violence and of being involved, or exploited, in sex work later in life (UNICEF, A Statistical Snapshot of Violence against Adolescent Girls, UNICEF, New York, 2014). Child marriage is another risk facing girls in Iraq. About 5% of girls were married before the age of 15 while approximately 22% of girls married before they turned 18 (UNFPA, 2016. Child Marriage in Kurdistan region-Iraq). Due to the recent conflict in Iraq, the child marriage has also been used by families as a negative coping mechanism against economic hardship; while honour-based violence continues unabated as the community and the legal system closes a blind eye. Overall levels of reporting on GBV are low as most women do not trust police to report violence. Fears of harassment and stigmatization are real barriers to accessing services and seeking justice for GBV survivors. Exposure to conflict and violence and chronic stressors may often inflict on their psychosocial well-being and mental health. The limited availability of specialized GBV and PSS services falls short of meeting increasing needs. Significant gaps exist in services for girls, especially age-appropriate PSS, educational and recreational support (UNICEF, UNFPA, Adolescent Girls Assessment Report, Kurdistan Region of Iraq, May 2016). GBV service provision is at risk due to the limited funding resources available, resulting in an urgent need to consolidate foundational work to strengthen specialized services and the local capacity to sustainably address this need.

Protection Risk Analysis

Due to the dynamic political and security situation in Iraq, particularly outside of the Kurdistan Region of Iraq, the overall political and social environment is subject to change, and there are moderate risks of access constraints affecting parts of the country, the presence of security actors and armed groups, and sectarian tensions which may impact UNICEF and partners’ operational presence in some areas. Key protection risks for programme participants include:Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (SEA). UNICEF identified Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (SEA) as a substantial risk during project preparation. Women and girls in particular are at risk of SEA who are perceived to have affiliation with ISIL group. Child protection partner who provide legal assistance and finding whereabout arrested children may receive threat and protection risk. Participant safety and confidentiality. There are numerous ethical and safety concerns related to participation in GBV programming and data collection in humanitarian settings, and these concerns are heightened when dealing with sensitive or taboo subjects such as violence and when working with children. In addition, certain categories of beneficiaries and programme participants may face increased risks or unintended negative consequences from participation in the project activities as it may lead to unintentional disclosure of their sensitive personal information or status. These include children born of rape and as a result of conflict and their mothers and LGBTQI persons. This is an extremely vulnerable category of people in need facing serious protections risks, which may be exacerbated by their participation in the programme.

Mitigation Measures

Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (SEA). UNICEF takes a proactive approach to protection from sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA): a Code of Conduct, PSEA reporting mechanism, support to and guidance for the survivors in case any instances of SEA were to occur within the context of the project, establishing a survivor-centered approach with multiple entry points for anyone experiencing SEA to seek the help they need; and these measures are taken in close coordination with the Iraq PSEA Network and partner organizations.UNICEF partners are trained on the Do No Harm Principle, PSEA, and Community Based Complaints mechanism. As part of partnership monitoring, UNICEF conducts monitoring visits and holds partnership review meetings with implementing partners to review progress and help identify challenges and solutions, including protection risks. UNICEF requires that all staff working on the project sign the organisation’s code of conduct, which includes the obligation to report any abuse by staff, or suspicion thereof. UNICEF has strict protocols against SEA.Participant safety and confidentiality. UNICEF is committed to ensuring the confidentiality and safety of project participants and of all data relating to participants. To minimize risks to beneficiaries’ safety, UNICEF will implement the measures recommended by the IASC GBV guidelines and by GBVIMS for addressing ethical and safety issues and to ensure confidentiality in GBV programming. To reduce risks or harm to vulnerable populations in the face of conflict sensitivity, beneficiaries will be systematically involved in all stages of the project cycle. UNICEF has established close working relationship with key government institutions, directorates, and ministries such as High Judicial Council in Baghdad, Ministry of Justice, Ministry of Labour and Social Affairs, and Directorate of Combating Violence against Women and periodically discusses matters affecting those categories of boys, girls and women to agree on steps and approaches to address key issues. UNICEF is closely working with implementing partners to document any critical protection risk and challenges and address it with appropriate channel. UNICEF recognizes that families and communities have the greatest impact on child well-being. By using a community based approach, using entry points such as schools to reach children, and by identifying and addressing social norms that constrain and affect positive outcomes for children, UNICEF works to lessen the stigma associated to experiences of violence, as well as to help-seeking.

How is your project accountable to affected population

• Planning: UNICEF and its partners conduct needs assessment focused on the identification of the unique and different needs, opportunities and capacities of women, adolescent girls, boys, and men, and use it to determine the project approach and strategies for community outreach and engagement.• Implementation: The technical design of the project recognizes the specificity of women’s and girls’ protection needs and opportunities for building protective assets. All project activities will be tailored and delivered as per the recommendations from women and girls so as not to increase the risks to their safety from their participation in project activities. • Monitoring and Evaluation: all effort will be made to collect feedback from women, girls, boys and men both informally (ongoing discussions with participants of various activities) and more formally through FGDs with programme participants, pre/post training assessments completed by male and female participants. UNICEF monitoring and reporting system includes beneficiary feedback to ensure the voice of beneficiaries are reflected in the monitoring and evaluation. • All data collected through the project activities will be disaggregated by sex and age, to the extent possible, and the project activities will contribute to promoting survivor centred approaches.
