Project Summary

NCA is an active member of the Mosul, Duhok, and the Iraq (national) MHPSS working groups, and regularly co-ordinates with other MHPSS providers to provide care to those who need it the most. The beneficiaries that NCA serves are vulnerable women, girls, boys, and men that have been impacted by trauma and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) as a result of conflict. Based upon the findings generated from NCA’s three years of experience providing GBV services in Iraq; collaborative discussions with local government, local and international NGOs, UN organizations, and other stakeholders; and other independent research, NCA proposes the following program in Iraq for 2019-2020 to address the country’s most dire needs as it strives to recover from the deeps wounds left by ISIS and other inter-group conflict:
The overall goal of the proposed project is to empower conflict-affected women, girls, boys, and men to overcome the traumatic impact of GBV and displacement. To achieve this, NCA will continue to offer holistic wrap-around GBV and MHPSS services to internally displaced persons (IDPs), returnees and host communities in conflict-impacted areas of Ninawa and Dohuk. With this project, NCA also proposes to expand its ongoing activities into conflict-impacted areas of Ninawa plains.
The target beneficiaries will continue to include women, girls, boys and men; families; and other vulnerable people in camps, non-camps and return areas in Ninawa. Key activities will include provision of high quality, culturally appropriate multi-sectoral support to GBV and CRSV survivors. Services will include wrap-around case management, community-based mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS), peer support groups, livelihood and economic assistance, and life skills courses. In line with rights-based aid, NCA is planning to include cash-for-protection as a component of its case management services. This will enable women, girls, boys, and men survivors of GBV and CRSV who have been subjected to trafficking, sexual slavery, and rape to depend on themselves; it will also enhance their resiliency, reduce vulnerability, and mitigate the risk of ongoing GBV.
Through MHPSS and GBV specific capacity-building activities with local partners, the Department of Health (DOH) of Ninawa and Dohuk, and other parties, NCA will seek to reduce the incidence of ongoing GBV by teaching boys and men more effective anger management skills. Finally, through carefully designed inclusive, participatory activities, NCA will seek to improve social cohesion so that communities can stabilize and effectively work together to eliminate GBV. The need for this project is based on the fact that the local authorities and its departments are currently unable to deliver the response that is so critically needed to assist the large number of traumatized women, girls, boys, and men overcome the debilitating effects of the complex traumatization that they experienced during the reign of ISIS.

Needs

Iraq continues to suffer from a severe humanitarian crisis that has serious implications for vulnerable populations including Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs), returnees, and host communities in conflict-affected areas. To protect their physical and psychological well-being, appropriate and responsive assistance is urgently needed. Over the past decade, these populations have been subjected to grave violations of international human rights law that include collective punishment, gender-based violence (GBV), and other related atrocities on a frequent and ongoing basis. This has contributed to widespread traumatization and rapid erosion of social cohesion. The Iraqi post-ISIS environment is characterized by political instability and an economic crisis. An element of fear persists as remnants of ISIS continue to lurk in various areas of the country. GBV - and more specifically conflict-related sexual violence (CRSV) - is also pervasive throughout Iraq. Several thousands of severely traumatized survivors of GBV and CRSV are in immediate need of life-saving assistance that includes MHPSS, medical care, economic assistance, and legal support. Many survivors are widowed as a result of the conflict and now face numerous obstacles that prevent them from achieving economic stability; this has forced some to resort to “survival sex”. If left unaided, these vulnerable GBV survivors that have already experienced so much loss and despair have little chance of escaping the cycle of violence in which they are entrapped. Existing structures in Iraq are unable to adequately address the ubiquitous and complex needs of these survivors. In camps and non-camps in conflict-impacted areas, there are significant gaps in terms of GBV services. Local and international service providers are inadequate to meet the legal, medical, and economic needs of GBV survivors. Additionally, the supply of trained mental health professionals is significantly insufficient to provide the level of specialized psychological care that is needed to support the recovery of highly traumatized GBV survivors. This is particularly true in Duhok and Ninawa governorate, which were both ISIS strongholds. The lack of appropriate support services is further exacerbated by the stigma associated with being a GBV survivor, and women and girls with perceived affiliation to extremists are especially affected. If these needs are not effectively addressed, a significant portion of Iraq’s current generation will continue to suffer, and these emotional scars will then be transferred to the next generation. The consequences for society – both inside and outside of Iraq - will be severe. Over the past year, serious concerns related to sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) have been reported, and women and girls in Iraq still struggle with fears for their personal safety. Female-headed households and adolescent girls are at a much higher risk of GBV, and in some Iraqi IDP camps, sexual violence has been utilized by militias and security actors as a reprisal for actual or perceived ties to ISIS . Along with health care and livelihoods, mental health and psychosocial support services (MHPSS) are notably scarce in this region. Access to this critical service is further complicated by social stigma. Another important factor that impacts the mental health of GBV survivors is economic. Currently, Iraq is unable to provide GBV survivors with the economic/ livelihood opportunities that they need to support themselves. Thus, in order to pay for basic needs such as food and shelter, many survivors are forced to resort to survival sex. Such desperate measures are re-traumatizing, and thus have serious consequences. Without assistance, many of these survivors will continue to suffer the consequences of complex trauma. In Sinjar and Ninawa governorate, much of the population has been subjected to GBV and/or multiple, prolonged displacement. However, it is important to note that host communities have also been impacted by these displacements as they have had to absorb large numbers of IDPs. This has overburdened security, protection, health care, and livelihoods resources that were extremely limited to begin with. The continued depletion of resources and opportunities will indubitably increase social tensions and deteriorate psychosocial well-being. If unaddressed, the situation described above will not improve, and will likely deteriorate. An assessment conducted by Reach and CCCM reflects that 86% of IDPs households in Dohuk governorate reported that they intended to remain in their current location, while just 2% of IDPs households reported that they intended to return to their area of origin in the immediate future. 74% of households from Sinjar stated that they did not think it was currently safe to return as there were ongoing issues with interpersonal clashes, poor infrastructure, explosive hazards, and movement restrictions.

Protection Risk Analysis

The Iraqi post-ISIS environment is characterized by security and political instability and there are still remnants of ISIS in various parts of the country. Thus, there are several possible risks for both beneficiaries and team members. In regards to team members, it is possible that members of the GBV team may receive threats and face protection risks as GBV is a sensitive topic and NCA is an international organization. Additionally, changes in political power may impact access to targeted operational areas. In regards to beneficiaries, some adolescent girls and children that are seeking support may be associated with armed groups in Ninawa and Sinjar areas, which may place them at an increased risk. There are also many cases of children being born out of rape as a result of the conflict. Many of these children have now been shunned by the families of their mothers, thereby placing them at-risk. Finally, women and girls with perceived affiliation to extremists may also face severe protection risks.

Mitigation Measures

NCA has been able to implement its projects directly through its own staff and through the staff of local partners in the most insecure areas of northern Iraq. NCA maintains a strong relationship with both the KRI and the central government of Iraq, which enables us to gain access and security permissions to in our areas of operation. NCA engages and consults with community leaders on an ongoing basis, and we actively encourage participation and project ownership in target areas. NCA also take steps to ensure all staff remain sensitive and respectful of local social norms. NCA’s staff and partners abide by to the ACT-Alliance Code of Conduct, and NCA demands that all staff fully comply with Core Humanitarian Standards (CHS). To ensure that all projects are being implemented safely and appropriately, NCA conducts regular monitoring and evaluation field visit. All of NCA’s on-going and proposed activities are in full alignment with UNSCR 1325; they are culturally appropriate, inclusive, and support confidentiality. NCA and its partners take steps to ensure the safety, dignity, and privacy of its beneficiaries at safe spaces as well as in their communities.

How is your project accountable to affected population

NCA and its implementing local partners will ensure that project activities are in line with UNSCR 1325, and are inclusive, culturally appropriate and confidential. NCA will not deny services or discriminate in its treatment of survivors based on ethnicity, religious affiliation, gender, age, or otherwise. The core of the proposed programme is participation and empowerment; thus, each beneficiary is recognized and treated as an individual that is fully capable of determining their own goals and treatment plan. Beneficiaries will play a key role in identifying their own treatment goals and their care plan. Prevention activities will also be planned in consultation with women, girls, boys, and men, and various leaders in target communities. Finally, beneficiaries will have various mechanisms available for them to provide feedback, including satisfaction surveys and a confidential complaint response mechanism that allows them to make suggestions or file complaints locally or (if needed) with our Home Office in Oslo.
