Needs

Despite improvements to the security situation in Iraq, socio-economic concerns abound, including repeat displacement due to military operations, financial difficulties and limited livelihood opportunities, and unsuccessful attempts to return to their area of origin due to lack of services. Iraq continues to suffer from a protection crisis, with severe implications for the rights of vulnerable IDPs, returnees, and host and non-displaced individuals, with specific protection needs. Displaced women and girls are among the most at risk – specifically to forms of gender-based violence. The separation of many displaced women and girls from community and familial support structures, as well as from traditional livelihoods activities, contributes to an increased reliance on hazardous livelihood strategies (Cohort Livelihoods and Risk Assessment Tool, Oxfam 2016.) Female heads of household are particularly vulnerable and have reported moving to camps after incidents of sexual harassment in non-camp settings (UNHCR Iraq, September 2018). As observed through IMC programs in Iraq, domestic or intimate partner violence (IPV) is a significant challenge. According to GBVIMS data collected by IMC between January 2017 and October 2018, 75% of reported GBV cases were related to IPV. Of concern also, adolescent girls assume roles and responsibilities that restrict their mobility and visibility, increasing their isolation and breaking bonds with their peers and with other social networks, and reducing access to education. In a participatory assessment conducted by UNHCR, in Iraq prevailing protection concerns related to child marriage and child labor. An estimated 80% of GBV survivors receiving services from IMC in 2017 and 2018 requested referrals to livelihoods services, including income-generating activities. The lack of livelihoods opportunities has been a topic of discussion at all recent GBV sub-cluster meetings, amid fears that financial constraints are forcing women and girls to adopt negative coping mechanisms. There is an absence of basic protection safeguards and assistance at IDP sites and returnee locations particularly in retaken and underserved areas. In 2018, IMC conducted safety audits, assessments and service mapping exercises in areas where displaced and host populations were identified in particular in Anbar. Key findings from assessments are as follows: women do not have access to health services or women’s centers/spaces; limited sources for women on location of available services; and limited local partner capacity to deliver support for women and girls. Families are reluctant to send young girls to schools due to concerns over their safety and female-headed households are in need of recreational activities (to strengthen self-esteem) and capacity building initiatives to foster economic empowerment. There is a lack of access to basic services such as electricity, potable water supply and sanitation facilities, as well as inadequate sources of food that exacerbate existing protection concerns as women and girls under risky travel or professions to meet basic needs.

Activities

Drawing upon IMC’s experience as a leading health, MHPSS, and GBV service provider in Iraq and utilizing the protection cluster’s sequenced response, IMC will build upon and complement existing health, MHPSS, and GBV activities, which are designed to improve access and services among conflict-affected women and girls to comprehensive and holistic support for recovery. IMC will continue to contribute to the Protection Cluster’s efforts to provide GBV prevention and response to the areas where conflict–affected Iraqis, especially girls and women, are most vulnerable. IMC will support static primary health care units, mobile medical units, outreach teams, and women’s centers to provide specialized protection assistance and support services for survivors of GBV. The women’s centers will provide survivor-centered case management services to improve access to GBV and psychosocial support services. In addition to GBV case management, women and girls will benefit from opportunities to gain and diversify skillsets through demand-driven skills building activities. To address the isolation that displaced women face, psychosocial and recreational activities that maximize interactions with other women and create opportunities to access information about services available. This will enable the women to create sustained social support networks. All PSS activities within the center will be structured to emphasize sustainability, and to foster resilience. In addition to direct service provision through static and mobile teams, IMC will strengthen the capacity of service providers and community-based groups to appropriately respond to GBV and ensure focused support for survivors, aligning with cluster priorities to support most at-risk conflict-affected populations. IMC will also develop a strong facility and community based referral pathways for further access to specialized services depending on the needs and consent of the survivor. By building the capacity of local partners (community-based organization, local authorities, and government counterparts) IMC will ensure that there is sustainable infrastructures to continue to provide quality services. IMC will implement programming targeting adolescent girls and deliver sessions from the Adolescent Girls’ Toolkit Curriculum designed to empower girls living in Iraq through the delivery of Life Skills, Reproductive Health, GBV, Financial Education and Leadership information. Sessions will equip girls with knowledge around sexual reproductive health and family planning, and life skills such as communication and financial literacy that will strengthen their coping mechanisms without encouraging the practice of child marriage. IMC remains aware of the child labor guidelines and the aim it to avoid these practices, but support girls in having financial and economic empowerment as part of their future planning. IMC will also utilize Engaging Men through Accountable Practice (EMAP) curriculum to reduce harmful behavior, increase gender equality at home, and equip men with knowledge to rethink belief systems and prevent GBV through individual behavior change. EMAP is a one-year prevention intervention that provides staff with field tested and evidence based curriculum for engaging men in transformative behavior change, guided by the voices of women. This framework provides a method and structure with takes into account women’s leadership and developing male engagement in a way that improves lives of women and girls. For women this program provides a safe space to voice their reactions and concerns on the intervention and their priorities moving forward. This will also provide female participants with opportunities to reflect on violence in their communities and develop strategies to influence programming with men in their community. IMC will identify male leaders, gatekeepers, and religious leaders (key strategic allies) and engage in dialogue and reflection on their attitude and experience with gender norms and violence against women. Developing and encouraging male participants to become allies in empowering and protecting women as part of improving the community is key part of this project. Additionally, the development of community-based referral pathways allows key community members to establish networks and mechanisms needed to provide initial care and support and emergency referrals for survivors of GBV, even after IMC transitions out. Focusing on displaced women, IMC will implement an Economic and Social Empowerment (EA$E) program to strengthen the financial stability of women and provide self-sustaining opportunities to create more equitable and safe gender dynamics with dynamics within their households. The EA$E Model combines tested economic interventions like Village Savings and Loans Associations (VSLAs) and business skills training with targeted gender discussion facilitated for female VSLA members and their spouses or key male guardians at home.

Mitigation Measures

1. IMC will advertise the vacancy in different areas within the governorates to hire qualified staff for PSS/GBV program positions. IMC will also dedicate to build the capacity of the national staff and community workers through regular in service trainings and monthly mentoring sessions. Community participation has been a key aspect of the design of this proposed project with community leader’s women and girl’s groups consulted during the ongoing projects as well as during the needs assessments. They will also be included as part of the participatory M&E system that will be put in place for this project.IMC will continue to work closely with the community, building capacity of GBV national staff based in areas of intervention, as well as the key community stakeholders, to encourage and increase community participation.2. If community participation decreases during the project implementation period, IMC will seek advice from community leaders and local representatives to find alternative ways of engaging the community. By including community members and marginalized groups, such as persons with special needs, female heads of households, disabled people, adolescents, and the elderly groups during ongoing assessments and project implementation IMC ensure contextually appropriate and tailored interventions that meet the unique needs of each group. 3. IMC will maintain regular security analysis of the areas closest to the front line – in particular, those areas where the conflict is the most fluid. Should it seem likely that insecurity will heighten, IMC will quickly identify new locations in which it can work to provide emergency response services, until the period of crisis has passed, and more sustainable programming can be put in place. Additionally, the logistics and accessibility challenges associated with the rainy season require careful planning and organization in order to ensure sufficient supplies and equipment are available during this time. IMC will liaise with MAG to have the teams trained on identification of IED or other explosive devices.

How is your project accountable to affected population

IMC operates on humanitarian principles including sustainability, care, engagement, transparency, and accountability. Accountability to affected population (AAP) is integral part of International Medical Corps’ programming for quality services provision as it endorses and uses five Commitments on Accountability to Affected Populations (CAAPs). The four commitments are 1) leadership, 2) participation and partnership, 3) information, feedback and action, and 4) results. Prior to commencing this project, IMC will develop a tailored AAP framework and the CAAPs will be incorporated into implementation strategies to promote their implementation within all the aspects of the project.IMC will work with community leadership, local staff, and beneficiaries to build capacity and promote active participation and ownership of the project. As part of IMC’s commitment to ensure the meaningful involvement of community members, including women, adolescent girls, persons with special needs, and others in particularly vulnerable situations, throughout the entire project cycle, IMC will use a series of focus group discussions, key informant interviews, 2017 data analysis, and staff observations to help make sure the project is consistently aligned with identified needs. Through the use of participatory learning and action tools, IMC actively seeks community participation in needs and resource assessments, identification of critical protection gaps, and development of community-based action plans to prevent and reduce risks. IMC operates with the belief that when solutions are developed from within communities themselves, they are more likely to be locally appropriate, feasible, and, ultimately, sustainable. For example, prior to establishing women’s centers, discussions with women and girls and community leaders will inform location. Additionally, women and girls will have the opportunity to express their needs for specific recreational and skills-building activities, to ensure they are demand-driven. IMC’s integrated, participatory approach takes into account the variable nature of the current situation and relies on the engagement of local actors to ensure appropriate implementation of all programs. IMC will continue to prioritize work with local stakeholders to strengthen their capacities and skills, while utilizing local knowledge and increasing community-ownership and leadership.
