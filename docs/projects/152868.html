Project Summary

The project will contribute to all three strategic objectives of the 2019 HRP and all three of the FSL cluster objectives through the support of CARE donors and FSL partners to support early recovery, improved livelihoods and resilience for vulnerable women, men, boys and girls by improving access to food consumption in the emergency /catastrophe phase, increasing agricultural production, protecting and building productive assets and restoring livelihoods including use of cash in target areas affected by conflict and natural disasters. It will also provide emergency regular life sustaining and life-saving food assistance, increase vulnerable household food production and strengthen households and community coping capacities by building productive assets and reduce reliance on general food distribution transfers to 100,000 (16,000HH) vulnerable men, women, boys and girls including IDPs/host communities/ returnees. CARE will promote positive coping mechanisms among the most vulnerable beneficiaries including returnees, IDPs and host communities. Targeted vulnerable beneficiaries identified based on IPC classification will be mobilised, assessed and identified based on a widely consulted agreed upon selection criteria for support through appropriate cash / food vouchers after-market assessments have been done to determine which modality is suitable. During community mobilisation, CARE will sensitize households on relevant and context specific messaging on proper nutrition, hygiene and protection, especially for lactating / pregnant women and for children. CARE will also provide season appropriate agricultural inputs i.e. seeds / tools / fishing kits after providing appropriate training, in consultation and collaboration with the targeted communities’ support cash for work activities that will lead to improvement of livelihoods at community level, as well as building longer term community resilience. Special attention will be given to female headed households in providing support for agricultural inputs, protecting and building productive assets and restoration of livelihoods.

Needs

In 2019 the food security situation is again likely to deteriorate for the sixth consecutive year with 5.2 million people (49% of the total population) severely food insecure (IPC 3, 4 & 5) in the January – March 2019 projection period excluding 2,467,911 million refugees according to UNHCR as of 30 September 2018. With the recent peace agreement signed and ratified, the number of returnees is expected to increase during the lean season and may even surpass the 6 million in the July/ August 2019 period. In light of the multi-context crisis: violence & conflict, food insecurity, economic crisis, disease outbreaks and the collapse of healthcare and social services, the 2019 lean season is projected to begin early and probably become worse than in 2019. The economic crisis is further exacerbating the dire situation and having a major impact on food security indicators for the market-dependant populations in both rural and urban areas. As a result, extreme levels of acute food insecurity will persist in many areas. Consequently, levels of acute malnutrition are expected to deteriorate in the first quarter of 2019 with an early onset of the lean season as most households are expected to deplete food stocks from own production. According to the UN OCHA (Humanitarian Needs Overview, Nutrition Cluster, and Save the Children research. (Save the Children operational estimates based on FSNAU) July 2018 update, as the lean season begins earlier than usual this year, 270,000 children in South Sudan are severely malnourished (SAM) and at risk of starvation. Some 20,000 could be expected to die from extreme hunger before the end of 2018. Based on the September 2018 IPC analysis, it is anticipated that between January – March 2019, Eastern Equatoria will have 385,000 people, Jonglei 1,180,000 people and Unity 615,000 people in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity or famine / catastrophe. According to REACH, in Bor South 57%, Twic East 50%, Koch 73%, (with 64% reporting hunger as severe), Mayom 73% (with 20 % reporting the large impact of newly arrived IDPs inability to access sufficient food), Rubkhona 62% (with 82% reporting going entire days without eating as a coping strategy) of assessed settlements reported the negative impact of health problems as a consequence of their inability to access sufficient food. According to the September 2018 IPC, in January - March 2019 catastrophe (IPC Phase 5) is expected in Pibor. In Kapoeta East, North and South, recent FSL cluster coordinator mission field reports indicate that food security has similarly deteriorated, with some households in the counties likely experiencing Catastrophe—IPC 5—levels of acute food insecurity and requiring life-saving assistance. With the signing and ratification of the peace agreement formalizing the revitalization of the 2015 agreement on the resolution of the conflict in South Sudan, huge numbers of returnees are expected further exacerbating the situation.

Nature of the consultations

Yes

Briefly describe the nature of the consultations

CARE is not new in areas proposed for this project. There is ongoing engagement will all relevant stakeholders including community leaders, youth groups, VSLAs, and project management committee’s leadership. These leadership’s structures and community entry points will easily be applied to make consultation with communities in the most time and cost effective ways.The approach of setting up project management committees (PMCs) will be applied intensely in this project. The PMCs composition will encompass at least 50% women and youth members. The participation criteria will also consider the disabled and other vulnerable/marginalized community groups and members. Targeting and site selection will be handled by community target committees. The committees’ members are conversant with context and risks that may arise in the project initiation and implementation. Their knowledge on risks mitigation will be applied so that food and livelihood assistance is implemented diligently and “Does no Harm’’ to the targeted communities. Critical decisions will be taken carefully to ensure assistance does not exacerbate the relationships between different social and ethnic groups.

Community Engagement?

Yes

Briefly describe how

CARE has robust internal and community based structures i.e staff, (1 M&E national coordinator, 3 field based officers and community volunteers in health, nutrition & protection) and systems (Individual grant indicators tracker) in place to monitor and evaluate (M&E) activities at output and activity level, and allow for effective data collection, analysis and reporting. CARE conducts M&E to ensure that project development, design and implementation is in line with community indicated needs, and humanitarian strategies. Progress against deliverables are religiously monitored, activities and challenges evaluated and potential amendments rapidly agreed upon and effected. CARE regularly at attend and update cluster level with partners (at national and state level) on the implementation progress. A monitoring and reporting plan for each approved project is developed to track and report on agreed upon outputs. Outputs/data are disaggregated by gender and captured on narrative reports as per donor templates and reporting requirements. At the field level, CARE will also work in partnership with the existing partners, community level structures, beneficiaries, other integrated response teams and other agencies to monitor progress. Community engagement is regularly collected using the established and active Feedback, Complaints and Response Mechanisms at proposed geographical sites.

Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

CARE considers the following factors that might bar access;• Inclusion and exclusion errors leading to tensions in the communities• Shift of power dynamics within the households• Increasing exposure to exploitation by employers, clients, suppliers• Inciting backlash from family or community members when women start earning money• Increasing vulnerabilities of theft and violence due to greater assets and wealth• Reinforcing inequalities by continuing to limit choices to more vulnerable groups• Causing false expectations and dependence on short term surge of cash or assets• Increasing costs and drawing on resources, such as providing small livestock without fodder or veterinary care• Exacerbating tensions between host community and affected community

Addresses inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

As the proposed project will be implemented in an emergency context, CARE mainstreams protection principles into project activities. The Do no Harm principle will be maintained to ensure that project activities do not expose communities and the most vulnerable groups such as women and girls to harm and they are provided with protection when receiving vegetable and seeds kits. Information provided by survivors or beneficiaries are safeguarded and kept confidential so as not to be used to expose them to harm. Our actions addresses both basic needs including dignity kits that improve protection of those at risk of violations. We also pay special attention to people living with disabilities, children, child-headed and female-headed households and communities to further harm.

Project activities address inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

CARE tackles inequality and incorporates protection threats throughout all stages of its response cycle. As a SAG member of the Protection - GBV sub - cluster nationally, CARE is ideally placed upon alert, to raise, consult and resolve any issues with the protection cluster to ensure an intervention will not cause harm. Regular protection assessments are conducted for all of CARE’s projects in close coordination with other partners and stakeholders and enforced by protection staff involvement during project implementation where possible. In the first stage of assessment, partners review the context for conflict sensitive and HLP issues, and referral pathways, in order to improve understanding of the context dynamics and therefore decrease harm on the community. CARE’s field assessments begin with safety audits to understand risks of every gender. Both the assessment and response stages of CARE projects incorporate a high level of community engagement, capacity building, and feedback mechanisms in order to ensure all people, especially marginalized groups, have an active role in the way CARE projects are carried out. With evidence about particular vulnerabilities, CARE takes special measures to support vulnerable beneficiaries. Where possible, integrated thematic outreach and protection messaging is carried out.

Does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

In all its operational areas, CARE is guided by the principles of fairness, high quality of work, respect, focus on community engagement and interaction, and integrity that have acknowledged by the communities and beneficiaries it serves. Strong community acceptance and buy in is secured during initial project design consultations, project inception during and after project implementation. Aside from the sporadic insecurity incidents of which community members and beneficiaries / CARE vice versa regularly warn of the threats and risks access is generally not a problem for CARE and beneficiaries since they are regularly consulted on appropriate site selection, developing beneficiary targeting criteria & selection and good open lines of communication have been established which ensure that only vulnerable beneficiaries are targeted and benefit. From previous experience, in new communities CARE is more than often invited to work with them, as opposed to the need to negotiate access into them. Regular joint supervision field visits are purposed and conducted in consultation with stakeholders and partners to monitor access and quality of services being proffered to beneficiaries.

Does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

CARE maintains a security team at country office whose responsibility is to conduct security risk assessments before project roll out, during implementation and after in all its operational sites. CARE works to obtain required security clearance before traveling to the proposed project locations in light of the context. In case of any impending or actualized insecurity incidents CARE endeavours to keep our beneficiaries, cluster members and other stakeholders duly informed of any associated changes. Before, during and after project implementation, CARE undertakes significant safety and security briefing for beneficiaries on the relevant context specific safety and security risks that face them. From experience, it is noted that communities and beneficiaries are very aware of the different risks and appropriate safety and security measure they can take. In some cases, as a result of the close collaboration, they do have prior information of such incidences and inform our staffs who then implement precautionary/ remedial measures. Continuous conducted safety and security trainings have ensured that staff have a growing security awareness.

Does the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

Yes

How the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

CARE bases its humanitarian response on impartial assessment of needs, vulnerabilities and capacities to identify the most appropriate response. These assessments provide CARE with an understanding of the humanitarian situation and analysis of threats to life, dignity, health and livelihoods, as well as people’s ability to help themselves. And further help project teams determine, in consultation with the relevant authorities, organisations and the affected population, what CARE's response should be and whom it should target. Considerable emphasis is placed on beneficiary targeting criteria and selection based on disaggregated data by sex and age to ensure that women, girls, boys and men are targeted appropriately. Some groups will be at greater risk because of their age, gender, disability, social status, ethnicity or religion. CARE through these assessments seeks to understand these distinctions and tailor its response accordingly. Our action endeavours to reflect impartial assessment, and the right of those affected by an emergency to the minimum conditions they need to live in dignity. It aims to reinforce local capacities for self-help by vulnerable and at risk beneficiaries, communities and the response of partners and authorities. Ultimately, CARE endeavours by building resilience to minimize communities’ future risk of disaster.
