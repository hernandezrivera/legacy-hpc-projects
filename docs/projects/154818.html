Project Summary

In 2019 and beyond, UNHCR’s SGBV programmes in Iraq will focus on strengthening the capacity of national institutions, creating conditions conducive to disclosure, ensuring lifesaving services for survivors, and promoting innovative approaches to change social and cultural norms that justify or tolerate violence against women and girls. UNHCR will support capacity building of UNHCR staff, partners, law enforcement bodies and government officials to identify, refer and effectively support survivors to reintegrate back into their communities. UNHCR will mainstream SGBV into other sectoral response (NFI, Shelter and camp management) and community-based SGBV mechanisms to ensure that communities gradually assume responsibility for psychosocial support programmes and case management of non-complex cases. Engagement with local organisations and civil society networks, including human rights defenders, will be key in ensuring access to prevention and response services in areas of return. The existing capacity building project for the Directorate for Combating Violence against Women (DCVAW) will be upscaled to ensure provision of comprehensive SGBV prevention and response services in Erbil, Dohuk and Sulaimaniyah, Mosul and Ninewa. UNHCR will reinforce and sustain existing partnerships and collaborations with UNFPA, UNWOMEN, UNDP and other protection partners within the framework of the GBV Sub Cluster and other coordination mechanisms including GBV TF, TF on Child Marriage, TF on Survival Sex, RTAP, MARA, and through its co-leadership of the GBVIMS to ensure prioritisation of interventions addressing the protection needs of women and girls, especially those subjected to ISIL related sexual violence.

Needs

Generally, SGBV remains widespread in Iraq. Underfunding of the humanitarian response coupled with socio-economic hardship continues to expose displaced women, girls, men and boys to major protection risks - with limited access to prevention, mitigation and response services. Different forms of SGBV including sexual violence, sexual exploitation and/or abuse (SEA), denial of access to services, domestic violence, trafficking, forced and/or child marriage and other harmful traditional practices, remain serious protection concerns. The humanitarian community in Iraq has predominantly focused on life-saving interventions in cases of sexual violence against women and girls and on strengthening SGBV response in four main sectors - health, psychosocial, legal and security. Other types of violence, for example economic and socio-cultural violence, psychological violence due to stigma and discrimination or SGBV against LGBTI persons of concern or men and boys are less commonly addressed in a coordinated way. While the increased risks of SGBV (including SEA, child marriage and survival sex) due to economic hardship and limited access to assistance and services has been highlighted by the humanitarian community, there is still a gap in terms of comprehensiveness, coverage and AGD sensitivity in livelihood programming given the high number of female headed households. The current context represents a moment when gender roles, responsibilities and practices are being re-negotiated by communities themselves. Assessments conducted in IDP camps, non-camp settings and liberated areas in 2018 reveal that IDPs have limited access to reproductive health and SGBV prevention and response services. Protection assessments reveal that thousands of women and girls have been subjected to ISIL related sexual violence, including forced marriage. Women and girls (either pregnant or with babies born from rape) who are associated with ISIL, face real risks not only of rejection and stigmatization but also of violence from their communities and government military actors. Reports of SGBV in camp settings and host communities are high, as are reports of SEA. The January 2018 GBVIMS report reports that 1 out of 6 reported cases is related to sexual violence (rape and sexual assault). Other types of violence include physical and emotional violence often from intimate partners, forced marriage, and denial of resources and opportunities, especially for youth and adolescent girls. While child marriage was an issue prior to the crisis, current assessments reveal that this practice is now a coping mechanism linked to protection and economic needs. Several initiatives are being implemented to increase communities’ awareness and understanding of the consequences and impact of child marriage. Despite this enhanced focus, the capacity of humanitarian agencies to meet the needs of SGBV survivors and those at risk of SGBV remains limited. The unpredictable security situation in different parts of the country, economic hardship, and forced, unsafe and undignified returns places IDP women and girls at heightened risk of SGBV. Despite this, specialised service in most return areas remain limited or non-existent. UNHCR reports and assessments conducted in 2017 and 2018 indicate that IDP women and girls (especially adolescent girls) face daily protection risks, including the risk of forced marriage, denial of resources and opportunities (access to school for girls), risk of harassment in public spaces, risk of having to exchange sex for money/ or assistance as well as exploitation as they strive to meet basic needs of their families. The aforementioned risks are further exacerbated by the presence of security actors inside and around IDP camps and return areas. Under-reporting of SGBV incidents remains high, due to social stigma, reprisal, discrimination and weak law enforcement. Additionally, disclosure of sexual violence can have life threatening implications for survivors and children born of rape or to parents with perceived affiliation to extremist, including risk of honour killing. There is a continued need for training of SGBV service providers, including the Directorate of Combating Violence against Women (DCVAW), as well as police and camp managers to reinforce their understanding of SGBV, and ensure effective implementation of the interagency GBV SOPs operationalised in 2018 through the GBV Sub Cluster. Further support will be required to strengthen the capacity of SGBV service providers and partners in order to ensure quality service provision and response to identified cases.

Activities

UNHCR’s project will be aligned to the strategic objectives and approaches agreed upon by the GBV Sub-Cluster. UNHCR will work with community-based structures, grassroots organizations and volunteers in return areas to support survivors of SGBV in their recovery process and mitigate negative attitudes, stigma and discrimination. In order to address SGBV risks affecting IDPs and improve quality of services, UNHCR will strengthen the capacity of local authorities DCVAW, MoLSA, and local NGOs to ensure that in country capacity is in place to address the long term needs of survivors while working also on prevention and risk mitigation. Emphasis will be given to trainings and on-the-job coaching of service providers to ensure that a survivor-centred approach is maintained and quality service is provided. The operation will continue shifting its attention progressively to strengthen community-based protection and involvement of the community in SGBV prevention and response through, amongst others, training community outreach volunteers, establishing a specialised network of volunteers and raising awareness on SGBV prevention, risk mitigation and response within the community and will continue to scale up efforts to engage men in SGBV prevention more actively. After the successful implementation of the EMAP pilot project in Iraq UNHCR we will continue to roll out the EMAP methodology across the country. Case management and PSS will remain a priority in 2019 by making SGBV services broadly and discretely available, including through mobile teams and multi-purpose community centres, SGBV survivors and those at risk will have improved access to specialised services. Support to individual case management, and especially the provision of peer support services, will be complemented with an increased focus on eradicating “the culture of impunity” and availability of safe shelter for survivors of SGBV (including SEA). UNHCR will prioritize working with communities, and local authorities, and will train and capacitate persons of concern and other relevant stakeholders on prevention and response, thereby broadening the knowledge base and awareness among displaced communities and in return areas. UNHCR will continue supporting the implementation of the GBVIMS across the country to facilitate access to real time information on GBV trends and gaps in service provision for the programme interventions and targeted prevention and response in the most critical areas. UNHCR will prioritise return locations, new arrivals, and areas where service needs are unmet. Further, an advocacy plan for legal change will be pursued jointly with legal partners thereby creating a more favourable protection environment for survivors seeking legal redress and thus addressing the culture of impunity. As the lack of access to dignity kits was raised in Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) throughout 2018 by IDP women and girls across Iraq, UNHCR will scale up efforts to support women to access these items in a safe dignified manner. UNHCR will maintain its active membership role in the GBV Sub – Cluster and SAG, GBVIMS and MARA working group, and will leverage its leadership of the CwC Task Force to strengthen GBV prevention and awareness of services.

Protection Risk Analysis

UNHCR continues to receive complaints of SEA and aid diversion, and continues to investigate all complaints in line with the PSEA Network SOPs and its own internal oversight and control mechanisms. In order to address this protection risk, UNHCR requires that all partners sign the UNHCR Code of Conduct, and UNHCR monitors compliance with the Zero Tolerance Policy on SEA and UNHCR SGBV policies and guidance. As co-chair of the PSEA Network, UNHCR will also actively engage to share key messages on complaints mechanisms and build capacity of partners on PSEA and investigations, while continuing to directly investigate any complaints of SEA, fraud or aid diversion. Through its leadership of the CwC Task Force, UNHCR will support IDPs and returnees to make informed decisions about solutions and to better understand how to access their rights and entitlements, and ensure that they are aware of complaints and feedback mechanisms. UNHCR is committed to localization, and will ensure that national partners are supported to strengthen their capacity to develop and implement protection projects that effectively mainstream prevention of GBV and PSEA, and that they implement a community-based approach that integrated AAP. UNHCR will also maintain regular meetings with partners implementing protection projects, in particular projects that may subject partner staff to harassment and/or threats (e.g. legal aid partners representing persons with perceived affiliation), and follow-up directly any allegations with relevant authorities to ensure that humanitarian protection partners are able to safely support IDPs and returnees. The lack of livelihoods and basic services is forcing 80% of people in need to adopt stress, crisis and emergency negative copying strategies. In line with the Cash Working Group’s (CWG) findings, UNHCR data indicates vulnerable families are accumulating debt to purchase essential items and over 60% live on less than IQD 480,000 per month (approximately USD 397 per CWG’s calculation), which is below the set amount within the Survival Minimum Expenditure Basket (SMEB). Socio-economic data collected through UNHCR’s Comprehensive Household Assessment Tool (CHAT) show the number of eligible beneficiaries for Multi-Purpose Cash Assistance (MPCA) has increased by 50% compared to last year, indicating that the needs of vulnerable families has extended beyond the emergency phase and increased in severity in the months following displacement and return. This has had a negative impact upon the ability to support themselves and for families to provide a protective environment for girls and boys, increasing the risk of exposure to exploitation, and leading some families and individuals to adopt negative coping strategies, such as child marriage among girls and survival sex among women and girls. UNHCR will work to ensure that the work of the Cash Working Group and its MPCA mainstreams SGBV prevention, and that all partners conducting vulnerability assessments have PSEA measures in place.

Mitigation Measures

The Government of Iraq declared in December 2017 it had regained control of all Iraqi territory from extremists. However, the scale and severity of humanitarian needs remain high and returns and rehabilitation have not taken place at the scale and speed anticipated. Many of the 1.87 million internally displaced persons (IDPs) and 4.11 million returnees face constrained access to basic services as well as security and protection risks while contending with destroyed properties and infrastructure, lack of livelihood opportunities and financial resources. This has led to secondary displacement or re-admittance to camps where return was not possible or sustainable. IDPs and returnees continue to face serious protection risks including unlawful and disproportionate restrictions on access to safety and freedom of movement, confiscation of documents, abduction and illegal detention, forced encampment, forced and premature returns, and increased risk of sexual gender-based violence. Some IDPs are barred from returning to areas of origin by security actors, tribal leaders or communities over alleged affiliation with extremists. This will result in challenges in identifying sustainable solutions for IDPs, and may require longer-term engagement of UNHCR and humanitarian partners in the provision of protection and assistance programmes. UNHCR continues to receive complaints of SEA and aid diversion, and continues to investigate all complaints in line with the PSEA Network SOPs and its own internal oversight and control mechanisms. In terms of mitigation measures, UNHCR will undertake the following actions: (i) capacity building interventions for Government and civil society actors on the Principled Returns Framework and the Guiding Principles on Internal Displacement and relevant international and national human rights legal frameworks with a view to strengthening their capacity to implement sustainable solutions in line with the principles of voluntariness, safety and dignity and so that Government actors gradually assume their responsibilities for providing protection to conflict-affected populations; (ii) UNHCR requires that all partners sign the UNHCR Code of Conduct, and UNHCR monitors compliance with the Zero Tolerance Policy on SEA and UNHCR SGBV policies and guidance. As co-chair of the PSEA Network, UNHCR will also actively engage to share key messages on complaints mechanisms and build capacity of partners on PSEA and investigations; (iii) Through its leadership of the CwC Task Force, UNHCR will support IDPs and returnees to make informed decisions about solutions and to better understand how to access their rights and entitlements; (iv) UNHCR, through the Protection Cluster and directly, will also continue to actively engage in protection advocacy with Government actors and the international community; (v) UNHCR will also strengthen its internal tools to gather and analyse protection information through increased IM capacity and through continuous support to partners using CHAT and ASSIST; and (vi) With some multi-year grants concluding in 2018, the UNHCR operation will proactively engage with donors to underscore the necessity of continued support, including looking to non-traditional sources of funding.

How is your project accountable to affected population

UNHCR is a member of the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) Task Team on Accountability to Affected Populations, including Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (the AAP/PSEA Task Team). AAP is reflected in UNHCR's Revised Age, Gender and Diversity (AGD) Policy (2018), which aims to ensure that all sections of populations of concern have equitable and non-discriminatory access to protection and assistance programmes, and a say in decisions that affect their lives. The AGD Policy defines key elements of accountability with respect to affected populations to which UNHCR is committed. They include: communication and transparency; feedback and response; participation and inclusion; and learning and adaptation. The policy also standardizes the language used in reporting; informs priorities; supports assessments of the extent to which UNHCR achieves its AAP commitments; and provides criteria when evaluating learning and improvements. UNHCR’s programmes integrate three key elements that support AAP: (i) an AGD approach: An AGD approach is a rights and community-based approach according to which planning, programming, implementation, and monitoring and evaluation should be guided by principles of equality and participation; (ii) Community-based protection: this requires UNHCR to integrate community engagement in all aspects of its protection work, and recognize the capacities as well as needs of communities in humanitarian crises, in order to avoid harm and ensure that humanitarian programmes do not inadvertently leave people and communities worse off. A core tenet of the AGD policy, community-based protection employs a rights-based approach that puts persons of concern at the centre of decisions that affect their lives; (iii) Participatory assessment: this tool builds partnerships with IDP, returnee and host community women and men of all ages and backgrounds by promoting meaningful participation through structured dialogue. Separate discussions with women, girls, boys, and men, including adolescents, enable UNHCR and partners to gather accurate information on the specific protection risks that different groups face, identify underlying causes, understand community capacities, and hear their proposed solutions. It helps communities to take collective action to enhance their own protection, and makes it possible to implement a rights- and community-based approach. In Iraq, UNHCR will implement the following approach by ensuring integration of the Revised AGD Policy in all of its partnerships, including through the organization of its annual Participatory Assessment to guide participatory planning, and to provide feedback to IDPs and returnees on project implementation. As lead of the CwC Task Force, UNHCR will support partners to integration community-based approaches in its programming and to ensure that two-way feedback is incorporated into protection programmes. In the specific area of GBV, an internal expert is currently deployed in Iraq to assist UNHCR to develop a National SGBV Mainstreaming Action Plan. The plan will include mainstreaming SGBV and PSEA into UNHCR’s sectoral programming and response, including child protection and work with adolescents and youth. UNHCR is also supporting the Protection, CCCM and Shelter/NFI Clusters to integrate SGBV into their planning and implementation. UNHCR will continue to closely coordinate with IDP Call Center to ensure that UNHCR follows up on reported issues, and challenges are addressed and appropriate referrals made.
