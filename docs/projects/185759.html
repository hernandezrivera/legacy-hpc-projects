Project Summary

BRAC will continue to support the Rohingya and host communities with access to correct information to tackle social concerns through joint engagement (e.g. school campaigns) of the two communities as well as reduce misinformation and rumors related to the extended impact of COVID-19. BRAC will develop print and e-materials to support relevant stakeholders in disseminating information on key humanitarian concerns, service delivery and protection issues ensuring evidence-based communication with special consideration given to social inclusion, protection, environment, emergency preparedness and culture. Materials (also including video shows and radio podcasts) will be developed (in a community led message development workshop) in local Bangla dialect and Myanmar language. BRAC, a PSEA network member, has policies and guidelines prioritizing protection and safeguarding with an emphasis on ‘Do No Harm’ to ensure meaningful access, accountability, and participation and empowerment of its beneficiaries. Consent is taken before implementation of any activity and Brac adheres to data protection policy, therefore beneficiary data is not shared with external authorities. We will create key messages and disseminate IEC materials that will uphold the major protection concerns in the community.
BRAC and partner staff along with volunteers (youth, imams and hafez) from both communities will be trained in peace building and social cohesion through intercommunity events. BRAC seeks to enhance social cohesion among host and Rohingya communities through participatory community-based activities. The volunteers and CBOs will motivate the communities to hold joint events on health concerns, especially communicable diseases, ills of drug use, anti-trafficking and other social ills. Information dissemination will also include public health messaging e.g. for immunization; services and service points (e.g. union parishads, health facilities) for health, protection, disaster management and other services. BRAC committedly mainstreams gender and disability inclusion in all programming, e.g. all facilities are designed considering the need of different gender, elderly and disabled persons. CwC activities will likewise reflect this in the messages and IEC materials responsive to need of these vulnerable people.
In line with AAP, information and services accessed and available with respect to needs of vulnerable women, girls, boys and men, disabled and elderly will be introduced and monitored. BRAC will scale-up its own and that of its partners, the accountability and feedback-complaint mechanism by installing service desks in both communities through engaging community volunteers who will report back to BRAC and partners to arrange for resolution of complaints and community issues. Brac will ensure our feedback mechanism should be accessible to all vulnerable people, inclusive of gender, age and disability to voice their concerns and uphold their rights.
BRAC will contribute to sector discussions on high priority concerns in information dissemination (e.g. rumours and panic; trafficking; host-Rohingya conflict, vulnerability to natural disasters etc.). BRAC”s DRR mainstreaming approach demands CwC activities to inform people of disasters and take preparedness.
BRAC will emphasize coordination, advocacy and effective communication strategies with its upward partners of which key are the government - RRRC, DC at district level CiCs at Camp level, UNO and other government officials at upazila and union level. BRAC will continue to maintain close ties with ISCG and other agencies through different platforms to ensure well-coordinated and combined community engagement efforts. BRAC will likewise maintain effective coordination and communication with downstream local development partners as well as with host and Rohingya communities and mobilise them in advocacy efforts.

1 - Identified Needs

Currently Cox’s Bazar, one of the poorest districts in Bangladesh hosts over 900,000 Rohingyas across 34 camps. When the largest influx of Rohingya fleeing Myanmar violence came Cox’s Bazar in 2017, the Bangladeshi host community were the first responders to support them, however the rapid influx created a high level of poverty and environmental vulnerabilities putting a strain on infrastructure and services of the host population. Conflict and social tension has since then been rising, within the camps among Rohingyas as well as between Rohingya and host community. The COVID 19 lockdown exacerbated these creating fear and panic among both communities. The COVID 19 since 2020 pandemic resulted furthermore economic and social impact on the lives and livelihoods. According to JSMNA 2021, only 26% of Rohingya refugee and host community households who feel informed about the kind of aid and services available to them, disaggregated by sex, age and disability. Considering changing context as a result of lockdowns in response to COVID-19, ISCG partners and BRAC have taken effort to disseminate appropriate messages with respect to Covid 19 and social health concerns however these messages have not always fully incorporated the traditional and general understandings of health, illness, and how religious beliefs interact with people’s understanding of health. The messaging needs to be reviewed and adapted better for different target communities to be clearly understood and received. According to the Rapid Gender Analysis conducted in June 2020, community leaders, volunteers and religious leaders are the most trusted information sources for the Rohingya (UN Women, CARE, Oxfam 07/2020). Persons with disabilities (PWD) and marginalized populations such as women, elderly and adolescent girls, are not able to fully access information among both host community and Rohingya. Restricted network access makes it difficult to inform the population of changes in services and developments in times of emergency and disasters. Women in particular have limited access to mobile phones. Pre-recorded messages delivered were in the Chittagonian dialect, considered the nearest to Rohingya language but it was found that the lesser educated Rohingya found it hard to understand. In terms of community feedback mechanisms, the Rohingya mostly mention preference of the Majhis as point of contact for complaints, however the majhi system does not meet the minimum standards of representation or impartiality (UNHCR/REACH, 2019) since majhis often abuse their position. Despite continued efforts by the sectors to reach information to the Rohingya and host communities, the JSMNA (Oct 2021) records gaps: 9% Rohingya households reported having faced challenges when providing feedback or complaints and 74% households reported that they were unable to access enough clear information on the types of assistance available to them. 36% Host community beneficiary households similarly report challenges when providing feedback or complaints regarding services received. Rohingyas also report a lack of open communication between themselves and humanitarian agencies and government authorities. They stressed their desire to play an active part in decision making in issues affecting their daily lives. Considering all the problems in proper message development and dissemination, the message development should be community led and channeled through sources culturally accepted and in their own language. There should also be more engagement of Rohingya with humanitarian actors, government and host community to ease tensions and create better understanding and cohesion.
