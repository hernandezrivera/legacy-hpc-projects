Project Summary

The United Nations (UN) estimates number of crisis-affected population in need of humanitarian assistance in Syria at 11.7 million people (49.8% male and 50.2% female) out of which 4.7 million and 5 million live in areas of major severity of needs (inter-sector severity 3) and acute need (inter-sector severity 4-6) respectively(HNO, 2019). The above include 6.2 million protracted Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs); 1.4 million returnees; 324,000 IDPs in hard to reach areas, 1.6 million-population movements and the rest overburdened host communities. In addition, 480,000 Palestine refugees are considered part of the people in need. 67% of the 11.7 million people in need of humanitarian assistance are accessible from within Syria according to Syria Response Access Dynamic December (OCHA, 2018). Overall, 9 million of people are estimated to be food insecure and at risk of food insecurity by Food Security Sector. In this context, FAO has designed a project to assist 90,000 small-scale herders' households (equivalent to 540,000 people) in Syria. The objective of the proposed action is to improve food security and strengthen resilience of 90,000 vulnerable herders' households through restocking, provision of animal feed and capacity building. The project expected outputs are:
Output 1. Production assets for 15,000 vulnerable crisis affected breeders is restored through re-stocking. This output aims at restocking through provision of small ruminants (2 female ewes/sheep per households in line with sector recommendations) for 15,000 vulnerable households for purposes of asset restoration
Output 2. Livestock productivity for 55,000 vulnerable crisis affected breeders is maintained.
The output aims at improving availability of feeds for purposes of assets protection of 55,000 vulnerable households (400 kg of the feed per households in line with sector recommendations).
Output 3: Improved skills and livestock management capacity of 90,000 households.
It is expected that the introduction of agriculture based livelihood protection support in current protracted nearly eight years of crisis will be able to increase local availability of food, self-reliance and improve food and nutrition security of the Syrian people, especially the most vulnerable ones.

Needs

Livestock keeping is a vital component of livelihoods for a large proportion of vulnerable rural households in Syria. However, the sector has suffered substantial losses during the crisis. Pre-crisis, livestock production represented around 40% of the agriculture sector and generated over USD 400 million in foreign currency earnings base on the fact that 46% of Syrian land area constitutes range land and only 28% arable land. Livestock supports a large portion of the poor, landless and marginalized herders in Syria particularly in arid and semi arid areas. The sector provides also key support for food security and nutrition of women and children as they have traditionally play a prominent role (female represent 63.3 % of the agriculture labor force) in livestock keeping especially small ruminants. Moreover, livestock are important in provision of milk and meat for domestic consumption and their sales also contribute to support herders’ livelihoods. Other products such as leather and wool are valuable commodities which bring in additional revenue to livestock keepers. Since the beginning of the crisis, livestock sector has been significantly affected by reduction in livestock numbers mostly due to assets depletion, destruction of infrastructure and services (veterinary and roads), contamination and land degradation, livestock theft, markets disruption, unavailability of livestock feeds or if available at very high prices; insufficient coverage of veterinary services; limited availability of pasture/fodder; migration and losses of human resources for production. The resultant effect is significant reduction in livestock numbers as compared to last comprehensive livestock census in 2010. As the crisis protracts, capacity of small scale livestock keepers to generate income and to access food erodes. A yearly joint FAO and WFP Crop and Food Security Assessment Mission (CFSAM) conducted since 2016 estimated sheep/goats and cattle losses up to 40% and 30 % respectively. Over the past two years, however herd sizes have stabilized, albeit at a very low level, following a reduction in herd size at household level ranging from 47-57 percent of poultry, goats, sheep and cattle (CFSAM, 2017) however 2019 HNO indicates that unprecedented livestock asset losses estimated to about half of the pre-crisis situation affecting livelihoods leading to irreversible coping strategies.In the most affected areas by insecurity and active fighting herders were forced to leave their villages and farms for their security, safety and hence leaving behind their livestock. The continuous devaluation of the local currency is constantly affecting livestock feed prices which has soared since 2011 and farmers cannot afford required quantities for their animals compelling them to sell their livestock at giveaway prices. Assets loss will specifically affect women as traditionally they are the caretaker of small ruminants and have less capacity to access resources for the replacement. According to FAO damage and loss needs assessment of the overall agriculture sector for the period 2011 2016, the cost of damages and losses to the livestock sector is estimated at USD 5.5 Billion. The livestock sector faces two principle challenges related to livestock production and animal health. The protection of the remaining livestock assets is essential and together with restocking of livestock for poor herders (women headed households in particular) who have partially/totally lost their herds is very critical. The provision of feed will help to preserve surviving herds particularly during the lean season from January to April. Failure to do so will result in an increasing number of small scale herders disposing of remaining livestock and migrating in search of better security and livelihood opportunities creating additional instability.

Implementation of activities

Currently FAO is operating in thirteen governorates – Aleppo, Al Hassakeh, Ar Raqqa, Dar’a, Deir e Zor, Hama, Homs, Idleb, As Sweida, Quneitra, Rural Damascus, Tartous and Lattakia. In governorates where UN staff presence is not permitted by UN security regulations, FAO operates through government seconded officers and NGO service providers who are directly supporting implementation of activities and report to FAO.FAO Representation in Syria will be responsible for the overall implementation of the activities, backstopped by FAO headquarters with technical support of FAO’s multidisciplinary team in the Regional Office for the Near East and North Africa. FAO is working closely with MAAR to ensure that the project goals are achieved through proper technical expertise and monitoring capacity. The implementation will be further supported by:• A central coordination committee composed of FAO, relevant MAAR directorates, community-based organizations and implementing partners will be formed. The committee’s role is to ensure facilitation of the project implementation and application of the criteria for beneficiaries' selection• Local teams at village/district level led by FAO and composed of local community representatives (traditional leader or “Moukhtar”), extension workers and implementing partners will be in charge of selecting project beneficiaries in line with FAO established set of criteria that take into account gender and sensitive considerations.• FAO Resilience Officer in the targeted governorates will operationally and technically supervise inputs distribution and training programmes.The proposed actions will benefit from existing capacity which can be scaled up at whenever required.Activities for Output 1:1) Finalization of target districts selection base on assessments, discussions with other participating sector working group members, and relevant Government authorities;2) Identification of implementing partners (IPs); development of beneficiaries' selection criteria in consultation with key partners and stakeholders; participatory identification of the most need and vulnerable groups;3) Procurement, transportation and distribution of 30,000 pregnant sheep and 1,500 MT of feed.4) Distribution of veterinary inputs to resume livestock production cycle (anti parasites, de wormers) through district veterinary services where existing or through formal /informal farmers networks and associations.5)Timely and effective monitoring and evaluation of activities including feedback from beneficiariesActivities for Output 2:1) Finalization of target districts selection.2) Identification of implementing partners and development of beneficiaries' selection criteria.3) Procurement, transportation and distribution 22,000 MT of animal feed.4) Distribution of veterinary inputs to resume the livestock production cycle.5) Procurement and distribution of 10 crasher machines for plant residue to produce feeds to maximize the benefit of farm residue and reduce the production costs.6) Establishing of six milk processing units to be operated by local communities and milk producer’s network.7) Timely and effective monitoring and evaluation of activities including feedback from beneficiaries Activities for Output 3: 1) Finalization of target districts selection.2) Identification of implementing partners and development of beneficiaries selection criteria in consultation with key partners and stakeholders.3)Technical assistance to beneficiaries, community based organizations, government institutions and implementing partners (women headed households will be specifically targeted by this activity) 4) Training to livestock owners on livestock management5) Timely and effective monitoring and evaluation of activities including feedback from beneficiariesNB: All the activities under each outputs will address specific needs of different gender and age groups.

Protection Risk Analysis

1) Risk: Beneficiaries being targeted as a result of being selected for project/risk of community tension arising from beneficiaries selection; Mitigation measures: Inclusion of different categories of affected population example IDPs and host communities; returnees and refugees; men, women, boys, girls and youth base on their specific needs and clear selection criteria agreed upon by local committees.2) Risk: Inclusion and exclusion errors; Mitigation measures: establishing a local beneficiaries selection committees with equitable representation of vulnerable groups; putting in place a clear and precise guidance on beneficiaries selection criteria and processes and ensuring an effective communication on the guidelines in local language.3) Risk of diversion of assistance/assets: Mitigation measures: establish a community based beneficiaries selection and community based monitoring systems.The design of the project has taken into account protection elements in line with "do not harm" principles. To the maximum possible extent all measures will be taken to limit the risk of beneficiaries being targeted as a result of the being selected for the project. Where IDPs are selected representatives from the host communities will be included as well to limit possible frictions between the two and a special attention will be provided to the returnees to help them in their settlement and resuming their production activities through protecting and rehabilitating their asset. Moreover, the project will target those breeders who receive food support (about 15% of the total targeted breeders) in order to help them shift from receiving food support into producing their own food or able to afford it. In addition to this, re-stocking and provision of feed can help in sustaining food production (dairy and animal products) in the local community, so more food will be available and accessible to people. An accountability to affected population system including beneficiaries’ participation, appropriate information and feedback/complaint mechanism will also be put in place to provide communities with mechanisms to express their grievance. This will be used to make adjustments and improvement on the implementation of activities.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks and assumptions beyond project control and their mitigation measures:1) Risks: Deterioration of security situation precluding access to areas of implementation; Probability: Likely; Impact: Severe; Mitigation measures: Preventive: continue to gather information from range of sources; Advocate with Resident Coordinator/Humanitarian Coordinator and others to regain access. Develop contingency plans in coordination with service providers and if necessary move activities to alternative locations.2) Risks: Major displacements/return that could put more pressure on livelihood strategies and/or limit the impact of activities; Probability: possible; Impact: insignificant; Mitigation measures: Continue with advocacy in order to scale up programmes to cover the additional requirements to promote livelihoods opportunities. Advocacy with other organizations to include new arrivals in assistance programmes.3) Risks: Changes in the conditions related to cross border movements and operations; Probability: Unlikely; Impact: Moderate; Mitigation measures: Ensure that service provider (SP) have capacities to deliver programme within the changed legal framework. Diversify activities complementing international procurement with local procurement and ensure different delivery modalities (scaling up cross lines to balance reduced cross borders).4) Risk: Limitations on partnership agreements; Probability: Unlikely; Impact: major; Mitigation measures: Continuous advocacy and negotiations with Government and counterparts for partners to have access to targeted areas. Establish agreements with counterparts to facilitate implementation of project activities. 5) Risk: Deterioration of the relations between IDPs and host communities in the period/area concerned; Probability: Unlikely; Impact: Insignificant; Mitigation measures: Assistance is balanced and not directed only to one specific group. Identification of beneficiaries is done in partnership with local communities including IDPs and host communities. Select implementing partners that are accepted by local communities and aware of conflict sensitivity.6) Risks: Increased control of land and water by party in conflict; Probability: Likely; Impact: Moderate; Mitigation measures: Preventive: continue to gather information from a range of sources. Accurate choice of target areas ensuring that farming is possible and beneficiaries will undertake the activity. Advocacy with local authorities by service providers. If necessary move to alternative locations previously identified7) Risks: Possible climatic shocks during the implementation period (drought/floods in particular). Probability: Possible; Impact: Moderate; Mitigation measures: Select distribution areas in different agro-ecological zones and in irrigated areas to reduce risks. Distribute adapted varieties, which possess specific desirable traits, including drought tolerance.
