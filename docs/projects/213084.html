Project Summary

The damage done to water infrastructure has had an impact on potable water, hygiene, and irrigation. Before the crisis, access to safe drinking water in Syria was estimated at 92% in rural communities and 98% in urban centres. Seven major water systems serve the country’s eight largest cities and account for 80% of the country’s total water supply, reaching about 9.5 million people. Between 2011 and 2021, except for the system servicing Syria’s coastal area, all other essential water facilities suffered direct and severe damage. These were compounded by ten years of inadequate maintenance which intensified their level of fragility. By 2019, annual public water production in Syria had fallen by 40% relative to pre-crisis levels (from 1,700 million cubic metres in 2010 to 1,020 million cubic metres in 2019). Daily per capita, water shares fell by 36% in urban areas and by 25% in rural areas, where the share had historically been lower. This already alarming situation significantly deteriorated from 2020 onwards, when electricity shortages in the country became acute. As shown in the graphs below, public production fell a further 40% and is now at almost a third of pre-crisis levels. Daily per capita drinking water shares are at 64% of pre-crisis levels in urban areas and at 38% of pre-crisis levels in rural areas. Levels in rural areas – at 30 litres per capita per day – are critically low.
Across the country, nearly two-thirds of water treatment plants, half of all pumping stations, and one-third of water towers have been damaged as a result of the conflict. 70 percent of all discharged sewage is untreated, with half the sewage network infrastructure out of function. In addition, continuous power outages have a devastating effect on the function of water systems. This, coupled with unusually long and severe drought periods, insufficient and poorly distributed rainfall, and extremely low water levels in the Euphrates River since January 2021, have reduced access to water for drinking and domestic use for millions of Syrians, but also triggered substantial harvest and loss of livelihoods, as well as an alarming increase in the transmission of water-borne diseases, due to contaminated water sources. 24 percent of populated communities rarely have access to a sufficient primary source of water. This has caused households to resort to negative coping mechanisms, including a significant decrease in hygiene. In September 2022, the Ministry of Health (MoH) announce an official cholera outbreak. The number of laboratories confirmed cases has reached nearly 25,000 by October 2022 and is only expected to increase with no solution to the water problem at hand. An estimated 8 million people require urgent Water Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) assistance to prevent the further spread of the disease.
The developments on the ground require UNDP to rapidly deploy assistance to strengthen the resilience of affected people Syria. Hence, this project aims to build resilience to the socio-economic crisis and improve the affected population’s access to essential services through the implementation of the following activities:
Medical Waste Management - (Construction / Operation[Collection/Disposing])
Providing solar systems for operating drinking well, 75 KW /well
Sewage Network - Restore / Rehabilitation / Repair/Expansion
Solid waste collection, transport and disposal
Water Network - Rehabilitation / Repair /Expansion
Water Station - Restoration / Rehabilitation / Repair /Expansion
Water Tank (Elevated - Ground) - Rehabilitation / Repair /expansion

0 - Project Summary (extended)

Ensuring the protection of project beneficiaries is a priority throughout project design, project implementation, monitoring, and evaluation. To mitigate against tension and project disruption, the project will engage and consult the target community through regular meetings, transparent information management, beneficiary selection, and engagement. Related to this specific action, the project will ensure accountability to affected populations through community feedback mechanisms, such as focus group discussions, regular meetings (with the representation of women, men, persons with disabilities, the elderly, youth, female-headed households, and other vulnerable community members) and joint project monitoring. The project has tailored activities to address gender inequalities and GBV. For instance, awareness-raising sessions will be tailored based on locally identified issues adopting UNDP-gendered context sensitivity methodology. The proposed activities will also take into consideration, the use of technologies and services that reduce women’s work burden; and contribute significantly to farming, women farmers who are particularly marginalized due to their limited access to land, inputs, extension advice, micro-credit facilities, and improved technologies; low levels of participation in leadership positions; and limited income generation opportunities. UNDP will also make efforts to remove gender-based constraints such as women’s limited access to work as paid labor, land, tools, and training in agriculture and livelihoods initiatives, as this is key to improving gender equality and ultimately reducing GBV. This includes orientation targeting stakeholders/ team members on gender mainstreaming, referral mechanisms, and PSEA.
