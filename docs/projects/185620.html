Project Summary

UNICEF will support access to inclusive early childhood development, learning and skills development opportunities for 245,994 children (50% girls) refugee crisis affected children, adolescents and youth aged 3-24 years, including 222,026 in the camps and 23,968 in Teknaf and Ukhyia upazilas of Cox’s Bazar District. UNICEF will work with partners to improve enrolment, retention and inclusive learning environment for girls, boys, and children with disability.
In the camps, over 2,800 learning centres will be supported by UNICEF to provide access to progressive learning in safe and protective environment. The capacity of teachers, technical officers and programme officers will be built to enable them effectively support the delivery of improved learning for 222,026 (50% girls) children aged 3-18 years using the Learning Competency Framework and Approach (LCFA) as well as the Myanmar curriculum for students in Grades 6 to 9 and Grades 1 and 2. These include, 28,316 (14,237 girls) refugee adolescents and youth (15-24) will be supported with secondary education (10,000) based on the Myanmar curriculum and vocational skills development (18,18,316) programmes. UNICEF also support capacity building of partners on inclusive education, learning outcomes assessment and the use of education technology solutions to enhance the delivery of education services as part of the COVID-19 response and recovery. The trainings for teachers will also include Code of Conduct, PSEA, Child Safeguarding so as to promote a protective learning environment.
In the host communities in Teknaf and Ukhyia, UNICEF will support 23,968 children, adolescents, and youth to access improved quality preprimary and primary education. The support to host community will include focus on out of schools chidlren and adolescents with access to vocational skills training and non-formal education. Schools will be supported to provide continuity of learning with appropriate safety measures in place as well disaster risk reduction. UNICEF will provide grants to schools to support implementation of school improvement plans in collaboration with school management committees. Through thesse interventions which support HC, the response will promote social cohesion as a conflict sensitive programming approach will be used that encourage participation of the host community.
Strong focus on girls' education will be ensured through a) awareness raising and sensitization of parents and traditional leaders, b) the scale-up of girls-only sessions, c) gender-segregated water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) facilities, d) recruiting and training more female Rohingya teachers and e) developing and sharing gender-sensitive teaching and learning materials with caregivers, teachers and students. A focus on including three per cent children with disabilities will be mainstreamed across interventions such as teacher trainings including across materials developed to support these actions.

1 - Identified Needs

By 30 September 2021, the number of Rohingya refugees in Cox’s Bazar District stood at 902, 947 people living in 34 congested camp settlements across the Ukhiya and Teknaf sub-districts (Upazilas),[1] with many individuals being psychologically distressed following their experiences in Myanmar and subsequent displacement to Bangladesh. Over 51 per cent of the refugee population are children and more than half are women and girls. Approximately one per cent of Rohingya refugees have been identified as being persons with disabilities. UNICEF and partners have significantly expanded access to education for Rohingya children, ensuring 80 per cent of boys and 76 per cent of girls aged 6-14 years had access to education until 17 March 2020, when the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic prompted the government of Bangladesh to close all education institutions in the country. All LCs in the camps along with education institutions across Bangladesh were closed for over 18 months to mitigate the risk of COVID 19 infection in the communities. This has had a major effect on new enrolment and potentially caused immense learning loss, besides increasing risks such as trafficking, exploitation and abuse, child marriage and child labour for the Rohingya children. Educational attendance of adolescent girls remains extremely limited in both the camps and host communities. In the camps, among those aged 15 to 18 years, only 3 per cent of girls and 28 per cent of boys accessed education in education facilities, with this trend increasing with age. Similar challenges are faced by adolescent girls in host communities. This trend is further exacerbated by social and religious barriers that restrict the mobility of girls in public once they reach adolescence as well as the high prevalence of early marriage. This contributes to social isolation and exclusion, low levels of foundational literacy, psychological distress, exploitation, and abuse. It has been found that parents are more likely to send their girls to education facilities if there are female teachers and sex-separated classrooms. In the Rohingya refugee camps there has been some success with recruiting higher percentages of female teachers, with 56 per cent of teachers being female. However, most female teachers are Bangladeshis from the host community, with female Rohingya teachers comprising only 25 per cent of the total teaching force. This presents a challenge for the roll out of the Myanmar curriculum (see below), with greater numbers of female teachers with sufficient Myanmar language skills needing to be recruited and trained. Another critical barrier to education has been the lack of a harmonized Government of Bangladesh-approved education framework, which has hampered provision of quality education in the camps for girls, boys and adolescents. While the Government of Bangladesh sanctioned informal education through the LCFA in 2018, this framework has no certification as part of any approved education system and is likewise being treated as a temporary solution. To replace the LCFA, the use of the Myanmar curriculum was endorsed by the Government of Bangladesh in January 2020 as part of the Myanmar Curriculum Pilot (MCP) project. While it was planned that the MCP would be rolled out in 2020-2021, significant delays were experienced relating to COVID-19 and as such, a new detailed roadmap for the Myanmar Curriculum rollout is being developed and expected to be implemented in 2022. The ongoing impact of the pandemic created a continued need for alternative learning platforms such as home-based support, care giver lead education, the Lockdown-Extended Alternative Pathway (LEAP) and the introduction of education technology for the purpose of reaching learners, especially those in hard-to-reach locations.
