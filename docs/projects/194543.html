Project Summary

The protracted conflict in South Sudan coupled with the 3 year unprecedented flooding and intercommunal violence has left left 8.9 million South Sudanese in need of Humanitarian assistance in 2022 (Draft HNO 2022). Additional shocks levied by the COVID-19 pandemic and on-going epidemic disease outbreaks including measles, cholera and Hepatitis E have further stretched existing coping mechanisms and increased vulnerabilities. Inspite of the many challenges, IOM's DTM have reported a mixed population flow reflecting increasing returns of IDPs and refugees in some of the counties including Raja, Morobo and Kajokeji counties. Health service delivery for vulnerable populations remains heavily reliant on humanitarian assistance. In locations such as Abyei, Unity, Upper Nile, Jonglei, western Equatoria, Warrap, Northern Bar el Ghazal and western Bar el Ghazal where the International Organization for Migration, IOM has been providing life-saving humanitarian assistance to internally displaced persons (IDPs), returnees, and host communities in conflict-affected and hard-to-reach locations across South Sudan, populations remain displaced in IDP camps or far off hard-to-reach locations due to flooding. IOM's life-saving health and MHPSS support to vulnerable IDPs and host community in Abyei, Wau, Rubkhona and Malakal counties and through deployment of 16 Rapid Response Missions in 2022 to crisis affected and IPC4 and IPC5 locations of Fangak, Panyijar, Twic East, Bor South, Abyei, Aweil East, Aweil Center, Aweil West, Aweil South, Gogrial West, terekeka and Juba among others has by epi-week 45 reached a total of over 2,196,659 beneficiaries including 520,726 outpatient consultations, 107,221 routine measles, BCG and DTP3 dozes and 7,372 assisted facility based deliveries in 2022. Through its Rapid Response Teams, IOM conducted 2 rounds of Oral cholera vaccination campaigns in Rubkona county and supported OCV vaccination campaigns in Malakal POC and within Juba town and Munuki Payams of Juba County to reach a total of 502,058 individuals above one year of age whilst supporting the emergency response mechanism to reach a total of 381,536 children with reactive measles vaccination in 11 out of 15 affected counties in the country. In 2023, IOM will target 1,277,947 beneficiaries including 191,692 persons with disabilities so as to continue to provide integrated emergency life-saving primary health care, including outpatient consultations, routine immunization, nutrition screening for children under five years, sexual reproductive health services, clinical case management of sGBV survivors including psychosocial support while increasing surveillance and response to disease outbreaks to underserved and conflict-affected IDPs, returnees, migrants/travelers, and host communities.
IOM will provide health services through 15 static and 25 mobile clinics, while maintaining its comparative advantage in rapidly deploying rapid response teams to address emerging health needs throughout the country. MHPSS services will be provided through existing recreational and counselling centers and will deploy rapid response teams (RRT) to any areas across South Sudan facing acute man-made or natural disasters with unmet health needs.
IOM will focus on leveraging existing community systems for sustainable solutions and strengthening both community-based health initiatives and mental health and psychosocial support. Therefore, IOM will provide direct service delivery in partnership with the respective county health departments, while continuing to explore options of partnerships with local NGOs and CBOs towards realizing the localization agenda of the health cluster. MHPSS will utilize static and mobile service in coordination with key humanitarian and developmental partners so as to avoid overlaps and ensure accountability to affected populations.

1 - Needs

Access to primary health care (PHC) services continued to be unavailable for a large majority of South Sudanese, with only 59 percent of the population able to access health facilities in an hour or less, and 80 percent of reporting barriers in accessing health services. According to the 2022 South Sudan Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO), an estimated 5.5 million people will be in need of humanitarian health services in 2022. Key contributing factors include – inter alia – protracted conflict and instability, large scale protracted displacement, economic deterioration, and significant flooding in large parts of the country. Only 8% of 1,869 health facilities in South Sudan are providing the full package of BPHNS, whilst 53% are moderately functional, with many poorly equipped and staffed, with humanitarian and development partners responsible for provision of support for a significant proportion of healthcare services in the country. Prevailing health system challenges are exacerbated by a lack of safe water supplies, infection prevention and control measures, healthcare waste management, as well as widespread malnutrition, and poor infrastructure to procure, transport, and store essential pharmaceutical and vaccines. As a result, South Sudan continues to have some of the worst health outcome indicators globally, including outbreaks of measles and polio, high instances of maternal and under-five mortality, and extremely low immunization coverage. The psychosocial consequences for displaced and conflict-affected populations – such as socio-economic hardship, disrupted community structures and lack of social support, as well as elevated levels of psychological distress – can lead to an increase in conflicts and violence at family and community level, alcohol and substance use, and other harmful coping mechanisms, and the development of suicidal thoughts and behaviours. According to 2020 data from the Ministry of Health outpatient department (OPD), rates of mental health attendance is extremely high; 272 per 100,000, compared to the high-income country average of 44 per 100,000. The prevalence of clinical conditions such as depression is also high, ranging from 36 to up-to 50 per cent of the population. High rates of depression were confirmed in 2021, as IOM rolled out the WHO Well-Being Index (WHO-5 WBI) to assess the psychosocial wellbeing of IOM MHPSS beneficiaries in IDP camps in Unity, Western Bahr el Ghazal and Upper Nile where 90 per cent scored below the ‘red flag’ threshold, whereupon it is recommended to screen for depression. Despite significant needs, qualified mental health capacity in South Sudan is extremely scarce, with only three psychiatrists, two nurses, two clinicians and 24 non-clinical psychologists operating at governmental facilities. There are only 12 beds for mental health patients available at Juba Teaching Hospital, and no psychiatric hospital elsewhere in the country. The source of information is directorate of mental health at Ministry of Health. Assessments conducted by IOM in Bentiu, Wau, and Malakal from 2015 to 2021 indicate persistent experiences of cumulative distress, grieving of multiple losses, accumulative stress, loss of self-esteem, and fatigue. Negative coping mechanisms such as abuse of alcohol, withdrawal and isolation, or aggressive behaviors are prevailing, further aggravated by negative implications of COVID-19, which had an additional negative impact on the level of distress observed in the general population and particularly on persons living in IDP sites, at which preventative actions such as physical distancing and regular handwashing presented a challenge.

2 - Project activities

IOM will provide a comprehensive package of primary healthcare while strengthening its community health response through a network of community health workers to provide integrated community case management of childhood illnesses alongside providing health promotion and community based surveillance services. These efforts are implemented in coordination with the community leadership within the IDP camps and with the Boma Health workers in locations outside the IDP camps. In line with the priority for integrated healthcare, IOM will continue to provide focused, non-specialized, community-based, remote and mobile MHPSS services, in need across the country, including counselling, support group discussions and care for caretakers, to be supplemented through coordination of MHPSS Technical Working Groups at the national and sub-national level. Response within IDP camps in Bentiu and Wau, and Malakal POC: IOM will provide comprehensive Primary Health care services to IDPs in Bentiu and Naivasha IDP camp and Malakal POC, through established health and MHPSS facilities, while conducting mobile outreach to Dingding, Yoangyang, Roriak, Pakur and Budang (Rubkona) and Bam, Akoka, Aburoc and Gelachol (Upper Nile). Response to IPC4 and 5 flood-affected populations in Twic East, Fangak, Ayod and Pibor (Jonglei State): In Twic East, IOM will continue to provide comprehensive primary health care services at Baping PHCC and through mobile health and MHPSS outreach teams to the highlands of Raap and Wuchung as well as in New Fangak at the IOM Primary Healthcare Unit in Phom and through integrated health and MHPSS outreaches to the islands in Thalier, with a focus on strengthening community-led service delivery. Support to locations of spontaneous refugee and IDP returns in Wau, Raja, Renk, Kajo-keji, and Morobo counties: IOM will continue to provide essential services to IDP returnee populations outside Wau town in payams of Baggari, Bazia and Besselia through static facilities at Farajallah, Bazia, Busseri, Mboro and Ngisa, and mobile health and MHPSS clinics to hard-to-reach populations in aforementioned payams. IOM will respond in counties of Raja, Morobo and Kajokeji, where there are mixed displacements and returns. Deployment of country-wide integreated Emergency Rapid Response Teams and response to disease outbreaks and other acute public health emergencies: IOM will maintain a surge team of emergency responders within its regular staff for rapid deployment nationally, as part of the Health Cluster Rapid Emergency Response Missions, and in close coordination with national and sub-national health coordination mechanisms. IOM will maintain three rapid response teams to enable concurrent deployments at the request of the health cluster, with integration of MHPSS staff prioritized. Activities: Preventive and curative health services for common morbidities; Surveillance, early warning, alert and response to epidemic prone diseases;Routine immunization for children; Nutrition screening; Sexual and reproductive health services;Basic emergency obstetric and neonatal care services; antenatal and postnatal care, PMTCT, family planning services; Clinical management of rape;HIV and TB testing and treatment;Integrated MHPSS through focused services in recreational and counselling centres associated with IOM clinics and via tele-communication;Non-communicable disease management;Emergency reactive mass vaccination campaigns and case management;Improving WASH in health facilities;Health promotion and development of key messages in collaboration with MHPSS teams; and referral to secondary level of care;Training for health personnel, from both the Ministry of Health (MoH) and national NGOs;Capacity building for MHPSS mobile teams, for social worker trainees of the Ministry of Gender, Child and Social Welfare;MHPSS mainstreaming for health workers, protection actors, and (I)NGOs;Coordination of MHPSS Technical Working Groups.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Feedback from the various vulnerable members of the community will be obtained through targeted focused group discussions that will be conducted in coordination with IOM’s disability and inclusion team to ensure that all members of the community are engaged.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

IOM will conduct community inception meetings at the start of project implementation while ensuring equitable representation of women, youth, and persons with special needs, during which the community will be provided an overview of the health projects and following which IOM will continue to maintain close community linkages through the community health workers, the county health authorities and community leaders. As part of its community feedback mechanism IOM will continue to conduct the quarterly continuous Quality Assurance (CQA) and client satisfaction surveys, and WHO 5 well-being index interviews. Additionally, IOM will submit weekly and monthly reports to the relevant key stakeholders including the County Health Department, the Health Cluster, and the camp management committees where applicable.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

Information on the Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) will be streamlined into routine health promotion activities and during community engagement activities including during focused group discussions with beneficiaries. Additionally, PSEA posters including reporting mechanisms will be made available at IOM supported health and MHPSS facilities and various strategic locations within the communities including at the offices of relevant health authorities within the payams, counties and states of implementation. PSEA orientation training will be provided to key health authorities and stakeholders within the community including integration of PSEA orientation into trainings for community health workers.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

In accordance with ensuring that the community feedback loop is completed, IOM will ensure to have community concerns addressed where possible or beneficiaries directed to where they can be accorded further assistance. Health and MHPSS team will provide quarterly reports on the complaints received and proportion of complaints addressed or closed.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

The risk of beneficiaries being exposed to violence is highest in Twic East and New Fangak where communities are scattered on different islands and have to travel long distances by motorboat to access health services, during which there are risks of attacks by robbers but also of SGBV to women and girls. IOM will engage with the community in identifying the most accessible location for service delivery, while additionally strengthening community health initiatives to reach to the villages through mobile clinics and outreaches and minimize the need to travel long distances to seek medical care. As regards risks that beneficiaries are not accessing/using complaint and feedback mechanisms, IOM will ensure adequate information sharing and community mobilization ahead of the focus group discussion sessions, which will form a key component of the community feedback mechanism. while taking great consideration to ensure that various gender and age groups and persons with disabilities are engaged. The risk of interference is usually during the selection of locally recruited volunteers. This will be minimized through employing IOM’s standard operating procedures for recruitment of field volunteers ensuring an independent process of advertising for available positions and equal representation of the community in the selection panel for the volunteers. As regards risks of explosive hazard contamination, this remains a risk in post -conflict areas, and IOM will conduct prior security risk assessments of the areas of operation before establishment of services at identified health facilities. IOM will continue to coordinate with UNMISS, UNDSS, UNMAS and the community in mitigating against the risk of explosive hazards from unexploded ordinances.
