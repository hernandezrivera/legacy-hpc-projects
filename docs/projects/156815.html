Project Summary

This project aims at providing the following services to IDPs sites:
a) upgrading infrastructure in IDPs sites, such as drainage systems, benefiting 30,000 beneficiaries (age and gender disaggregated) in 3 -4 sites;
b) graveling and rehabilitation of alleys, and floors among tents, as well as ground leveling for tent floors taking into considerations that the tenets’ floors are suitable for male, women, boys, girls, the elderly and people with disabilities (PwDs) and age –appropriate when necessary;
b) equipping and training emergency responders and IDPs committees in three IDP sites; and
c) tent replacement and distribution for 100 tents culturally suitable for all beneficiaries.

Needs

During sudden displacements, IDPs have often no choice but to seek shelter in planned camps, informal sites, transit/reception centres and collective centres. Of the estimated 6.2 million internally displaced persons in Syria, an estimated 871,150, or 14 per cent of all IDPs, are living in IDP sites where they receive humanitarian assistance and services. (HNO, 2019)Although IDP sites (including planned camps, informal sites, transit/reception centres and collective centres) are designed to act as an option of last-resort, the past year has witnessed an increase by 16 per cent in the number of IDPs seeking shelter in these sites. Against the backdrop of overcrowding, there is limited absorption capacity in IDP sites. Some sites are running by more than 400 per cent above their actual capacity. The absence of accountable camp management and maintenance of facilities in IDP sites, particularly for informal/self-established IDP sites, are deeply concerning and contribute to alarming reports of uncovered needs in these sites.According to HNO 2019, between September, 2017, and August, 2018, the CCCM cluster has tracked almost 1.1 million population movements across northern Syria – a significant increase from the 804,300 reported in the 2018 HNO. During this time period, an estimated 18% of IDPs resorted to, or transited through, IDP sites. Across Syria IDP site population increased by an estimated 16 per cent, with such increases witnessed almost uniformly across all parts of the country. To cope with sudden displacements, the CCCM cluster has actively promoted the establishment of new Reception Centres for sudden displacements in Idleb and northern Aleppo governorates. These centres have hosted 42,034 IDPs since September 2017. To give the real picture of the IDPs’ situation in the affected camps and to assess their needs in NW Syria, Shafak’s teams followed up the recent camps’ situation and tracked the displaced families flux. Shafak teams have visited the affected camps and assessed the situation on 30 December, 2018 in a report entitled The Humanitarian Situation in the NW Syrian Camps after the Rainstorm, which covers in details the situation in 112 camps in 11 camp’s clusters in northern Syria camps. Total number of families in those clusters reaches 49,831, and the displaced families 30% of were affected by the rainstorm.49,831 displaced families are staying in the assessed camps in norther Syria. Around 15,134 families were strongly and badly affected by the rainstorm that occurred during the last days, and they are stuck in mud and in need to all kind of aids. Now hundreds of thousands of people have been forced to leave their damaged tents (partially or totally) in the affected camps with cut off access to food, fuel and shelter. The current conditions are getting worse and worse. It is freezing time with no access to heat fuel/winterization tool and materials. There are a growing food and shelter problems, because all the rain made conditions worse with inaccessible road. Many appeals for emergency intervention were raised asking to support those families. This powerful storm brings more misery to the IDPs families who are living now in a very harsh conditions with lack of basic winter supplies. The most needed aids to alleviate the current situation are: - repair and expansion of the sewage network;- provide tents for those who lost their tents; - rehabilitate of the infrastructure of roads and ferries;- NFI Kits;- food items; - kitchen furniture; - fuel for heating and winterization tool and materials;- winterization kits; and- all protection activities.

Implementation of activities

Shafak will implement the proposed activities depending on it usual practices and SOPs derived from working with different INGO and UN Agencies. Shafak will follow the following implementation steps: - Needs assessment to determine the IDP sites which are in need for construction, emergency services, tent replacement and/or rehabilitation services. - Technical assessment and preparing BoQs. - Contracting with service providers to implement the rehabilitation activities in the IDPs sites linking with Cash-for-Work activities if applicable with a particular focus on enrolling youth and mid-age adults.- rehabilitation activities will include, and will not be limited to: 1) maintenance and rehabilitation of water and drainage systems as well as graveling and preparing alleys between tents to benefit 30,000 beneficiaries. 2) Equip and train emergency responders and IDPs committees in three IDP sites. 3) tent replacement and distribution for 500 tents.- Monitoring and evaluation for the activity.- Exit strategy for the project and hand over to the camp management. It is worth mentioning that Shafak has good experience in implementing such activities, as Shafak has previously implemented tent replacement, and rehabilitation of infra-structure.

Protection Risk Analysis

Risk (1): Sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA), including harassment, against women and girls by distributors and male beneficiaries while queuing and before/after distributionsRisk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure:- recruit male and female volunteers who are respected in the community and independent of armed groups. No children to be employed (under 18 years old)- make sure staff are already trained on Do No Harm, protection from SEA (PSEA), and child safeguarding; and sign and adhere to code of conduct that respects and uphold the rights of beneficiaries and PSEA- set up communication and complaints mechanisms or desks that are clearly marked; face interviews with beneficiaries, including women and children Risk (2):Tension between host community and IDPs (who were evacuated)Risk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure:- clearly communicate selection criteria to the community, community leaders and beneficiaries prior to the announcement of the activity.- harmonize the assistance delivered as per Cluster instructions and guidelines.- coordinate with local council in the targeted area as well as being active in field coordination Risk (3): Difficult access for women, unaccompanied children, elderly, people with disabilities (PWD)Risk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure:- Register all adult women, unaccompanied children, elderly, people with disabilities as main recipients of Kits distributed- During post -distribution follow-up, understand how women, children, and other groups accessed the distributed goods and their feedback on the process and materials Risk (4): Resistance to gender mainstreaming from male leadership structures.Risk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure:- Programme activities which engage women will be endorsed by community and religious leaders to gain support from male community members to reduce the likelihood of resistance to these activities.Risk (5): An increase in tensions within the camps, as space (a scarce resource) is occupied by these infrastructure and create differences between the camps in terms of services. Likelihood: highRisk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure:Residents should be consulted before the construction of any infrastructure and be made aware of the risks that the current project was attempting to mitigate. Risk (6): Construction contractors could create some tensions or harm based on their salary paymentsLikelihood: highRisk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure:- Maintenance roles on constructed facilities are clarified early to prevent labour-seeking vandalism- Strengthen and involve IDP Committees in engaging with service providers and also the residents to report to the feedback through pre-established Risk (7): Individual residents may over-rely on any civil response/DRR committee for responding to a natural disaster and may shirk individual responsibility.Risk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure:- incorporates general awareness raising focused on the individuals’ capacities of men, women, and children 2) Ensure that training indicatives include sessions on DNH and protection.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1st Risk: lack of approval from property owner which may prevent from implementing the activity. Likelihood: High Mitigation:a) No start of the activity in the camp without obtaining the approval from the land owner or the stakeholders (local authorities).b) Include HLP documentation into the MoUs or agreements signed, 2nd Risk: risk of PSEA and CSG.Likelihood: High Mitigation: a) Clear complaint mechanism in placeb) Train staff on code of conduct and PSEA and CSG policiesc) field staff should be accompanied with female staffd) Incorporate strong consideration for protecting the rights of the beneficiaries and maintaining their dignity, e) Safe recruitment of staff f) Safe response standards
