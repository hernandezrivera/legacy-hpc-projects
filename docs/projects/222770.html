Project Summary

Despite a high attendance rate of 96%, 7% of children face safety concerns when traveling to and from learning facilities. Issues such as fear of abduction, threats from armed groups, poor road conditions, and long distances significantly deter school attendance. These concerns are particularly pronounced for households with adolescent girls, underscoring the need for enhanced safety measures and proximate learning centers.
Gender inequality in this context severely limits girls' educational access, influenced by poverty, early marriage, and restrictive societal norms. Safety concerns, child labor, and parental resistance further deter school attendance, particularly affecting girls, children with disabilities, and gender-diverse individuals. Deep-rooted stigma, limited resources, gaps in rehabilitation services in camps, inaccessibility of educational materials, and a lack of capacity to address diverse gender and disability needs worsen these barriers, restricting the educational and future opportunities of marginalized groups.
To address the emotional and psychological toll of displacement, integrating Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) into educational programming is crucial. Educators trained in MHPSS can provide trauma-sensitive support, fostering resilience and creating a safe learning environment for children to thrive. Addressing emotional well-being alongside academic resources is essential to engage and retain children who have experienced trauma.
Leveraging technology-based learning, such as distance education, is essential for reaching out-of-school children. Flexible, alternative learning pathways tailored to adolescent girls’ needs—such as community-based education programs—are key to accommodating the realities of displacement, including limited mobility and diverse educational backgrounds.
Engaging parents and caregivers, particularly mothers, in the education process is vital to changing entrenched cultural attitudes that hinder educational access. Programs encouraging parental involvement in school activities and decision-making can foster a more supportive learning environment. Awareness initiatives for parents about the importance of education for both boys and girls are essential for fostering an inclusive mindset that values education equally across genders.
Educational programs must adopt a multi-faceted approach prioritizing inclusivity, gender equity, and emotional support. This includes addressing the specific needs of girls and children with disabilities by ensuring safe, segregated facilities, adequate menstrual hygiene support, and flexible programming. Efforts such as advocacy for weather-resilient facilities, separate classrooms for adolescent boys and girls, and more qualified teachers are essential for providing safe, equitable, and gender-sensitive educational opportunities.
Providing pathways to tertiary and technical training for Rohingya children is critical. Advocacy for recognized accreditation or certification is essential to support educational continuity and help children access higher education and vocational opportunities. This comprehensive, culturally responsive approach not only promotes academic success but also strengthens resilience and well-being, empowering the next generation to thrive despite the challenges of displacement.
SC will assess 27,925 learners. Additionally, 6,700 learners in the Rohingya camps will receive ALP book support, and 5,000 learners from Host GPS will be provided with educational materials.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

Despite a high attendance rate of 96%, 7% of children face safety concerns when travelling to and from learning facilities. Issues such as fear of abduction, threats from armed groups, poor road conditions, and long distances significantly deter school attendance. These concerns are particularly pronounced for households with adolescent girls, underscoring the need for enhanced safety measures and proximate learning centers (J-MSNA Report, 2023; ISNA Report, 2024). Gender inequality in this context severely limits girls' educational access, influenced by poverty, early marriage, and restrictive societal norms. Safety concerns, child labour, and parental resistance further deter school attendance, particularly affecting girls, children with disabilities, and gender-diverse individuals. Deep-rooted stigma, limited resources, gaps in rehabilitation services in camps, inaccessibility of educational materials, and a lack of capacity to address diverse gender and disability needs worsen these barriers, restricting the educational and future opportunities of marginalized groups (ISNA Report, 2024; Education Sector Factsheet, Cox’s Bazar, 2024). To address the emotional and psychological toll of displacement, there is a critical need to integrate Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) into educational programming. Many children experience trauma, and without support systems in place, they struggle to remain engaged in school. Educators require MHPSS training to provide trauma-sensitive support, creating a learning environment that can foster resilience and well-being (ISNA Report, 2024). Meeting children’s emotional needs alongside academic support is essential to help them process trauma and stay engaged in their learning (Education Sector Factsheet, Cox’s Bazar, 2024). The need for technology-based learning solutions, such as distance education, is also pronounced, particularly to reach out-of-school children whose educational continuity has been disrupted by displacement. Adolescent girls need flexible, alternative learning due to mobility and cultural barriers. Community-based education programs can help bridge these gaps, accommodating the diverse educational backgrounds and unique needs of this population (J-MSNA Report, 2023). Engaging parents and caregivers, particularly mothers, in the education process is essential to addressing cultural barriers that limit educational access. According to the J-MSNA Report (2023), approximately 47% of parents report safety concerns as a primary reason for keeping children, especially girls, out of school, with 35% citing traditional beliefs regarding gender roles as additional barriers. Programs that actively involve parents in school activities and decision-making processes can address these concerns, creating a more supportive environment for learning. Additionally, targeted awareness initiatives emphasizing the importance of education for both boys and girls can help foster a more inclusive mindset, contributing to gender-equitable educational access across the community. Educational programs should prioritize addressing critical needs, including the creation of safe, segregated facilities, providing menstrual hygiene support, and implementing flexible programming for girls and children with disabilities (J-MSNA Report, 2023). To overcome barriers such as inadequate infrastructure and lack of qualified teachers, the approach must include advocating for weather-resilient facilities, ensuring separate classrooms for adolescent boys and girls, and training more qualified teachers (ISNA Report, 2024). Additionally, creating pathways to recognized accreditation and vocational training will empower Rohingya children and provide access to higher education and employment opportunities, ensuring long-term resilience and empowerment (Education Sector Factsheet, Cox’s Bazar, 2024).

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

In Cox's Bazar, Save the Children (SC) is at the forefront of protection mainstreaming, implementing comprehensive strategies to mitigate the risks of any kind of danger and risk for the children. SC has ingrained a zero-tolerance policy within its organizational culture, leaving no room for ambiguity regarding the prohibition of any form of sexual exploitation and abuse. This policy serves as the cornerstone of SC's protection mainstreaming efforts. The Centrality of Protection (CoP) is also the main pillar of any kind of SC programming. The organization conducts extensive training programs for its staff and volunteers, emphasizing the importance of upholding ethical standards and fostering a culture of accountability. Through community engagement initiatives, SC empowers local residents with the knowledge and skills to recognize and report incidents of abuse, thereby creating a network of vigilant community members actively involved in safeguarding efforts. SC's protection mainstreaming extends to advocacy and policy development, where the organization collaborates with local authorities, international agencies, and other stakeholders to influence policies that prioritize the protection of vulnerable populations. By actively participating in shaping the broader humanitarian response, SC contributes to creating a safer and more secure environment for children and families in Cox's Bazar. Through a multifaceted approach that includes internal capacity building, community empowerment, and advocacy, Save the Children is actively working to mainstream protection measures in Cox's Bazar, ensuring a comprehensive and sustained effort to safeguard the rights and well-being of those affected by displacement.

2.3 - Age & Disability

To prioritize age and disability inclusivity, this project will create adaptive, supportive educational environments in partnership with Organizations of Persons with Disabilities (OPDs) and Disability Support Committees (DSCs) to ensure lasting impact. Infrastructural accessibility will be a priority, with adjustments made to ensure that learning spaces are physically accessible to all children. Individualized Education Plans (IEPs) and individual rehabilitation plans will be developed for learners with disabilities, allowing for tailored support and consistent progress tracking. Accessible teaching and learning materials will be provided, along with home-based education support for children unable to attend regular sessions. Inclusive sports and recreational activities will further promote social integration by allowing children with and without disabilities to engage together. These comprehensive efforts aim to reduce participation barriers, foster empowerment, and establish a sustainable, inclusive educational environment for all learners.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

SC’s accountability commitment focuses on identifying program effectiveness through a comprehensive framework. Accountability as a core value of SC, is crucial for projects that will set up Feedback Response Mechanism (FRM) channels to create an inclusive and accessible accountability system for children, communities, and stakeholders. There are 17 age-specific tools that will be adapted to the project needs based on consultations with children and the community through various channels i.e. home visits, feedback boxes, hotlines, help desks, meetings, FGDs, PDM, and exit interviews. Through applying Child Friendly Response Mechanism (CFRM) tools, children/adolescents will also get the opportunity to provide feedback, express their views, and influence decision-making processes aligning with Core Humanitarian Standards (CHS). These systematic sessions are supported by the required capacity strengthening of staff and other stakeholders. An information-sharing plan following the project's detailed activities with accessible communication material will be shared. Feedback will be verified based on the CO established FRM SOP, allowing the program team to track, investigate, resolve, and close the feedback loop. Findings are compiled in accountability reports and will be shared in the project reviews to inform project improvements. Enabling accountability: The project will establish a contextual information-sharing plan at the beginning to disseminate key information about the project and its accountability mechanisms, and it will be reviewed annually. SC will also conduct quarterly sessions with the beneficiaries, involving them in project components that affect them directly. A unified message will be shared, explaining SC and partner roles, project objectives, expected behavior, the FRM system, and feedback channels with all the stakeholders for consistency. Quarterly accountability reports will be provided as required.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

SC has established a rigorous framework to integrate Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) in Cox’s Bazar, underscoring a profound commitment to safeguarding vulnerable populations. Central to SC’s strategy is an uncompromising zero-tolerance policy towards any form of sexual exploitation and abuse, permeating every level of its operations. This commitment is not merely procedural but deeply embedded within the organizational culture, reinforcing a climate of integrity and accountability. To ensure the effectiveness of this policy, SC has implemented robust reporting and investigative mechanisms that guarantee prompt, impartial handling of allegations. Any breach is met with decisive action, including immediate dismissal and potential legal consequences, underscoring the organization’s unwavering dedication to ethical conduct. Furthermore, SC actively collaborates with local authorities, international bodies, and relevant stakeholders, contributing to a cohesive protection network in Cox’s Bazar. This partnership approach, grounded in SC’s zero-tolerance principle, fosters a collective commitment to a safe, respectful environment for displaced communities. SC’s zero-tolerance policy thus transcends commitment; it is an operational ethos, exemplified through comprehensive PSEA integration, stringent enforcement, and strategic partnerships. By setting and maintaining these high standards, SC not only safeguards vulnerable populations but also elevates the humanitarian response to prioritize the safety and dignity of those impacted by displacement.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Focusing on disaster risk reduction and management in education and enabling students to be aware of and take action on the local climate risks prevailing in the camps and host like floods, landslides, cyclones and others. Anticipatory actions include school-based early warning systems and evacuation drills that provide safety during climate-induced hazards. Environmental awareness through activities such as tree planting, waste management, and conservation of water further enhances sustainability in schools and builds a culture of environmental stewardship. Additionally, the proposed intervention's infrastructure will be resilient to climatic vagaries by reinforcing learning areas that are likely to face extreme weather events. The intervention will, therefore, help include both students and the wider community in ways that contribute to enhancing local climate resilience, making education one of the catalysts for climate-responsive practices in the camps and host.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

The project considers full EPR measures for ensuring educational continuity and ensuring safety during emergencies. Save the Children put in place an EPP incorporating stringent measures such as a business continuity plan and emergency response SOPs which contribute to ensuring continuity of education during the emergency period. Our Emergency Response Teams are trained and ready to deploy, along with Mobile Medical Teams, for rapid support to schools and communities. Stocks of essential education and emergency items have been prepositioned in strategic locations that can respond quickly to minimize disruption in learning. There is also contingency funding from humanitarian funding allocated to cater for unforeseen needs so that resources are available to respond effectively and on time. This will, therefore, help an integrated approach towards a resilient education system, capable of adapting and responding in emergency contexts under the Cox's Bazar context.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

The project aims to equip young and adolescent women and men aged 15-24 with the necessary skills, knowledge, assets, attitudes, and networks needed to advance agency and resilience in their transition to decent work and adulthood. The Livelihoods and Skills Development component addresses both the immediate and long-term needs of youth by integrating education, language support, community engagement, and child protection measures. Interventions in this project build on growing evidence of the impact of vocational skills training on youth employment and labour market participation. Through this project, SC enhances educational opportunities and life skills, fostering socioeconomic conditions and access to quality education for youth and adolescents. The project will conduct a capacity assessment to identify relevant skills based on the context and interests of targeted youth, aiming to increase income-generating opportunities and provide basic literacy and numeracy education within the Livelihoods and Skills Development programs. The project will utilize educational facilities as an entry point to identify adolescents interested in vocational education and training. This will enable them to acquire basic literacy and numeracy skills, transition into vocational and skills training, and ensure they are equipped with foundational education and technical skills to engage in income-generating activities. These may include sectors such as handicrafts, agriculture, and homestead gardening for family consumption, as well as providing additional income opportunities to support their families. Additionally, the project will introduce the "Teach to Transform" program to address the shortage of qualified teachers, particularly within the Rohingya community. This program will offer training, mentorship, and practicum opportunities to unemployed women from Rohingya camps over a twelve-month period, preparing them for a teaching career.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

Aligned with the Joint Response Plan (JRP), this project fosters resilience and self-reliance among refugees by providing capacity-building, empowerment, and inclusive support. The Myanmar Curriculum (MC) equips children for potential repatriation or employment in Myanmar, while the Accelerated Learning Program (ALP) prepares them for livelihoods. Local teachers from both host and refugee communities gain sustainable skills, and Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) builds emotional resilience. Gender-sensitive and inclusive practices ensure equitable access, reducing dependency on assistance.

4.2 Do project activities in the host community link to development initiatives, and/or align with development priorities in the Cox’s Bazar district? If yes, briefly describe.If yes, briefly describe.

Project activities SC aligns with Cox’s Bazar District Development and Growth Priorities (December 2022), focusing on inclusive education and capacity-building. Support is extended to 20 government primary schools and three Learning Centers, ensuring access to quality education for all, including children with disabilities. Initiatives empower School Management Committee members and establish Home Based Learning Groups, providing educational support to 60 groups. This approach promotes resilience, and social integration, and aims for 100% enrollment and retention. In addition, SC collaborates closely with the offices of the Deputy Commissioner (DC), Upazila Nirbahi Officer (UNO), District Education Officer, and Upazila Education Officer on planning, project implementation, and reporting.
