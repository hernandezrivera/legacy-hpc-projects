Project Summary

The project will target conflict-affected men and women, in particular displaced populations/host communities, female heads of households, persons with disabilities/injuries (PWD/Is) and youth, as well as staff members of local stakeholders, including representatives of authorities in Syria. The target group encompasses all displaced persons/host community members who directly participate in project activities (skills training, cash-for-work (CFW) etc.) or directly receive services or input (business grants, agricultural input etc.) as well as staff members of local organizations or other local stakeholders, e.g. representatives of local authorities who participate in training or discussions.
The overall objective is Contribute to enhancing the resilience of the affected population in Syria (displaced populations and host communities).

Needs

According to HNO 2019, an estimated 6.5 million Syrians are food insecure and an additional 2.5 million people are at risk of food insecurity. From the assessed households, an estimated 40 percent of Syrians spend more than 65 percent of their expenditure on food, 57.2 percent of food insecure households reported to have outstanding debt and 65 percent of food insecure people are adopting crisis or emergency negative coping strategies. Eight years into the crisis, the main drivers to food insecurity are prolonged displacements, loss and lack of livelihoods and reduced production capacity which in turn resulted in limited financial access to food, high prices and inflation contributing to reduced purchasing power and continuous depletion of livelihoods assets of the most vulnerable populations. Almost 40 percent of the surveyed households reported to be an IDP or returnee of whom 74.5 percent reported to be displaced for more than 12 months. Among them 478,283 displaced people living in last resort IDP sites/camps are of particular concern as they have limited access to a diversified diet or income opportunities. With an 83 - 89 percent people living below poverty lines , and with cumulative GDP loss of $226 billion between 2011 – 2016 out of which $16 billion in losses and damages attributable to agriculture sector alone, the relationship between poverty and food security is becoming inseparable. A monthly food ration with staple items costs at least 80 percent of an unskilled laborer’s monthly salary and 50-80 percent of a public service employee’s monthly salary thus demonstrating the existence of “working poor” in Syria. The worst drought in 30 years affected cereal production in 2017/2018 winter season in Syria. This has come as an additional burden on the prolonged crisis with a shortfall of 1.2 million metric tonnes of wheat to meet domestic requirements (food use) of 3.4 million metric tonnes. Unprecedented livestock asset losses estimated to about half of the pre-crisis situation are affecting livelihoods leading to irreversible coping strategies. The humanitarian needs in Syria are expected to remain high in 2019 and multiple actions are needed to ensure that all four pillars of food security related to access, availability, utilization and stability of food are adequately addressed through lifesaving, life sustaining, appropriate resilience, and early recovery interventions. Emergency response at the early onset of any sudden population movement as well as targeted food assistance and lifesaving emergency agricultural assistance to meet the nutritional and kilo-calorie deficits of the most vulnerable population as per food security indicators is crucial across Syria. Agriculture sector is a major contributor to GDP with more than 25/30 percent of Syria’s GDP reliant on this sector, and it is also producing food for more than half of the population. Therefore, saving, restoring and protecting agricultural and related livelihoods and value chains will be critical to increase the self-reliance and strengthen resilience of Syrians

Protection Risk Analysis

Although all Syrian population has been heavily affected by the current crisis, due to traditional local norms, women are exposed to additional protection related risks. All types of violence are cited as occurring in opposition areas of Syria, with domestic violence, sexual exploitation and child marriage as top three. While sexual exploitation is somehow new, child marriage and domestic violence are often normalized and ingrained in the culture. Women and girls are exposed to structural and societal risks as the lack of safe spaces and public transportation, limited freedom of movement, poverty, unemployment, displacement, stress associated to war, gender role change, lack of documents. All these poses enormous pressure, men feeling they failed in protecting and securing a good life for their families. With men perceiving women as a threat to their control due to gender roles change, domestic violence has increased in Syria. Whereas men actions are explained as a result of stress, women are often blamed for provoking the violence or allowing it to happen. Parental violence against children has also been reported as increasing. Displacement and living in camps are noted as being particularly unsafe environments for women/girls due to the lack of privacy (in tents or in latrines), overcrowding and mixing of people, poverty, financial desperation, and lawlessness.The WoS Protection analysis reveals that 17 sub-districts in Idleb and 23 In Aleppo are facing severe, critical and catastrophic situation regarding protection. While some services are available, GBV cases are less addressed and the assistance is focusing mainly on survival pathway support and less on community-based interventions, mandatory for mind-set change. Women and girls occupy a central role in WATAN’s strategy for 2018-2023. Part of the development approach, WATAN seeks to empower women to become more resilient in the conflict setting while securing their safety at all levels. When designing the project, WATAN has applied all its security protocols to ensure that interviewed GBV survivors and leaders of communities are not exposed to any risk for their involvement. For this purpose, WATAN and its partners have a set of policies and procedures to be respected when working with GBV cases.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1- Risks: Deterioration of the security situation or escalation of conflict/natural disaster preventing access to project site.Mitigation measures:- Contextual information is gathered through staff on the ground and fed into rapid feedback mechanisms to be established.- In case the situation requires temporary suspension or adjustment or permanent suspension of activities in a given area, the consortium, through the PMU will immediately inform the NMFA and discuss options for re-programming.2- Risk: Threat of warring parties.Mitigation measures:- During the start-up phase, WATAN develops solid security management plans to mitigate risks for beneficiaries and staff, and joint scenario-based contingency planning is conducted at the level of implementation area.- WATAN Security department coordinate and constantly monitor the security situation.3- Risk: Corruption and diversion of assistance.Mitigation measures:- Operating livelihoods programming with a cash transfer component in conflict-affected areas, particularly where various armed actors have a presence, is considered to be a high-risk activity, with the potential for assistance to be diverted. WATAN proposes the following mitigation measures to minimise those risks:- Common principles for remote management and monitoring procedures are developed during the current pilot phase inception phase by their relevance for and application through the staff will be reviewed periodically.- WATAN staff are trained in accountability, and, wherever possible, feedback and complaint mechanisms are established.- WATAN have well-established financial and support systems.- Contingency plans take into account the risk of corruption or aid diversion and detail measures to be taken should the situation arise.- Third-party monitoring and where possible, peer monitoring mechanisms are established to produce information for triangulation.4- Risk: Conflict around access to project support.Mitigation measures:- Common do-no-harm approaches are developed, and staff and local partners are trained on them.- Discussions and awareness raising sessions are organized for local stakeholders, and community members to identify and address perceptions around resource allocation and related risks.- WATAN commit to open and transparent communication.- The value of support packages is standardized across each of the geographical implementation areas.- Local communities and municipalities are strongly involved in the identification and monitoring of CFW schemes.- Standard operating procedures are developed for CFW interventions, agreed upon and monitored by a third party.5- Risk: Non-approval from local communities and authorities regarding cash transfer programs.Mitigation measures:- Elaborate common communication strategy and messages for engagement with local stakeholders through the stakeholder engagement plans (SEP-see result 5).- Communicate transparently and widely, get approval from key stakeholders
