Project Summary

Save the Children (SC) is working to reduce vulnerabilities amongst children and their families, for displaced, evacuated or returnees along with host communities in Northwest Syria (NWS) and Northeast Syria (NES) through life-saving and sustaining humanitarian Health interventions. There will also be a focus on increasing knowledge of beneficiaries for resilience in the long term.

In order to achieve this, SC will provide quality out-patient care in accordance with standard service package. In NES, SC will support two established Primary Healthcare Centres, directly in Al-Hurriya (Ain Issa) and through its partner Kurdish Red Crescent (KRC) in Ain Issa camp.
Child Health
- Prevention and treatment of common communicable diseases, non-communicable diseases, and other curable conditions
- Provision of essential child health medical supplies including drugs and consumables
- Immediate stabilisation and referral of trauma cases
- Routine laboratory investigations
- Mentoring and supportive supervision for health workers and outreach workers
- Train education staff on identification key child health issues
- Routine immunisation services as per the Syrian national schedule

Reproductive Health (RH)
- Provision of ANC and PNC and safe delivery at fixed HF, including Minimum Initial Service Package (MISP)
- Breastfeeding counselling and preparedness for optimal infant feeding practices during ANC-visits of pregnant women
- Referral to basic and Comprehensive Emergency Obstetrics and Newborn Care (CEmONC) facilities for complicated deliveries
- Provision of essential reproductive health medical supplies including drugs and consumables
- Routine laboratory investigations
- Mentoring and supportive supervision of health workers and outreach workers
- Family planning and post abortion care services
Community mobilization and awareness
- Trained community health workers (CHWs) in areas such as RH and CP
- Increase accessibility and coverage of health and CP services through strengthening of facility-community linkages
- Delivery of integrated messages on IYCF, hygiene, CP and care seeking and management of childhood illnesses among communities within the catchment populations of the supported HFs as part of the daily activities conducted by CHWs
- Increased knowledge of the beneficiary communities on common health, nutrition and CP problems
- Identification and referral of critical cases to HF
- Screening of malnourished cases and referral to the nutrition treatment centres
- Screening and referral for IYCF using the simple rapid assessment

Needs

The Health Resources Availability Mapping System (HeRAMS) implemented by the Health sector in Syria indicated that of the public health facilities across all 14 governorates and 105 health districts (including hard to reach and UN-declared besieged areas), more than half of public hospitals and health centres have either closed or are only partially functional. Of the 111 public hospitals, 50 per cent (55) were reported as fully functioning; 22 per cent (25) were reported as partially functioning (i.e. facing shortages of staff, equipment, medicines or damage of the building in some cases); and 28 per cent (31) were reported as not functioning at all. Of the 1,802 assessed public health centres, 46 per cent (834) were reported as fully functioning; 22 per cent (396) as partially functioning; 31 per cent (565) as non-functioning (completely out of service) with the level of functionality unknown in one per cent (seven). Access to healthcare remains poor, especially in UN-declared besieged and hard to reach areas due to a lack of functioning facilities; shortages of health staff, drugs and medical supplies; and persistent clashes between various armed groups. Referral systems for trauma, emergency obstetric care and surgical services are insufficient, while there remains limited capacity for mobile clinics to reach out to fluid temporary settlements and host communities. The maintenance of medical equipment is also a core challenge, compounded by restrictions related to international sanctions on Syria, which prevent partners from importing critical spare parts. Livelihoods depletion, limited financial and physical access to food, continued high levels of displacement and unexploded hazard contamination/insecurity relating to the conflict have exacerbated vulnerability. The limited access to quality water as well as significantly reduced purchasing power that inhibits access to hygiene items, may contribute to a high prevalence of childhood diseases. Combined, these factors have the potential to further jeopardize the health status of girls and boys under five and pregnant and lactating women (PLW), increasing the overall risk of undernutrition as well as the short and long term consequences impacting their nutritional and health status such as stunting and growth faltering, morbidity and mortality. -Idleb: The health situation in Idleb is particularly dire as a result of more than 500,000 displacements to/in the area since January 2017, coupled with limited access to health services. Improvised explosive devices, targeted assassinations, kidnappings and poor living conditions already made the situation in Idleb insecure and dangerous. -Afrin: In Afrin, 136,000 individuals are estimated to reside in the district, including more than 40,000 in Afrin city. An additional 134,000 people displaced from Afrin remains in the Tall Refaat sub-district, Nabul and Zahraa towns, and surrounding settlements. WHO has been responding to health needs of these populations since their displacement earlier this year, although it is unclear how future hostilities will impact them. Displaced people are under increased risk of infectious diseases due to limited access to safe water and sanitation, overcrowding and other risk factors. Some of these individuals are patients suffering from trauma injuries requiring post-surgical care, disability, and rehabilitation services. A majority of displaced requires continuous care for the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular and renal diseases, diabetes, cancer, psychosocial and mental health, and as well as maternal and child health services. -Ain Essa: The camp currently has a population of about 12,000 individuals and the only health service provider is KRC supported by SCI. Technical support as well as strengthening the disease identification, reporting, treatment and referral to Tal Abyad , Kobane or Raqqa Hospitals for specialized healthcare services needs to be strengthened.

Implementation of activities

HealthThe health sector will provide both child and reproductive health (RH) services. SC will increase access to quality diagnosis and treatment for common child, maternal, and RH problems and communicable diseases as well as early detection and response to disease outbreaks. SC, through local partners, will increase the technical capacity of both male and female health workers to deliver high-quality services, such as the treatment of communicable diseases, management of routine immunization and vaccination campaigns, IYCF, and management of a disease surveillance system. SC will also work to reduce maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality through the provision of antenatal care (ANC), safe delivery, early initiation of breastfeeding, postnatal care (PNC), family planning, treatment of sexually transmitted illnesses, and tetanus toxoid vaccination. Services will be provided through the RH units/clinics in HFs, and RH trainings will covered under a separate project. Communicable diseasesAccording to the EWARN system in Syria, outbreaks of polio and measles have been reported which 7 people have died from polio and 9 people have died from measles in the areas of operation during January to September 2018. Organisations such as the WHO, MSF and IRC are conducting polio supplementary and measles vaccination campaigns (aged 5 to 15) in NES. The outbreaks of polio and measles have been reported in camps, and in overcrowded unsanitary makeshifts in Ar Raqqa. The bad living conditions will exacerbate the risks of communicable diseases and the likelihood of spread. SC is responding through two PHCs and outreach teams. Reproductive Health (RH)The programme will work to reduce maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality through provision of ANC, safe delivery, PNC, family planning, treatment of Sexually Transmitted illnesses, and Tetanus Toxoid vaccination. Services will be provided through the RH units/clinics within the supported HFs. Note that RH trainings are covered under another project. Patients who have severe RH needs (basic needs are addressed at both of the HFs) referred to the maternity hospital in Kafr Talharim, which provides Comprehensive Emergency Obstetrics Care. Community mobilization and awarenessSC will take an integrated (health, nutrition and CP) approach to community sensitization and awareness raising. The programme will have 40 outreach workers across all the HFs, selected from the same community, in consultation with the local community and partners. The CHWs will have individual work plans including when and where to conduct group sessions, community events, home visits and group discussions. The team leader (selected from the outreach team) and partner staff will set work plans to monitor the workers. The workers will receive trainings on integrated community sensitization key messages, to disseminate to the community, following a standard national community mobilization package. In addition, the areas of intervention will be determined through health facility data collected every month. More prevalent diseases, and more critical locations will be covered. The workers will be disseminating IEC materials and information to communities in order to encourage better prevention reporting and prompt treatment. IEC materials were developed by adapting already existing materials under the supervision of TA's, and program personnel and reviewed and revised by the partners and shared with the community to ensure the materials are culturally sensitive and fit to context. The workers are also connected with the Local Councils to verbally raise any major issues regarding health concerns amongst the communities and report back to HF's. Provision of Supplies: The provision of medical supplies and equipment will not be the main component of the project. However, SC is forecasting average consumption and budget it in each award according to monthly consumption reports to ensure services are not interrupted

Protection Risk Analysis

Risk: Barriers to access for marginalized groups Mitigating measures: Specific measures are taken to ensure input is received from marginalized groups such as women and girls, children with disabilities and the elderly. Feedback is used to inform planning, implementation, monitoring and evaluating activities. Mitigation measures are brainstormed with the community to ensure that girls and boys (incl. children with disabilities) have equal access to SC healthcare.Monitoring Mechanism: FGDs, feedback mechanism Risk: SCI or partner staff violating SCI CSG rules harms children; damages the reputation of the organisation; and can lead to loss of funding and prosecution. Mitigating measures: All SC staff, volunteers and representatives sign the Child Safeguarding Policy and Code of Conduct, child safeguarding risk assessments are conducted for all project sites and mitigating actions and put in place, all staff are trained on the Child Safeguarding Policy and Code of Conduct. Partners have received CSG training via Hurras. Partners have their own reporting mechanism as well as CSG focal points in the field and main CSG focal point in Turkey. Risk: Affected populations and SC staff and volunteers, are placed at risk of sexual exploitation and abuse, including harassment. Mitigating measures: Training will be provided to SC staff and volunteers on SC’s Anti-Harassment Policy and Code of Conduct. SC will also continue to provide trainings on the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) through SC’s PSEA focal points. Risk: The wellbeing of health workers are often neglected in the heath of urgent response and overwhelming demand for services.Mitigating measures: SC and partners have the duty to take care of the physical and mental wellbeing of their personnel. As every personnel is entitled to benefit the same services and rights of other SC workers around the world, SC ensures that its partners are following the same standards. Overall Monitoring: SC upholds the importance of setting up various channels of communication and complaint to mitigate the risks or solve the issues in a rapid and most culturally sensitive way. As some of the activities are harboring high risks for both beneficiaries and humanitarian workers (including violence against health workers/facilities, risk of sexual exploitation or harassment), SC and partners’ staff will be trained on various topics on potential risks and how to mitigate them and provided with confidential complaint mechanisms to report in case of an event. SC and partners will be promoting different communication channels to provide their feedback and complaints such as complaint boxes, phone lines, email and one on one meetings.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Safety & Security (S&S) All staff movements must be pre-approved and refresher security trainings are regular. The Hostile Environment Awareness Training is mandatory for NES deployments; local staff trained on Advanced Personal S&S. SC partners engage with relevant stakeholders and receive timely updates. SC works on a common response for risky conditions. Airstrikes SC partners immediately report security incidents to be analysed to develop and implement comprehensive safety plans. Loss of access - Disruption in border crossing/accessSC and partners conduct thorough security assessments and maintain updated contingency plans. In need, SC will suspend programs and work to resume activities. If not viable, SC would assess the relocation option and in a worst-case scenario, project termination considered. Work permits To mitigate the Gov. of Turkey (GoT)'s legislative environment hindering SC’s ability to operate, I.e. using a third-party HR firm to overcome work permit challenges. Diversion of resources to terrorist/ proscribed groups In 2018, SC identified areas for increased risk mitigation; an action plan was developed. SC launched an initiative with OCHA to provide external training on international humanitarian law and negotiation with armed actors. Partners have their own systems of anti-terrorism vetting checks for all contracted suppliers and SC’s Syria office vets against the third-party database for procurements $750+. Escalation of military activity at Turkey/Syria borderSC program locations and contingency plans are constantly reviewed and the Hibernation, Relocation and Evacuation plan is shared with staff. Offices and guesthouses are located away from military bases and equipped with S&S infrastructure. Attacks by armed groupsSecurity assessments are conducted on potential project sites. Proximity to military locations is monitored and a back-up vehicle is used in high-risk areas. Staff transit must be pre-approved and travel in daylight. Improvised Explosive Devices ShellingWhilst most of the shelling take place in southern rural Idlib and northern rural Hama, no area is immune. SC coordinates with Tetra Tech, Mines Advisory Group cleared new project locations and Staff to receive Mine Risk Education trainings. Road accidents SC drivers are tested; daily vehicle checks; vehicles equipped with tracking devices and satellite phones; mandatory seatbelts. Disruption to cash transfer operations In NES, SC is reviewing Hawala delivery to identify mitigation measures/additional suppliers. In NWS, NGOs faced challenges in money transfer due to GoT restrictions and temporary suspension of Bab Al Hawa. Resistance from local authorities to SC's programsSC has established relationships with relevant local authorities and stakeholders (Local Councils, Ministries) and will be involved in design and implementationAssumptions Qualified staff available for recruitmentCross border access and one-year extension of UNSC
