Project Summary

This project will be a continuation of an existing Explosive Remnants of War Risk Education ERW - RE programme in Idleb and Aleppo. Shafak proposes that RE activities continue on and that both Contamination and Casualty Data Gathering are conducted by existing teams.
Shafak is providing ERW-RE to children in the communities of Idleb and Aleppo. Because ERW’s are a long term problem, Shafak believes that long impact approaches for children must be sustained; the more a child is taught safe behaviours, the more likely it is that they will become ingrained in their day-to-day activity.
Under the ERW-RE part of the project, 6 teams of 5, each consisting of 2 groups of 2 and a team leader, who have already been trained by Shafak, will target locations in Idleb and Aleppo providing school-age children with RE activities, and materials to inform them about dangers of ERW. They will also provide RE material to the older generation, putting up posters about the ERW risks in the public streets and communities to increase the awareness of ERW threats. Schools and locations that were not reached in previous messaging will be the first priority.
In addition to, Risk Education will be conducted in IDP camps and host communities. Whilst the sessions will be open to all community members, the teams will be placing particular emphasis on reaching those who are most at risk of accident, including children, young men and IDPs who may not be familiar with the type of threat present in the area. The sessions will be held in both schools and community spaces. The teams will also distribute flyers and posters with Risk Education messaging to reach the wider community.
In addition to RE, Shafak proposes that teams begin gathering data on contamination and causalities with the use of Kobo. This will require technical training which will be provided via Skype from our office in Gaziantep, Turkey. The teams will gather data by talking to local councils, hospitals and medical centres, who will direct them to affected families and communities. The information collected will be recorded into a database, and Shafak's experience within Syria of data collection and remote monitoring; through it, information will be made available to share with other humanitarian actors. Data collected will then be used as a tool to plan ERW clearance in the future.
This information can then be used to better understand protection gaps and improve future programming. It will also be used in advocacy initiatives.
A referral mechanism will be established whereby those who are identified as being particularly vulnerable, can seek additional protection or support in accordance with international best practice for services and protection intervention. This process is already underway in the area where Shafak has worked to develop relationships with other humanitarian actors, notably with Humanity and Inclusion..

Needs

For more than 8 years, the Syrian crisis has resulted in the widespread contamination of ERW and the associated threat of harm to vulnerable communities and IDPs. More than 10 million people (about half of the population) are at risk of ERW in Syria, with close to 2,000 communities reporting contamination and explosive incidents (draft 2019 HNO). Mines and ERWs in civilian areas have and will continue to threaten the lives of ordinary girls, boys, women and men. Idleb and Aleppo Governorates have seen intense shelling and fighting among armed actors and resulting in high level of landmines, UXO and IED contaminations. The use of cluster bombs is also reported in the area. Even some areas that are currently not under attack its land tends to be contaminated from the past shelling and battles. Mine/ERW contamination impedes people’s right to move safely and to access livelihood, such as farming. Many people, both host communities and IDPs, are unaware of the risky behaviours that can lead to mine/ERW accidents, while some are forced to engage in dangerous activities in contaminated areas. Among the “unaware” people living in affected areas, children are most at risk of mine/ERW accidents due to their curiosity. Children are in particular danger from ERWs due to their natural curiosity and high mobility, increasing the need for risk education and age-specific survivors’ assistance programmes. UNICEF suggests children’s risky behaviour of playing in contaminated areas. Through its RE project in Idleb and Aleppo Governorate, Shafak has found that many children pride themselves on touching and collecting ERWs, and are unaware of risks. Girls, boys, women and men are affected by ERW differently and programming must be reflective of this. It is well known for example and is outlined in the HNO, that boys are at particular risk of engagement in dangerous child labour, including scavenging for explosive devices. Accordingly, RE will highlight the risk they face when tampering with explosive items. Although worldwide, men and boys made up the majority of direct ERW victims – those who take on a care giving role towards the survivors of ERW are disproportionately female. Additionally, the population that remains across much of Syria is more commonly female, as men have left to fight or to seek better lives elsewhere. Accordingly, the project will enable girls, boys, women and men to equally participate in, and benefit from Mine Action. The project will highlight gender mainstreaming issues and contribute to improving future programming, by collecting data which is disaggregated by age and sex, and sharing this information where appropriateThey also tend to pay more attention and listen to RE experts who come from outside than parents and teachers. Women and men, especially IDPs, also face significant risk. IDP families seeking shelter or travelling through unknown areas may behave in a risky way without knowing. In addition, upon their return to the communities of origin, the risk of accidents during reconstruction and rehabilitation is immense as mines and ERWs may be buried under rubbles, in their farmland, and on the way to school. As it is extremely difficult for the returnees to accept they cannot simply go back to their lives before the war, it is essential to raise awareness about the lingering effects of mines/ERW. Among people “forced” to take risky behaviours, poor families are reported to be scavenging in contaminated areas to find explosive devices for economic profit or scavenging garbage for any flammables for heating and cooking. Children, especially boys primarily engage in such dangerous activities.

Implementation of activities

The project will be implemented through Shafak office in Gaziantep with the collaboration of local councils and the Directorate of Education in Idleb and Aleppo. 6 x teams (RE, CIS, VDC) will be trained directly by Shafak, This training will also include community liaison, safe behaviour messaging and the use of KoBo (reporting platform). Additionally, teams will receive training on Do No Harm principles from Shafak's Senior Protection Coordinator. In accordance with Shafak's recruitment policies, each staff member will sign Shafak's PSEA code of conduct, where a protection specialist provides orientations and regular PSEA training sessions on child protection policy and PSEA code of conduct. 6 x teams (RE, CIS, VDC) (3 teams in Idleb and 3 teams in Aleppo) each comprising a team leader and four team members will be deployed to target beneficiaries most at risk of ERW accidents, including IDPs, children and agricultural workers. The teams will be fully mobile, able to respond to the changing security situation and target groups. Provision of awareness materials (leaflets for families, posters for the community) to the student's families and communities designed using the experience of Shafak staff.Shafak will identify the work advancement and any challenges. Targets will be assessed each month as a proportion of the indicated number of beneficiaries reached. These reports will also provide information required on the quality of performance. The Shafak media team will provide photos and videos of the activities, conditional on permission and consent of the actors, and we aim to confirm 70% of reports through this method.Shafak will have open communication channels with local initiatives running complementary projects. After each Risk Education session, there will be discussion and questions about the key elements of the presentation, trying to ensure that the participants have a good understanding of the lecture content. A pre and post-test will be conducted with three participants in approximately 20% of sessions. Shafak will also hold focus group discussions with beneficiaries both during and after project implementation. This will be an opportunity for beneficiaries to provide feedback on the sessions.After each Risk Education session, a form will be signed by the school authority/ camp facilitator/ community leader. Photographic evidence will form an important part of monitoring and reporting. Data collected will be shared with the Mine Action Sub Cluster. Feedback and complaints will be addressed very seriously.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks are divided into security and program risks Security risks: A- Shelling / It is one of the major threats in Syria, especially with the escalation of military conflict in northern Syria in this period. The shelling can be direct or indirect. Solution/ Mitigation measures: Choose response centers which they are far from military locations. Coordination and continuous communication with the S&S assistant and local authorities to ensure the safety of staff and beneficiaries. Shafak will maintain threat contingency plans and SOPs for local use. Field supervisors, informed by field security staff, can adjust the daily/weekly work plan and avoid areas of elevated threat. All teams deploy with mobile phone communications and a first aid kit, knowing where the nearest medical facilities are located. Partial or total suspension of the activities. If activities cannot be resumed, Shafak will assess the possibility to operate in other areas. A wide range of candidates will be interviewed and competitive salaries and contract duration will be offered. Inability to properly control or confirm the quality of training: Ensure that the training location facilitates the best possible outcomes.
