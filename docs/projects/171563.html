Project Summary

The Somalia Humanitarian Needs Overview for 2020 indicates that 66% of the displaced population in Somalia are children who are exposed to a constant threat of exploitation, neglect, abduction, recruitment, abuse, and violence. A perception survey conducted by the CP AoR in April/May of 2020 reveals that 70% of child protection actors reported that there were children experiencing psychosocial distress. This includes fear and anxiety among children due to disruption of their daily routines. This is attributed to the extended conflict for more than 30 years in Somalia that has subjected boys and girls to extreme mental health and psychosocial risks. This emotional distress is compounded by effects of child labour, recruitment in armed forces/groups, early marriage for adolescent girls, separation from caregivers, and discrimination due to gender, family economic and displacement status, and different forms of disability that the children face on a daily basis.
This Child Protection and Gender-based Violence project will aim to reduce the suffering of boys, girls and their caregivers in Banadir and Galmudug regions of Somalia though increasing their access to basic CP and GBV services. According to the CP AoR approximately 1.87 million children, of which 10% have disabilities, need child protection services as of October 2020. The project will thus promote inclusion of children with disabilities and those from displaced communities in humanitarian response activities to ensure their needs are well understood and addressed. Identified unaccompanied and separated children will be registered and tracing, follow up and reunification will be arranged through the support of the cluster and other actors. Health care providers will be trained on basic PSS, survivor-centered approach to case management, and clinical management of rape to strengthen their capacity to respond to mental health, PSS and rape cases. The project will also raise awareness on child protection, GBV and disability inclusion in the community and to address existing attitudinal institutional barriers that hinder children (including those with disabilities) and their caregivers from basic CP and GBV services. Information-sharing and capacity building workshops will be conducted with CP and GBV service providers, government officials and community volunteers to build their capacity on GBV and CP issues, as well as promote mainstreaming of disability inclusion in humanitarian response. This includes ensuring all locations where project activities will be conducted will be accessible to people with disabilities; continuous consultations with people with disabilities to contribute to decision-making; conduct mobile clinics to reach remote areas that are too far, making it difficult for people with disabilities to access basic services; and involving families of people with disabilities in the project awareness activities (especially those with mental health issues). All data will be disaggregated by age, gender and disability and shared with CP and GBV AoRs to improve programming.

Project Activities

CP AoR Outcome 1: Activity 1.1: Provision of age and gender-sensitive mental health and psychosocial support to children. Group counselling and art for therapy approaches will be employed for children of various age groups, gender and those with different forms of disabilities. Health care providers and community volunteers will be trained on identification of children with psychosocial or mental health challenges, basic PSS, and mental health to build their capacity to respond to specific MHPSS needs. Activity 1.2: Conduct awareness campaigns targeting caregivers on PSS, parenting, care for children with disabilities, forms of child protection and GBV, and available services for reporting or seeking assistance. Activity 1.3: Identity, document, trace and reunite unaccompanied and separated minors with appropriate caregivers. CISP will further strengthen mechanisms for registration and receiving information and for the active tracing of immediate family members and relatives through capacitating child protection committees. Case management procedures for follow up of children who have been reunified with their caretakers will be strengthened and IDTR coordination improved. Activity 1.4: Provide interim care for UAM, or other vulnerable children through foster care, interim caregivers or safe houses. CISP through partners and child protection committees will continue to identify and train foster families on child protection and family tracing processes. CP AoR Outcome 2: Activity 2.1: Provision of specialized case management that targets children with disabilities, including MHPSS and family counselling to address attitudinal barriers towards children with disabilities, and sharing information about available protection services in the community for preventing and responding to their needs. Activity 2.2: Coordinate information-sharing and capacity-building workshops CP and GBV service providers, government officials and community volunteers to build their capacity on GBV and CP issues, as well as promote mainstreaming of disability inclusion in humanitarian response. This will be done in consultation with organizations for people with disabilities who have partnered with CISP in previous projects. GBV AoR Outcome 1: Activity 1.1: Provision of case management including medical care and clinical management of rape (CMR) to survivors of GBV (including those with disabilities). Health care providers will be trained on survivor centered approach to case management and clinical management of rape to build their capacity to respond to rape cases. Activity 1.2: Provision of dignity protection materials (hygiene and dignity kits, solar lamps, etc.) to survivors of GBV. GBV AoR Outcome 2: Activity 2.1: Conduct community sensitization campaigns in target locations to raise awareness on available GBV services, prevention and mitigation of GBV.

Mitigation Measures

Risk1: Limited access to/awareness on protection services: The project will periodically conduct mobile clinics to reach remote areas that are too far to be accessible by foot. the project will also include community volunteers and service providers from remote areas during capacity building activities who will go back to their communities and create awareness on protection as well as information on available services. Risk 2: Limited participation in decision-making by marginalized groups: Adopt inclusive approaches to implementation such as increasing child participation (child safety walks), consult with community leaders/volunteers to identify populations that are usually left out of community discussions such as people with disabilities and also include a family member of people with mental health challenges. The project will also ensure an equal representation across participants of different age groups, gender and forms of disability in its activities, and disaggregate as such to constantly monitor inclusion of all groups is maintained. Risk 3: Limited access to community feedback mechanisms: Identify and strengthen existing community feedback mechanisms and work with community volunteers to disseminate this information in the community. Hotline numbers will also be made available during all awareness activities and availed at health facilities and service providers offices for sharing with the community.

Please provide further explanation

The main beneficiaries are local communities, and the project will identify existing structures such as feedback mechanisms, humanitarian actors and existing humanitarian efforts to avoid duplication and ensure community ownership from start to finish.Also, the project will be including people with disabilities (including children) in the proposed activities through consultations, promoting access to services, and service provision for children and adults with mental health challenges. These groups are among the most dependent groups of people in the community. Increasing their resilience to violence and abuse contributes to durable/sustainable solutions for the community. CISP is legally registered to work in the target locations and has employed local staff who are familiar with the context and language . CISP also upholds all humanitarian rights as per the minimum standards stipulated in the SPHERE standards across all sectors, including protection (CP and GBV).
