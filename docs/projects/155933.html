Project Summary

The United Nations (UN) estimates number of crisis-affected population in need of humanitarian assistance in Syria at 11.7 million people(49.8% male and 50.2% female) out of which 4.7 million and 5 million live in areas of major severity of needs (inter-sector severity 3) and acute severity of need (inter-sector severity 4-6) respectively. The above include 6.2 million protracted Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs); 1.4 million returnees; 324,000 IDPs in hard to reach areas, 1.6 million-population movement and the rest overburdened host communities in addition to 480,000 Palestine refugees. 67% of the 11.7 million people in need of humanitarian assistance are accessible from within Syria according to Syria Response Access Dynamic December report (OCHA, 2018). Overall, 9 million of people are estimated to be food insecure and at risk of food insecurity by Food Security Sector.
In this context, FAO has designed a project to assist 75,000 vulnerable crisis affected food insecure farming households (equivalent to 450,000 people) in Syria. About 15% of the targeted households will be selected from those who receive food support to help them shift from depending on food support to producing their own food and secure their lives. The objective of the proposed action is to protect and re-establish livelihoods of vulnerable crisis affected farmers and internally displaced populations through restoration of critical irrigation infrastructures to increase household food production and security. The expected outputs of the projects are:-
Output 1: Critical small-scale irrigation infrastructures in the targeted governorates are assessed and light rehabilitation undertaken.
Output 2. Capacity of technical staff and relevant stakeholder’s strengthened
A training programme on enhancing irrigation management, water use efficiency in small-scale irrigation, agricultural land management will be based on FAO standards and approaches. Stakeholders and farmers will join this training.
This project is expected to contribute to increase access to irrigation water for crisis-affected farmers and their families in the targeted areas through light rehabilitation of critical small scale irrigation infrastructures. Cultivation and other agricultural practices including livestock and pastures production will consequently benefit from the water supply plans as an emergency response to crisis. The proposed intervention will strengthen household food production capacities and knowledge; and address immediate food and nutrition security. It will also increase availability of food within the country. This may in turn promote flourishing of agricultural based livelihoods in the rural community, which would contribute to social cohesion, peace and stability. The project seeks to utilize intensive labor in order to reach the maximum possible community participation and inject cash into the local community.

Needs

The crisis in Syria is in its eighth year and the humanitarian situation keeps deteriorating. According to the 2019 HNO, 11.7 million (49.8% male and 50.2% female) Syrians will require humanitarian aid in 2019 out of which 6.5 million are food insecure while a further 2.5 million at risk of becoming acutely food insecure according to food security sector. Similar to any other key sectors, the crisis has fragmented the structure of the agriculture sector, degraded soil and water resources, reduced agricultural production and thus lowered its contribution to national income, seriously affecting stability of rural and farming society and risking food and nutrition security. The financial cost of the damages and losses to the agriculture sector from 2011 to 2016 is estimated to be US$ 16 billion (FAO Agriculture Damages and Losses Need Assessment, 2017). Agricultural infrastructures including irrigation systems have suffered extensive damages, theft and neglect since 2011. The persistent droughts have further worsened the situation. The results are disruptions of large sections of the agriculture systems with consequent substantial weakening of its components including irrigation systems. The Ministry of Water Resources estimates that Syria’s average annual water requirements is about 17.743 billion cubic meters, of which 15.613 billion cubic meters (88%) are required for agriculture. The average annual deficit, which is about 1.533 billion cubic meters have increased to 5 billion cubic meters in the recent dry years. Overall, it is estimated that over 25% of the irrigation infrastructures are damaged especially in the major areas of wheat production. Before the crisis, small-scale irrigation in Syria were done mainly using electric pumps powered by the grid or diesel engines. With the lack of electricity from the grid, farmers have been coping by either switching to diesel-operated pumps or buying small diesel generators that they use to produce electricity for their homes and electric water pumps. The reduced availability and high cost of diesel have forced farmers to reduce the use of diesel for pumping water. Farmers’ livelihoods are greatly compromised by so many challenges that continue to place more pressure on their lives including high costs of agricultural inputs, less availability of water for irrigation, disrupted economy, increased diesel prices and unsecured roads. Given the high cost of irrigation, damage to pumping stations, irrigation structures and equipment, unreliability of electricity and the poor availability of fuel, many farmers have abandoned irrigation and turned to predominantly rain fed production which are also prone to climate related risks. For example CFSAM 2018 report indicate that only 1.2 million tons of wheat were produced in 2018 as compared with 1.8 million tons in 2017 and pre-crisis production of 4.1 million tons which is very low when compared with the national cereal requirements of 3.4 million tons. Light rehabilitation of critical infrastructures to support crop production especially wheat (major staple) has become an urgent concern that calls for immediate actions to save lives and livelihoods of farmers. The top priority actions are to support farmers to get access to water for irrigation through light rehabilitation of critical small-scale irrigation infrastructures to support household food production and ensure food and nutrition security. The light rehabilitation of critical damaged irrigation infrastructures and supporting small-scale irrigation projects while introducing efficient irrigation techniques in affected areas of the country will be key in strengthening resilience of crisis affected farmers particularly IDPs, returnees and host communities.

Implementation of activities

Currently FAO is operating in thirteen governorates – Aleppo, Al Hassakeh, Ar Raqqa, Dar’a, Deir ez Zor, Hama, Homs, Idleb, As Sweida, Quneitra, Rural Damascus, Tartous and Lattakia. In governorates where UN staff presence is not permitted by UN security regulations, FAO operates through government-seconded officers and NGOs service providers who are directly supporting implementation of activities and report to FAO.FAO Representation in Syria will be responsible for the overall implementation of the activities, backstopped by FAO headquarters with the technical support of FAO’s multidisciplinary team in the Regional Office for the Near East and North Africa. FAO is working closely with MAAR to ensure that the project goal is achieved through proper technical expertise, monitoring and capacity building. The implementation will be further supported by:• A central coordination committee that includes representatives from FAO, relevant MAAR/MoWR directorates, community-based organizations and implementing partners. The committee's role is to ensure facilitation of the project implementation and application of the criteria for beneficiaries' selection• Local team(s), at village/district level, led by FAO and composed of local community representatives (usually the traditional leader or “Moukhtar”), extension workers and implementing partners, respectively will be in charge of selecting project beneficiaries in line with FAO established set of criteria that take into account gender and age sensitive considerations.• FAO Resilience Officers in the targeted governorates will operationally and technically supervise inputs distribution, training and monitoring of the project.The proposed actions will benefit from existing capacity, which can be scaled up at short notice whenever required. Below are activities that will be implemented per output. Activities of output 1 1) Conduct field missions with the MoWR, GCWR, and MAAR to the main accessible irrigation projects in the targeted Governorates and assess the irrigation infrastructure damages and needs. 2) Agree with counterparts on a priority lists for urgent interventions (rehabilitation of critical irrigation infrastructures) and introduce efficient irrigation systems3) Development of detailed beneficiaries selection criteria in consultation with key partners and stakeholders; participatory identification of the most needy and vulnerable groups taking into consideration gender and age concerns;4) Formalize and finalize agreements with pre-selected implementing partners. 5) Rehabilitate the prioritized irrigation infrastructures.6) Monitor and evaluate the project activities in a timely and effective manner Output 2. Capacity of technical staff and relevant stakeholder’s strengthened Activities:1) Prepare training programme and capacity building materials.2) Select beneficiaries base on developed criteria giving priorities to women households and youth.3) Conduct a short training programme on good agriculture practices.4) Conduct a short field visit training: Practical field training on project relevant features including: Practical support of installation and operation of drip irrigation systems and solar panels and practical support for operating and maintaining of small-scale irrigation systems While such activities will benefit the entire population, they will focus locally on the gender and age based approach by recognizing different roles of women, men, boys and girls. All the activities under each outputs will address specific needs of different gender and age groups.

Protection Risk Analysis

1) Risk: Beneficiaries being targeted as a result of being selected for project/risk of community tension arising from beneficiaries selection: Mitigation measures: Inclusion of different categories of affected population examples IDPs and host communities; returnees and refugees; men, women, boys, girls and youth base on their specific needs and clear selection criteria agreed upon by local committees.2) Risk: Inclusion and exclusion errors; Mitigation measures: establishing local beneficiaries selection committees with equitable representation of vulnerable groups; putting in place a clear and precise guidance on beneficiary selection criteria and processes; ensuring an effective communication for guidance in local language.3) Risk of diversion of inputs/assistance: Mitigation measures: establish a community base beneficiaries selection and community based monitoring.The design of the project has taken into account protection elements, in line with the do not harm principles including GBV and PSEA risk mitigation measures. Measures will be taken to limit the risk of beneficiaries being targeted because of the being selected for the project. Inputs and training will take account of the differential requirements of women and men, boys and girls. Returnees will also be targeted so that re-settlement will be aided. Where IDPs are selected, representatives from the host communities will be included as well, to limit possible friction between the two. The implementation of field activities will be in line with the principles and guidelines on protection developed by the Protection Cluster. An accountability to affected population including beneficiaries’ participation and appropriate information and feedback/complaints mechanism will also be put in place to provide communities with pathways to express their concerns and grievance. This will be used to make adjustments and improvement on the implementation of activities. Special attention will be provided to the returnees to help them in their settlement and resuming their production activities. To protect natural resources, rural women and men must be empowered to participate in decisions that affect their needs and vulnerabilities. Within this project, FAO will aim at creating environments that assure gender equity including equal training for men and women on irrigation and related practices; equal opportunities to access water for irrigation, to information, to land, to irrigation technologies; equal participation in community (water use associations) and household decision making about water use and allocation and equal workload to water for irrigation distribution.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks and assumptions beyond project control and their mitigation measures:1. Risks: Deterioration of the security situation precluding access to areas of implementation; Probability: Likely; Impact: Severe; Mitigation measures: Preventive: continue to gather information from range of sources; Advocate with RC/HC and others to regain access. Develop contingency plans in coordination with service providers and, if necessary, move activities to alternative locations.2. Risks: Major displacements/return that could put more pressure on livelihood strategies and/or limit the impact of activities; Probability: possible; Impact: high; Mitigation measures: Continue with advocacy in order to scale up programmes to cover the additional requirements to promote livelihoods opportunities. Advocacy with other organizations to include new arrival in assistance programmes.3. Risks: Changes in the conditions related to cross border movements and operations; Probability: Unlikely; Impact: Moderate; Mitigation measures: Ensure that service providers (SPs) have capacities to deliver programme within the changed legal framework. Diversify activities complementing international procurement with local procurement and ensure different delivery modalities (scaling up cross lines to balance reduced cross borders).4. Risk: Limitations on partnership agreements; Probability: Unlikely; Impact: major; Mitigation measures: Continuous advocacy and negotiations with Government and counterparts for partners to have access to targeted areas. Establish agreements with counterparts to facilitate the implementation of project activities. 5. Risk: Deterioration of the relations between IDPs and host communities in the period/area concerned; Probability: Unlikely; Impact: Insignificant; Mitigation measures: Assistance is balanced and not directed only to one specific group. Identification of beneficiaries is done in partnership with local communities including IDPs and host communities. Selected IPs that are accepted by local communities and aware of conflict sensitivity.6. Risks: Increased control of land and water by party in crisis; Probability: Likely; Impact: Moderate; Mitigation measures: Preventive: continue to gather information from a range of sources. Accurate choice of target areas ensuring that farming is possible and beneficiaries will undertake the activity. Advocacy with local authorities by SPs. If necessary, move to alternative locations previously identified7. Risks: Possible climatic shocks during the implementation period (drought/floods in particular). Probability: Possible; Impact: Moderate; Mitigation measures: Select project areas in different agro ecological zones and in irrigated areas to reduce risks.
