Project Summary

The proposed response aligns with HRP 2023 SO 3 and responds directly to the FSS Cluster Objectives 1, 2 & 3. It seeks to reduce suffering, maintain human dignity, and save lives among vulnerable and marginalized farmers and herders in Area C of the WB by improving their access to protection and vital agricultural inputs. The project aims to improve the resilience of these highly vulnerable households by strengthening their access to agricultural livelihoods , therefore improving their food security. The proposed project, interventions, and target locations have been coordinated with the MoA and with all the agricultural directorates in the governorates. Through this response, MA’AN will target vulnerable farmers, herders, and livestock bearers in 52 communities from 5 governorates (Jenin, Tubas, Qalqilia, Bethlehem, & Hebron). Particular focus will be put on prioritizing the provision of tailored assistance for female-headed
HHs and HHs with PWDs.
Overall, the project will benefit 2491 vulnerable farming HHs thus directly benefiting 11,225 individuals (2804 men, 2636 women, 2749 boys, 2636 girls, 190 elder men, 210 elder women - including 191 PWDs). The intervention will achieve the aforementioned through four main components:
1) Rehabilitation and provision of productive assets and inputs for farmers and herders For HH level assistance through both service delivery and CFW modality of labor.
2)Increased access to Agricultural Resources through Rehabilitation of community agricultural infrastructure 3) Building the capacity of farmers and herders on crop management and on livestock rearing (through the MoA ). This component will also include tailored trainings for women, covering topics of marketing, packaging, and sales food processing 4) Building the Capacity of community members to deal and
mitigate with protection risk (Gender, Safeguarding which includes PSEA and protection), & advocacy.
Overall, the project will achieve the following: • Rehabilitation of 540 dunums of lands, 70 Green Houses,15 km of agricultural roads, 1 water wells, 12 km of water carrier lines, 60 livestock sheds, Rehabilitation/ provision of 60 water storage • Provision of diary production equipment for 20 HHs • distribution of feed crops seeds for 300 HHs • Conduct technical trainings on Crop management and livestock health management to build technical capacities of vulnerable agricultural HHs to deal with natural and manmade
shocks to their livelihoods through the MoA Directorates technical staff. • Conduct technical trainings for females on farming management, quality control, market requirements, marketing and packaging to enhance their knowledge of the market through the MoA • Conduct technical trainings to enhance women households’ knowledge in food processing and conservation, through the MoA. • Supporting 831 needy and vulnerable HHs where farmers targeted for rehabilitation works will be prioritized for CFW with payments being provided at the completion of activity milestones. • Provide awareness and capacity building sessions to the established Community Based Project Support Committees (CBPSCs) on PSEA including gender, protection, and safeguarding. The proposed project has been coordinated with all relevant bodies (MoA, DoAs and FSS cluster). MA’AN has designed the above intervention to complement the current and foreseen work of partners and other FS aid actors. The project ensures protection mainstreaming as MA’AN is guided by principles of safety, dignity, privacy, meaningful access, participation and accountability in all stages of the project.

1 - Needs

According to HNO 2023 & MSNA, a coercive environment has led to unstable livelihoods in the WB. Farmers and herders living in Area C land are constantly facing various threats. Up to September 2022, more than 220 agriculture structures were demolished. The target of demolitions/confiscation includes food commodities, animal sheds, water tanks and reservoirs, and irrigation systems. Farmers and herders also face Israeli settlers' violent practices. These attacks result in injuries and casualties, or damages to property, including vandalizing trees, crops, and other agricultural properties. Food security in Palestine continues to be affected by prolonged conflict, economic stagnation, restrictions on trade and access to resources, and high unemployment and poverty rates. An estimated 1.8 million individuals (33.6% of the population) suffer from moderate to severe food insecurity. 23% of those in need of humanitarian assistance are in the WB (353k people). People with a female-headed HH are found to be more in need of assistance. In September-October 2022 MA’AN conducted an overall needs assessment as part of the design of its Humanitarian Strategy, within which the present proposal is framed. The needs assessment includes consultations with variant stakeholders including the MoA and its directorates. MA’AN also collected data from conducted focus groups with the local communities and CBOs. The needs assessment included interviews, meetings, field visits and focus groups with female & male farmers and herders. MA’AN’s field assessments identified 60 vulnerable communities where farmers and their households are in need of emergency support to improve resiliency, agricultural based livelihoods & protection. The assessment revealed that the needs of vulnerable farming households in WB remain vast, and disproportionately addressed by aid actors. Qualitative findings confirm the main cause of food insecurity in the WB is limited economic access to food, which is driven by unemployment, poverty, and the adverse socio-economic effects of COVID-19. The restraints in sustenance of livelihoods and needs include: Constrained productive capacities due to damages of vulnerable farmers agricultural assets resulting from settler violence & extreme weather conditions; Subjection of lands nearby settlements to stop work orders, movement restrictions & land confiscations; Shortage of water for agricultural purposes and lack of networks; Lack of farmers’ access to agricultural lands due to inadequate agricultural roads; Further the COVID 19 pandemic caused significant increase in food and inputs prices; Poultry and cattle production unfeasible with consequences on vulnerable breeders; Poor knowledge of the market and deficiency in market information and requirements especially for women in Bedouin communities who are in charge of certain processes. Assessment also revealed that while female-owned agricultural holdings only comprise 7.2% of overall holdings, females contribute to more than 60% of the working force in agriculture. Women are the main caretakers of the cattle, they are responsible for feeding, milking, and in many cases herding and face threats by Israeli forces, and settler violence. Women herders interviewed in target locations expressed how animal shelters have poor ventilation in the summer months, and muddy unhygienic dirt floors in the rainy season. These conditions present clear health and personal risks to women, who are generally the caretakers of cattle, as well as the cattle themselves. Assessed shelters provide no protection from snakes, insects, & wild animals. According to women herders, having better floors in the shelters would alleviate their suffering & save them 2-3 hours per day. Also, women farmers and young women expressed how they face challenges in accessing water, they are often the ones tasked with fetching water. There are 29 communities in Hebron governorates mostly impacted by settlers’ violence & there is an urgent need for seeds crops distribution as a humanitarian assistance to support these communities. Also, there is need for support to farmers for rehabilitation of 540 dunums belonging to 236 HHs (1156 persons: 289 men, 272 women, 283 boys, 272 girls, 40 elderly – including 20 PWDs) in 20 communities in Hebron governorate & 4 in Qalqilya. There’s a need for rehabilitation of 70 greenhouses belonging to 70 HHs (86 men, 81 women, 84 boys, 81 girls, 12 elderly – including 6 PWDs) within 5 communities in Qalqilya. Farmer HHs (480 persons: 588 men, 553 women, 576 boys, 553 girls, 82 elders – including 40 PWDs) in vulnerable communities (3 communities in Jenin and 3 in Qalqilya) could greatly benefit from water carrier lines rehabilitation for agricultural purposes to bypass reduced access to water. It was prevalent that mainly Eastern slopes Bedouin farmers relying on livestock as main source of livelihood need urgent assistance with rehabilitation of sheds, food processing equipment, the assessment identified 60 farming HHs (294 persons: 74 men, 69 women, 72 boys, 69 girls, 11 elders – including 5 PWDs) from Bethlehem, Hebron eastern slopes & Tubas in need of assistance to enhance living conditions of livestock and technical know-how on livestock management. Further consultations with MoA directorates & farmers revealed the need for rehabilitation of 15 km of agricultural roads (13 in Hebron & 2 in Qalqilya) to enhance access of vulnerable farmers to their lands. While this project does not address access to formal economic activities for women, its design has recognized the division of labor that exists within the targeted communities & aims to address the different needs of both men & women in terms of supporting livelihood activities & coping with prolonged threats to their livelihoods. A gender assessment conducted at the outset of the project will identify the risks & opportunities for gender responsive programming & monitoring in the target areas.

5.2 - Please briefly explain why the specific modality/ies for this response were chosen.

The project will be implemented through a combination of response modalities for maximum efficiency of implementation with equal assistance to target groups. Service delivery: Serves to accommodate the varying capacities of target HHs to conduct works within needed quality. Serves to accommodate the lack of heavy machinery in the targeted communities. Cash: - the CFW modality serves as an optimum approach for ensuring mitigation of risks during performance of works, serves as an assistance for maintenance of livelihoods, provide ownership of beneficiaries over work while also maximization of fund utilization with quality of work. As well as it is employing the equal access principle of protection and taking in consideration preserving the dignity of targeted women by tailoring the activity in accordance to what is most suitable for them in terms of timing, form, location, method of payment and consequence household and child care burdens. Existing farming HHs that rely on working within their existing agricultural assets as a main source of livelihood. Hence, paying external laborers to conduct the rehabilitation works is redundant as the beneficiaries are already skilled laborers in their own lands, and would further be apprehensive to allow external laborers to perform works in their lands that they can perform needed works in. In this case, the CFW modality is best suited for works and further increases HH and community ownership of project interventions when customized to their needs. In Kind (for crops & dairy production equipment distribution): to ensure quality and unified specifications and assistance to groups of farmers with similar needs. This further ensures obtaining reduced prices as MA’AN will tender bulks.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

The proposed intervention ensures protection mainstreaming as MA’AN is guided by protection principles, considering safety, dignity, privacy, meaningful access, participation &accountability in all stages of the project. Addressing the needs of vulnerable farmers, herders, and their HHs, involving community members as leaders throughout the project’s implementation, the proposed intervention seeks to minimize implementation risk & enforce protection mainstreaming. Since, some communities targeted by the project are risky locations (surrounded by settlements or under ICA threat of demolition), MA’AN adopts key principles that serve to reduce unintended negative impact of the project: CBPSCs will be involved throughout every phase of project implementation; the security situation is monitored closely on an ongoing basis by MA’AN to ensure timely advice & relevant security precautions can be implemented; MA’ANs local coordinators & continuous presence in the communities along with the CBPSCs members will report regularly on any risks to safety & security during implementation to mitigate time & effectiveness impact; to ensure safety & security of HH’s & beneficiaries during implementation & in line with the “Do No Harm” principles, MA’AN ensures that infrastructure work in all its forms in Area C do not affect the arial view of the community to reduce chances of future demolition orders; MA’AN will ensure that the assistance is provided in times where control is limited (at night, during weekends) in coordinated times with the CBPSCs and beneficiaries; during provision of materials, MA’AN ensures all material provided are hidden under existing structures to avoid confiscation. The CFW modality serves as an optimum approach for ensuring mitigation of risks during performance of works & provides ownership of beneficiaries over work. It employs the equal access principle of protection and takes in consideration preserving the dignity of targeted women by tailoring the activity in accordance with what is most suitable for them in terms of timing, form, location, method of payment and consequence household and childcare burdens. The implementation team will ensure all planned rehabilitation work by the beneficiaries through CFW and project is completed with project closeout. In instances where HHs are unable to effectively conduct the CFW rehabilitation, MA’ANs technical team will directly supervise the rehabilitation work for disadvantaged households (female-headed households, PWDs).For each phase of the proposed intervention, MA’AN will conduct risks analysis aiming at identifying and mitigating possible risks including PSEA associated with the action. The risks identification will be done through assessment and community consultations. Furthermore, MA’AN employs female MEAL staff as focal points for the CFRM which also handles PSEA. CFRM info along with PSEA networks will be present on all project outreach materials. The program activities include gender and safeguarding including PSEA trainings for community members for clear PSEA messaging and awareness. On institutional level MA’AN staff are required to be trained on PSEA policy, Staff code of Conduct and Child protection policy. Furthermore, MA’AN staff follow strict health, hygiene, & safety measures (that were adopted during the spread of COVID-19) in order to mitigate the risk of harm being done to targeted beneficiaries and community members.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

The intervention is designed to promote and help protect and the rights of people who have historically been marginalized or discriminated against, such as women, children, the elderly and PWDs practicing agriculture and herding in communities in area C. For target HHs, the selection criteria is designed to be transparent and ensure equal and more importantly equitable access to aid. The criteria will focus on the socio-economic conditions of the households as well as on the vulnerability of the land and rehabilitation needs and the gender makeup of the household (& presence of PWDs). As women are responsible for the household in Palestinian society, the number of females in the HH will also be an important part of the application process. The needs of both genders are addressed equally. The entire family, regardless of sex, age, and disabilities will equally benefit from the intervention. As a positive measure towards women, the project team will take all necessary measures to assure equal access of women farmers to benefit from this project. These measures will include but are not limited to ensuring women participation in the CBPSCs, project orientation session, and monitoring activities. All beneficiaries will be treated with dignity and respect. Considerations to the needs of the elderly and PWDs are taken and will ensure that special arrangements are made to rehabilitate or construct green houses and sheds to them. CBPSCs will be formulated to assist in the project implementation and to ensure accountability and transparency with beneficiaries as it is part of MA'ANs community integration and ownership approach to community development. The CBPSCs will consist of members of the targeted communities, Municipality representatives, representative from the DoA and representatives from local CBOs and the project team. Women and people with disabilities will be encouraged to be part of the committee. The committee will assist in the beneficiary selection according to the set criteria and provide information to the project management as needed on any special cases or circumstances. CBPSCs will supervise the public announcement of the project among the inhabitants of the targeted communities. CBPSCs and beneficiaries will be encouraged to provide feedback about the project. All suggestions and complaints will be taken seriously and needed action will be taken accordingly. It is important to note that MA’AN adheres to the codes of conduct and standards that have been developed by the international humanitarian community. MA’AN has set standards of behavior in humanitarian settings seeking to maintain the high standards of independence, effectiveness, and impact. MA’AN recognizes its obligation to provide humanitarian assistance wherever it is needed. We strive to alleviate human suffering amongst those least able to withstand the stress caused by disaster. We base the provision of relief aid upon a thorough assessment of the needs of the disaster victims and the local capacities already in place to meet those needs. We endeavor to respect the culture, structures and customs of the communities we are working in. We do not tie the promise, delivery or distribution of assistance to the embracing or acceptance of a particular political or religious creed. Additionally, in order to protect our independence, we seek to avoid dependence upon a single funding source. We place a high priority on the proper co-ordination of our emergency responses, and we make sure to engage and build local capacities while doing so. Intended beneficiaries are always involved in the design, management and implementation of the assistance we provide. We also endeavor to minimize the negative impact of humanitarian assistance, seeking to avoid long-term beneficiary dependence upon external aid. Most importantly, we hold ourselves accountable to both those we seek to assist and those from whom we accept resources.
