Project Summary

The humanitarian crisis in South Sudan continues: 4.43 million people are displaced, including 1.96 million IDPs (OCHA Humanitarian Snapshot Sept.’18), with up to 85% estimated to be women and children (HNO 2018). This is exacerbated by continued economic decline, with inflation up to 183% year on year, as well as severe food insecurity over already more than 5 consecutive years.
In this context, in line with South Sudan’s HRP objectives and, more specifically, the objectives as they are outlined by the Protection Cluster and Child Protection Cluster for South Sudan for 2019, Mercy Corps proposes an intervention that aims to provide life-saving assistance to the most vulnerable population to address their protection needs, as well as to prevent, mitigate and respond to their protection risks and strengthen their resilience.
More specifically, Mercy Corps proposes a 11-months project that aims to empower and strengthen the resilience of the most vulnerable adolescent boys and girls – which will include supporting the socio-economic reintegration of CAAFAG (Children associated with armed groups and armed forces) and GBV (Gender-based violence) risk mitigation/prevention activities – in Koch County, Unity State. This will be achieved through psychosocial support, economic skills building, community engagement and GBV risk mitigation/prevention activities. In particular, the intervention will address factors associated with voluntary and forced participation of children and adolescents in armed forces and armed groups, especially the social alienation of this age group and the lack of livelihood and educational opportunities. The ultimate aim is that adolescent boys and girls will safely transition to adulthood, be a full member of the society and have lasting positive multiplier effects on their families, communities and social and economic structures.
Project activities will be based on four components:
1. Providing quality and age- and gender-appropriate psychosocial support (PSS) and skills building activities to adolescent boys and girls in safe spaces
2. Strengthening the skills of adolescent boys and girls to seek healthy economic and livelihood opportunities
3. Enhancing awareness and understanding amongst parents/caregivers, gatekeepers and community members to facilitate an integrating, enabling, peaceful and protective environment for adolescents
4. Enhancing the safety and well-being of vulnerable adolescent girls through GBV risk mitigation and prevention
Through this, the project aims to target a total of 17,035 people (5,630 boys, 5,455 girls, 2,975 women, 2,975 men).

Needs

The crisis in South Sudan is alarming: 4.43 million displaced people, incl. 1.96 million IDPs, and up to 85% are estimated to be women and children. This is exacerbated by economic decline, inflation up to 183% year on year, and severe food insecurity over more than 5 years. Unity State has been strongly affected. Bentiu has become the largest PoC site, hosting about 115,020 IDPs. At least 65% are children. While locations outside the PoC have increasingly become stable and people started going back, the situation remains volatile, particularly in Koch County that was characterised by violent conflict in 2018. Yet, humanitarians started returning to provide life-saving assistance to the affected population. In all locations, the conflict has had severe negative impacts on the population’s access to basic services, incl. livelihoods, economic infrastructure, health and education services. Adolescent boys and girls (10 -19 years) have particularly suffered the devastating impact of this crisis in Unity State:• Lack of protection and support structures: Family and community structures that would normally support adolescents have broken down. With displacement and the disappearance of protective structures, they are at an especially high risk of violence, exploitation, abuse and neglect, and often required to take on greater roles and responsibilities in the absence of family members. Many have become marginalized and economically isolated. • Psychosocial well-being: After witnessing killings, the destruction of homes, and loosing family and friends, adolescents’ psychosocial well-being has seriously been affected. Difficulties in accessing food, water, education, recreation etc. further contribute to their stress. Negative coping mechanisms have increasingly been observed amongst those displaced and who have been exposed to stressful situations for several years. UNICEF estimates that about 1 million children are in need of psychosocial support in South Sudan - a devastating reality that cannot be ignored for adolescents in Unity State.• CAAFAG: Over 17,000 children are reportedly associated with armed forces and groups in South Sudan (CAAFAG-WG in SSD CPSC Strategy 17/18). In Unity State, the recruitment of children and adolescents, notably boys (as to what is known) in armed groups and forces is also widely known. They may be either forced or voluntarily join in the hope for food, money etc. Some are coming back, be it in the search for refuge and/or support (economic, educational, social etc). Yet, they often have particular problems to fully reintegrate back into the family and community and may be alienated from daily life. Employment and training opportunities to generate an own income are rare, which has already lead children and adolescents going back to armed groups and forces. • GBV against women and girls is pervasive–the 3,585 cases reported in the national GBVIMS in 2017 are only a fraction. Adolescent girls often suffer from targeted harm in particular due to their age. Sexual violence may be the most destructive type of GBV they face, being at a particularly high risk when carrying out most basic survival acts (e.g. venturing to collect firewood). Economic desperation has resulted in an increasing number of families seeking to ease hardship by forcing their daughters to marry. Adolescent girls are also more disadvantaged in accessing education (due to marriage, increased care role etc): In 2017, only 36% enrolled in schools were girls. Adolescents are often taken together with children, youth (18+) or adults – yet their needs are different. Even adolescents’ needs vary from each other; yet humanitarian actions addressing their particular needs are rare. In fact, at present, no static, active child protection actor is present in Koch County that addresses the particular needs of adolescent boys and girls. Thus, MC proposes a project aiming to empower and strengthen adolescents’ resilience in Koch County.

Activities

1. Provide quality age- and gender-appropriate PSS and skills building activities to adolescents in safe spaces• 1 Adolescent&Youth Center will be established in Koch County, where adolescents can have a safe space to meet, interact and participate in structured, gender- and age-appropriate activities • Structured, gender- and age-specific recreational and creative activities will be organized in this Center (games, arts, drama, etc) to help adolescents build and strengthen social relationships with peers, reduce stress, have fun, and explore new skills. • Life skills trainings will be organized for 130 adolescents over several months. Topics will focus on teamwork, resilience, peace and conflict, responsibility etc. Sessions will be interactive, using sports as one of the approaches to help enhance learning. • Help Desk: Recognizing that adolescents are undergoing a difficult period in their life in a crisis context, and that a smaller percentage might face particular emotional, social and other types of problems requiring more focused support interventions, a help desk will be operated in the Adolescents&Youth Center by well-trained case workers, offering counseling and referral services to specialised services (e.g. for FTR services, specialised GBV actors) • Peer-to-peer engagement: Adolescent volunteer groups will organize outreach (sports etc) and awareness-raising activities on topics relevant to them (e.g.CP, peace building, GBV) for their peers in nearby areas. This will not only help reach those unable to come to the Center, but also build their leadership skills and self-confidence. 2. Strengthen adolescents’ skills to seek healthy economic and livelihood opportunities• Vocational trainings (e.g baking, hairdressing) will be offered to 75 adolescents. Training types will be identified by themselves and cross-checked with the current local market situation.• Business Skills and Financial Management (BSFM) Trainings will be offered to 100 adolescents• Small business start-up grants/kits: Based on vulnerability criteria, age and successful completion of vocational or BSFM Trainings, 120 older adolescents will receive a small business grant/kit to start their own small business. This will be accompanied by economic preparation and follow-up workshops. 3. Enhance awareness and understanding amongst parents/caregivers, gatekeepers, community members to facilitate an integrating, enabling, peaceful and protective environment for adolescents• Parents/caregivers, gatekeepers, community leaders will be engaged in trainings, meetings and consultations to help facilitate an integrating, enabling, peaceful and protective environment for adolescents. Topics will include the importance of meaningfully engaging adolescents as an alternative to violence and joining armed groups, advice sessions on problems parents might be facing with adolescents, good parenting techniques etc• Community awareness-raising activities will be organized on topics related child rights, the importance of protecting adolescents in conflict, the prevention and reintegration of CAAFAG, GBV, referral pathways, available services etc. through campaigns, theatre, local radio etc 4. Enhance adolescent girls’ safety and wellbeing through GBV risk mitigation and prevention • Community awareness-raising activities related to GBV risk mitigation, prevention and response strategies will be organized to help reduce harmful practices against adolescent girls and facilitate changes in attitudes and behaviours, through campaigns, theatre, local radio etc Overall, MC will coordinate and collaborate with actors working in CP,GBV,FSL etc relevant for the project to avoid potential overlapping and find solutions how interventions can complement each other (e.g allocate areas, for referrals). MC will actively participate in all relevant coordination and cluster meetings (e.g. CPSC,GBV-SC, community meetings) at local&national level to strengthen coordination

Consultations with affected people

Yes

Provide breakdown by women, men, boys and girls

Consultations with affected people have been held to inform the project design. This has been done in the framework of MC’s ongoing projects implemented not only in Child Protection (CP), but also in other sectors (WASH, FSL etc.) in Unity State. More specifically, since 2018, MC implements a CP Project focusing on enhancing youth and adolescent resilience through socio-economic strengthening – including CAAFAG reintegration and GBV risk mitigation – in Bentiu PoC, Rubkona and Bentiu, with UNICEF financial support. This includes PSS, early economic recovery, community engagement and GBV risk mitigation / prevention. As part of those activities, beneficiaries provide ongoing feedback and recommendations on needs and potential future activities. In this regard, adolescent boys, girls, women, men and the broader community have continuously provided vital insights to the design of the proposed project. This consultation mechanism was further formalised through the base- and end line surveys, and post-distribution monitoring (PDM) activities recently conducted as part of this project. In numeric terms, it is estimated that 66 people (24 adolescent boys, 22 adolescent girls, 11 men, 9 women) gave concrete inputs that informed the project design. In addition, since 2016, MC has an office in Koch Country where it implements WASH activities. Through this, MC has gained first-hand experiences and a profound understanding of the target area, built a relationship with the affected population and gathered important insights regarding the particular needs of the affected population and their ideas and views for potential humanitarian interventions – all of which has significantly contributed to the design of the proposed project. Further important sources for the project design have been community leaders’ meetings, humanitarian coordination and cluster consultations and meetings where MC is an active member, as well as community consultations and assessments undertaken by other partners.

Accountability to affected population
Does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

Yes

How does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

In line with the Humanitarian Standard on Quality and Accountability (CHS), MC will ensure that the affected population and other stakeholders have access to timely, relevant and clear information about the project, organisation, their rights, entitlements, and when and where to access the services offered. People have the right to accurate and updated information about actions taken on their behalf. The information may also reduce potential anxiety and is an essential foundation of community responsibility and ownership of the project. Furthermore, MC will inform beneficiaries how to provide feedback on problems and achievements with the services rendered and support them to monitor them jointly. For this, MC will leverage from its already established CARM (Community Accountability and Response Mechanism) that compares well with other accountability frameworks e.g. the CHS. All those information will be communicated in various ways to beneficiaries: At the start, the intervention, together with all other relevant information as outlined above, will be formally introduced to the targeted population, and relevant ministries, local authorities, community leaders, members etc. In the course of the project, these information will continuously be shared. For ex., this may be done in the Adolescents & Youth Centre, when preparing and organising trainings etc. Depending of the audience, the information will be shared in the appropriate way and language they understand (e.g. local language, in written or oral format, in pictures) In so doing, MC will leverage from its reputation it has already: MC opened its South Sudan Country Office in 1995 focusing on services for children and youth, peace-building/conflict resolution, early economic recovery and, since 2005, MC has operated in Unity State. Through this, it has been become and is perceived as an important and reliable humanitarian actor, receiving widespread acceptance and appreciation by communities in Unity State.

Are messages context-specific or/and tailored?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Messages communicated as part of the project will be context-specific and tailored for gender and age groups. For example: • Messages disseminated as part of all activities (trainings, meetings, consultations etc.) will be tailored to the particular needs of adolescent girls and boys, women and men, etc. For instance, while “good parenting techniques” and “advice session” might be particularly relevant for parents, adolescent girls may be particularly interested in learning more about menstrual hygiene management (MHM) and adolescent boys on how to deal with peer pressure. • Messages related to child protection, peace-building, GBV, available services etc. - that will be communicated as part of the various trainings, meetings and community awareness sessions.- will be based on the needs and realities on the ground and the particular context. • Messages and information provided through the Helpdesk – which will be operated by well-trained case workers - will be based on the specific needs of each individual and take into account the local context so that they are able to make themselves informed decisions. Furthermore, depending of the audience, the messages will be shared in the appropriate way and in the language the audience will understand (e.g. local language, in written and/or oral format, through pictures, through the theatre).

Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse

Yes

Briefly describe how

Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA) is of crucial importance to MC. In March 2018, MC undertook a global Gender, Diversity and Inclusion survey to assess and strengthen its culture, policies and procedures related to gender diversity, inclusion and sexual misconduct in any form. Based on its results, MC’s goal and commitment is to generate a multi-year action plan and further continue strengthening and improving existing policies and procedures to promote an inclusive and safe workplace and programs and –most importantly- prevent sexual exploitation and abuse - including those on whistleblowing and hotline complaints, anti-sexual harassment, anti-trafficking and child safeguarding - all of which take a zero tolerance approach to sexual misconduct in any form. MC’s goal and commitment is to ensure that these stronger policies are effective, relevant, go beyond industry standards and donor requirements, and take emerging best practices into account. Over the past 2 years, MC reinforced its global Integrity Hotline (that can be accessed in various ways, e.g. email, and is posted all over) and its response to complaints. MC has a dedicated, independent Ethics Team staffed by trained, professional investigators to monitor the hotline and oversee investigations. An in-house team works to identify options for stronger processes and procedures for vetting new team members such as background-checking mechanisms, safeguarding-screening questions and improved selection processes. Another team is working to find legal ways to ensure anyone terminated for sexual misconduct at MC cannot find work in the NGO sector again. In addition to this, MC will ensure that staff on the ground will be specifically trained on PSEA. Messages on PSEA will be posted in offices and public spaces (e.g. in the youth centre) to ensure that communities will receive the information. Information on PSEA will also be shared and discussed during meetings with beneficiaries.

Feedback /complaint mechanisms?

Yes

Briefly describe the mechanisms

In addition to intensive community engagement, Mercy Corps uses feedback mechanisms in adapting its response accordingly to ensure that the needs of the most vulnerable are met and continually ensure their risks are minimized. One of the tools used to support this quality of programming is the Community Accountability Response Mechanism (CARM). CARM is utilized across Mercy Corps programming globally to report achievements, problems and involve community in problem solving and ensure feedback loops reap the appropriate response through a meaningful and ethical accountability structure and also includes a community complaints mechanism. CARMs work through trained focal point staff who report on feedback and issues and which is embedded into routine program monitoring. In this framework, the following systems will be put in place to ensure feedback and/or complaints are received: • A secure box will be installed in accessible locations (e.g. outside the youth centre) if suitable/possible so that communities and beneficiaries can provide their feedback or lodge their complaints• MC field staff may hold informal gatherings alongside their field activities where community members can confidentially communicate a complaint• Anyone who wishes to make a complaint can directly visit the MC Office• MC may periodically reach out to communities and administer a questionnaire that captures feedback and complaints• If feasible, communities and beneficiaries can call or SMS Mercy Corps to provide feedback or lodge complaints• Etc…

Centrality of Protection in humanitarian action
Protection Risk Analysis?

Yes

Briefly describe how

The project is based on a protection risk analysis and activities will be realised so that they address the specific needs of all various groups, incl. adolescent girls and boys and –most importantly- those with special vulnerabilities and needs. That is: MC recognizes that adolescents have particularly suffered the shocking impacts of the crisis in Unity State and may have distinct needs. Thus, they are the project’s focus. For ex., with displacement and disappearance of protective structures, adolescents are at a particular risk of violence, exploitation, abuse neglect, and taking on greater responsibilities. After witnessing killings, losing family etc, their psychosocial well-being has seriously been affected, which comes at a time when they are already going through a pivotal time in life given the physical, cognitive and emotional changes triggered by the onset of puberty. Teenage years can be a period of heightened sensitivity, social isolation etc, putting them at an even higher risk of psychological distress in a crisis context. MC recognizes that adolescents’ needs are different from those of children, youth (18+) and adults; even adolescents themselves are not a homogenous group. Their life experiences and needs vary, all of which must be taken into account. Adolescent boys and girls also face different protection risks and have thus distinct needs. While adolescent girls are often at risk of GBV, boys may be at risk to join armed groups. Their particular needs and protection requirements must thus be allowed for. Adolescents with special vulnerabilities and needs (particularly CAAFAG, in addition to those in psychosocial distress, child-headed households, survivors or being at risk to physical and/or sexual violence, disabled adolescents etc) are another group with distinct needs. In fact, MC will focus on this group as it recognizes their heightened and particular needs to benefit from the program, and their particular constraints to participate

Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

MC recognises that those most in need are often not able to benefit from the offered services. Barriers to access may be encountered due to logistical, economic and social/cultural reasons, insecurity and a lack of information on available services. The project will intentionally outreach to harder to reach adolescent girls and boys to support them accessing the offered services they might be desperately in need of. The “I’m Here” approach and tools will provide a valuable basis and guidance for this. Regarding adolescents with special needs and vulnerabilities (e.g. CAAFAG), MC will closely collaborate with all relevant stakeholders (e.g. UNICEF) to further decrease potential barriers that those adolescents may have to participate in the program. To help adapt structures and activities for adolescents with disabilities and thus ease their access to the offered services, the Intervention will be guided by the “Washington Group Questions”. Project facilities established (e.g. adolescent &youth center, latrines etc.) will be designed in age- and gender-specific ways to help limit further potential barriers to access offered services. The intervention also recognises that, parents/caregivers and “gatekeepers” (husbands of married adolescent girls, relatives, etc) of adolescents may be resistant to the participation of their children in the project due to various reasons (e.g. cultural norms, household duties). To address this potential barrier, the project will promote the participation of parents, caregivers and gatekeepers by engaging them in various creative activities throughout the project, such as: engagement during the registration process of adolescents in the youth centre, regular consultations and feedback session, advice sessions on problems parents might face with adolescents, etc. Overall, MC will ensure that information on the rendered services will be provided, that services are in safe and easy to reach locations, and are free

Addresses inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

The project addresses inequalities and threats, notably by aiming to: 1. Reduce gender inequalities: • Recognizing that the conflict has increased gender inequality – the root cause of GBV - the project focuses on GBV mitigation and prevention activities. This is reflected in the community awareness sessions organised as part of the activities to help reduce harmful practices against adolescent girls – who are particularly affected by various forms of GBV - and facilitate a change in attitudes and behaviour at the community level. • To further help reduce gender inequalities, the ratio of girls participating in the project will be particularly promoted and sex and age disaggregated data will be regularly collected. • The ratio of female staff and casuals will also be constantly monitored to ensure equal opportunities and promote female employment 2. Prevent, respond and report protection threats: • Peace-building activities to prevent and reduce violence are a cross-cutting component. For ex., life-skills training for adolescents will include topics on peace and conflict; community awareness sessions will include topics to prevent adolescents from joining armed groups; meeting sessions with parents will include topics on the importance to meaningfully engage adolescents as an alternative to violence. • The project will focus on adolescents with particular vulnerabilities and needs and thus, those who might be neglected or marginalised. Through different activities, the project will actively promote and facilitate the inclusion of all adolescents so that no one is left behind. • Through the provision of PSS activities, the project recognises that those who might have been victims of protection threats might have particular needs and require a targeted response.• When identifying and/or observing threats or risks of grave violations of children’s rights, MC will contribute to the MRM and share confidential MRM reports with the UNICEF MRM focal point.

Does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

A variety of monitoring activities will be realised to monitor and measure access of beneficiaries to the offered serviced, such as by monitoring and evaluating the extent to which:• Services are provided at an adequate time when individuals can realistically access them• Services can be accessed by adolescent girls• Services can be accessed by persons with reduced mobility (e.g. persons with physical disabilities) • Services can be accessed by persons with non-mobility-related disabilities (e.g. the blind, hard of hearing, intellectually disabled)• Project indicators are disaggregated by age, gender, location and/or specific group (e.g. persons with disabilities, ethnic minorities) • Some might be able and others might not be able to access the services, as well as what their particular barriers are. The ultimate aim of those M&E exercises is to minimize or eliminate barriers for beneficiaries to access the offered services.

Does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

The safety of beneficiaries is the first concern of the proposed intervention. Prioritizing safety also entails actively seeking to reduce risk and ensure that the project will “do no harm” in its work. In this context, a variety of monitoring activities will be realised to monitor and measure the safety of beneficiaries, such as by monitoring and evaluating the extent to which:• The location of facilities and the routes to them are distant from threats of violence; especially the risk or threat of GBV and attacks from armed groups.• Infrastructure and facilities are adapted so that all individuals and groups can access and use the facilities in safety. The ultimate aim of those M&E exercises is to minimize or eliminate threats to beneficiaries’ safety when accessing offered services.

Does the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

Yes

How the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

Ensuring dignity and that people feel valued and have a sense of self-respect form another important part or the project. As for safety, prioritizing dignity means seeking to ensure that the project will “do not harm” in its work. In this context, a variety of monitoring activities will be realised to monitor and measure the dignity of beneficiaries, such as by monitoring and evaluating the extent to which: • Confidentiality and privacy are respected in all forms of consultation, counselling and personal information sharing. • Identifiable information (e.g. names, addresses, or traits and characteristics about the case that can lead to identification, etc.) are not shared unless consent has been given by the beneficiary • Qualified psychosocial workers are on staff to receive and counsel survivors of violations, especially when dealing with children and/or survivors of GBV.• A variety of staff (male, female, members of minorities, etc.) is present who have first-hand knowledge of gender and cultural sensitivities. The ultimate aim of those M&E exercises is to minimize or eliminate threats to beneficiaries’ dignity when accessing offered services.
