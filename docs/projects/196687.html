Project Summary

The intervention is in line with the framework of the HRP 2022-2023, objectives 1, 2, 3 taking into consideration the health system capacity in the context of COVID-19 and Cholera and complementing the efforts of the Health sector in all locations of intervention.
The huge burden of disease in Syria is due primarily to the fact that the health system is emerging from more than 11 years of crisis characterized by physical and psychological trauma, mental health disorders such as trauma, depression, post-traumatic stress disorders (PTSD) and others trauma and gender-based violence, particularly among displaced persons (WHO Syria Annual report 2016) as well as a wrong lifestyle that determines diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, heart diseases, cancers which remain a major threat, therefore an important proportion of the population is at risk of developing a PTSD; or have suffered lasting disabilities and gender based violence ( GBV).
As reported in the WHO Syria annual report 2020, 53% of the 1,800 fixed primary health care centers monitored in 2020 were reported as fully functioning, while 28% were reported as completely non-functioning. In addition to an extreme lack of appropriate infrastructure, health workers in Syria struggle due to a shortage of essential medicines and working medical equipment, despite continued to support WHO health sector through medical equipment and and medicines shipments. The provision of quality essential health services is also limited due to a shortage in qualified health workers. Indeed, half of medical professionals fled the country over the past decade, resulting in limited access to medical services for child health, nutrition, communicable and non-communicable diseases, sexual and reproductive health, and mental health.
The project seeks to implement life-saving services for the diagnosis, follow up, treatment and covering medical prescriptions of host communities, returnees and IDPs in Rural Damascus ( Kisweh), Al-Hasakeh (Al-Hasakeh city) governorates and Aleppo (Jabel Samaan), not only by establishing new Primary health care centers with three main clinics of Internal medicine, Genecology & Obstetric, Pediatrics and laboratory to provide medical counselling, treatment, Cholera and COVID-19 management and referral if needed in coordination with the implementing partners, but also by supporting physical rehabilitation activities including Physiotherapy/PSS for PwDs through mobile teams and also covering medical treatment of beneficiaries. So unmet needs of the most in need amongst the vulnerable returnees, refugees and IDPs in Rural Damascus, Al-Hasakeh and Aleppo governorates can be served. Also in the context of COVID-19, supporting the primary health care system in areas of implementation – understanding its health facilities capacity and context – will increase the capacity of its secondary and tertiary health care system to response to more complicated medical cases.
The project plans to conduct focused trainings for the staff and volunteers of SARC (Syrian Arab Red Crescent), and MoH to build their capacity, and provide structured trainings on GBV, MHGAP, CMR, case management and referral and disseminate updates on COVID-19 and Cholera shared by Health sector.

3 - Needs

As the Syrian crisis enters its second decade, the health system remains heavily disrupted. Based on HRP 2022-2023 draft, more than 7.97 million people in 100 sub-districts were targeted and living below the critical emergency health standards. With just 58% of hospitals and 53% of HPC fully functional, the COVID-19 pandemic and Cholera have exacerbated weaknesses in the health system. As the outbreak continues, self-reported compliance with preventive measures remains below 50% while laboratory capacity and access to testing are also below recommended levels. Due to cumulative effects of concurrent crises, the cost of health services; lack of medicines, supplies, or health staff; fear of exposure to COVID-19, over-crowding and long wait times as the main self-reported barriers to health access. Among the 12.2 million people in need of health services in 2022-2023, 1.33 million are children Under 5 while 3.38 million are WRA. An additional 300,000 older people are requiring health services, while for cholera between 25 August and 23 September 2022, a total of 5,973 suspected cases have been reported in nine of the 14 governorates in Syria. Most of the cases were reported from, Aleppo (1,336 cases, 22.36%), Ar-Raqqa (753 cases, 12.6%) and Al-Hasakeh (465 cases, 7.78%). This reflects not only the burden on the health system in areas where thousands of IDPs reside, but also the demands posed by patients arriving from all parts of the country

4 - Implementation of activities

The intervention is in line with the framework of the strategic objective 1,2, and 3 of HRP 2022-2023. It is designed to complement the efforts of the Health sector in areas of Implementation by Increasing access to PHC through provision of essential PHC to cover health care needs of vulnerable populations, including RMNCH, and continuity of treatment for NCDs ensuring comprehensive referral pathways for acute and post-acute management phases. Medical referral systems must also address needs of people living with chronic disease who develop complications, emergency obstetric and newborn care (EmONC) and patients with acute illness who cannot access treatment in the area. Implementation will be through implementing partner as follows: Provide essential primary and secondary health care services, EmONC and referral Provision of life-saving services through well equipped unit and well trained staff; Provision of access to PHC services through fixed clinics; Identification of pregnant women and follow up on antenatal, delivery and postnatal care through voucher system; Identification of cases in need of physical rehabilitation and follow up on Physiotherapy and PSS; Strengthen the capacity of health care providers and CHWs to provide essential health services;Training of mobile team staff and CHWs on health topics and identification of epidemic cases. In addition to the required monitoring mechanism for each health project at the program

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

R1: Tension in community for perceived inequality, discrimination, and favouritism, including risk for project staff. M1: Communicate regularly and clearly to the community, including local authorities, about what services are available and for whom they are intended. Avoid the exclusion from assistance of persons with no identity documents by adopting alternative mechanisms for identification while referring the beneficiaries to legal aid service providers. R2: Different vulnerable groups might not have the same access to services and assistance. M2: Coordinate at local level with other protection actors to ensure balanced coverage responding to needs; avoid overlapping or concentration of activities if possible and unless motivated by needs; consider focusing on underserved areas. Ensure consultation with communities that may be at particular risk of exposure and risk of exclusion. Work with national actors and community based groups to ensure sustainability of the intervention. Select staff for all activities (especially outreach volunteers) with adequate knowledge of the community, but also respecting balance in gender, age, social profile. R3: Risk of receiving distorted information due to communities' perceptions of humanitarian staff expectations or to communities' expectations of a type of response. M3: Triangulate information received with other secondary sources of protection related information or information from other sectors (post distribution monitoring).


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

TDH adapts a zero-tolerance policy against sexual exportation and abuse, and to ensure all PSEA standard are respected: • All TDH staff have signed the Code of conduct upon recruitment. • All TDH staff are oriented on PSEA upon recruitment on PSEA topic via induction sessions • TDH has its own internal reporting mechanism on PSEA, and has clear reporting channels that are accessible for all staff • All TDH staff including field staff that is working directly with beneficiary are regularly trained on PSEA, and reporting channels. • Beneficiaries are oriented on PSEA reporting Mechanism, through multiple channels (lines, Posters, email, Focal points). • TDH includes Gender-based violence (GBV) and child protection services in its intervention to support women facing sexual Abuse.


9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

R1: Local partners lose access to the project locations and are unable to implement activities. M1: SARC displayed an outstanding ability to implement humanitarian projects despite constraints and limitations, However, if SARC lost the access to project locations, TDH would either identify other suitable partners or, in coordination with them and with local actors, identify other locations with same high needs, where the programming could continue. R2: bureaucracy and delay in getting needed approvals from local authorities. M2: TDH already obtained approvals to work in the Northern and southern areas of Syria, in addition, TDH will advocate through the local partner to have the needed approvals based on the signed MOU. R3: delay in receiving the fund due to the sanctions on the banking system in Syria. M3: TDH has different banking accounts in Syria with different pipelines to receive fund from different sources. R4: new quarantine by the local authorities due to COVID outbreak or other Pandemic. M4: TdH has on-line policy for approvals and working from home, also, the PHCs will follow COVID measurement protocols , in addition, TdH health Advisor will conduct training to TdH staff and local partner volunteers for COVID measurements during humanitarian response and will distribute PPEs to both beneficiaries and staff.
