Project Summary

The armed clashes that erupted on 15th April 2023 between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) have devastated people and infrastructure throughout Sudan. The conflict that started in Khartoum soon spread to other states – namely those in Darfur, North Kordofan and Marawi in Northern State. The IOM-DTM estimates that approximately 843,130 individuals (168,354 households) have been displaced internally as a result of the conflict. A further 253,591 individuals have fled neighboring countries, notably Egypt, Ethiopia and Chad. A substantial number of people are unable to leave their homes because of continuous fighting and insecure routes.
This a lifesaving multi-sectorial project to contribute to lifesaving of the affected population through responding to immediate WASH, Education and Protection in the targeted localities of West and South Darfur. HH capacity will be strengthened through the improvement of access to different services, as this will include implementation of WASH-IWRM, Education, GBV, Child protection, and general Protection to 75,000 (20,000 women, 15,000 men, 20,000 boys and 20,000 girls), (75,000 (WASH), 23,483 (Protection) and 10,000 (Education). IAS will target host communities, IDPs, and nomads affected by conflict or recurring natural disasters in South Darfur, (Kateila, Beleil, East Jebel Mara, Alradoum, Greida, As Sunta localities) and West Darfur in (Geneina, Sirba, and Jebel Moon localities). IAS will utilize an Integrated Water Resources Management (IWRM) approach. Activities to be implemented with WASH will include the drilling of 20 new boreholes equipped with hand pumps and the construction and of 4 solar-powered Mini and Water Yards, rehabilitation of 4 Water Yards, construction, water quality monitoring targeting 20,000 and rehabilitation of 30 HPs and implementation of sanitation and hygiene to scale up the sanitation and promote the hygiene practices of at least 20,000 in the targeted communities of West and South Darfur through distribution of soap and jerricans, rehabilitation of 6 latrines, construction of 10 HWFs and distribution of 500 hygiene kits and cleaning tools.

1 - Needs

The armed clashes that erupted on 15th April 2023 between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) have had a devastating impact on people and infrastructure throughout Sudan. The conflict that started in Khartoum, soon spread to other states – namely those in Darfur, North Kordofan and Marawi in Northern State. The IOM-DTM estimates that approximately 843,130 individuals (168,354 households) have been displaced internally as a result of the conflict. A further 253,591 individuals have fled neighbouring countries, notably Egypt, Ethiopia and Chad. There remains a substantial number of people who are unable to leave their homes because of the continuous fighting and insecure routes.Nearly 1.4 million people have been forced to flee their homes since 15 April. This includes over 1 million people displaced inside the country by 23 May, according to the International Organization for Migration (IOM)’s Displacement Tracking Matrix. Most of the people internally displaced have taken refuge in West Darfur (24 per cent), White Nile (20 per cent), River Nile (16 per cent) and Northern (14 per cent) states. The majority of internally displaced people fled Khartoum (69 per cent). The number of newly internally displaced people in just over five weeks (15 April to 23 May) is comparable to that recorded for all displacement in the country from 2020 to 2022. In addition, about 345,000 people have crossed into neighbouring countries as of 26 May, according to the UN Refugee Agency (UNHCR). The majority of people who crossed the border went to Egypt (126,000 people) followed by South Sudan (71,000), Chad (60,000), Central African Republic (9,700), and Ethiopia (5,000).In West Darfur, Conflict between nomads and farmers in Jebel Moon locality in early March 2022 has led to the displacement of up to 12,000 people into the Jebel Moon mountains, into neighboring localities, and across the border into Chad, according to locals. These clashes are a continuation of a major conflict that erupted in mid-November 2021 over a land dispute and claimed dozens of lives, loss of property and large displacement.Internally displaced people (IDPs), returnees, individuals stuck in conflict zones, refugees from South Sudan, Ethiopia, and other neighboring countries, poor groups from agro-pastoral and pastoral communities in rural parts of Western, Eastern, and Northern Sudan—whose livelihoods are directly impacted by the impact of lean season and macroeconomic crises—are the groups most affected (HNO 2022).

5.2 - Impact on environment/natural resources

Due to our experience and lessons learned in the multi-year DFID funded IWRMprojects in Darfur and eastern Sudan, IAS uses IWRM principles to enhance theenvironment in the design, implementation, and closing of our WASH humanitarianprojects. For 2022, IAS will be implementing Hafirs using the community-involvedcatchment approach, the assessments wil also be based on catchment whilecoordinating with WES the decisions governing all things related to the intervention,including environmental actions. Rather than continuing to use up existing preciouswater by drilling more boreholes, this approach guides rainwater (90% of which isnormally lost) through the use of sand dams and other subsurface dams to replenishexisting wells. The environmental impact for safe water provision is naturally verypositive in terms of supporting healthier lifestyles, a better quality of lives, andsupporting plants and agricultural projects, etc., but with the use of cement andconstruction materials, great care will be given to cleaning up after each project site;that each water point intervention area will be cleaner than before IAS commenced theproject. Inclusive community/ main actors project design, initiation, and planningmeetings will be the stage for environmental protection and planning advocacy.Beneficiaries will be tasked with developing a long-term environmental plan and willsign-off and be accountable to the authorities on their progress. For every water point,IAS will conduct Environment Impact Assessments (EIAs). Taking the data from thesesurveys, we educate targeted beneficiaries to take the necessary steps to improve theenvironment. Communities will be involved in cleaning up water points and latrineconstruction sites. Fences and shrubs will be set up around the area. Conservation ofnatural resources, especially water, will be central themes in WMC meetingsthroughout the project, including close-out activities, including inspection, ensuringthat the environment activities were carried out and the environment enhancementplan was completed for the future each beneficiary community where IAS isintervening. As well as budgeting for water point maintenance needs, WMCs will berequested to work with the community to budget environmental enhancementactivities. Finally, those receiving environmental advocacy and planning training willbe encouraged to bring their knowledge into the local schools.

6.2 - Explain how your project aims to ensure protection mainstreaming during its design and implementation

IAS will ensure meaningful participation and engagement of affected communities throughout the program. In carrying out community-based protection services, IAS will pay attention to the choice of locations and targeting of beneficiaries to avoid perceptions that assistance is delivered in a discriminatory or non-equitable manner. IAS will maintain transparent, inclusive, and adequate communication with communities on available services, consulting communities in the planning, selecting services and feedback after service provision. Partners will also be guided on ensuring prioritization of services to groups considered particularly vulnerable, such as young children, children with disability boys and girls, children without adult care. Child Protection services and response interventions will continue to be based on a quality and integrated approach, including coordination and capacity building with other humanitarian actors to support Protection mainstreaming and a do-no-harm approach as well as principled response. IAS will build on and integrate, wherever possible, feedback mechanisms to strengthen accountability and support the interagency coordination mechanism to respond to relevant complaints. IAS will continue working closely with partners to monitor beneficiaries' feedback, share information, and conduct community consultations, ensuring adequate accountability to affected populations (AAP). During the implementation of this project, IAS will ensure equal participation of all vulnerable groups, including children with disabilities and other vulnerable populations, in all processes concerning needs identification. During latrines rehabilitation, facilities will have enough lights to ensure girls' and children's safety during usage. In FSL interventions, IAS will apply different standards and best practices in emergency response including SPHERE standards, LEGS, Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR), Natural Resource Management (NRM) and "Do No Harm'' approaches in the implementation of this project activities. The transfer of resources into a resource-scarce environment can trigger conflict in the communities. To ensure that no negative impact should result from this project the following will be undertaken: just distribution of resources, creating/strengthening networks of relationships across divisions, strengthening good governance, the use of participatory processes for decision making, supporting traditional/indigenous mechanisms for conflict resolution and reconciliation, inclusion of diversity of ethnic or religious groups, gender and youth in project activities and leadership structures. Selection of beneficiaries will be performed with due consideration of “Do No Harm” principles, ensuring fair representation of different vulnerable groups regardless of their ethnicity, religion or gender. Criteria for selection of beneficiaries will be socialized with communities and opportunity will be provided to raise concerns or complaints.

7.2 - Please outline how the project or your agency will inform and interact with inter-agency approaches to accountability to affected people and feedback mechanisms, including SEA and abuse complaints?

Safety and dignity for affected people, following the guidance of the Global WASH Cluster and the HCT. The minimum obligations are operationalized in the accountability framework developed by WASH and other sectors and partners, which guides partners to 1) provide the minimum information to the public, 2) involve the community in decision making, 3) learn from feedback and complaints, 4) encourage appropriate staff attitude and behaviors. 5) use information from project learning, and 6) develop programs based on participatory assessments. The WASH cluster will continue to guide partners in adopting the framework by sharing best practices and peer learning.The implementation of the frameworks will be monitored through a self-reporting mechanism, while the clusters will also advise partners on relevant indicators, they can include in their monitoring efforts (including post-distribution monitoring). As part of its community-based response, IAS will engage and involve affected populations among others, establishing community-based protection networks and committees, including IDPs, women, and youth, to foster two- way communication and feedback with the humanitarian community and mobilize capacities toward the protection of all members of the community, particularly the vulnerable. Community protection initiatives also involve the community's participation in raising awareness of crucial protection issues relevant to women, girls, boys, and men to prevent or resolve conflicts and foster peaceful co-existence. IAS will also establish feedback and complaint mechanisms, such as protection hotlines that provide information on services, suggestion boxes in child-friendly spaces, safe spaces for women, CFRM-Complaints Feedback Response Mechanism and regular trends analysis. Other accountability mechanisms include the Accountability and Impact Assessment Framework of the GBV Sub-Cluster, as well as Child Protection Minimum Standards, which supports accountability between partners and meaningful child participation in program implementation. All interventions will include SEA mechanisms.
