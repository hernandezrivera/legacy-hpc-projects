Project Summary

Children in Somalia live in a complex environment with scant protection mechanisms. Recruitment or use of children by armed groups, the Somalia National Army (SNA) and allied militias, and others – places children at great risk. In some areas families feel obliged to send their children to serve in clan militias. Impunity is widespread resulting in grave child rights violations including the arbitrary arrest and detention of children.
Population displacement due to chronic conflict, droughts and famines has resulted in the separation of children from their families, which exposes them to exploitation, violence and abuse children of IDPs and minorities are particularly vulnerable.
Years of armed conflict in Somalia has resulted in widespread contamination of explosive remnants of war (ERW) and landmines, the proliferation of small arms and light weapons and a steady rise in the use of improvised explosive devices (IEDs).
UNCEF will respond to the above needs in the following ways:
• Provision of psychosocial support services through open spaces reaching 120,000 (50,000 boys & 50,000 girls) children and (5000 men and 15000 women) caregivers
• Provide community-based reintegration for 3,000 children formerly associated with armed forces/groups (1,700 boys & 400 girls) and other vulnerable children (600 boys & 300 girls)
• Tracing and reunify unaccompanied and separated children with their families (Boys: 6500 & Girls: 3,500)
• Raise awareness on the threats posed by unexploded remnants of war reaching 45,000 people (17,500 boys; 17,500 girls, 5,000 women & 5,000 men)
• Raise awareness on main CP concerns on child labour, child marriage and prevention of family separation reaching 250,000 people (102,500 boys, 102,500 girls, 25,000 men, women 20,000)

Needs

Grave violations, against children continue in Somalia, with abductions and child recruitment being the main ones affecting the lives of boys and girls below 18 years old. From January to July 2018, the Country Task Force on Monitoring and Reporting (CTFMR) verified and documented. 3,370 cases (2,398 boys and 380 girls) of violence against children with abduction and recruitment/use of children accounting for 46% and 53 % of the overall number of children affected. A total of 1,568 children including 43 girls were recruited by armed forces and groups in various areas compared to 1185 (33 girls) in the same period in 2017. Per the 2019 HNO, family separation is one of the main child protection concerns that children in Somalia face especially during population movement. The phenomenon of forced displacement of children fleeing attempted child recruitment by armed groups has been a major protection concern and a driving factor for family separation. The UNICEF September 2018 sitrep indicates 5,320 (3,005 boys & 2,315 girls) UASC documented by the members of the child protection sub-cluster. Endemic violence, flood and other emergency situations disrupts routine and normal living patterns, preventing normal social activities and deprive numerous children from accessing to schools. Such situations contribute to increased psychological and emotional distress among children.Explosive hazards affect both adults and youth, to various extents. According to the Mine Action (IMSMA), from January 2017 to April 2018, out of 81 recorded casualties caused by ERW, 49 were boys and 16 girls. This indicates that 80% of deaths and injuries caused by ERW were among children and youth. . UNICEF will work jointly with the MA sub-cluster to prioritize provision of risk education as a lifesaving humanitarian intervention. Given the scale of the Somalia crisis, and the high priority to addressing child protection needs, the demand for strengthened coordination in CP AOR continues.

How is the project linked to durable solutions/resilience?

Restoring normalcy in the lives of children includes providing boys and girls with culturally and age appropriate activities, such as sports, play and perceptual, memory and creative games that are structured, safe and stimulating. Such activities develop children’s life skills and coping mechanisms, as well as promoting children’s participation in daily family and community life through cultural or media activities or community service.A community based psychosocial support implies that the interventions seek to build on individual and community resources and capacities and their resilience. On the other hand, using community networks or networks mandated by the states as partners for programme delivery ensure that UNICEF support contributes to sustainable outcomes. Child protection psychosocial interventions will contribute to mental health and the well-being of children affected by the conflict or/and drought. Also, mobilizing child and youth groups and networks can be a key strategy for promoting peer-support. Collective community activities such as cleaning up schools and after-school groups, parent-teacher committees and parent-child groups can promote a sense of togetherness.

Protection risk analysis & Mitigating measures
Preventing risks of exclusion based on displacement

Risk: Children receiving CP services through case management (UASC & CAAFAG) may be construed as belonging to humanitarian agencies and hence loosing community protection and support. Mitigation: Incorporate community participation in decisions affecting children. As well as, incorporate local child friendly solutions in all children’s services.

Preventing risks of exclusion based on gender and age

Risk: Girls in some areas, especially those leaving groups independently, risk not benefiting from reintegration support, simply because they are not known or considered as potential beneficiaries. Mitigation:Raise awareness with communities and civil society to recognise girls, and to work together to change the narrative around perceptions of a ‘child soldier’. In doing so we can ensure that all children are given appropriate, sustained support to improve their reintegration back home.

Mitigating the impact of armed conflict and violence

Risk 1: Increased risk of inter-community tension due to varied assistance services across beneficiaries Mitigation: a) Agree to a minimum assistance package across sectorb) Identify and exclude from variance any items particularly prone to create tensionsRisk 2: Impact of activity is minimal on our population of concern Mitigation:a) Ensure evidence based and targeted advocacy with as clear outcomes envisaged as possible b) Develop outcome-focused advocacy with key deliverables and responsible persons Risk 3: Hostility from some communities that conceive child rights messages as a threatMitigation: Mobilise opinion leaders and provide information on child protection. Provide information on the cultural responsibility of men and women to cater for the growth and development of children
