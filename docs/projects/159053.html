Project Summary

Physicians for Human Rights Israel (PHRI) will contribute to Health and Nutrition Cluster objective 1, as its purpose is to provide quality lifesaving and essential services to the most vulnerable Palestinian communities, while strengthening the local health system though emergency preparedness and other types of trainings in specialties lacking in the Occupied Palestinian Territory (OPT).
Through mobile clinics, volunteer specialist physicians will give direct medical aid to the most vulnerable, provide free medication, and organize referrals for over 200 cases in need to hospitals in East Jerusalem and Israel. Medical teams will travel to Gaza 8 times for two to three days, in coordination with the Palestinian Ministry of Health and local NGOs. PHRI doctors will also carry out 100 surgeries in Gaza, including of patients wounded during March of Return demonstrations. In the West Bank, mobile clinics of doctors will visit villages and areas with limited access to specialized care once a week, conducting health screenings in some of the clinics. In 2020, PHRI doctors will provide direct services to about 12,000 patients, 60% of whom are women and girls who will also benefit from specialized women-only clinics.
For those patients who need treatment unavailable in the OPT and whose Israel-issued exit permits have been denied, and for health staff needing to exit the OPT for training, PHRI will intervene to reverse these denials in 350 cases. PHRI will sometimes use legal action combined with a range of advocacy tools to tackle individual cases and will also resort to strategic litigation to challenge general criteria according to which permits are denied.
While providing medical aid, the intervention also aims to enhance preparedness within the health sector and reinforce the capacity of the Palestinian health system. Health professionals will be trained as per their own priorities, based on academic and practical gaps identified in collaboration with the Ministry of Health. Trainings will take place through hands-on surgical coaching, simulation-based coaching, and conferences. The surgery coaching in the key fields of orthopaedics, vascular and chest surgery (among others) will benefit around 50 Palestinian surgeons and health staff in Gaza. In between visits, PHRI’s surgeons continue to provide advice and support to their colleagues in Gaza via calls and emails. In addition, 20 medical professionals will participate in hands-on training at MSR, the Centre for Medical Simulation at Sheba Medical Centre in Israel. PHRI will also facilitate six large-scale medical conferences in the West Bank and Gaza with over 1,000 participants in total, bringing up-to-date medical knowledge to meet the needs of medical professionals.
The intervention will also address the Health and Nutrition Cluster strategic objective 2, carrying out advocacy to protect the right to health of the most vulnerable. Palestinian prisoners detained in Israel will be supported to overcome barriers to health and access medical treatment. Particular focus will also be given to persons with disabilities and children in PHRI’s advocacy work. Indeed, PHRI found out that over 60% of children in need of treatment outside of the OPT must travel without their parents who are denied permits. The presence of the parent, however, is essential during medical treatment, and compromise on this accompaniment constitutes a violation of his right to health. In 2020, PHRI will carry out a wide range of national and international advocacy activities to tackle this issue. Similarly, PHRI will advocate for the right to health of orthopedic patients from Gaza, most of them persons with disabilities, who require life-changing interventions such as spinal surgeries and whose permits have been consistently denied on grounds that the expertise for these surgeries is available in Gaza.

1 - Needs

PHRI’s needs assessment is based on the 2020 HNO, WHO reports and its own assessments after over three decades advocating for the right to health of Palestinians. This has been made possible and efficient through a constant dialogue with local partners, the Ministry of Health, and local physicians who point at the various needs in basic and secondary services, drugs and equipment stocks, as well as capacity enhancement. Community engagement with balanced gender representation, through patient consultations, as well feedback from trainees in conferences and surgeries, also constitute a key source of information to assess the needs of the project. The project will tend to the needs of those most affected by the sharp deterioration in the humanitarian situation in Gaza. Indeed, the health system, on the verge of collapse following years of blockade and de-development, is now overburdened with casualties from the ongoing demonstrations. In the West Bank, the local health system is particularly challenged to respond to patient needs in numerous fields, and access to health services are challenging in some vulnerable communities due to movement restrictions, especially for people with disabilities. As the humanitarian situation deteriorates, women’s needs for specialized services, including reproductive health screenings, has increased. Chronic depletion of essential medical supplies and drugs must be addressed through medication and equipment deliveries. Palestinians detained in Israeli prisons are among the most vulnerable detainees, as they are subjected to a series of restrictions and may endure gunshot wounds or violence during arrest, followed by cruel, inhumane and degrading treatment and torture during interrogation. In addition, as they are detained for the long periods, their health deteriorates in prison where services are limited and sub-standard. Israeli approval rates of exit permits for treatment unavailable in Gaza and the West Bank have been plummeting, and PHRI receives many more requests than it can handle. Specific challenges are identified for cancer patients – in particular women, as described below-, orthopedic patients and children whose parents are barred from accompanying them for treatment. PHRI’s dialogue with patients has revealed that women are most impacted by the closure to access lifesaving or life-changing healthcare. Indeed, women bear the brunt of medical permit denials in several ways (permits have been withheld on grounds of family proximity to Hamas, relatives living outside of Gaza, etc.) In addition, according to COGAT statistics from 2019, 60% of the children who need treatment outside of Gaza must travel without their parents as these are denied permits. The presence of the parent, however, is essential during medical treatment. PHRI has also noted an unusually low success rate overturning permit denials for patients referred for orthopaedic surgeries outside of Gaza, which are greatly needed on the back of the Great March of Return and its scores of gunshot wounds. Those injured during the demonstrations are being prioritized for surgeries such as limb reconstructions, which means that other types of orthopaedic surgeries are delayed, including life-changing interventions such as spinal surgeries for which people have to wait three years because of a lack of surgeons and equipment. Israel rejects permit applications on grounds that such expertise is present in Gaza, without considering the patient load. Palestinian physicians face pronounced challenges in their pursuit of studies, continuing education, and specialized training. To travel from Gaza or the West Bank to trainings in other parts of the occupied territory, Israel or other neighbouring countries, they must obtain a valid travel permit, yet often fall victim to Israel's non-transparent movement and access restrictions, preventing them from reaching residencies, trainings and jobs.

2 - Activities

Activity 1: Mobile clinics will offer specialized care to 12,000 patients (over 60% of them women and girls) in both the WB and Gaza, in the most vulnerable communities. In the WB, a fixed clinic in an underserved village will be staffed once a week with a GP. The needs of people with disabilities will be addressed by making all clinics accessible. Female health-staffed clinics will address special women’s health needs. Local women grassroots organizations will help ensure high patient turnout and that marginalized beneficiaries will not get overlooked. Volunteers inquire about patients’ personal circumstances to identify possible GBV situations, refer the patient to the clinic psychologist if relevant and ensure that he or she is referred to a social worker. Activity 2: 230 mobile clinic patients in need of treatment unavailable either in the West Bank or Gaza will be supported with referrals to hospitals or appointments in East Jerusalem or Israel. Activity 3: Chronic depletion of essential medical supplies and drugs will be addressed through medication and equipment deliveries based on Ministry of Health lists. The mobile clinic will also distribute free medication during consultations, while PHRI surgeons will bring equipment needed for surgeries. Activity 4: Through its team of highly skilled surgeons, the project will address the need for a wide array of both emergency and essential elective surgeries, in particular orthopedics and vascular surgery, to tend to the needs of March of Return victims and other orthopedic cases in need of life-changing surgeries. Activity 5: The project will enhance the skills and knowledge of over 2,000 Palestinian medical staff to improve health services. Conferences will bring volunteer experts from Israel to the OPT to present recent advancements in their fields, including mental health and women's health. Activity 6: Palestinian health professionals will participate in a medical training at MSR, the Centre for Medical Simulation at Sheba Medical Centre in Israel. The surgeries conducted by PHRI doctors are also an important training opportunity for Palestinian health staff. In between visits, PHRI surgeons continue discussing the cases’ follow-up by videoconference, phone calls and emails and consult on other cases. Activity 7: PHRI will support patients whose permits to exit Gaza and the WB for treatment have been denied. Particular attention will be given to female patients, as well as parents who need to accompany their children. Permit restrictions affecting orthopedic patients will be addressed. New restrictive regulations issued by Israel will be examined from a gender perspective, highlighting how they affect women’s access to health. PHRI may take legal action to appeal refusals and use strategic litigation when it becomes aware of a restriction affecting a grouping of patients. Activity 8: Through individual casework, legal action and advocacy, the project will support Palestinian detainees in Israel overcome barriers to health, with a special emphasis on the most vulnerable (torture victims) Activity 9: A percentage of the women in mobile clinics will be asked a questionnaire to further examine the impact of the conflict on the health of women in Gaza in underserved communities. The questionnaire will help shed light on specific health issues affecting women. The data which will be presented in a position paper. Activity 10: The project will protect the right to health of Palestinians through advocacy activities targeting Israeli dutybearers, including security officials and MKs, combined with media work to increase pressure. International advocacy activities will challenge Israel's policies and their adverse effect on Palestinians' health.

8.6.c - How will feedback be used?

The direct inquiries, issues, and complaints raised by our beneficiaries constitute the primary focus of the project’s needs assessment, design and implementation. The project strives to respond to patients’ needs, highlighted by its medical professionals who meet with the patients and their Palestinian counterparts. The Ministry of Health is a key partner in all activities. Palestinian organisations, most of which are grassroots NGOs well embedded in the host communities, play an important role in pointing at patient needs. Beneficiary comments are transferred through local doctors, field-based staff and partners. To make the project even more attuned to needs and offer a direct means of communication, patients are interviewed (10% in each clinic in Gaza) and provided a form with contact details that enables them to follow-up through phone or email. Forms will also be made available to medical staff attending conferences. All feedback will be analyzed and presented during regular staff meetings and activities will be fine-tuned when necessary. Regarding freedom of movement cases, PHRI interviews two cases handled every month (one successful and one unsuccessful case), to get a better understanding of its working modalities and how they are perceived. After each MSR training in Israel, the Mobile Clinic Director will ask for feedback from the participants. A monitoring and evaluation consultant will provide advice on how to best integrate beneficiary feedback. PHRI is in regular contact with prisoners through lawyers and families. Cases are followed-up on until a final resolution. PHRI receives feedback and cooperates with other organisations working on Palestinian prisoner issues. In its current advocacy group, PHRI is increasing its network of local and international civil society organizations, with a focus on medical groups, either practitioners or patients. PHRI consults regularly with international medical groups as to strategy and framing.

9 - Protection mainstreaming
9.1 - Do No Harm

Do No Harm principles are considered throughout all project activities. All staff and volunteers have experience working in the OPT and have a well-integrated conflict sensitivity. The physicians are volunteers working on their free time in solidarity of their Palestinian colleagues and patients and have great professional and personal integrity. PHRI’s work is also guided by our safeguarding policy document, which describes how PHRI is committed to preventing and responding appropriately to harm to children and vulnerable adults, how PHRI openly communicates a lack of tolerance towards abuse, commits to preventing a person from working with children and/or vulnerable people if he/she poses an unacceptable risk; PHRI places clear responsibilities and expectations on its staff and associates and supports them in understanding and acting in line with the safeguarding policy. The policy is implemented by providing safeguarding training for personnel, contractors, volunteers and board members. Furthermore, PHRI has a robust recruitment‐screening processes and employment contracts containing provisions for dismissal, suspension or transfer to other duties for any employee who breaches the safeguarding code of conduct. PHRI prioritizes the safety and dignity of its patients. The Mobile Clinic director and our partners ensure that the facilities in which the clinics are conducted allow for privacy during checks. The number of patients is also adapted to the location – in a smaller facility, or with limited staff, we ensure that partners don’t invite too many patients to avoid putting pressure on physicians and disappointing patients that would have to be turned away. Before each clinic starts, volunteers, staff and partners are reminded to pay special attention to vulnerable individuals, give them priority, and ensure their access to the clinic. The women-only mobile clinics are also organized to ensure a higher turnout of women in a more comfortable setting and ensure better dialogue with patients who can raise their concerns. In-depth interviews’ aims are explained before taking place. Information shared by beneficiaries is always kept confidential, unless otherwise instructed. PHRI’s activities support the development of self-protection capacities and help empower beneficiaries, allowing them to claim their right to health. Patients who approach PHRI needing referrals and appointments outside the West Bank and Gaza, or who ask PHRI to try to obtain an exit permit after their application has been turned down, must claim their rights themselves – this is particularly true of female patients which are empowered to own and defend their cases. Indeed, each permit request requires quite a commitment on the part of the patient, who needs to go back to her doctor for more information in her file, understanding fully her case and convincing the Palestinian civil committee (one of the first steps in requesting a permit) to reapply. It happened that one of our patients, who had given up hope, finally decided to reapply and she succeeded to exit the Gaza Strip for treatment thanks to her assertiveness. Similarly, in-depth interviews of women during clinics will aim to shed light on the particular health challenges they face, giving them a space to express needs and voice concerns.

9.2 - Equal and impartial access to aid

PHRI provides assistance in accordance with basic humanitarian principles, based on identified needs (impartiality), without any adverse distinction, as indicated by the different categories of patients that are treated by our clinics, have underwent surgeries or received referrals or permits in the past decade: women, men, children, youth, persons with disabilities, with a special focus on the most vulnerable that our partners help identify (humanity). Vulnerable groups are targeted through our network of well-recognized partners on the ground – women grassroots organizations, rehabilitation organizations, cancer organizations among others. They participate in organizing the mobile clinics in various locations, informing beneficiaries ahead of our interventions and are instructed to publicize the clinic’s visit among the most vulnerable. The mobile clinics visits remote locations where the population has limited access to healthcare services (area C, restricted areas.) The Mobile Clinic director meets with representatives of both the Palestinian Authority and Hamas to ensure security and highlight the organisation’s neutrality and independence from political or other objectives. Acceptance levels within the target population are high – PHRI is well known after over three decades of regular activities and is vetted by partner organisations on the ground. Physicians who enroll into PHRI’s MSR training programme in Israel have never expressed concern exiting the Strip and spending several days in the country in this framework. PHRI –an Israeli organization- is perceived as a professional, trustworthy and neutral. In its work advocating for the right to health of Palestinian prisoners, the latter’s families or lawyers contact PHRI on a hotline made available at fixed hours which are advertised with Palestinian partners, lawyers, and on the PHRI website. Volunteer physicians and lawyers are sent to visit the most vulnerable cases. Torture victims in particular benefit from a very close follow-up.
