Project Summary

Given the expertise of OVCI on disability, the project aims to provide rehabilitation services to vulnerable groups, in particular children with disabilities, women and adults at risk, through the action of Usratuna Rehabilitation Centre acting as referral point for Juba and other Counties and States. In addiction the project supports 4 PHCC/Us included in the MoH network to strengthen the provision of basic and specialized health services.

1 - Needs

People with disabilities (PWDs) in South Sudan are among the most marginalized in society, facing stigma and discrimination. PWDs struggle to access healthcare, rehabilitation (including assistive devices), and nutrition services. OVCI intends to respond to three main needs with a holistic approach that is necessary because the well-being of PWDs, especially children, must be assess not only from health point of view, but also from rehabilitation, nutritional and inclusive one. 1) Lack of access to health services (Data from HRP 2020). The Country presents one of the highest under-five mortality rates (90.7 deaths per 1,000 live births) and maternal mortality rates (789 deaths per 100,000 live births) worldwide. Around 75% of all child deaths are due to common and preventable diseases. STDs rate is still high among women and young girls. Juba health system is deficient concerning basic services and there is a strong correlation between health services malfunction (inadequate structures and skills) and new cases of preventable disabilities2) Lack of access to Rehabilitation services. There are no official data about disability in South Sudan, but is it estimated that at least 1.2 million of men, women, girls and boy live with disabilities (Disability in South Sudan, Brigitte Rohwerder, Institute of Development Studies - 2018.) According to data collected at Usratuna Rehabilitation Centre (the only rehabilitation service available for pediatric patients in South Sudan), in 2020, the data show around 50.000 children with disabilities in Juba (physical disabilities 53%; visual disabilities 20%; related to language/deafness 17%; intellectual 10%), in need of rehabilitation services and dedicated assistive devices. About 7% needs surgery, sometimes life-saving, such as neurological cases, that South Sudanese Hospitals cannot offer. 3) Extreme poverty and food insecurity. High rates of acute malnutrition among children and women are predicted to be above the emergency threshold of 15 percent in 2021. An estimated 1.9 million people will need treatment for acute malnutrition in 2021, of whom 821.000 are children and 193.000 children with disabilities. Nutrition and disability are intimately linked: malnutrition can directly cause or contribute to disability and disability to malnutrition. This has intergenerational and life-course implications: poor maternal nutrition can lead to permanent childhood impairments; early life malnutrition can affect health and disability in later life. But disability itself can also lead to subsequent malnutrition. Though infants and children are especially vulnerable, older children and adults are also at risk, not least because early life malnutrition has long term effects. A malnourished child (whether disabled or not) is at increased risk of developing further debilitating disease, delayed development, and disability or compounded by further disability.

2 - Project activities

Aligning the project to the Health Cluster’s objectives, the sectorial objectives are:1- Reduce excess morbidity and mortality of epidemic-prone diseases and health insecurities and 2- Improve access and utilization of integrated life-saving quality health care services to crisis-affected and vulnerable populations through static, mobile and community-based health service delivery systems, with the following activities implemented by the network of PHCC/Us run by OVCI:• Ante Natal Care: First visit, with ultrasound, blood test and check-up for STDs and TBC. Routine follow-up visits. Support for PLW through the distribution of micronutrients as folic acid, iron and vitamins. Delivery of mama kits • Post Natal Care: monitoring during infancy 0-6 months and early childhood 6-24 months. Vaccination. Specific support to children with cleft lip and palate. Referral system to nutrition department or URC for early detection and prompt management. • Management of epilepsy and Nodding Syndrome cases (first visits and follow up), performing EEG, prescribing therapy and providing epileptic drugs.• Medical examination, laboratory analysis and drug’s delivery for the most common diseases and small dressings 3 Increase access to services for survivors of SGBV, people with disabilities, mental health disorders and the elderly, with the following activities:• Management of CWDs, acting as referral point for Juba and other counties: Assessment and treatment at the Physiotherapy Department (Cerebral Palsy, Ataxia, Motor/psychomotor delay, Microcephalus, Hydrocephalus, Spina bifida, Erb's palsy, PIP, Guillain Barre Syndrome, Down's syndrome, Muscular Dystrophy); Assessment and treatment at the Speech Therapy Department (Speech disorder, delay, Dysarthria, Hearing loss, Cleft lip/Cleft lip and palate, Cognitive impairment, Swallowing disorder, Dysphagia, Down's syndrome); Clubfoot Program which offers treatment and support, provision of supplies including casting, tenotomy, braces and follow up; Referral of Neurological cases (hydrocephalus and spina bifida) to CURE Hospital in Mbale, and severe Orthopaedic and Plastic cases to CORSU Hospital in Kampala, forl surgery. Follow up at URC; Production of dedicated assistive devices made at Usratuna Orthopaedic Workshop; Trainings on "Early Detection of Disability" to qualified and specialised staff in CES and other States; Improve the referral system around South Sudan strengthening the network with NGOs.• Individual support, counselling and group coaching on best practise to prevent or manage disabilities: Caretakers of children with special conditions need a specific training and support according to child’s pathologies regarding on how to effectively feed a child and how to teach children how to feed themselves. This is especially important for children with conditions such as cerebral palsy, who may need special seating or positioning to control muscle spasms, cleft lip and palate, who may need assistive eating aid, down’s syndrome, who are at increased risk of choking, hydrocephalus, who need assistive device; Workshops and trainings on swallowing, feeding disorders, correct posture, assistive eating aids, dietary diversity; Mothers’ meeting, to create support and mutual aid. The activities will be implemented also through the Community Based Inclusive Program, active in Juba, with home visits support and monitoring; nutrition, health and hygiene message, anti-stigma awareness and, in partnership with DPOs, awareness sessions for promotion of right for PWDs

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

Disability is still stigmatized in South Sudan and as a result children and adults with disabilities are hidden and isolated. Such negative attitudes contribute to discriminate them, and they have been subject to various forms of verbal, physical and sexual abuse by their families and the wider community. Children with disabilities face many barriers to inclusion. Traditional gender roles limit women and girls with disabilities access to education and employment making them more vulnerable and dependent. Women with disabilities also experience higher levels of physical, psychological and sexual violence. OVCI takes seriously this issue and has a zero-tolerance policy towards sexual harassment, exploitation, and abuse which was finalized in 2020. The policy ensures a professional approach and attitude by OVCI staff and collaborators, and certain complaint mechanism too. In the project’s cycle, different steps will be implemented, in order to provide information and minimize the risk.BEFORE IMPLEMENTING THE ACTIVITIESNew staff and partners may not possess the knowledge or skills to handle SEA issues. OVCI recognizes the importance of equipping staff, volunteers and consultants. Specific trainings will be organized, as appropriate preventive measures, in order to inform and train them about the policy, the process and the procedure, the approach in the work place, the complaints mechanism. All staff yearly attends a course on PSEA, as part of on-going staff training. All staff and partners sign a Code of Conduct declaration. It is a detailed one, since OVCI activities deal with vulnerable groups, in particular children with disabilities and adults at risk. Any violation is cause for immediate dismissal. PSEA focal person will be introduced to all staff, and she will organize periodical meeting in each departments.In collaboration with the staff, an assessment and a map/list of potential risk will be prepared in each department, in order to minimize them and as part of prevention. PSEA activities will be integrated in the chronogram and operational plan.DURING THE IMPLEMENTATION OF THE ACTIVITIESTo prevent SEA effectively, all staff and partners must make sure that the beneficiaries are aware of their rights and entitlements. They should know that they are entitled to assistance, which they should never be expected to provide any form of sexual favor, and that demands for such favors are strictly prohibited. They should also know how and where to report SEA incidents safely and confidentially when they occur. Information sessions will be organized, including the distribution of information sheets or community meetings, focus group discussions, etc. Organizations of Persons with Disabilities (DPOs) will be involved in order to guarantee a proper and effective communication for the target groups. Moreover we will involve sign language.The focal person will be responsible for this critical action, in order to facilitate an effective interagency complaint mechanism that permits and encourages individuals to report instances of SEA easily, confidentially, and safely.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Feedback from the most vulnerable group is the daily activity for OVCI staff targeting especially:- Children with disabilities and their relatives- Children and adults with epilepsy- Children, adults and older who need devicesObstacles such as social or cultural restrictions, including those related to gender or a disability, literacy limitations should not exclude beneficiaries from being able to give a feedback.OVCI is committed to ensure that feedback mechanisms are established, appropriate and accessible to all relevant interest and special needs groups. The approach used during the all cycle of the feedback mechanism is participatory bottom-up, taking in more specific attention vulnerabilities. During the preparation. The staff will plan the tools and the instruments, according to the age, gender and special needs of the beneficiaries, including suggestion boxes, surveys, mobile phone calls or text messaging, focus group discussions, one-to-one interviews, community meetings.Organization of Persons with disabilities (DPOs) will support the process, providing help in finalizing the tools. Form in braille will be given out, draws for illiterate will be used, etc. DPOs will share suggestion regarding the approach too. During the implementation of the activitiesWe expect that the majority of feedback will be given verbally, since face-to-face mechanisms are preferred in contexts with lower literacy and high poverty.The feedback mechanism is run by the staff, with the relevant competences and attitudes Selection of the staff with different ages will be involved in order to guarantee a different approach and way of communicating.The partnership with Organizations of Persons with disabilities (DPOs) and appointed social workers will facilitate the relation with the most vulnerable beneficiaries.Sign language interpreters will be involved by the staff for an effective communication. When necessary, DPOs and the appointed social worker will read and interpret feedback and they will be involved in giving back to the beneficiaries the feedback received and analysed.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

OVCI promotes information sharing, transparency, participation and learning with project participants. Feedback and complaints mechanisms are planned and based on community preferences and cultural norms to ensure that they are accessible, safe, easy to use and with a timely response. Feedback ensures timely information regarding efficiency and effectiveness of the activities. OVCI is committed to actively and routinely seeking the feedback of all the stakeholders. Everyone must have access to feedback mechanisms, with particular attention to access by the most vulnerable groups, in particular PWDs and illiterate, who needs special communication channels. Complaints and response mechanisms ensure that there is a safe and confidential way for people to raise concerns and receive response, from everyday problems with the quality of the services or for sensitive complaints (corruption, staff misconduct, SEA). OVCI recognizes the importance and value of listening to feedback and complaints. In fact they contain useful information to guide and adjust project implementation: they can highlight good practices and underline where activities are not working. OVCI can learn from the information received, either through individual cases, or through trends in feedback and complaints, and should consider them as valuable management information and potentially project cost saving strategies.The process will be implemented:DURING THE PLANNING OF THE ACTIVITIESOVCI recognizes the importance of the staff, who can support and facilitate the feedback and complaints process. A specific training will be organized in order to inform and train them about:- the process and the procedure, - the approach, - the instruments and formsThe project will ensure that partners and people we work with and for have accessible, safe and discreet points of contact through which to raise concerns or complaints.We expect that the majority of feedback will be given verbally, but we will prepare different tools, including suggestion boxes, surveys, a dedicated phone and e-mail address, focus group discussions, one-to-one interviews, community meetings making the process effective, safe, confidential and accessible to all, irrespective of age, gender identity, status, disability, or background. In particular we will involve sign language facilitator and we will prepare tools with braille language and drawers. A specific monitoring activity related to the feedback and complaints mechanism will be scheduled in the chronogram, every 6 months. DURING THE IMPLAMENTATION OF THE ACTIVITIESThe procedure for the feedback and complaints mechanism will be explained to all the participants, sharing the appropriate and critical information: the appointed focal person, an appointed local phone number and e-mail address,…. Beyond the procedure and mechanism, feedback and complaints will be part of the daily activity of OVCI staff. Informally communication will be considered as well as the formally ones. AT THE END OF ACTIVITIESThe feedback and complaints mechanism will be part of the final evaluation of the activities, providing suggestions and lessons learnt useful for future activities.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

OVCI has been working in South Sudan from 1983, dealing with the most vulnerable groups, in particular children with disabilities and their relatives, supporting them in the rehabilitation path and in the critical process of accepting disability. A path and a process which involve different professionals and skills. A path and a process based on trust, where a continuous mutual feedback let beneficiaries focus on abilities rather than disabilities. For this reason, there is an emphasis in OVCI on ensuring that the voices of beneficiaries are heard: comments, suggestions, statements of appreciation and critics. Establishing feedback mechanisms required flexibility and willingness to adapt mechanisms during implementation. The beneficiary feedback mechanisms give the beneficiaries the opportunity to comment, make suggestions, express gratitude or criticize the services provided. We have already explained how the feedback will be collected; now we summarize and explain how these perspectives and suggestions will be used within the Monitor and Evaluation System (M&E) in the different steps mechanism. Preparation Selection of the channels to receive feedback, identifying the information to collect, taking into account the different barriers affecting different groups of people (women and girls, vulnerable groups and persons with special needs).Staff with different ages will be involved in order to guarantee a different approach and way of communicating.Definition of the tools and the instruments, according to the age, gender and special needs of the beneficiaries, with the support of DPOs Design the Feedback Plan, including timeframes, frequency and person in each department responsible for the collection. Inform and Collect Inform the beneficiaries about why and how their data and answers will be collectedEnsure people consent before collecting feedbackAllow anonymous feedback ResultAll the feedback will be considered, bot formally and informally. The results will be collect and shared with the Head Quarter, in the ongoing evaluation process in improving the project assistance.Adjustments measures will be discussed to be implemented.Periodical review will be scheduled.

12.1.1. Decreased prevalence of global acute malnutrition among children under age 5 in severity levels 3 and 4

Malnutrition is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in children age under five years. It is important to note that children with disabilities also face significant issues related to malnutrition. Their impairment can be a direct cause of malnutrition and they may need specific health and nutrition support. For example, children with cerebral palsy, down syndrome, cleft lip and palate face difficulty swallowing, chewing or eating and they may be more at risk of micronutrient deficiencies. Parents may also lack knowledge about how to feed their child effectively or how to teach their child to feed themselves. This is especially important for children with conditions such as cerebral palsy who may need special seating or positioning to control muscle spasms or for children with who are at increased risk of choking.During the physiotherapy or speech therapy assessment, recognition of signs and symptoms of malnutrition will be critical for the referral to the appropriate service.It is important to organize specific feeding training, according to the pathology, will be organized, as well as supporting and monitoring the child, for example with specific training on how to modify food to make it easier to chew or swallow, how to obtain and use suitable alternative food in case of food shortages, how to share food fairly within the family, based on need and not on distribution of power, for example, providing access to food for girls with disabilities on an equal basis with their siblings.
