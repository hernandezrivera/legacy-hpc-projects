Project Summary

Given the reduced capacity of local and national authorities to deliver critical services to communities, ACTED will help to fill the informal education and protection service delivery gaps through the proposed action, with Ubari and Murzuk pre-identified as target locations. ACTED’s approach to enhancing the resilience of and strengthening the protective environment of conflict-affected children in the South of Libya, who face heightened vulnerability in the absence of access to effective protection assistance, is threefold.
In line with CP Sector Objective 1-- Strengthen community-based CP to enhance protection of children from violence, abuse and exploitation in targeted locations—as well as Education O.1, Enhanced access to formal and non-formal education for vulnerable school-aged children affected by protracted crisis—ACTED will tackle the need for safe and inclusive learning spaces and activities for conflict-affected children in the South of Libya through the establishment of Child Friendly Spaces (CFS). ACTED will put in place extracurricular activities, life skills classes as well as after-school academic support for children participating in the CFS programming. The recreational activities for conflict-affected children will include a range of age and gender-appropriate recreational activities such as sports, music, dance, arts and crafts, games and theatrical activities. These structured activities will encourage children to play, socialize, learn, and safely express themselves in safe and inclusive environment, aiming to protect children from physical and psychosocial harm, and to support their continued learning and development.
Secondly, ACTED will organize outreach and awareness activities around CP for local communities, targeting parents and caregivers to enhance the project’s sustainability.
In line with CP Sector O. 3—Strengthen child protection capacity to prevent and respond to CP concerns in Libya-- ACTED will provide trainings to local child protection stakeholders, such as local protection CSOs, teachers, members from the Psychological Counselling Units, and relevant civil servants in the municipalities and in the Ministry of Education responsible for providing child protection and education services to vulnerable children— helping to ensure that children of all backgrounds have access to quality and inclusive services and attain their fundamental rights.
At least 3300 boys and girls aged 6-18 (including 330 children with disabilities) and at least 500 caregivers are expected to directly benefit from the project activities.
This action offers the opportunity to complement and expand existing programs where ACTED has a demonstrated technical capacity in education and protection. ACTED's protection activities, which include protection monitoring and assessments, a dedicated helpline, case management, and ‘cash for protection’ assistance, are well established in Benghazi, and can thus capitalize on existing methodologies, SOPs, and data collection tools. ACTED has implemented education activities through the rehabilitation of schools and the provision of educational equipment in the South and East. ACTED has also gained experience implementing education and CP projects through its 2016 and 2020 (ongoing) UNICEF-funded project, aimed at enhancing the resilience of and strengthening the protective environment of conflict affected children in Benghazi. ACTED will also bring its significant programming experience in the South of Libya, where it has established links with the community and an in-depth knowledge of local dynamics.
ACTED is an active member of the Education Sector and Child Protection WG, as well as a member of the Protection Sector SAG. To implement this project, ACTED will continue its active participation in these sectors, including through participation in meetings and the submission of timely and accurate reporting, to ensure a coordinated approach with other actors in the South.

1 - Needs

Inter-communal tensions in the South of Libya have grown increasingly worse since early 2019, escalating into a violent conﬂict in August. The conﬂict has led to intense urban ﬁghting, identity-based civilian killing, as well as mass displacement from Murzuk to neighboring areas in Libya. The deterioration in the security situation due to armed conﬂict had resulted in the displacement of over 5,643 HHs (28,215 ind.) in search of safety by October 2019 (IOM-DTM).Host communities, IDPs, returnees, and migrants and returnees face signiﬁcant protection concerns in the South of Libya. According to the 2019 MSNA, 22.8% of HHs in Sebha and 9% in Ubari had an unmet protection need. In the REACH Rapid Assessment from August 2019, many IDPs were reported to be under intense psychological stress as a result of their experiences and general lack of security. Other protection issues include movement restrictions due to activities of armed groups and general violence, and access to legal documentation. ACTED’s protection team in Benghazi identiﬁed severe protection needs among recently displaced persons in the South of Libya, with most HHs having faced numerous violations to their rights, safety, and dignity. Additionally, refugees and migrants are subject to arbitrary detention, torture, forced labor, extortion, unlawful killings, sexual exploitation and a variety of other human rights abuses – in the face of any access to rule of law or judicial processes. Despite acute needs and an important migrant population in southern Libya, most of the interventions are currently concentrated in northern Libya, creating a substantial gap in providing adequate response to protection risks and to social exclusion.The protracted conflict in the South has had a particularly devastating impact on boys and girls, who face unmet needs regarding protection, PSS, and educational and recreational spaces, with urban-based migrant and refugee children suffering additional protection risks. As a result of the conflict, many children have been exposed to severe protection risks including forced recruitment by tribal militias, high levels of violence, urban combat, indiscriminate shelling and bombing, and unexploded ordnances. These traumatic experiences have substantially affected children’s well-being and mental health. Across all cities, KIs in the 2019 MSNA consistently mentioned that children were experiencing psychological distress and lacked psychosocial support to help them cope. Although the majority of children are reported by the 2019 REACH MSNA as being enrolled in formal education in the South, the low quality of public education and the lack of social and extracurricular activities have compounded the problem and hindered children’s personal and academic development. The proposed action will benefit vulnerable children from conflict-affected communities in the South. It will be tailored to the specific needs of each age groups and genders, through adapted age and gender-specific catch-up programs and PSS/recreational activities, and to the specific vulnerabilities of girls, children living with disabilities, IDP children, out of school children (OOSC), and other vulnerabilities resulting from the impact of the conflict. A specific emphasis will be put on providing girls with catch-up classes and extra academic support so that they are not left behind. Through the provision of catch-up classes, ACTED seeks to bridge the learning gaps created by conflict-related disrupted access to education. Through its holistic and integrated approach, ACTED will ensure that parents, caregivers, teachers and school administrators also benefit from the action, hereby contributing to creating a safer and more protective environment and building their children’s resilience. The larger community will be involved in the project, benefiting from awareness raising activities, with a view to maximize the impact of the action and to build its sustainability.

2 - Activities

ACTED’s approach to enhancing the access of children to safe and inclusive learning environments in the South of Libya is three-fold.The project will strengthen the capacities of local CP stakeholders, notably CSOs, educators, staff of Psychological Counselling Units, and relevant civil servants in the municipalities and the MoE. The trainings will be based on a child rights, gender and age sensitive approach. They will also underline the specific vulnerabilities of IDP, migrant, and refugee children, as well as children living with disabilities, to encourage the promotion of inclusive learning environments.Secondly, the project will enhance the psycho-social and emotional well-being of children and make their immediate environment more inclusive and safe through the establishment of 2 CFS and light rehabilitations of each school. ACTED will provide extracurricular activities for conflict-affected children, establishing a wide range of age and gender-appropriate recreational activities that could include sports, music, dance, arts and crafts, games and theatrical activities. ACTED will also put in place an extra academic support program during after-school hours with catch-up, remedial, and life skills classes. This program will have a particular focus on meeting the educational needs of girls and out of school children. Finally, awareness of child protection issues in the community will be increased through targeted outreach to parents and caregivers. These sessions will address topics such as: registration to the child friendly spaces, gender-sensitive and positive parenting, mine risk education, Violence Against Children (VAC), overcoming negative coping mechanisms by both parents and teachers, and inclusion of children with disabilities. These activities will ensure a sustainable response to the children’s needs for safe and inclusive environments. ACTED will ensure coordination with other CP and education actors in the South – including Intersos and IRC.

4 - Protection risk analysis

Risk 1: The Security Situation in the South Prevents Access to Beneficiaries and Endangers Field Staff: The security situation remains problematic and unpredictable in the South of Libya. A sudden escalation of intercommunal violence could limit the access to areas of implementation. Should the situation deteriorate, it is possible that direct implementation would no longer be possible without risk to staff or beneficiaries. Travel disruption remains a possibility--periodic outbreaks of violence of all kinds (tribal, military, political, extremist, criminal) have been known to greatly increase the danger of travel between cities in Libya, and could limit movement between ACTED’s base in Sebha and the area of implementation. Risk 2: Restricted Freedom of Movement in the South: According to the 2019 MSNA, 34% of HHs in Sebha mantika reported that they had observed or been aware of movement restrictions in their mahalla, with the most commonly-cited causes were ‘activities of armed groups’ and ‘general violence.’ Consequently, parents/caregivers and children may restrict their movement to the Child Friendly Spaces for safety and security, limiting their access to the project site and consequently the project outcomes.Risk 3: Risk of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (SEA). The involvement of humanitarian workers in acts of SEA is a serious violation of the ‘do no harm’ principle, and humanitarian organizations’ obligation to protect populations affected by crises. Risk 4: Data protection: Upholding the beneficiaries’ rights to protection of their personal data remains of paramount importance. Personal data must be safeguarded from unauthorized access and unlawful processing or disclosure, as well as accidental loss, modification or destruction.

5 - Mitigation measures

R1. ACTED dedicated security staff continuously monitor conflict developments and security incidents in the South in order to anticipate access concerns and modify procedures in advance. ACTED has in place standard operating procedures designed to minimize the exposure of staff and beneficiaries to security threats. Should changes in conflict dynamics cause a deterioration of the security situation, threatening the safety of staff or beneficiaries, ACTED will potentially adapt the program if needed (whether delivering assistance through partners, proxies, or by shifting the area of activities to more secure locations).R2: ACTED will conduct detailed needs assessments in the set-up phase of the project to determine the ideal setting for carrying out child protection and education activities (community centers, operational schools). Doing so will build community acceptance, ensure that target populations are comfortable accessing the CFS, and reduce protection risks for beneficiaries resulting from their participation in the program. R3. ACTED’s CRM provides a mechanism for beneficiaries or community members to report any instances of SEA on the part of ACTED staff or partners. Where possible, ACTED will ensure a gender-balanced group of staff for all program field teams. ACTED also has a Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse policy that all staff have to abide by, and any violation of this policy results in immediate termination of staff. Furthermore, through the GBV sub-sector PSEA task force, ACTED will facilitate PSEA training for its staff as well as the staff of its partners, in order to mitigate the risk of sexual exploitation and abuse occurring as a result of ACTED activities in Libya. R4: Access to all sensitive specific data will be strictly limited to those staff members directly involved in processing it and working copies will be deleted once no longer needed.

6.1 - Other accountability mechanisms

ACTED’s AME Unit will conduct Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) with parents, caregivers, and community members in order to receive feedback and suggestions and improve the project activities accordingly to ensure that none of the activities are indirectly harming any of the beneficiaries. Consultations will be held in the start-up phase of the project to adapt the project design and the implementation methodology to the priorities of beneficiaries and communities. ACTED will also continuously monitor the quality and effectiveness of project deliveries including by collecting feedback from beneficiaries directly and through the complaints and response mechanism (CRM), ensuring that the proposed action remains relevant to the needs of beneficiaries throughout the project implementation. ACTED’s Complaints Response Mechanism (CRM) will be a key means of ensuring accountability towards beneficiaries and external stakeholders. The CRM is managed by AME staff to ensure independence from program teams and for which confidentiality is a fundamental principle. Complaints are received via a dedicated CRM phoneline, WhatsApp, Viber and email. Complaints are also received in the field by AME and program staff to be recorded and transferred to the CRM confidentially. All complaints are registered and recorded by the CRM Focal Point in a password-protected database and categorized based on the level of sensitivity. The CRM Focal Point leads to resolve complaints raised, with the involvement of program, AME or senior management staff, depending on the case and its severity. Information is shared on a strictly need-to-know basis and restricted as much as possible. The CRM Focal Point responds to all individuals who have made a complaint to inform them of the outcome of their complaint, within 15 days or sooner. The CRM will be systematically communicated to all beneficiaries, including the children participating in the center, their parents and/or caregivers, and community members participating in awareness sessions, in order to ensure that awareness of the CRM is high. Within this project, ACTED will actively work to ensure that the CRM is able to be accessed by any parent or caregiver regardless of their level of literacy, to ensure that marginalized groups can access it—including female headed households. This served as an active line of communication for ACTED to receive any grievances from community members regarding the implementation of the project activities or any suggestions for changes. These calls therefore directly translate into recommendations to programs for improvement of programming, and the resolution of such complaints communicated directly back to the beneficiaries.
