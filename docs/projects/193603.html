Project Summary

In alignment with the Shelter Cluster’s (SC) proposed Cluster Objective 2 for 2023 - vulnerable households exposed to harsh weather and protection concerns are supported to meet basic needs and enhance their coping capacities - Catholic Relief Services (CRS) in partnership with Development and Peace (D&P) and Palestinian Organization for Development (POD) propose critical shelter rehabilitation for acutely vulnerable households in substandard shelters, with complementary non-food items (NFI) assistance, targeting 350 households (2,450 individuals) over 12 months. These households are acutely vulnerable due to chronic economic, environmental, and protection factors, leaving them in substandard shelters without the resources to improve their living conditions. This project will also maintain a flexible approach that allows it to target conflict-affected housing units (following a similar approach to rehabilitation of substandard homes), should the need arise during implementation as the security situation remains volatile. Completed works will reduce vulnerability and enhance resilience for targeted households through improved access to adequate shelter and basic household needs. The project will target the most acutely vulnerable households in Middle Area who face significant and sustained risks to protection, safety, and dignity as a function of their substandard living situation. Recent data points to Middle Area as being an area with particularly elevated shelter need, as 68.7% of households in Middle Area reported shelter damages, defects, or issues. Female-headed households (FHH), as well as households with elderly family members or family members living with disabilities, will be prioritized. Focusing on the distinct needs of women and men, girls and boys, people with disabilities (PWD), and people of different age groups, this intervention uses an approach of accompaniment and empowerment to promote the full participation of all household members. This inclusive approach seeks to effectuate outcomes that improve accessibility and meet the distinct needs and priorities of each of these vulnerable groups. To engage all household members, CRS and POD will mobilize gender-balanced teams to accompany households throughout the shelter rehabilitation process. Staff will provide technical guidance and facilitate equitable access to information through household-level discussions and joint decision-making to ensure rehabilitation plans reflect all household members’ needs. This approach, highly attentive to the needs of women and girls, is designed to mainstream protection and to advance a gender-responsive program strategy. The intervention builds on more than nine years of CRS experience in shelter programming in Gaza with deep engagement of women and girls, PWD, and the elderly, as well as with improved protection, health and safety practices. CRS has implemented three similar projects funded by the Canadian government totaling 6.5 M CAD since 2016 is currently implementing shelter rehabilitation and repair as part of its resilience programming in North Gaza. POD has significant experience working in Middle Area, and has implemented several projects focused on shelter rehabilitation and repair. CRS will be responsible for program quality, household selection and accountability, including PSEA, monitoring and evaluation, liaising with the SC, adherence to protection principles and overseeing health and safety standards. This project will also benefit from the support of regional Technical Advisor for Gender, that will support the in-country team to ensure gender mainstreaming throughout. D&P’s primary responsibilities will include donor reporting and compliance, and gender mainstreaming. POD will be responsible for activity implementation, including community engagement and field monitoring. POD will be a sub-recipient to CRS and CRS will provide technical and monitoring support to POD and will ensure overall quality .

1 - Needs

The preliminary HNO 2023 analysis identifies 591,000 Gazans in need of access to an adequate shelter in 2023, including both a physical shelter that meets the SC Minimum Standards of Shelter Repair and Upgrade as well as access to critical household items and supplies. The direct impact of the recent conflict escalations in May 2021 and August 2022, as well as the ongoing economic crisis in Gaza stemming from the 15-year blockade and the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic, limit the ability of families in Gaza to access and maintain safe, adequate, and dignified shelter. Findings from the 2022 Multi Sector Needs Assessment (MSNA) indicate that 59.8% of households in Gaza experience some kind of shelter damage, defect, or issue. The per capita shelter-related need is particularly high in the Middle Area governorate of Gaza, where 68.7% of surveyed households reported shelter damages, defects, or issues in the 2022 MSNA (nearly 10% higher than the average across Gaza), but only 0.3% had received shelter assistance in the six months prior to data collection pointing to persistent assistance gaps in this governorate. Many of these households are among the 21,500 homes in Gaza classified by the Ministry of Public Works and Housing (MoPWH) as substandard, often lacking sufficient living space, facilities and privacy, and frequently exposed to elevated safety and protection risk. Though the reported need is high in Rafah and North Gaza governorates as well, CRS continues to rehabilitate substandard shelters in Rafah under concurrent programming, and a preliminary survey showed that a number of other actors intend to focus shelter activities in Rafah in the 2023 HRP. In North Gaza, recent CRS programming demonstrates that much of this need is derived from housing units that have been completely destroyed and require reconstruction, or from units that have been partially damaged but still meet Minimum Requirements. This protracted need has been exacerbated by damages from the May 2021 and August 2022 escalations. The May 2021 escalation resulted in 60,599 partially damaged and 1,689 totally destroyed housing units, and the August 2022 escalation added an additional 1,445 partially damaged and 26 totally destroyed housing units, according to data released by the SC Palestine. Of the 37.2% of households who reported in the 2022 MSNA having sustained shelter damages during the May 2021 escalation, 28.8% reported lingering damages at the time of data collection in mid-2022, because they lack sufficient resources to repair their homes. As noted, CRS has observed that much of the remaining conflict-damage from previous escalations consists of housing units that have been completely destroyed, or which have sustained partial damage but continue to meet minimum standards. The security situation remains volatile, however, with the possibility of renewed conflict and conflict-damage to housing units. Recent CRS programming has demonstrated that the damages and issues most prevalent among substandard and conflict-affected homes include lack of safe roofing, leading to water infiltration during the winter; windows that inhibit privacy or protection from inclement weather and insects; poor or missing electrical installations; lack of basic and accessible water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) facilities including unfinished bathrooms and kitchens; and unhygienic sewage connections. These characteristics contribute to articulated feelings of insecurity and uncertainty among families, as well as to immediate and significant risks to safety and protection. Additionally, the absence or insufficient supply of essential shelter or household items such as mattresses, tarps, blankets, gas cylinders, and basic kitchen and hygiene items in the household limits families’ ability to perform daily household activities with dignity and further compromises health and safety. CRS’ current shelter project data validates a diversity of needs across family members. Women tend to prioritize kitchens, bathrooms, and privacy whereas men prioritize electricity, insulation, and high ceilings. Children of both genders desire space to play, while adolescents of both genders desire well as on doors for privacy from siblings and visitors and adequate lighting, both of which enable more productive conditions to complete homework. Girls, especially adolescent girls, often prioritize having separate spaces within the home while children of both genders value systems that improve hot water. Meanwhile, people with disabilities and the elderly often have difficultly safely maneuvering around the home and using the facilities due to uneven flooring and lack of assistive devices, so these groups prioritize accommodations such as even flooring, ramps, handrails, and wider doorways to facilitate access and mobility. Substandard housing has been found to disproportionately affect women, girls, PWD and the elderly due to the comparatively greater amount of time they spend at home due to cultural norms or limited mobility. According to the MSNA (July 2022), findings revealed that 62% of HHs with a member with a disability reported an unemployed member of their family. FHH remain particularly vulnerable due to marginalization from the workforce and lack of other adult family members who can support the household, financially or otherwise. According to a recent study by the Shelter and NFIs Cluster in oPt, there is limited knowledge within vulnerable groups—such as widows, FHHs, and women with disabilities—regarding the services offered by different humanitarian actors and the terms and criteria for receiving assistance. Women with disabilities similarly face unique vulnerability due to fewer employment prospects and greater risk of domestic violence, both inside and outside the home. As a result of continual exposure to substandard conditions, these groups suffer from chronic health issues, limited privacy, and increased exposure to the elements.

2 - Activities

Based on need, the project anticipates targeting 350 families (approximately 2,450 individuals) in Middle Area, providing households with assistance to undertake critical shelter rehabilitation of substandard homes. Given the continued volatility of the security context, CRS, D&P, and POD will also maintain the flexibility to respond to needs following an emergency–i.e., also including conflict-affected shelters, if needed. During selection, the project will prioritize the most acutely vulnerable households including FHH, those with elderly family members, and those families which include a person(s) living with a disability. Adhering to the SC’s Minimum Standards, the project will work closely with each family to ensure that the assistance meets the needs of highly vulnerable groups (i.e. women and girls, people with disabilities, elderly). CRS will also engage a gender expert throughout the project to ensure gender quality measures are in place. The value of rehabilitation assistance is tailored based on a household assessment. CRS anticipates the rehabilitations will average $3,000 per household to meet the Minimum Requirements of the SC’s Minimum Standards of Shelter Repair and Upgrade whilst meeting the protection needs of vulnerable groups. Where possible, the project will use a self-help model wherein households work with the project team to develop a bill of quantities (BoQ) which CRS ensures is responsive to the needs and priorities of all family members. The project then provides restricted cash and technical support to the household according to the BoQ to execute their rehabilitation plans, though a contractor approach is available as an option to households (see Activity 3). Complementary NFI assistance will ensure families have the supplies to live in a dignified, healthy, and safe environment. Activity 1, Household Assessments: As part of the project’s protection mainstreaming strategy and to ensure the project delivers assistance to acutely vulnerable households, CRS and POD will assess social vulnerability (e.g. people with disabilities or elderly family members, FHH, income levels, etc.) and technical housing conditions of households nominated by the MoPWH, partner teams, or other CRS projects. Social and technical vulnerability criteria will be analyzed across households on the nominated list, but also in comparison to national statistics on the prevalence of vulnerability characteristics in Gaza (e.g., FHH, disability status, elderly) to determine the most acutely vulnerable households who have low capacity to meet their shelter needs independently of humanitarian assistance. Activity 2, Family-Focused Rehabilitation Planning: CRS and POD will offer sex-segregated orientation sessions for both spouses (home-based options available for any households unable to travel to orientation locations), which are key to promoting transparency and ensuring equal access to information. Topics include protection measures; rehabilitation modalities; environmentally friendly options; NFI options and orientation to CRS’ e-voucher system; and CRS’ feedback mechanism. CRS will sign contracts with households after both spouses are briefed on assistance terms and conditions, the value of assistance, and the participatory nature of the rehabilitation process. Gender-balanced staff will facilitate a rehabilitation planning discussion at the household level to foster joint decision making and provide technical guidance to ensure that rehabilitations meet all household members’ critical needs. This accompaniment approach with project staff promotes opportunities for vulnerable groups to express their opinions and ensures respect for cultural norms. Activity 3, Critical Rehabilitation Support: CRS staff engineers will review rehabilitation plans for alignment with SC Minimum Standards. Households will select a rehabilitation approach: 1) self-help (households manage their own rehabilitations), or 2) contractor (a CRS-vetted contractor manages the rehabilitations). The self-help model affords greater flexibility and ownership of the rehabilitation, fostering autonomy and dignity. This self-help model is comprised of three payment tranches: two advances and a third (final) reimbursement conditioned upon the completion of planned rehabilitation works. The contractor approach provides an option for vulnerable groups unable to carry out rehabilitations themselves, or for rehabilitations that may be more technical or complex (CRS anticipates approximately 5% of households will select this option). Project staff will regularly monitor rehabilitations through in person site inspections to ensure that quality and safety standards are met and that vulnerable households are sufficiently supported. If monitoring reveals any issues, credentialed staff will work with the household to advise safe and appropriate ways to move forward with the rehabilitation and help them to identify workers or experts to support. Activity 4, NFI Support: Households will receive NFI assistance through a mixed wallet modality that enables them to purchase basic shelter and household items at local vendor shops. Based on past programming experience, CRS determines NFI assistance that considers 1) priority NFI needs identified by the SC as necessary for meeting minimum standards (as per the SC’s Standardized Shelter NFI Packages and Combined Kits), 2) market prices, and 3) number of household members. CRS estimates that the value of NFI assistance will be around $200 per household. Assistance will be transferred as 50% e-voucher (restricted for the purchase of shelter-related NFIs at pre-approved vendors), and 50% restricted cash (to be withdrawn at pre-approved vendors and spent based on the households’ priorities and choices). CRS will conduct post-distribution monitoring to ensure that the assistance is effective and relevant and that the unrestricted cash portion is spent in line with project objectives.

5.2 - Please briefly explain why the specific modality/ies for this response were chosen.

CRS employs a restricted cash modality for rehabilitation, wherein cash is transferred to households in tranches following the successful completion of successive components of the rehabilitation. CRS has learned from experience that most participating households prefer the self-help approach since costs are slightly lower and this method affords more choice and greater control over the process. Based on learning from recent programs, between 90%-100% of households typically select the self-help approach. The contractor approach, meanwhile, enables the project responsiveness to vulnerable groups who may otherwise struggle to carry out rehabilitations, or for rehabilitations that are more technical or complex (recently, this modality has accounted for less than 5% of households). Meanwhile, NFIs will be delivered through a mixed wallet approach: 50% restricted e-voucher, 50% restricted cash. Recent CRS programming has demonstrated that this balance provides households with the autonomy and dignity to select items in line with their needs and priorities, while also ensuring that spending is aligned with the project goal and objectives. In recent programming, roughly 90% of the total assistance value for each household has been comprised of shelter rehabilitation assistance payments, while 10% has been comprised of NFI assistance.

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

CRS is committed to continued improvement based on feedback. All non-sensitive feedback will be routed by a CRS staff member or affiliate from the feedback, complaint, and response mechanism (FCRM) channel through which it was received into the feedback management system, where it will be registered and assigned a reference number. The project FCRM focal point will check all feedback cases on a weekly basis to verify that registered cases are correctly coded and categorized with sufficient detail in case descriptions, to uphold accountability. All non-sensitive cases will be followed up by assigned project staff, who may resolve the case and respond directly to the person of concern on their own. The Program Manager or CRS Country representative may support on more complex cases. All actions and responses are communicated back to the person of concern within 14 days. Sensitive feedback will be immediately sent to the CRS Country Representative (CR) via a confidential email address and excluded from the database and reports due to the confidential nature of such cases. The Country Representative will review and manage such cases in accordance with CRS policies and procedures, including the Fraud Allegation Management Procedure and the Safeguarding Allegation Management Procedure. The CR is responsible to respond to the person of concern within 72 hours. The project FCRM focal point will regularly review the FCRM database and will flag any trends in suggestions, cases of dissatisfaction, or requests for information to the project team. Additionally, the focal point will produce an FCRM report on a quarterly basis, after which the project team will have a formal reflection meeting to discuss trends in feedback and complaints as well as programmatic changes or adaptations that take such feedback or complaints into account. CRS will ensure that the FCRM allows community members and project participants to have a voice in project design and implementation.

9.1b - If 'Other', please specify :

9.1d - Explain how you have ensured that mechanism are accessible to all population groups?

CRS maintains a phone hotline, WhatsApp channel, and email address for feedback and complaints. CRS ensures these mechanisms are accessible, providing safe complaint channels for all target groups. The diversity of the FCRM channels ensures all groups, including women and people with disabilities, have multiple options to reach the project in ways that are both culturally sensitive and consider different communication preferences. For example, people with a hearing impairment can contact CRS by WhatsApp or email. Women, if they feel more comfortable, can ask to speak to a female staff member. Data from recent projects has shown that nearly all participants know how to communicate feedback or complaints and find the channels safe and easy to use. To continue to ensure that FCRM channels remain accessible, trusted, and operational, questions about FCRM awareness and accessibility will be built into the initial project assessment, regular monitoring tools, and quarterly quality checks will be conducted. CRS and POD will also use face-to-face communication to capture feedback and complaints on an ongoing basis from people who may prefer not to use the above channels. Details about available feedback channels are shared with key stakeholders via written (i.e. FCRM cards, posters, leaflets, blast SMS) and verbal (i.e. formal events and face-to face as well as during data collection activities) means. “FCRM cards” are distributed at all project events, workshops, and field visits with participants and other stakeholders, which include CRS’ toll-free hotline number, WhatsApp number and email address. Additionally, the orientation for households (sex-segregated for both spouses) includes FCRM key messages, including participant rights, expected behavior from CRS and POD staff, what types of cases can and should be reported, how to report cases, and the timeframe during which a response can be expected, ensuring access to this important information between men and women.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

Informed by the SC’s Guidance and Checklist on Protection Mainstreaming and CRS’ own Joint Protection Mainstreaming Framework and Global Safeguarding Policy, CRS mainstreams protection principles into every phase of project design, implementation, and monitoring. These principles include safety and dignity (do no harm); meaningful access to assistance and services (in proportion to need and without any barriers such as discrimination); accountability; and participation and empowerment. CRS staff sign a Code of Conduct and CRS ensures partners have appropriate safeguarding measures (including policies) in place, including on Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA). CRS and partner responsibilities are included in staff onboarding, project start up, and through regular refreshers. CRS also ensures that project participants are fully informed of their rights. Beneficiaries’ rights and responsibilities are explained during participant orientation—this includes setting expectations for the roles and responsibilities of CRS and partner staff. In consideration of the range of protection concerns created by inadequate shelter, the intervention seeks to mainstream the above principles in the following ways: o Training of all CRS and partner staff on gender and protection principles,o Deployment of gender-balanced staff to accompany households during orientation, contract signing sessions, and throughout rehabilitation; o Facilitation of a participatory decision-making process involving all household members (inclusive of women/girls, people with disabilities, and the elderly) by trained, gender-balanced staff; o Ensuring accessibility of project participation for participants with limited mobility; o Flexibility to use a self-help and/or contractor-led approach, with specialized support for households with unique needs (e.g. FHH; households with elderly members or members living with disabilities) to implement the rehabilitation plan; o Robust promotion and management of CRS’ feedback and response mechanisms. As part of its efforts to strengthen protection mainstreaming, the project team has strengthened its approach and processes to ensure cases of gender-based violence (GBV) are safely and appropriately referred to specialized actors according to the Inter-Agency Standard Operating Procedures for GBV and Child Protection Case Management and Referral in Gaza. CRS has instituted regular refresher trainings for CRS and partner staff on this referral mechanism. D&P will also provide technical support to CRS to ensure that gender is mainstreamed throughout all stages of the project. CRS has agreed to sign onto the UN inter-agency PSEA standard operating procedures to ensure appropriate reporting and follow-up of any suspected SEA. The incorporation and tracking of additional indicators related to project gender and protection mainstreaming commitments (see “Indicators” section above) enable the project to monitor the project’s responsiveness to the specific needs of women and men, girls and boys, people with disabilities, and the elderly, as well as the project’s progress toward its protection and gender-responsive targets. All elements of the project are designed and implemented to promote equal access to information, decision making, and benefits of the project for both women and men. Orientation and contract signing sessions ensure both spouses receive the same information about the project as well as the terms of the assistance. A gender-balanced team accompanies families throughout the rehabilitation process, including female staff present to encourage the participation of women and girls as the families develop their rehabilitation plans.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

CRS has taken steps to ensure equal and impartial access to aid, in accordance with its commitment to address the needs of women and girls, people with disabilities, and the elderly. First, CRS’ current selection criteria are based on inputs from consultation sessions with male and female community members in July 2019 coupled with an internal validation review in August 2022. At the beginning of this project, the team will conduct an additional participatory review to ensure continued prioritization of households that are both in need of assistance and may also face barriers to receiving assistance, such as households led by women (FHH), elderly individuals, or people with disabilities. CRS will conduct home visits to all households identified as potential participants to verify vulnerability data and ensure the inclusion of the most acutely vulnerable households to access shelter rehabilitation services. CRS also facilitates vulnerable groups’ participation in decision-making processes; home-based orientation sessions are offered to any household experiencing difficulties attending orientation sessions (e.g., people with disabilities). The project will offer separate sessions for both spouses to ensure equal access to information and assistance. During the rehabilitation planning process all family members, including women, people with disabilities, and the elderly will be encouraged to contribute their needs and ideas, ensuring that the rehabilitation assistance is aligned with the needs of these vulnerable groups. Rehabilitation plans will be reviewed by CRS and POD to ensure attentiveness to gender and protection mainstreaming best practices. CRS and POD will also conduct regular field visits throughout the project; if the team identifies substandard homes during these visits, CRS and POD will register them for future assistance or refer them to the MoSD/MoPWH to increase awareness of potential avenues for shelter support.
