Project Summary

Approximately 5.9 million people are in dire need of nutrition assistance in Syria, an increase from 5,502,575 people in 2022. Over 609,979 CU5 are stunted in Syria. Approximately 363,556 CU5 suffer from acute malnutrition. The number of severe acute malnutrition (SAM) cases among children aged 6-59 months increased by 45.6% - from 51,013 in 2022 to 75,726 in 2023. Of the 363,556 children, approximately 287,830 are suffering from moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) in 2023 - an increase of 55% compared to the 185,371 recorded in 2021 (Nutrition HNO 2023).
The project aims at improving equitable, safe and dignified access to nutrition services targeting PLW and CU5 by strengthening the local institutional primary healthcare and activating community-based initiatives. Emphasis will continue to be placed on ensuring a comprehensive approach to primary care services, including malnutrition assessment, prevention and management, integrated with infant and young child feeding (IYCF) practices and mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS). This will be achieved through the provision of nutrition-specific services through Community-Based Management of Acute Malnutrition (CMAM) clinics, PHC reproductive health units (RH), mobile medical teams, and active screening and community-based interventions by community health workers (CHWs) to address malnutrition, maternal depression, disease outbreaks, baby WASH and effective use of food resources. Adolescents and young mothers visiting health centers will benefit from IYCF and MHPSS services and participate in recreational activities for an integrated mental and maternal health response. Gender responsive counselling will be promoted, while applying gender lens through all activities. Capacity building and on-the-job training will be implemented for healthcare staff to ensure implementation of national protocols and guidelines consistent with quality nutrition care delivery. A comprehensive MEAL plan will support collection and tracking of sex and age disaggregated data in line with proposed interventions and is undertaken directly by MEAL in collaboration with health and nutrition department. Systematic collection of information on the risk environment, gender roles and responsibilities, gender relations and decision-making dynamics, access to and control of resources, and social norms will be done with a conflict sensitivity approach. As part of AAH’s accountability towards affected populations, qualitative interviews and satisfaction surveys will complement monitoring activities. AAH will ensure to gather community feedback and input in each activity whether through focus group discussions, key informant interviews and hotlines, throughout program cycle.

3 - Needs

Despite a reduction in armed conflict, vulnerability continues to increase, disproportionately affecting women and children. Hassakeh, Hama and Aleppo governorates rank among the highest on the inter-sectoral severity needs scale at 4 out of 5 in the HNO 2022. According to the draft HNO 2023, 3,751,900 children (2,203,506 (37,4%) girls and 1,548,394 (26,3%) boys) and 2,142,864 women are in dire need of nutrition assistance in Syria, an increase from 5,5 02,575 people in 2022. Over 609,979 CU5 are stunted in Syria. About 363,556 CU5 suffer from acute malnutrition. The number of severe acute malnutrition (SAM) cases among children aged 6-59 months increased by 45.6% in 2023. Of the 363,556 children, about 287,830 are suffering from Moderate Acute Malnutrition (MAM) – a 55% increase compared to 185,371 in 2021. In late 2021- 2022, AAH, conducted a Maternal Health and Nutrition study aiming at understanding the health and nutrition status of WRA and PLW in rural Hama and Aleppo. Results indicate that malnutrition cases among WRAs were 16.9% and 13.9% in Aleppo and Hama respectively. Compared to the SMART 2019, the malnutrition rates have tripled. 85% of households reported no consumption of any kind of meat (red, chicken and fish) over 7 days, the remaining 15% of households reported fewer than 3 times a week. Households’ declining purchasing power, the rising cost of food, and the lack of basic services in Syria have a direct impact on people’s present and future health.

4 - Implementation of activities

AAH will continue to restore essential health systems, as well as activate community structures targeting PLW and CU5 to provide meaningful access to public services to prevent undernutrition. Capacity building will be provided to scale up early identification of malnutrition through community-based and family-based MUAC screening. Referral of detected cases for treatment will be facilitated through supported CMAM clinics. Mobile medical services will be activated to provide health and nutrition services in hard-to-reach communities. Micronutrient supplementation will be provided through the supported facilities for anemia prevention and management. AAH will strengthen awareness on integrated MHPSS-IYCF practices by supporting provision of consultations at PHCs and community levels. On-the-job training will be applied to ensure implementation of guidelines consistent with quality care delivery and adequate reporting. To ensure sustainability, activities will be integrated with comprehensive WASH and Health interventions, including hygiene promotion, provision of medicines, and rehabilitation of facilities. Periodic satisfaction surveys and beneficiary feedback will be collected during activity monitoring to ensure accountability towards affected populations. Beneficiary data will be disaggregated by sex, age and disability. A comprehensive MEAL plan will support collection and tracking of sex and age disaggregated data in line with proposed interventions.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk: people receiving nutrition services, particularly children, girls and women, are over-exposed to the risk of harm. Likelihood: low. Impact: high. Mitigation: Child Safeguarding polices and systems in place, regularly monitored and corrective action taken. Sufficient safe, clean and sex segregated bathroom facilities should be provided at facility level. Risk: nutrition facilities could be attacked. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: apply the designated de-confliction mechanism for facilities. Continue reporting attacks. Continue advocacy efforts with all parties. Risk: gender inequality and GBV. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: train all IYCF counsellors on how to supportively engage with GBV survivors and provide information in an ethical, safe and confidential manner. Ensure referral mechanisms to specialized GBV support, health care, MHPSS, and child protection. Ensure locations, times and methods of nutrition services are safe and accessible for women and other at-risk groups. Risk: not equal provision of and access to nutrition services for vulnerable groups. Likelihood: high. Impact: high. Mitigation: analyze and disaggregate all data by sex, age and disability. Implementation of nutrition services in line with global and nutrition sector guidance, including improved quality reporting and monitoring. Review informed consent procedures. Ensure that complaints and feedback mechanisms are accessible and facilitate inputs for all persons.


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

AAH recognizes the right of everyone to be treated with dignity and respect and protected from sexual exploitation and abuse. AAH’s policy on PSEA complies with the IASC six principles relating to SEA. Implementation of humanitarian aid activities can sometimes have the unforeseen impact of creating unequal power balances within the AAH team and for recipients of aid; the organization is committed to mitigating against such risks. AAH prohibits SEA by affirming a zero-tolerance approach. Disregard of PSEA polices is regarded as gross misconduct, which could result in summary dismissal. AAH reserves the right to refer such cases to the relevant legal authorities in the abuser’s country of origin and in the host country. All AAH staff will always treat people with dignity and respect; support awareness raising of the PSEA policy and report any instances of a breach of this policy. All employees and representatives of AAH are trained on Safeguarding, Protection and PSEA and are signatories to AAH’s ethical policies and Code of Conduct, and are therefore obligated not to engage in sexually exploitative behaviour linking sexual favors with the receipt of aid, engage in any sexual activity with a child under 18 years of age or be involved with any forms of prostitution or payment for sex. AAH staff and communities AAH works with have access to safe and effective reporting systems. AAH will investigate allegations of SEA in a timely, professional, and confidential manner.


9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Assumptions: security situation allows safe response; access to target areas and beneficiaries is granted; partners are willing to collaborate on timely manner; availability of suppliers and items in the market, prices remain stable; no major displacements occur. Risk: security situation is unstable and/or worsens, AAH has no access; there are additional administrative procedures imposed on D-INGOs, and restrictions on access to beneficiaries; lengthy processes to obtain approvals. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: AAH coordinates with local authorities, Ministries, and stakeholders to monitor the situation, to maintain access to target areas and beneficiaries, to develop relationships and mitigate restrictions. Security rules and plans are in place and regularly reviewed. Re-planning of activities to recover eventual delays. Advocacy and coordination with the humanitarian community. Risk: significant devaluation of EURO/ USD against SYP; International bank transfers are not fluid and timely. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: significant changes in the value of the Syrian currency will be monitored and, if materialized, communicated to donors and options discussed. Risk: aid diversion, financial fraud, and corruption. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: strict policies and procedures in place for procurement, financial management and risk management; internal controls set up. Monthly monitoring of financial spending.
