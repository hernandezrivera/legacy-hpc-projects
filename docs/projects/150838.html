Project Summary

HALO will conduct risk education, survey and clearance operations in eastern Ukraine, to respond to the needs of mine-affected communities across Donetsk and Luhansk oblasts, focusing on high-priority areas within the 15km buffer-zone from the line of contact. HALO will also provide training and capacity building sessions for the key mine action stakeholders including the newly established mine action authorities in order to contribute to an effective and efficient mine action sector. Risk education sessions are a preventive measure that will inform on the threat related to mines and other explosive items and decrease the probability of accidents. Survey work will establish the extent and nature of mine contamination in the region, while clearance will permanently remove these threats, improving the physical safety and livelihoods opportunities of those affected. HALO aims to clear two million square metres of land contaminated with explosive hazards during 2019.
HALO began landmine clearance in Donetsk and Luhansk oblasts in April 2016, and is the largest humanitarian demining organisation working in the region. Since the start of operations, HALO has cleared over 3 million square meters of land, with 81,389 people benefitting so far from completed or ongoing tasks. Current capacity comprises 360 staff in 22 clearance teams, 1 mechanical clearance team, 2 technical survey teams and 3 non-technical survey teams.
In 2019, HALO aims to continue risk education, survey and clearance operations in government controlled areas of eastern Ukraine including along the contact line, and is investigating the possibility of starting work in non-government controlled territory.

Needs

Landmines and unexploded ordnance have taken a heavy human toll in eastern Ukraine, causing more than 1,959 casualties since the start of the conflict in 2014. Ukraine is one of the most contaminated countries in the world - in particular it has been the country most affected by anti-vehicle mine accidents for the past three years. In the past six months, over 100 casualties caused by mines or explosive remnants of war (ERW) were reported, of which over 60% civilians. Around 65% of civilian mine victims are adult men. This may be a result of traditional gender roles in Ukraine meaning that men are more likely to work outside, and in the agriculture sector, as much of the mine contamination is located on agricultural land. However, it is not just men, who are affected –over 110 women have been killed or injured by mines since 2014. More than 120 children have been victims of mine or ERW accidents during the same period, killed or injured while playing, walking in the fields or travelling with their families. The gender imbalance is even more pronounced amongst child victims, with 90% of those killed or injured being male. Women and girls do face specific dangers in relation to mines, for example, when seeking privacy to relieve themselves, particularly when waiting to cross the contact line often risk injury by straying far from the road. Older people also find themselves at risk when collecting firewood and mushrooms in the forests. High rates of poverty amongst the elderly drive them to forage for these items even when they are aware of the mine danger. The ‘buffer zone’, the area within 15km of the contact line, is where the vast majority of mine-related accidents take place. Many areas near the contact line are heavily contaminated with ERW, including anti-personnel mines, anti-vehicle mines, unexploded projectiles, and cluster munitions, all of which pose a direct threat to human security. Those living near, or travelling across, the contact line are at risk: on average 36,000 people cross the contact line each day, and around 1,160,000 people live within 20km of the line of contact. Humanitarian mine clearance will not only improve the physical safety of its beneficiaries, but also provide opportunities for renewed economic productivity. Mine- and ERW-contamination brings a high economic cost, preventing cultivation of agricultural land, use of forests and rivers for wood collection and fishing, fruit and vegetable production, and the development of land for industrial or commercial purposes. This is true not only of the current residents of cleared settlements, but also those that have been displaced - the conflict has displaced approximately 730,000 people, many of whom are prevented from returning home by the threat of mines, even after the fighting has finished. Some of those fleeing fighting have found that the settlement to which they have moved is also mine-contaminated, making the decision to stay or return even harder. Rapid assessments conducted in government controlled areas of Donbas since 2016 have noted 214 settlements in which land is thought or confirmed to be contaminated by landmines, cluster munitions or explosive ordnance. Non-technical survey (NTS) of these areas has so far identified 14,000,000 sq. m. of land that requires systematic clearance. HALO is expanding its work in the buffer zone, where the highest levels of mine contamination are being found. So far, HALO has been able to visit 203 buffer zone settlements to conduct Rapid Assessment, 53% of which show evidence of mine contamination. Non-technical survey in the buffer zone to identify this requires extensive negotiations with the Ukrainian authorities and HALO has received permission to survey 65 such villages so far, carrying out this survey where the security situation permits. HALO works in coordination with the Ministry of Defence CIMIC teams to arrange access and inform Ukrainian agencies of HALO’s presence.

Activities & outcomes

HALO is looking to increase its capacity to 500 operational staff next year, and to this end, plans to train 8 new manual clearance teams and 3 new mechanical clearance teams and an additional MRE team. Manual teams will be trained in manual demining, surface and subsurface clearance of cluster munitions and other explosive remnants of war (ERW), and Explosive Ordnance Disposal (EOD). The mechanical teams will include two teams trained in mechanical ground preparation and one team trained to operate an armoured excavator. The excavator team will learn clearance methods for the challenging environments of rubble, collapsed buildings, and high metal contamination, where manual demining is not possible. HALO will ensure that female deminers, who currently make up 15% of demining staff, are recruited as part of these teams, by targeting job advertisements to encourage female applications and by ensuring a comfortable working environment for all genders. All clearance and technical survey teams will be deployed onto areas identified as high humanitarian priority during non-technical survey. Much of this clearance will take place in the buffer zone, provided security conditions allow demining to take place safely. Thus far, HALO has worked on 13 minefields within 15km of the contact line, finding the first mines of any humanitarian demining organisation in the buffer zone last year. Mechanical clearance capacity will expand significantly over 2019, and will clear abandoned military positions and trenches along with critical infrastructure sites, all of which are difficult and slow to clear manually. This will allow land to be released back to the community more quickly than through manual clearance alone, and ensure that items of infrastructure, such as water pipes and electricity pylons, can be safely maintained. NTS teams will complete a Rapid Assessment of all accessible settlements that may be mine-contaminated, and will continue to improve our understanding of the mine threat in the buffer zone through non-technical survey reporting. The programme will also deploy a specialized mine risk education team which, alongside the cross-trained NTS teams will work to increase awareness of the dangers of mines amongst all age groups and genders of mine affected communities, particularly targeting adult men and buffer zone settlements. NTS teams will also facilitate explosive ordnance disposal (EOD) through the State Emergency Services (SES), in instances where civilians report the presence of hazardous items in their settlements. During the year HALO will advocate for access to explosives to be able to carry out EOD independent of the SES. As the new Mine Action Law became effective in April 2019 and a new National Mine Action Authority (NMAA) is to be formed, HALO believes it is crucial that the organisation and its members will be equipped with knowledge, skills and procedures in line with international mine action standards. The future NMAA will draw from the institutional capacity already present in the sector and HALO has already delivered quality management training to the Ministry of Defence, non-technical survey training for Ukraine’s State Emergency Service and GIS training for representatives of government bodies. Additional specialised training for the key mine action stakeholders and government representatives will support an effective mine action sector with national authority that is capable of coordinating activities across eastern Ukraine and respond to the humanitarian needs of the civilians. The project will contribute to all three HRP collective outcomes, improving access to services and infrastructure by clearing roads, roadsides and land around electricity and water infrastructure. The removal of explosive items protects civilians from physical injury as a result of mines, while clearing land that can be used for income-generating activities supports access to livelihoods.
