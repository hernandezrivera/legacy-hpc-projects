Project Summary

The United Nations (UN) estimates number of crisis-affected population in need of humanitarian assistance in Syria at 11.06 million people, out of which 4.6 million are in acute need. This figure above on people in need includes 6.1 million protracted internally displaced persons (IDPs). estimated 341,000 spontaneous IDPs return; 75,000 refugees return; 480,000 Palestinian refugees. According to the Syria Response Access Dynamic report by OCHA (December 2018), more than 70% of the people in need of humanitarian assistance are accessible from within Syria.
Overall, 7.9 million people are estimated to be food insecure (including 0.57 million severely food insecure people and 7.3 million moderately food insecure) and 1.9 million people at risk of food insecurity. In the coming year, safeguarding the livelihoods of farmers and herders will continue to be essential to ensuring availability and access to food.
In this context, FAO has designed a project to assist 130,000 small-scale herder households (equivalent to 780,000 people) in Syria. The objective of the proposed action is to protect the livestock assets of these vulnerable herders through the provision of vaccinations and treatment of livestock. The expected project outputs are:
Output 1. Access to veterinary services to protect against and treat transboundary animal diseases (TADs) and common livestock diseases increased benefit 130,000 herders. The output aims at providing animal healthcare, vaccinations against foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), Enterotoxaemia and treatment against ecto endo parasites (as per sector response package under emergency livestock vaccination and treatment).
Output 2. Capacity of veterinarians and veterinary technicians for vaccination and treatment of livestock strengthened. This output aims at training of veterinarians and veterinary technicians and equipping them on TADs and zoonotic diseases control and eradication.
It is expected that the support to protect livestock assets through improving access to animal health services against TADs and common livestock diseases and enhancing the capacity of veterinarians and technicians to conduct vaccinations will help ensure the survival of the remaining livestock herd while increasing their productivity, which will lead to improvement of livelihoods, increase local availability of food, support self-reliance and improve the food and nutrition security of small-scale herders in Syria, especially the most vulnerable.

4 - Needs

Syria’s animal health system has suffered significant damage during the crisis, meaning that many livestock owners do not have access to either preventative animal treatments or vaccination programmes. This has increased the scale of livestock losses and has led to the widespread distress sale of livestock to neighbouring countries. Since 2011, the capacity for the delivery of field veterinary services has been seriously eroded. Consequently the capacity for transboundary animal diseases (TADs) surveillance and response has been totally eroded, including cold chain infrastructure required to carry out livestock vaccination campaigns. Services such as vaccination campaigns, preventive treatment programmes and disease surveillance are now available only in safer areas and often not to all farmers and herders. In addition, the veterinary vaccine production facilities were damaged, which resulted in a shortage of vaccines. As a result, many livestock are left without immunity against high livelihood impact, contagious diseases and subjected to higher risks of infection. The continuing movement of livestock out of the Syrian Arab Republic into neighbouring countries, either with refugees or smuggled out to more lucrative markets, gives rise to increasing concerns about the spread of TADs and zoonosis. Being in the same epidemiological unit and considering the frequent movements of animals between countries, the risk of diseases spreading across the region is high. This will also threaten the health of livestock in Syria due to the high number of unvaccinated animals in the country. This threat does not necessarily manifest itself in major outbreaks and sudden mass deaths of animals, but when uncontrolled, contagious diseases lead to continuous significant reductions in production, resulting in increased losses of young animals (e.g. Enterotoxaemia), higher costs of treatments (e.g. Pasteurellosis) and elevated abortion rates (e.g. Brucellosis).

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

1) Risk: Beneficiaries being targeted as a result of being selected for project/risk of community tension arising from beneficiaries selection. Mitigation measures: Inclusion of different categories of affected population (examples: IDPs and host communities; returnees and refugees; men, women, boys, girls and youth based their specific needs and clear selection criteria agreed upon by local committees).2) Risk: Inclusion and exclusion errors. Mitigation measures: Establish a local beneficiary selection committee with equitable representation of vulnerable groups; putting in place clear and precise guidance on beneficiary selection criteria and process; and ensuring an effective communication for guidance in local language.3) Risk of diversion of assistance/assets. Mitigation measures: Establish community-based beneficiary selection and community-based monitoring.The design of the project has taken into account protection elements, in line with the do not harm principles. To the maximum possible extent, all measures will be taken to limit the risk of beneficiaries being targeted as a result of being selected for the project. Where IDPs are selected, representatives from the host communities will be included as well, to limit possible frictions between the two, and a special attention will be provided to the returnees to help them in their settlement and resuming their production activities through protecting and rehabilitating their assets.. An accountability to affected population system including beneficiaries’ participation and appropriate information and feedback/complaint mechanism will also be put in place to provide communities with a mechanism to express their grievance. This will be used to make adjustments and guide improvement on the implementation of activities

7 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks and assumptions measures:1.Risks: Deterioration of the security situation precluding access to areas of implementation. Probability: Likely. Impact: Severe. Mitigation measures: Preventive: continue to gather information from range of sources; advocate with RC/HC and other stakeholders to regain access. Develop contingency plans in coordination with service providers and, if necessary, move activities to alternate locations.2.Risks: Major displacements that could put more pressure on livelihood strategies and/or limit the impact of activities. Probability: Possible. Impact: Insignificant. Mitigation measures: Continue with advocacy in order to scale up programmes to cover the additional requirements to promote livelihoods opportunities; advocacy with other organizations to include new arrival in assistance programmes.3.Risks: Changes in the conditions related to cross-border movements and operations. Probability: Likely. Impact: Moderate. Mitigation measures: Ensure that service providers have capacity to deliver the programme within the changed legal framework. Diversify activities complementing international procurement with local procurement and ensure different delivery modalities (scaling up cross lines to balance reduced cross borders), through more coordination with the hub in Damascus.4. Risk: Limitations on partnership agreements. Probability: Unlikely. Impact: Major. Mitigation measures: Continuous advocacy and negotiations with Government and counterparts for partners to have access to targeted areas. Establish agreements with counterparts to facilitate the implementation of project activities. 5.Risk: Deterioration of the relations between IDPs and host communities in the period/area concerned. Probability: Unlikely. Impact: Insignificant. Mitigation measures: Assistance is balanced and not directed only to one specific group. Identification of beneficiaries is done in partnership with local communities including IDPs and host communities.
