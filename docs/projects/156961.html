Project Summary

The proposed intervention aims to target vulnerable internally displaced persons (displaced, evacuated, or returnees) and host communities in Northwest Syria (NWS). The overall objective of this project is to ensure that vulnerable children and their families, who have been forced to flee inside Syria, are able to meet their basic needs and are protected. Save the Children (SC) will scale up the acute response to continuous displacements caused by the GoS’ military offensive and the active conflict in NWS. Through the rapid response mechanisms, SC and partners will deliver critical services through different modalities (cash or in-kind) *Please note that based on the needs, Hygiene Kits (HKs) could be distributed in camps and different IDP sites hence this is to be also consulted with CCCM cluster.
Specific activities include:
● Monitor displacements and returns; coordinate food assistance needs with the Food Security Cluster
● Conduct a rapid market assessment to ensure no negative unintended consequences with a cash distribution; conduct rapid needs assessment to determine appropriate mechanism
● Register beneficiaries; refer CP cases to appropriate services
● Distribute relevant and appropriate support (e.g. HK,) to beneficiaries
● Conduct Post-Distribution Monitoring
● Train staff and community members on child safeguarding, safe identification and referral and CP CM supervision and coaching
● Identify and refer most vulnerable children to CP team and other services

Needs

Newly displaced and people with urgent protection considerations are often at risk of not being able to meet their basic needs, particularly if they have not yet become established in a community. SC conducted a rapid needs and market assessment in Idleb and Aleppo governorates over 13th-19th May 2018, which indicated that the majority of the target beneficiaries have been displaced at least twice in the last five years. As per the latest estimates cited by the Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO), there are currently 6.1 million people internally displaced inside Syria. The situation for civilians remains hugely volatile, with over 1.7million people internally displaced over the course of this year to date, alongside thousands of civilians killed or injured. In addition to movements agreed through area-level reconciliation processes, the offensives have led directly to significant displacement and life-threatening living conditions for the affected communities. NWS is facing a potential escalation, with estimates of up to 700,000 people will be displaced in any initial offensive (Turkey Health Cluster Bulletin, August 2018), adding pressure on an already over-stretched humanitarian response within the area. These households (HHs) are expected to move toward the Turkish border, particularly to Salqin, Dana, Afrin and A’zaz, where there are already camps hosting thousands of displaced populations (REACH, September 2018). In NES, there are ongoing displacements from Deir Ez-Zor Governorate to Al-Hasakeh and Raqqa Governorates, including Al Hole, Areesha and Ain Issa camps.

Implementation of activities

SC will monitor the ongoing security situation to anticipate when displacements occur. SC will identify economically vulnerable new arrivals on a rolling basis, through engagement with LCs and community leaders, as well as through referrals from CP and CM outreach teams, and the other sectors of this programme. In determining the selection criteria, it is assumed that HHs with PLW, as well as children under two (the threshold for meeting children’s long-term nutrition needs) and children under five (the threshold of increased morbidity and mortality) are more economically vulnerable than others and that such vulnerability engender more detrimental consequences for the children we are mandated to assist and protect. SC will leverage this activity to provide referrals and outreach to beneficiaries who are eligible to participate in the other cross-sectorial interventions offered within this programme. Through its partner, SC will distribute HKs to newly displaced HHs. In the event of a new displacement or emergency, an alert is shared to SC including simple information on location; expected numbers of affected population; and nature of the emergency. Initial alert information may come from the partners, LCs, other organisations or SC. Following the initial alert, and based on a review of security information, partners will conduct a rapid need assessment to gather information on priority needs among the population. By targeting the most vulnerable following a displacement, and confirming their most pressing needs, SC anticipates that beneficiaries will utilize their grant to meet the most basic needs. SC and partners will also coordinate with other agencies on whether they are planning to respond and communicate plans to distribute HKs to the WASH Cluster. Partners will then orient LCs and community stakeholders on beneficiary selection criteria and sign a MoU for cooperation, setting out clearly the responsibilities of the LC and community, and partners, in the beneficiary selection process, as well as the final agreed targeting criteria. LCs then register beneficiaries, with support from other relevant stakeholders (such as, Relief Committee) on the beneficiary registration form. If a LC does not have the capacity, then Violet will work directly to register beneficiaries according to the criteria. The beneficiary registration form will include basic information on the HH (head of the HH name, family name, father’s name, national ID number, HH demographics etc.) to enable cross checks for duplication. Beneficiaries will be HHs (not individuals). Partners beneficiary selection criteria take several factors into account such as: lack of productive assets; no ability to afford basic needs; no permanent shelter; no support from family members or other agencies; presence of people with disabilities or chronic illnesses in the family; HHs headed by children, females, persons with disabilities or elderly persons; and the presence of PLWs, children under five, and children under two in the HH.

Protection Risk Analysis

Risk: Increased tensions among IDP and host populations due to beneficiary targeting Likelihood = Medium; Impact = HighMitigation measures: Community-based targeting with representative group, community mobilisation/sensitisation on objectives of assistance, monitoring and feedback/complaints systems in place. Risk: Increased risk profile of target beneficiaries results (e.g. theft of assistance provided) Likelihood = Medium; Impact = HighMitigation measures: Low profile for targeting and selection of cash transfer beneficiaries, engagement with beneficiaries on distribution methods, delivery modalities etc.Risk: Female Headed Households (FHHs) excluded from livelihood assistance or cash for workLikelihood = Low; Impact = MediumMitigation measures: Provision of unconditional cash for HHs that are unable to work, inclusion of FHHs in livelihood support accounting for particular barriers (e.g. market and value chain assessments to have a gender lens) Risk: Inclusion and exclusion errors in beneficiary targeting Likelihood: Medium; Impact = HighMitigation measures: Community-based targeting with full verification by project staff; functional feedback and complaints mechanism; clear targeting criteria and SOPs, agreed upon with key stakeholders (if feasible), communicated wherever possible at community level; regular post distribution monitoring exercises undertaken; training for staff on child protection and child labour, including referral of child labour cases. Risk: Increasing prices or declining market functionality leads to reduced ability of cash transfers to cover needs targeted Likelihood: Medium; Impact = HighMitigation measures: Regular market monitoring and analysis, contingency to increase cash transfer value in line with MEB increases if needed Risk: Cash based response has risk of sexual exploitation (in beneficiary selection) and unintended use of cashLikelihood: Medium; Impact = HighMitigation measures: Recruit both male and female implementing staff to engage with beneficiaries (i.e. not only male); train staff on ‘do no harm’ principles as well as on gender sensitivity and child safeguarding; community mobilization and sensitization on humanitarian principles; regular post distribution monitoring; limit modalities to restricted or conditional vouchers; and ensure vendor monitoring. Risk: Humanitarian staff and ‘gate keepers’ are mostly male.Likelihood: High; Impact = HighMitigation measures: Recruitment processes to encourage gender balance of staff; training of staff on gender sensitive approaches; ensuring women are represented in beneficiary selection committees; and dissemination and sensitization on humanitarian principles to gate keepers at various levels.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Safety & Security (S&S) All staff movements must be pre-approved and refresher security trainings are regularly conducted. The Hostile Environment Awareness Training is mandatory for NES deployments; local staff have been trained on Advanced Personal S&S. SC’s partners engage with relevant stakeholders and receive timely updates. SC works on a common response to unacceptable conditions. Airstrikes SC supports partners to immediately report security incidents to be analysed and to develop and implement comprehensive safety plans. Loss of access SC and partners conduct thorough security assessments and maintain updated contingency plans. If necessary, SC will suspend programs and work to resume activities. If this is not viable, SC would assess possibilities for relocation. Only in a worst-case scenario would project termination be considered. Work permits Measures are in place to mitigate the Gov. of Turkey (GoT) legislative environment hindering SC’s ability to operate, I.e. using a third-party HR company to overcome work permit challenges. Diversion of resources to terrorist/ proscribed groups In 2018, interviews were conducted to identify areas for increased risk mitigation; an action plan was developed. SC launched an initiative with OCHA to provide external training on international humanitarian law and negotiation with armed actors. Partners have their own systems of anti-terrorism vetting checks for all contracted suppliers and SC’s Syria office vets against the third-party database for procurements $750+. Shelling Whilst the most frequent attacks of shelling take place in southern rural Idlib and northern rural Hama, no area is immune. Escalation of military activity at Turkey/Syria border SC program locations and contingency plans are under constant review and the Hibernation, Relocation and Evacuation plan is shared with staff. Offices and guesthouses are located away from military bases and have the necessary S&S infrastructure. Attacks by armed groups Security assessments are conducted on potential project sites. Proximity to military locations is monitored and a back-up vehicle is used in high-risk areas. Staff transit must be pre-approved and take place during daylight. Improvised Explosive Devices SC coordinates closely with Tetra Tech, new project locations are cleared by the Mines Advisory Group and follow up trainings in Mine Risk Education are planned for staff. Road accidents SC drivers are tested; daily vehicle checks; all vehicles are equipped with tracking devices and satellite phones; mandatory use of seatbelts. Disruption to cash transfer operations In NES, SC is conducting a review of Hawala delivery to identify mitigation measures/additional suppliers. In NWS, NGOs have seen temporary suspension of Bab Al Hawa.Assumptions Qualified staff available for recruitment Cross border access and maintenance of one-year extension of UNSC Resolution 2449
