Project Summary

While continued political instability, population’s displacements, ongoing armed conflict, and long-standing environmental stress have rendered many of the country’s estimated 15.7 million people acutely food insecure, 2022 presents the potential for drought to be one of the primary drivers of food insecurity in Somalia. Without sustained humanitarian food assistance, 3.5 million people across Somalia are expected to face Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or Emergency (IPC Phase 4) outcomes by the end of 2021.
FSNAU and FEWS NET Post-Gu Assessment in September 2021 projected that nearly 3.5 million Somalis would be in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) and Emergency (IPC Phase 4) through at least December 2021, in the absence of humanitarian assistance.1 That includes 1.1 million Internally Displaced Persons who will face moderate to large food consumption gaps through late 2021.2
Out of the total 3.5 million people in need of urgent food assistance, 640,000 people face significant food consumption gaps.3 In addition, 3.7 million people are classified in IPC phase 2 (Stressed), engaging in negative coping strategies to meet their food and non-food needs, bringing the total number of Somalis experiencing acute food insecurity to 7.2 million by late 2021.
Displacements due to armed conflict and political tensions, drought, and lack of livelihoods are expected to continue in 2022, further exacerbating food insecurity in many areas. It is important to note that this multitude of crises affects women, girls, men and boys differently. Key to note is that women are likely to be forced to take on, or seek opportunities to provide food and water, which will increase their work burden.
Rural populations in particular are experiencing multiple declines in food and income sources. The 2021 Gu harvest in southern Somalia is 60 percent lower than the long-term average for 1995-2020. It is the third lowest Gu harvest since 2010. The lower production in 2021 Gu mainly reflects the impact of the poor rainfall, ongoing civil insecurity, river floods, shortage of farm inputs and pests. In pastoral and agropastoral livelihood zones, although heavy rains between late April and mid-May partially replenished pasture and water resources, these resources are inadequate to support normal livestock production until the start of Deyr season rainfall in October. Furthermore, floods caused further population displacement and damaged crops and farmland in riverine areas of Hiiraan, Shabelle, and Juba regions.
A majority of the estimated 2.9 million Internally Displaced Persons across Somalia are poor with limited livelihood assets, few income earning opportunities, low communal support, and rely heavily on external humanitarian assistance. As a result, one-third of Internally Displaced Persons (in both rural and urban settlements) face moderate to large food consumption gaps through late 2021.
Alta Juba Relief and Rehabilitation organizations aims to provide unconditional food
assistance to 3,000 persons affected by emergencies in Lower-Shabelle region of Somalia, particularly Afgoye district, in order to prevent malnutrition and starvation among the affected households in Lowe Shabelle region of Somalia.

2.1.1 - What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

Cash transfer or vouchers increase food security and asset ownership. It is also cost-effective and allows vulnerable and marginalized households to purchase a more diverse set of food and non-food items. Similarly, cash transfers or vouchers have fewer administrative costs and recipients may prefer the flexibility of cash and improve a wide range of outcomes,

2.1.2 - Explain how the project design considered market functionality?

Market assessment will be done on the planning stage and after every voucher distribution and cash transfers to assess the market prices for the predetermined food ratio for the beneficiaries. The prices of essential basic items will be determined during the assessment.

4.6 - Mitigation Measures

AJRRO will strengthen inclusion of marginalized communities in its humanitarian response by: • Collecting disaggregated data that includes a question on clan affiliation/membership in marginalized communities in order to identify impact on vulnerability and needs. • Mapping IDP sites hosting marginalized communities in order to identify their needs.• Applying a systematic outreach process in registering beneficiaries in order to ensure that vulnerable persons, such as older people, are not excluded • Separating male and female queuing areas and ensuring that female staff presence at distributions so that they are accessible to women and girls. The following measures will be taken to ensure that women and girls are not exposed to sexual violence, including sexual exploitation and abuse • Integrate CT and GBV programming in the design of the project but also in the training of staff.• Include activities and support to ensure sustainable protection outcomes beyond the period of cash transfers (such as referral and enrollment in activities to support income generation).• Consultation with beneficiaries and local communities, including at-risk groups (e.g. women, adolescent girls, people with disabilities) as part of the initial needs assessment and routine monitoring to identify locally relevant risks and protection measures • Mitigate beneficiaries’ exposure to risks from receiving CTs by building in strong qualitative monitoring and feedback mechanisms.Community engagement will be conducted in order to take into account the views and decisions of the target beneficiaries. A hotline will be given to the beneficiaries so that they can give their feedback on issues they want to relay to the organization.
