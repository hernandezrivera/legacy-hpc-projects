Project Summary

The project aims at strengthening the capacities of the National and Community Child Protection Mechanisms in the West Bank, including East Jerusalem, in responding to the rehabilitation and reintegration needs of children affected by Military Detention. The project will specifically support the successful reintegration of 300 ex-detainee Palestinian children within their families and local communities through Child Protection including MHPSS Services and legal support. It will also involve and benefit 100 ministerial employees and 100 Community-Based Organization and NGO staff members through capacity-building awareness raising activities advocating and raising the voices of the children victims of the military detention for comprehensive and highly responsive rehabilitation and reintegration services .

1 - Needs

All Palestinian children face a myriad of protection risks, but one sub-set of children is uniquely vulnerable and suffers consistent violations of their fundamental rights: children who endure the Israeli military detention system. Approximately 800-1,000 children are detained each year, and children as young as 12 years can be prosecuted in the military courts. Israel is the only country in the world that automatically prosecutes children in military courts. There, Palestinian children lack fundamental fair trial rights and protections. Children typically arrive to interrogations bound, blindfolded, and sleep-deprived. They often give confessions after verbal abuse, threats, and physical and psychological violence. They have no right to legal counsel during interrogation, and Israeli military court judges rarely exclude confessions obtained by coercion or torture. Children when detained are placed in adult detentions and no child appropriate services or facilities are provided. Approximately half of all detained children are arrested at night and report physical and psychological abuse during arrest, transfer, and interrogation (according to Military Court Watch). Violations against minors include late night arrests, being transferred to interrogation and detention center without their parents` knowledge, questioning for long periods of time without a lawyer, rest, or food and drink. Confessions are often extracted under threats and intimidation, usually written in Hebrew, a language they do not understand. This situation resulting from the constant human rights violations committed by Israel has continued over the reporting period with no foreseen political solution. Save the Children’s research on the Impact of military detention on the wellbeing of the children reveals the lasting impacts of detention on children’s lives. Owing to the children’s and their families’ willingness to share their experiences and challenges after release, it is now also clear that these experiences have profound and sweeping repercussions on their lives. Some boys and girls feel that irrevocable damage to their lives has been done, and nearly all of them bear emotional or physical scars from their time in detention. This manifests as anxiety, depression, behavioral changes, eating and sleeping disorders, PTSD-Posttraumatic stress disorder, separation anxiety, alienation and acute stress reaction. and physical symptoms including chest pains, exhaustion and numbness.Detention and the subsequent repercussions on health and well-being diminish children’s ability to return to their lives, maintain relationships with their families and friends, and feel secure in their communities. Other complex social dynamics hinder children’s ability to reintegrate back into the fabric of their old lives, such as the normalization of the experience due to the prevalence of the practice of detention and the ubiquitous presence of military personnel and checkpoints across the West Bank. Other children are considered – or regard themselves – as ‘heroes of the Palestinian cause’ after release, the emotional distress that the children experience. Their place in society has shifted – or at least their perception of their place in society – leading to a fractured sense of self and complicated social relations. According to DCI report, at the end of June 2020, 151 Palestinian children were detained in Israeli prisons and detention centers, an increase of six percent from May, according to data released by the Israel Prison Service (IPS). 48 percent of Palestinian child detainees were held in pretrial detention, according to IPS data. Israeli authorities held 79 percent of Palestinian child detainees at prisons and detention centers inside Israel, which amounts to unlawful transfer in violation of the Fourth Geneva Convention. Children continue to endure violations when held in Israeli military detention in the West Bank and East Jerusalem and will even continue to face the psychosocial impact after their release. Even after release, ex-detainee children have little access to psycho-social support and often drop out of school, meaning the impacts of their arrest affect them for years to come. (Exceptions are children who are part of Save the Children`s Rehabilitation and Reintegration program for child ex-detainees, implemented together with the TRC). From testimonies of 739 Palestinian children detained by Israeli forces from the occupied West Bank and prosecuted in Israeli military courts between 2013 and 2018, DCIP found that:73 percent experienced physical violence following arrest95 percent were hand tied86 percent were blindfolded49 percent were detained from their homes in the middle of the night64 percent faced verbal abuse, humiliation, or intimidation74 percent of children were not properly informed of their rights96 percent were interrogated without the presence of a family member20 percent were subject to stress positions49 percent signed documents in Hebrew, a language most Palestinian children don’t understand Once released from prison, children in general develop common symptoms that include: low self-esteem, depression, lack of trust, poor communication skills especially with family members, insecurity, immense fear, loneliness, nightmares, anxiety and sleeping disorders to name a few. The relevant ministries of the Palestinian Authority in charge of the protection of ex-detainee children, among which the Commission of Detainees and ex-Detainees (CDED) and the Ministry of Social Development (MoSD), are not yet able to provide effective and systematic support to child ex-detainees. This results from the still precarious effectiveness of the CPN and the National referral Mechanism for Child Protection; the fund limitations and staff capacities.

2 - Activities

The project will aim at strengthening the capacities of the National and Community Child Protection (CP) Mechanisms in the West Bank, including East Jerusalem and Area C of the West Bank in responding to the rehabilitation and reintegration needs of children victims of Military Detention. The project will support the successful reintegration of ex-detainee Palestinian children within their families and local communities.1- It will target 300 ex-detainee children with Child Protection including Psychosocial support (PSS) Services; among them, 150 children will be assisted with legal support during detention. The project will provide Legal Support to the Children in Military Detention (Legal Representation) and for the Ex-Detainees Legal Counseling and Consultation, mainly for the minors with suspended sentences who will be subject to repetitive administrative definition. 2- In addition, 150 family members/parents will benefit from psychosocial support. The project will also involve 100 ministerial employees and 100 Community-Based Organization and NGO staff members’ awareness raising activities advocating and raising the voices of the children victims of the military detention for comprehensive and highly responsive rehabilitation and reintegration services. Activities will include strengthening resilience and capacity of ex-detainee children to recover the psychological distress and protection risks due to the negative coping strategies, provision of case management services as needed Individual and/or group psychosocial and resilience sessions for ex-detainee including low intense PSS and recreational activities. The children will benefit from 8 to 12 PSS structured sessions, extended over 8 to 12 days based on a weekly basis. The vast majority (90%) of the children victims of Military Detention are males who are more prone to be arrested between the ages 9 to 18 years old (under 18 of the time of arrest). The low intense PSS are structured sessions, as some children might need PFA and /or crisis intervention protocol, usually accomplished over one to three sessions.3- Reintegration services including support for the return to school through catch-up classes and vocational trainings to facilitate access to the education services and employability and labour market placement services. Legal counselling and assistance for detainee and ex-detainee children to be included in the rehabilitation. Improvement of families and communities` awareness and ability to support the social reintegration of child ex-detainees Identification of new cases and evaluation of the psychosocial condition of the families of ex-detainee children 4- Individual and group PSS for the families of ex-detainee children.5- 12 recreational day will be implemented over the life time of the project for the families of ex-detainee children and creation of community support peer support groups Improvement of the child protection system in the West Bank , East Jerusalem and Area C of West Bank. 6- Capacity building of the CDED, MoSD, MoE and Ministry of Labour to strengthen their capacity to respond to the needs of ex-detainee children. This includes stress and trauma crisis management and politically motivated and COVID-19 generated stress and needs (preventative and infection, spread containment measures) and different skills in stress management. skills needs assessment of different targeted groups will be conducted at the initiation phase of the project that will be administered through context specific and tailored communication means (Face to Face or Virtual) with provisions of training kits and materials.7- Improving the coordination between relevant ministries and NGOs members of the CP networks to consolidate the referral mechanism to the services in charge with reintegration of ex-detainee children. SCI and MOSD agreed on setting a system for case management of ex-detainee children based on SoPs for case referral pathway service and case management for children victims of military detention. SCI formed a technical committee that includes MOE, MOL, MOSD and The Commission of Detainees Affairs to provide guidance on CP case management activity and to consolidate the individual referral system for acute and severe protection risk cases that require referral to multisector diversified service provision. 8- Actively participate and support the UNICEF-led CP Working Group and the technical committee on child military detention. The project will have two implementing partners: Treatment and Rehabilitation Center for Victims of Torture (TRC) has been working in partnership with SC since 2020 and will focus on the rehabilitation and reintegration programme for ex-detainee Palestinian children. TRC has developed a unique expertise in this sector and their field offices in all governorates of the West Bank have become the main address for the ex-detainee children and their families Defense for Children – Palestine (DCI-PS) will work in partnership with SCI-oPt on the rehabilitation and reintegration programme for detainee and ex-detainee children in Israeli prisons to provide legal assistance and collect and report data on violations. DCI is a lead actor in the provision of legal services to children experiencing detention; since 2017 they are involved in a collective effort to improve the quality of legal representation for children in Military Courts and have provided trainings for lawyers of different Palestinian agencies on quality standards. SCI is Co Lead the Education Cluster and together with our partner TRC who has long standing history working with the MoE which will enhance the collaboration and coordination among the different stakeholders in the CP/MHPSS WG. All activities inside schools or with school counselors will be done in coordination with the Education Cluster which needs to be regularly updated.

9.2 - Equal and impartial access to aid

To ensure the participation of the targeted people and the equitable access to the services provided, a series of discussion groups and consultation meetings will be conducted at all project stages with girls, boys, women, men, elders and people with disabilities. This will enable communities to raise their concerns and together come up with solutions to ensure that all the interventions do not exacerbate risks or create new risks.To ensure children, youth and adolescent girls’ and boys’ voices are heard and fairly/equally represented, SC will be facilitating discussion with children and youth/adolescent girls with and without disabilities to hear their perspectives, concerns and suggestions regarding the current situation. SC will consider using the Basic Needs Assessment tool, which incorporates child participatory methods and child/adolescents-centred approach. While their leaderships in this is crucial for sustained response, participation of communities, including children and adolescent girls, will be carefully considered given the risks and the need for social distancing. The Gender Advisor will ensure gender mainstreaming in the interventions. SCI’s Field Officer will supervise the implementation in cooperation with the partners to ensure equal access to the project services.Inclusion and equity are dealt with by SCI at two levels; the first through streamlining in existing activities and indicators as part of SC policy to reach the most marginalized, and the other through promoting specific interventions to serve the children with disabilities in communities and schools, to assist in their access to protection services and to education. SCI has designed programmes that addressed the needs of girls and boys with disabilities through specific interventions that included the provision of assistive devices for CwD in cooperation with the MoSD as well as establishing four sensory rooms at governmental schools to support the inclusion of CwD. In addition, SCI has completed a ToT for SCI staff, partners, and MoE and UNRWA staff on SNAP (Special Needs Action Pack). The SNAP is a first step to inclusion, it provides strategies that require no resources and is not based on a diagnosis. In addition, the children national council members are part of the child monitoring groups and have been elected by them through a democratic process. There are two monitoring groups for CwD. In addition to that, seven boys and five girls with disabilities are participating in the other established groups including deaf children and children with physical disabilities. Many of CwD were elected at the local child council at a district level, where one female and four male members became local councils’ members. Their disability included partial deaf, mild mental disability and physical disability. They were provided with additional support and some special attention to ensure their active participation as well as the acceptance from their peers. In some cases, some groups of children were provided with session of the importance of inclusion. Furthermore, children with hearing difficulties were provided with sign language support. The groups received trainings on inclusion from a rights perspective, and received technical sessions through SC partner (QADER) that is specialized in disability. The protection from sexual exploitation and abuse of children is dealt with under our Child Safeguarding policy. This policy is specifically concerned with the Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) of adults. This includes direct or indirect beneficiaries of our programming and adults in the wider communities in which we work. For anyone under 18, refer to the Child Safeguarding Policy. For issues in relation to staff refer to the Global Anti - Harassment policy which covers sexual harassment, SEA and bullying of SCI staff. The PSEA policy contains essential principles and rules that reflect the behaviours and standards expected from employees, trustees, volunteers, interns, trainees, consultants, partners, and other representatives working for, or contracted on behalf of SCI in any capacity and covers our international development and humanitarian work.SCI is committed to reducing the opportunities for abuse and exploitation to happen through effective prevention, ensuring that survivors are not exposed to further harm through good reporting and responses, and ensuring that survivors receive appropriate care. Effective prevention and providing safe responses requires all staff, volunteers, interns, partners and other representatives to be clear about their individual accountabilities and responsibilities. It also requires all senior staff and managers to ensure that their working environments minimise the opportunities for sexual exploitation and abuse, and to ensure that reporting and response do not expose survivors or reporters to any further harm. Sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) is a gross breach of both standards of aid, and of the rights of those in need. The PSEA policy applies 24 hours a day, 7 days a week and 365 days a year. Any breach of the policy will be taken extremely seriously and responded to. Breaches of the policy may be grounds for termination of engagement with SCI. SCI takes the position that early reporting of any concern supports both prevention of further abuse and safe response through the hotline available 24/7 (1800-724-726). There must be increased awareness about reporting, including the importance of reporting of any and all concerns, however apparently minor. SCI’s commitment to Zero Tolerance of sexual exploitation and abuse means all programme design and planning must include a gender power analysis, which also looks at the roles and structure of SCI staff, volunteers, interns and partners and a safeguarding risk assessment SC has a PSEA Focal Point at the country level and the field sites, who is responsible for driving awareness of the policy and on how to report, inviting, receiving and collating all causes for concern. When these collected pieces of information generate a picture of a site, or a programme, or a particular person, the PSEA Focal Point must escalate the matter to trigger further inquiry and/ or investigation.
