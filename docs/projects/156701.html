Project Summary

The project would include activities that would ensure high number of displaced people with disabilities living in the community to be aware of the risks of war remnants and mine actions and are better linked with the medical services provided within the community.
Hurras will make sure that will be able to reach all affected people. Hurras will target 172 schools in Idleb and Aleppo including 15,000 children between 6-18years old including male and female,300,000 beneficiaries (male and female) 40,000 of them from the targeted children’s families and 270,000 beneficiaries including people with disabilities and their families. Moreover, Hurras will be distributing 20,000 magazines included awareness brochures about the mines and 180,000 posters will be distributed in the communities through the local councils and other community leaders. Furthermore, Hurras will reach out to 400 children between 0-18 years old who have physical disabilities due to being exposed to war remnants and mine actions.

Needs

Entering its eighth year of consecutive conflict, the crisis in Syria has been compounded by the extensive and indiscriminate use of various types of explosive weaponry and the deadly threat explosive hazard contamination. Explosive incidents with wide-area effects continue to be used in populated urban and rural areas, increasing casualties and compounding the vulnerability of people on the move, such as those seeking refuge from the conflict, internally displaced persons (IDPs), and those voluntarily returning to unsafe areas. The presence of landmines, unexploded ordnance (UXO), cluster munitions, improvised explosive devices (IEDs), and various other explosive remnants of war (ERW), deprives civilians from accessing basic services, hampers the delivery of humanitarian assistance, and endangers those seeking refuge from violence or returning to areas affected by the conflict, in addition to threatening the lives of humanitarian workers. According to the 2019 Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO), 1 in 2 people in Syria are potentially at risk from the threat of explosive hazards. Based on current population estimations, 10.2 million men, women, boys, and girls live in the 1,980 communities reporting explosive contamination. These communities are spread throughout the country and correlate to areas that have seen most explosive incidents over the past 12 months. The impact of explosive hazards on communities is immediate and devastating, with the loss of community members, an increased number of people with permanent injuries and loss of access to properties, basic services and livelihoods. It also prevents safe delivery of humanitarian aid, affects population movement and limits the scope for recovery and reconstruction efforts. A wide range of infrastructure, such as private property, schools, hospitals, markets, agricultural land, wells and roads may be blocked by explosive hazards, impeding access to education, health services, marketplaces and safe livelihood opportunities. Respondents in the assessed communities reported that agricultural land, roads, and private property were the most contaminated, followed by other public infrastructure, schools, and hospitals . Provision of risk awareness on these hazards is crucial to provide civilians with basic safety messages to reduce the daily risks posed by contamination. The survey of suspected hazardous areas, followed by the marking and clearance of located items is the only way to remove perceived and confirmed threats. Explosive hazard accidents can result in serious injury and death, leading to long-term physical and psychological trauma for survivors and their families. Based on available data from where collection has been possible, 1.5 people are killed and 2 are injured in explosive accidents, with approximately 1 in 3 survivors suffering at least one limb amputation. The number of casualties from explosive accidents has steadily increased the burden on the health sector, in particular, on the provision of specialised services including trauma care, physical rehabilitation, and psychosocial support on the short and longer term. In addition to physical and psychological impact, explosive hazard contamination can have severe socio-economic implications on families and communities, depriving them of livelihoods and sources of income, increasing vulnerabilities. While all population groups are vulnerable to the threat of explosive hazards, certain groups are at a higher risk in correlation with gender and age, as well as social roles and responsibilities. Certain identified activities expose particularly children. In correlation with previous findings, farming, herding, moving or travelling, tampering or playing with items, and rubble removal were reported to be the most at-risk behaviors or activities by communities reporting deaths or injuries from explosive hazards . Implementation of activities

Hurras Network was started as a Non-Governorate Organization dedicated to providing psycho-social support and child protection services. In January 2012 in Daraya, a city close to the capital Damascus, Hurras initiated its activities.After growing its activities, Hurras was registered as NGO in Turkey and established the headquarter office in 2015. It endeavors to raise awareness about children's rights and how to guard their interests in the community, and to establish safe environments for children.Hurras has worked in case management, PSS and NFE activities for long, by the same time Hurras has trained nearly 6000 people on the child protection in emergencies and child protection minimum standards across Syria over the past 4 years. That lead us to introduce child protection principles in the communities and raise awareness about child protection issues, to move to the next step which is mainstreaming of child protection in different sectors. Currently, we are the lead organization of MANAHEL project of Chemonics which aims at child protection mainstreaming in education institutions. Our recent projects- other than MANAHEL- are working in the Northern Area "CBCPS (community-based child protection system)" and "Building a Future for Conflict Affected Children in Syria" in which Hurras provide non-formal education, psycho-social support, and case management. Also, Hurras provide vocational training, life skills, and psycho-social support for adolescents and youths.Moreover, Hurras took the lead in MANAHEL project under Chemonics funding which is targeting 340 schools in Idleb and Aleppo and aims to mainstream protection into education. Also, Hurras had "United Communities for Youth Resilience" which recently ended (July), and "Education and psycho-social support for vulnerable children in Syria" which ended last month (June).Hurras already implemented its Activities in the Southern of Syria (Duma, Dera’a and Al Qunaitrah) but due to escalating of the security situation, Hurras has been suspends all its activities in these areas.Furthermore, HRS is mainly working on Child Protection programs in Syrian response which includes the follows activities: Case Management, Psychosocial support, Capacity building, Non-formal education and awareness raising.All these activities are implemented by 3 offices in Idleb which are Ariha, Termanin and Muarshorin and 1 office in Aleppo which is in Atareb. Recently, Hurras is starting two new projects in Idleb (Ariha, Saraqib and Binish), the main office will be based in Ariha. As well as, HRS had a partnership with Save The Children organization to introduce Child Safe Guarding policy to 7 local partners in Idleb, these experiences helped Hurras in building good relationship with communities, local councils and other international and local NGOs.in the meantime, Hurras launched emergency response plan for Idleb to be able to support children in case of any risk threatening them. This plan including distribution of CP and dignity kits. In this project, Hurras will make sure that will be able to reach all affected people. Hurras will target 172 schools in Idleb and Aleppo including 15,000 children between 6-18years old including male and female,300,000 beneficiaries (male and female) 40,000 of them from the targeted children’s families and 270,000 beneficiaries including people with disabilities and their families. Moreover, Hurras will be distributing 20,000 magazines included awareness brochures about the mines and 180,000 posters will be distributed in the communities through the local councils and other community leaders. Furthermore, Hurras will reach 400 children between 0-18 years old who have physical disabilities due to being exposed to war remnants and mine actions. Hurras has had previous experience in humanitarian mine action projects whereas Hurras included outreach campaigns about protection against mines in its magazine (Tayara Warak) and distribute posters with the same.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Assumptions:• A safe environment allows the staff to implement the activities (low airstrike bombing).• No delay in the provision of financial resources.• Cooperation of local authority.• Engagement of local community with the committees.• Commitment of partner groups to Hurrass SOP.• Availability of services providers or services required as part of case management plan. Risks:Safeguarding incidents (Medium)HRS has a well-established safeguarding allegation system that is accessible by children, families and community members. Each activity is assigned a safeguarding officer and stakeholder are made aware of how to report incidents through multiple ways: online system, verbal communication with safeguarding staff and complaint boxes.Community and local council acceptance (Medium)Before engaging in any new community HRS outreach and community relation teams establish formal relationships with independent local councils and civil entities. The long history and positive reputation of HRS helped in establishing strong relations. Designated staff are assigned the role of dealing with any community conflict and all programs are reviewed prior to implementation to ensure they are both conflict sensitive and safe.The risk to personal safety of field staff (Medium)HRS has a duty of care policy and all staff are trained on safety measures including emergency locatorsSecurity incidents (Medium to High)HRs has a strong security department with qualified staff that monitors the security situation and put in place prevention and response plans. All staff receive safety training and all facilities are screened and provided with first aid and fire extinguishers in addition to a selected safe room and evacuation planInterference or influence by armed groups (Medium)HRs has strong relationships with the community and independent civil entities such as the health directorate, ED, and local councils and has been successful in the last 4 years in mitigating the risk and using such entities as a buffer. In addition, HRS has a clear SOP on how to behave in such incidents and clear disengagement criteria in case the risk could not be mitigated. Poor staff capacity (Medium)HRS invests in 4 weeks training of staff when they join, and ongoing coaching and supervision processes are in place. HRs work closely with partners to identify the capacity building gaps and put in place training programs to fill the gaps.Poor evidence-based and knowledge management due to remote management (Low)HRS has a strong M&E team that is independent of the program team. HRS uses staff from different offices and projects to cross-check its programming, in addition, the area managers who worked for 4 years with HRS are empowered to manage the day to day implementation.A potential loss of money due to theft or airstrikes (Medium)HRS’s finance team limit the cash on hand to a certain ceiling to minimize the risk and rely on an established network of cash transfer offices.
