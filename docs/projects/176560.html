Project Summary

This project that Sudan Evangelical Mission (SEM) shall contribute to addressing the food security gap in Jur River and Wau Counties of Western Bahr El Ghazal State in South Sudan to reach 8500 beneficiaries (4250 male and 4250 female) whose access to food is at risk and are likely to result in their households adopting coping strategies like eating less nutritious food, reducing consumption and/or going for days without food.
We shall contribute to achieving FSL cluster objective CO1: Provide food assistance to prevent famine and improve food consumption, dietary diversity, and coping strategies for vulnerable populations; CO2: Enhance and sustain emergency food production through the complementary vegetable and crop livelihood inputs, fishing, and livestock support in a way that prevents the spread & transmission of COVID 19; CO3: Reduce dependency on food and agricultural inputs to support and strengthen households’ ability to absorb shocks in a way that prevents the spread & transmission of COVID 19.
We will work with households rather than individuals, to ensure that families can work together in the growth and management of crops, the provide crop seeds, as well as socially-distanced agricultural training on how to grow, care for and harvest them; training will take place at demonstration plots in each village. Literacy-sensitive learning key cards will be developed for short-term duration crops, meaning households with low literacy levels can grow their crops without the recurrent physical presence of a Field Officer. We will ensure our materials are literacy-sensitive and thus accessible for those with no or low levels of reading and writing skills.
In South Sudan, women are typically decision-makers in food consumption and nutrition. We do not want to compound long-held stereotypes on gendered labor or add to existing labor burdens placed on women, specifically related to agricultural work and nutrition management. We will therefore train household members together (with social distance measures between households) and - in educating men and women and boys and girls together - encourage the joint responsibility of food security at the household level.

1 - Needs

South Sudan and its people continue to reel from the impacts of years of conflict, violence, and limited development investment. Food insecurity is the main driver behind the number of people in need. Nearly 6.4 million people or 54 percent of the population were acutely food insecure in August 2019, according to the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) analysis. Poor farming practices which are also subsistence in nature coupled with the seasonal armed conflict in Jur River and Wau area has hampered livelihood activities in most parts of South Sudan and left many household vulnerable to food insecurity and low income. Livelihoods have been further eroded by climatic shocks, such as prolonged dry spells and flooding, the economic shock in market prices caused by Covid-19, is likely to exacerbate the severity of acute food insecurity in the Jur River and Wau area. The income level of the households in this area is also low due to prolonged exposure to political instability that raged in South Sudan and deteriorated peoples’ living conditions and affected the livelihood opportunities for both rural and urban populations, thus limiting their purchasing power to buy food. Increased food prices curtail households’ purchasing power, putting households’ access to food at risk, and forcing them to activate coping mechanisms ranging from switching to cheaper, less nutritious foods, to reducing their consumption and/or going for days without food (WFP, 2020). There is therefore little to no access to affordable food, even at a subsistence level.Vegetables, which are essential to a nutritious diet, are rarely grown at the household level in these communities, with most vulnerable rural farmers electing for the less-water dependent and less costly long-duration crops. With food market prices increasing, fewer vegetables will be consumed, meaning a less nutritious diet for many households.

2 - Project activities

Sudan Evangelical Mission (SEM) proposes to undertake the following activities that are aligned to achieve the set FSL cluster objectives:(1) Provide food assistance to prevent famine and improve food consumption, dietary diversity, and coping strategies for 1500 male and 1500 female prevent vulnerable populations (in IPC 5, 4 & some 3) in a way that prevents the spread & transmission of COVID 19 (compulsory use of new SOP & guidelines); IRRM: food, fishing & quick maturing vegetable seeds. (2) Emergency food production: main season agriculture seeds & tool distribution; fishing, vegetable & crop kits; distribute agricultural tools to 2000 IDPs (1000 male and 1000 female) and most vulnerable households in Jur River and Wau county. We will support 2000 vulnerable households (1000 male and 1000 female) with main season agriculture seeds to enhance emergency food production at households; seed varieties like maize, beans, sorghum, and assorted vegetables seeds are the primary target due to high yield, resistance to drought, diseases, suitable for any soil type and easy to multiply as mentioned by community (3) Emergency food & livelihood support: fishing kits/ vegetable kits; rapid response kits (fish kit/ cowpeas & three variety of fast-maturing vegetable seed) Distribute ﬁshing kits to 500 (250 male 250 female) households living along rivers to improve ﬁshing and access to emergency food & livelihood support: ﬁshing kits/ rapid response kits (ﬁshing kits/ cowpeas ). We will support 500 households (250 male 250 female) with three variety of fast-maturing vegetable seed & crop kits to establish kitchen gardens at households level to supplement dietary needs; shall provide short-term duration crop seeds, as well as agricultural training on how to grow, care for and harvest them. Literacy-sensitive learning key cards will be developed for each short-term duration crop, meaning that households with low literacy levels can grow their crops without the physical presence of a Field Officer.(4) Training & capacity building for 500 farmers (250 male 250 female): both adaptive and absorptive on-farm production (farmer/agro-pastoral field schools, demonstration plots), income generation (business skills), vocational training etc. This project shall focus on restoring the food security and strengthening the livelihood capacity building of vulnerable households Capacity building, skills & knowledge development, and community engagement on agriculture will be practical, hands-on training and participatory delivered in village-level demonstration plots for both main crops and vegetable in real-time to maximize comprehension, learning, eﬃciency, and impact delivered at the community level, by local Field Oﬃcers. Vulnerable households will learn agronomic practices including land selection and preparation, planting, tending, harvesting, and post-harvesting handling to reduce post-harvest losses. In South Sudan, women are typically decision-makers in food consumption and nutrition. We do not want to compound long-held stereotypes on gendered labor or add to existing labor burdens placed on women, specifically related to agricultural work and nutrition management. We will therefore train households together and - in educating men and women and boys and girls together - encourage the joint responsibility of food security at the household level.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

While consulting beneficiaries on implementation, and during community awareness-raising, we shall inform the beneficiaries and community members of our stand on zero tolerance of PSEA, and inform them on channels that they can use to report their concerns for the integrity of this project.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

During inputs/seed distribution and training events, field workers will hold meetings for members of households being supported, in which participants can share their thoughts and feedback on food security interventions to date. This means that those with no/low levels of literacy – who cannot contribute to a suggestions box independently - will have a route to feedback. Field workers will conduct a home visit, during which a short and inclusive survey will be taken, in which the thoughts and opinions of those with physical disabilities, who may not be able to attend the public feedback meetings due to mobility challenges, will be captured. Field workers will use a smartphone and Kobo Toolbox, as it collects data offline and saves it in real-time.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

SEM has a dedicated safeguarding Lead based in the Head Office, who manages a confidential hotline dedicated to responding sensitively to safeguarding concerns, complaints, and grievances. The phone number will be shared at public community ‘touchpoints’ such as in village offices and inputs/seeds distribution venues and with the community and religious leaders.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Information gathered from beneficiaries will be collated by the Project Coordinator on a weekly basis and shared with the management team in the weekly meeting. This will be used to adapt interventions and ensure the suitability of the project delivery methodology.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

We will verbally inform our beneficiaries as we interact with them in the community and through written means in posters placed in public spaces on beneficiary complaints/ feedback mechanisms. Community awareness raising will also be another platform for information dissemination of our feedback/ complaints mechanism channels that they can use. Complaint and feedback mechanisms will be established via suggestion boxes that will be strategically placed in public spaces and display of phone numbers for complaint and feedback focal points as well as dissemination of the information to all project stakeholders to provide an avenue where they will freely share complaints and feedback on this project. While consulting beneficiaries on implementation, and during community awareness-raising, we shall inform the beneficiaries and community members of our stand on zero tolerance of PSEA, and inform them on channels that they can use to report their concerns for the integrity of this project.
