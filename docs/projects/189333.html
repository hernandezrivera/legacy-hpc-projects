Project Summary

The project targets 20,0000 individuals in Pibor County (Jonglei), Magwi and Kapoeta East (EES) Nagero and Terekeka Counties (CES), through an integrated approach including nutrition and GBV/protection using in-kind modalities and CVA for distribution of WASH items, hygiene promotion and construction/rehabilitation of water points and sanitation facilities. The latter will incorporate a small component of Cash-For-Work for the construction and rehabilitation of WASH facilities. All interventions will be carried out following Covid-19 preventive measures; social distancing, use of PPE and preventive messaging through IEC.
YEDA will provide WASH kit to 3570 units SAM child/caretaker, explaining to the caretakers how to use the WASH items, including demonstration of the use of chemical products for the treatment of the water inside houses and clearly explain how/when to use the different items. This will be coupled with promotion of basic hygiene practices at household level, including hand hygiene and household water treatment and safe storage. The content of a standard kit will include: (1) one bucket with tap, 20l; (2) one bucket without tap, 20l or one jerrycan (10 or 20l), (3) chlorine tablets for 3 months (e.g. Aquatabs 67 mg), (4) Sedimentation/Disinfection powder for 3 months (e.g. PUR) with one piece of filter clothes and (5) soap, 250 grs per person per month for 3 months. The content of the kit might very according to household size, and could be adapted in case the items are not available from the WASH cluster core pipeline or local market.
Additionally, YEDA will assess the WASH conditions at nutrition facility level and support nutrition partners with the provision of safe water in the facility, by rehabilitating 3 water points at facility level. This will facilitate nutrition programming and reduce the risk of Hospital Acquired Infections (HAIs) transmission.
Concurrently, access to safe water will be improved in payams/communities with high GAM prevalence and
high vulnerability to WASH, by rehabilitating 5 water points, and constructing 15 water points (10 permanent and 5 temporary) and 10 sanitation facilities. All structured will be rehabilitated/constructed with a flood-resilient design, to improve the population’s ability to recover from stresses and shocks.
Concerning water points, quality testing and treatment using Del Agua water kits will be conducted on regular basis. After the initial tests, bacteriological testing will be done on a monthly basis, which will determine the water treatment according to the results (e.g. chlorination where bacteriological contamination). The treated water sources will be monitored daily or according to need, measuring that the Free Residual Chlorine (FRC) at point of use is within recommended standards (0.2–0.5 mg/l).
Furthermore, 9,855 vulnerable people (1,642 HHs) will be targeted with distribution of soap paired with hygiene promotion using the PHAST/CHAST approach to improve hygiene behaviors, to reduce diarrheal diseases and encouraging effective management of WASH services. 60 trained Community Hygiene Promoters (20 per County) will conduct house-to-house visits and hygiene awareness sessions in health and nutrition clinics, on diarrhea prevention, hand washing, MHM, infant and young child feeding (IYCF), water treatment, food storage and waste disposal. Additionally, key messaging including HIV/AIDS, COVID-19 prevention, EVD and cholera prevention will be passed on during distribution and trainings, as an EPR mainstreaming.
Finally, WASH-GBV mitigation will be prioritized in all the targeted counties, after conduction of a site level safety audit, with protection/GVB partners on ground. Under this objective, 2,829 vulnerable women and girls will be reached with improved access to water and/or sanitation after a safety audit.

1 - Needs

The impact of floods occurred in the past three years, linked to the effects of climate change, and high levels of food insecurity has increased water and sanitation needs across South Sudan. This is due to destruction and damage of water and sanitation facilities, which has left people in dire need of WASH services, intensifying the vulnerabilities of marginalised groups. Lack of access to WASH services which increases the likelihood of water-borne diseases, malnutrition and school dropout (HNO 2022).People’s access to safe and improved water and sanitation is critically low in all three counties targeted. Access to WASH facilities is reportedly hindered by long lines and congestion at water collection points, leading to disputes and tensions.Access to water is critical; according to the Humanitarian Needs Overview 2022, about 41% of the South Sudanese population have access to safe water. The economic downturn has exacerbated the disruption of water services, limiting resources available for the maintenance of wells and boreholes, and protection concerns have risen. In fact, about 18% of households, both in rural and in urban settings reporting that they felt unsafe while collecting water (HNO 2022).Additionally, lack of latrines and/or overfull latrines lead to open defecation resulting in waterborne diseases. On average, 80% of the total population has no access to latrines. In States like Jonglei and some areas of EES, the access to latrine is constantly under 10%, which leads to widespread open defecation, with associated risks of GBV and water-born diseases. The lack of sanitation facilities is prominent in remote areas of Pibor, Terekeka and Kapoeta East Counties, among others, where zero to 10% of households reported using latrines. (REACH, 2021). Limited access to appropriate and dignified sanitation and hand washing locations, in particular in urban and peri-urban settings, increases the risk of cholera and other diarrheal or waterborne diseases.WASH-related GBV remains a reality for majority of the population. The findings from the 2021 FSNMS + analysis indicate that access to WASH facilities by women and girls, boys, persons with disabilities and elderly and services remains a challenge. Elderly people were cited as amongst the most vulnerable to protection risks and incidents, especially while accessing WASH facilities (HNO 2022). Women and girls frequently face physical and sexual assault and harassment at water collection points, bathing areas and latrines (Qualitative component of the FSNMS+ 2021). Finally, hygiene practices, and especially hand-washing at critical moments are reportedly low across CES and particularly difficult to follow in Pibor and Kapoeta East Counties, increasing risk of cholera and EVD outbreaks and highlights the need of spreading preventive hygiene messages and establishing hand-washing facilities. The critical health needs also related to the Covid-19 epidemic, insufficient food access, high cases of SAM and GAM, and poor WASH services may contribute high levels of morbidity and mortality if not responded rapidly. Inadequate WASH services contribute not only to disease outbreak, but also to increased SAM cases. Lack of improved sanitation and limited knowledge of hygiene practices contributes to poor feeding habits of children leading to malnutrition. Data from IOM, OCHA and WASH partners revealed that displaced persons are settled in areas without clean water or improved sanitation, and have little opportunity to practice proper hygiene in what turns out to be flooded areas for most of the year.

2 - Project activities

The project activities will include:(1) Assessment, Analysis and Planning: (a) identify needs and gaps, and market functionality; (b) and investigate community norms and practices related to WASH that may increase the risk of GBV; (c) assess the level of participation and leadership of women, adolescent girls and other at-risk groups in WASH, (d) analyze physical safety of and access to WASH facilities to identify associated risks of GBV, (e) assess awareness of WASH staff on basic issues related to GBV, (f) review existing/proposed community outreach material related to WASH to ensure it includes basic information about GBV risk reduction. (2) Resource mobilization: (a1) Mobilizing items from the WASH cluster core pipeline o field warehouses in case of in-kind response or (a2) make arrangements through MoUs with local suppliers for ensuring availability of stocks and fixed prices in case of CVA; (b) training for WASH staff and community WASH groups on the safe design and construction of WASH facilities that mitigate GBV risk, (c) targeting women for job skills training on operation and maintenance of water supply and sanitation.(3) Programming: (a) activity planning involving women and other at-risk groups in the siting, design, construction and maintenance of WASH facilities and in hygiene promotion activities; (b)Training of hand-pump mechanics, Water Management Committees (60% female members) in water resource management/maintenance and HHWT use, and of hygiene promoters; (c) Kit distribution at nutrition facility level or at household level and to unit “caretaker/SAM child”; (d) Hygiene promotion campaigns and distribution of soap; (e) construction/rehabilitation of water points and sanitation facilities using a flood-resistant design; (f) water quality testing and treatment using Del Agua water kits on regular basis; (g) regular community consultations, improving their mechanisms of self-protection to increase the availability and accessibility of water for women, girls and other at-risk groups and strategies to maximizes the safety, privacy and dignity of WASH facilities; (4) Communication and Information Sharing: (a) Consultation with GBV specialist are consulted to identify safe, confidential and appropriate systems of care (i.e. referral pathways) for survivors, (b) GBV messages (including where to report risk and how to access care) are incorporated into hygiene promotion and other WASH related community outreach activities using multiple formats to ensure accessibility.(5) M&E: (a) Monitoring of WASH facility construction/rehabilitation, and monitoring of hygiene promotion activities (house-to-house, CHP sessions); (b) M&E visits to the field by the WASH and M&E team, jointly with community leaders and partners for quality, measuring results against KAP baseline; (c) PDM 4-6 weeks after distribution of famine prevention kits/WASH items; (d) end line evaluation with external consultant to measure impact of the project, documenting best practices while providing evidence-based recommendations for improvement; (e) documentation and reporting to the cluster.NOTE: At all stages, the CHP, WMCs and CRC members, volunteers and YEDA staff will all be provided with PPE (washable masks) and IEC materials in local languages will be used for sensitization in all areas. Gathering of people will be limited to the essential and any activity that involves that will be conducted following the WHO prevention guidelines for Covid-19, including social distancing and provision of PPE for each head of household, as well as hand-washing stations. Each activity will incorporate beneficiary sensitization on disaster risk reduction and Covid-19 prevention, as well asl PSEA, in collaboration with GBV/protection and health partners in the project locations.

5 - Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA)
5.1. Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA)?

Yes

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

In South Sudan, CVA has been proved to be an appropriate form of assistance, because the following preconditions are met: cash is a familiar form of exchange for commodities and services within the community, and it is used to meet WASH needs in areas with functional markets, and when accompanied by a careful planning informed by context analysis, cash assistance does not increase the level of risk or vulnerability faced by any recipient.For this specific project, CVA is preferred to in-kind assistance in areas where markets are functional, there are low risk of conflict and all target beneficiaries have access to the market. The latter includes absence of economic, social, cultural and physical barriers to access. In such areas, CVA can revitalize the local market, creating a multiplier effect that benefits the whole community, and can help restore dignity and coping abilities. It can also ensure more flexibility and rapidity in terms of aid delivery.Evidence shows that in many contexts, cash transfers are more cost-efficient to deliver than in-kind aid as they require fewer staff and lower logistical costs. Additionally, multipurpose cash will give the maximum amount of flexibility to meet the beneficiary needs, as well as choice at a low cost – ensuring value for money. This has been proven by YEDA’s experience in implementing Cash-Based Interventions in Mundri East, Mundri West, Yei and Nagero County. The interventions empowered people by increasing their purchasing power, increasing their resilience to shocks, rehabilitating community assets such as access roads to the market, and providing them with essential skills to be employable in the future, either by NGOs or within the community. Finally, careful project and market monitoring, also through the Complaint, Feedback and Response Mechanisms embedded in this project, will allow the YEDA team to ensure that the grants will be directed and used by the intended beneficiaries (e.g. selecting women as head of households/direct beneficiaries for cash delivery), that suppliers are able to meet the supply demands and to stock quality items in time.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

Market functionality will be considered at all stages of the project cycle: preparedness, planning, design and implementation set-up, Distribution cycle and monitoring, exit and feedback.YEDA has put in place a contingency plan in order to be able to respond rapidly with targeted CVA in cases of crisis, since this kins of response can be implemented quickly on a large scale. Strategic placement of YEDA’s field office in Pibor, as well as the preparation of guidelines and templates, and staff training on them, has been taken on during the preparedness phase of this project. The YEDA’s preparedness plan and guidelines are informed by its participation to the national Cash Working Group and the WASH cluster MBP/cash working group, which together with information collected from field partners, allow us to monitor market functionality in different rural areas.At the planning phase, need and gap analysis will be complemented by a market analysis in all locations, to establish the best way of implementing Cash and Voucher Assistance. The response analysis report will inform the course of action by providing information on; beneficiary needs and preferences for assistance delivery, community and political acceptance of CVA, market conditions and operational conditions, including beneficiary access to the market, and supplier’s ability to restock and willingness to take part to the project with full awareness. Design and implementation set-up will be informed by the response analysis report, which inherently consider market functionality, including all actors involved in the market system where the CVA will be implemented, obstacles to access, protection risks and risk of harm to the local economy by introducing CVA, and related mitigation measures. Community consultations, including representatives of all vulnerable groups, are key at this stage to assure the best outcome of this intervention. Also at this stage, contracted partners and suppliers, such as financial service providers, will be required to establish all relevant operational processes and support mechanisms necessary for successful cash or voucher distribution, in order to ensure timely and efficient delivery. YEDA’s prior experience has shown that it is key that vendors and supplier undergo a careful vetting process and sign a MoU to ensure ability and commitment to provide quality items in a timely manner and at an agreed maximum cost. In this view, a risk analysis must also consider factors that might affect the cash transfer, for instance insecurity on the road and inflation which can cause the cost of items to increase or the exchange rate to be unfavourable. At the stage of distribution, rapid monitoring, monitoring of market prices and the established CFRM will allow a smooth distribution of cash or voucher transfers to beneficiaries and their subsequent exchange/utilisation by beneficiaries to purchase commodities and services. Due to this monitoring mechanisms, it will also be possible to make adjustments to the programme design and implementation processes if deemed necessary and appropriate, after consultation with the WASH cluster coordinators and UN-OCHA.Finally, through the exit strategy developed during programme planning, YEDA will determine if the project will be handed over to local stakeholders, or formally closed. The exit strategy and outcome of the project will be communicated to the beneficiaries and target communities, and will involve restitution and closure of documentation and related contracts. Success stories and lessons learnt will be shared with all stakeholders, donors and cluster coordinators and will inform future programming.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The establishment of a Complaint Feedback and Response Mechanism (CFRM)as an essential component of this project, through the establishment of complaint desks at every distribution center and of the Community Representation Committees will allow to adjust the interventions in the course of action. Such system will see key stakeholder act as a bridge between YEDA and the beneficiaries, in order to inform the organization about the best modalities for aid delivery, as well as issues, concerns and obstacles to the current intervention. For YEDA it is paramount to ensure effectiveness and efficiency in the aid delivery, and ensure that the interventions do no cause any harm to the local economy, social relations and fabric, and beneficiary wellbeing. To this effect, rapid monitoring will also allow to determine if the beneficiaries perceive the aid receive to be adequate, catering their main needs and ensuring that all communications are done appropriately, for instance using appropriate IEC, language and communication channels to reach a given aim. Such aim could include explaining purpose of response, targeting criteria and NGO capacity in order to manage expectations, enforce behavioural change for COVID-19 prevention, effective training in hand-pump rehabilitation, sensitization on the use of the WASH items and materials distributed, explanation on the scope of the response and GBV referral pathways among others. Continuous project monitoring through the collaboration of the CRCs will allow us to determine if the modalities used are appropriate or need to be adjusted to that specific context or situation.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Community consultations will be conducted at inception phase of the project, as integral part of the multi-sector analysis in the targeted project locations. They will not only include the community leaders, but also Focus Group Discussions with representatives of the vulnerable groups (women, youth, elderly and disable persons), in order to make sure that views, challenges and needs of the vulnerable groups are addressed. Also, in each distribution location YEDA will form a Community Representation Committee, including representatives of women/youth/PWD and other vulnerable groups, IDP and host community leaders, and sub-chiefs (50% female). The CRCs provide great insight for activity planning and project design, Complaint Feedback and Response Mechanism (CFRM), as well as context-specific information, changes, and early warning in case of conflict.The CRCs will work with YEDA in order to identification the most vulnerable households to be targeted by in-kind or cash-based interventions, based on the shared vulnerability criteria. The composition of the CRCs will ensure that each vulnerable and minority group is taken under consideration. The CRC will also be responsible for ensuring that IDPs, returnees and host communities are aware of the priorities for the selection of beneficiaries for distribution, and how vulnerable households have been verified. They will also assist the YEDA team in the distribution of WASH items (including a complaint desk), they will report to the YEDA staff on a regular basis about in case of any feedback and complaint about the intervention modalities (both in-kind and CBIs), in order for the project team to be able to make due changes in the project activities, if need be. Finally, the CRCs will assist the YEDA team by mediating in case of discontent among the population in regards to the prioritization of the beneficiaries, quality of items, and any other complaint raised.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

Addressing inequality and protection threats is key in this project. YEDA has trained and continuously reminds its staff on PSEA, including GBV referral pathways to ensure GBV risks are prevented and/or mitigated, and it is already in contact with the GBV FP for any referrals. Under this project, any new staff, volunteers and local partners will be trained in gender integration and protection mainstreaming to better enable them apply a gender and protection lens (do no harm principle) throughout program activities. This also includes a zero tolerance to Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (SEA). Criteria for any activity implementation will consider 3 key components; gender appropriateness, safety and accessibility by all groups. Appropriate, safe and accessible sites and schedules for distribution will be agreed upon in consultation with the elderly, PLW and PSN/PWDs, who normally face difficulties in accessing distribution sites due to distance and SGBV risks. The selection criteria for the beneficiaries will prioritize gender and protections concerns to ensure persons with disability, pregnant and lactating women, female heads of households, and the elderly among other groups with protection concerns receive the assistance needed. Beneficiaries, as well as the community as a whole, will be encouraged to report any suspected behavior/acts of SGBV in their community by humanitarian staff/volunteers, using the confidential CFRM that will be put in place, as well as the different channels (GBV/Protection referral pathways, or PSEA taskforce) for remedial. In order to prevent SEA, YEDA will mostly target female-headed households (widows, single mothers), providing items which are desperately needed to reduce the risk of exchanging sexual favors. During the distributions, the beneficiaries will use separate queues by gender, in order to avoid harassment towards women, while the YEDA volunteers will handpick the most vulnerable people and assist them in receiving and transporting their items. YEDA will also work to ensure that the contents of aid are sensitive to any protection risks, monitored through community assessments and PDMs. YEDA is already in contact with the GBV FP and partners in the targeted locations, and referral pathways will be used to ensure PSEA risks are prevented and mitigated, and will collaborate with GBV/protection partners for joint activity implementation. GBV mitigation will be incorporated in all phases of the project in order to take into account all gender perspectives, ensuring protection of all community members and adopting concrete strategies to mitigate WASH-related GBV risks, either already present in the community, or that might arise from the implementation of the project activities. All activities will include community sensitization on GBV and creation of Community Representative Committees (CRCs), with the aim to reduce dangerous behavior and find common strategies to reduce inequalities and protection threats within the community.Finally, YEDA staff will ensure to inform beneficiaries that the assistance is free of any cost, and confidential complaints, feedback and protection mechanisms are in place to report any violation. Sensitization on GBV, in relation to creating a safe environment for women and girls, will be done with protection partners using gender-tailored messages.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Community consultations will be conducted at the inception phase of the project, including FGDs with representatives of vulnerable groups (women, youth, elderly, PWDs), in order to make sure that views, challenges and needs of the vulnerable groups are addressed. Also, in each distribution location YEDA will form a Community Representation Committee, including representatives of vulnerable groups, IDP/returnee and host community leaders, and sub-chiefs. The CRCs provide great insight for activity planning and project design, Complaint and Feedback Mechanism, as well as context-specific information, changes, and early warning in case of conflict. They will work with YEDA in order to identify the most vulnerable households to be targeted by in-kind or cash-based interventions, based on the shared vulnerability criteria, and will be responsible for ensuring that all stakeholders are aware of the priorities for beneficiary selection, and providing feedback to the community about the project activities. They will also assist the YEDA team in the WASH distribution and other field activities, and assist the YEDA team by mediating in case of discontent among the population in regards to the prioritization of the beneficiaries, quality of items, and any other complaint raised and be part of the project monitoring team. For quality assurance and guidelines’ adherence, a Complaint and Feedback Mechanism will be in place, in consultation with the community to allow beneficiaries to provide feedback on project activities including the conduct of staff. This will be supplemented by continuous monitoring, PDM and end line evaluation (third party monitoring) that will be conducted jointly with community members/local leaders to assess satisfaction, timeliness, quality and impact of our intervention. Data collected will inform action during the activity implementation, while lessons learnt will be shared with the different stakeholders including the WASH cluster to inform and improve its future work.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

To minimize the beneficiaries’ risk of being exposed to violence during registration/distribution as a result of the distance to or location of humanitarian assistance and services, all activity locations and other key activity details will be agreed upon the members of the Community Representation Committees. For instance, the distribution locations, time and days will have to be favourable for all vulnerable categories to minimize their protection risks on the way. The CRCs include representatives of vulnerable cathegories, which will be able to advise appropriately on the matter, and local authorities that will negotiate access and ensure that no armed actors are present at the distribution site. The long-standing positive relationship of YEDA with the local authorities will also be helpful to this end. Additionally, there is often a risk of interference from local leaders and sometimes authorities in the impartial and needs-based selection of beneficiaries. This will be mitigated by leveraging the long-lasting positive relationship that YEDA has maintained with the local authorities during the years, sensitizing the local population on the need to maintain certain prioritization criteria for assistance and on the risk of conflict over resources and negative effects on social cohesion, and by creating project ownership by involving beneficiaries and affected communities at every stage of the project implementation (as local staff, volunteers, trainees, community representatives, etc.). Additionally, data triangulation and third-party monitoring, coupled with a carefully implemented CFRM, will guarantee to always target the intended beneficiaries of the project.
