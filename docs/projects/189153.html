Project Summary

In response to the continued growing humanitarian crisis in South Sudan, Confident Children out of Conflict (CCC) is proposing the following interventions to expand lifesaving Child protection response services to vulnerable populations in Juba, kajo-keji, Lainya and Yei Counties of Central Equatoria State and Ezo and Tambura counties of Western Equatoria state. The complexity of the operating environment requires multifaceted approaches that are tailored to each location including; Family Tracing and Reunification (FTR), Community Based Psychosocial Social Support (CBPSS), and maintaining a functional interim care Centre for Unaccompanied and Separated Children (UASC) and Other most vulnerable children (OVC) in need of protection and response services in Central and western Equatoria State. The overall objective of the proposed one year child protection in emergency intervention is to contribute towards improving the lives of children and caregivers affected by the humanitarian crisis through preventive and remedial child protection in emergency efforts and responsive actions in Central and Western Equatoria State. The proposed project has two outcomes; 1. Unaccompanied and Separated Children (UASC) and Other most Vulnerable Children at risk of grave Child Abuse are supported through FTR, case management, PSS and other Child Protection assistance. 2. Children affected by humanitarian crisis have access to safe healing and learning spaces and appropriate existing services and support in Central and Western Equatoria State. The outcomes of the Project inherently complement and support the objectives of the Child Protection Area of Responsibility (CPAoR) for 2022. CCC will utilize and increase on the existing internal emergency response capacity to staff and be able to provide timely, effective and efficient Child protection (FTR), PSS and ICC services for UASCs during the project period. This will offer an opportunity to provide much needed CPiE humanitarian services in IDPs and Host communities targeted with limited child protection services and strengthen Child Protection interventions in these communities. The child protection intervention has been specifically designed to be integrated within CCC’s existing child protection program in Juba which are aimed at strengthening community based protection mechanisms (CBPM) and facilitate access to information and appropriate services. Through the existing Community Protection Committees (CPC), this provides a platform to establish community based child protection mechanisms (CBCPMs), Child Help Desks and Child Rights clubs (CRC’s) in primary schools to facilitate the identification of and response to child protection concerns, the development of community based mitigation plans, prevention messaging (e.g. on forced early marriages etc.) and child protection surveillance mechanisms. In addition, the CBCPMs will identify and refer vulnerable children in need of individual support. Operate community based safe healing and learning spaces for children that facilitate their recovery, restore a sense of normalcy, and promote their cognitive, physical, social and emotional development and provide parenting support. The Youth/Adolescents will be engaged to provide peer support, recreational activities and referral to other multi sector support services. CCC’s own experience testifies to post-traumatic distress occurring in high numbers in crisis-affected children. Children affected by crisis need to be assisted to use and rely on adequate coping mechanisms, which include developing self-confidence and expression of emotions. An integrated approach to ensure broad community support for psychosocial support to both children and their caregivers is critical to ensure children are adequately cared for. CCC’s ongoing work through child friendly spaces will continue but this project will ensure they are more deeply rooted and owned by the community.

1 - Needs

According to OCHA, in 2022, the humanitarian community in South Sudan estimates that more than two-thirds of South Sudan's population, 8.9 million people, is in need of humanitarian assistance, an increase of 600,000 since 2021 and out of this, 53 percent are children affected by the humanitarian crisis in the country (Humanitarian Needs Overview, February 2022). Localized conflict accounted for most displacement (60%), while further 20 per cent was the result of natural disaster (flooding). 44 percent of the IDPs were displaced by localized conflict to locations in Warrap, and 10 percent in Upper Nile (Humanitarian Needs Overview, February 2022). According to IOM, between January and June 2021, Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM) reported on 268 spontaneous and 11 organized instances of population movement comprising of 411,105 IDPs (82%) and 90,764 returnees (18%) across ten states and Abyei Administrative Area. For instance, in Tambura county, close to 80,000 people have been displaced by fighting since June 2021, with reports of civilians killed and wounded and out of this, an estimated 45,000 people fled to Ezo County, with thousands of others moving to Yambio town, Nagero, Namutina, Wau, Nzara and Moso according to OCHA (Tambura conflict flash update 1, as of 14th September 2021). Notably, the Country Task Force on Monitoring and Reporting on grave child rights violations verified 196 grave violations including the recruitment/use of 129 children (five girls) in 2021, compared to 62 children (one girl) in 2020, presenting a surge in recruitment despite the signing of a Comprehensive Action Plan by the Government to put an end to the six grave child right violations (Humanitarian Needs Overview, February 2022). Besides, Food insecurity levels remain elevated in South Sudan with 5.8 million people expected to face acute food insecurity from December 2020 to March 2021 and 7.24 million from April to July 2021 respectively according to IPC (IPC Acute food insecurity and Acute malnutrition Analysis, Oct 2020 to July 2021, Issued 18, Dec 2020). Under these conditions, children continue to be disproportionately affected by the crisis and exposed to multiple protection risks including recruitment by armed groups, psychosocial distress, family separation, violence, exploitation and displacement. For example, according to the Save the Children International and its partners, the number of documented child protection cases including Unaccompanied and Separated Children (UASC) increased from 26,009 (12,128 girls and 13,881 boys) in 2020 to 30,441 (14,185 girls and 16,256 boys) by the end of 2021, with Unity, Jonglei, Upper Nile, and Lakes states having the highest number of documented child protection cases, including UASCs, child neglect, child labour, domestic violence and child trafficking (CPIMS+). CCC interim Care Center received over 18 cases of girls referred by CP partners or self-referral for protection from early forced marriage and child survivors of sexual violence for protection and comprehensive case management services. CCC is proposing to maintain its specialized child protection programming approach and mainstream child protection measures across other humanitarian sectors/actors (Wash, Education, Nutrition, Health, food security and GBV) to prevent, mitigate and respond to protection threats facing children affected by the humanitarian crisis in South Sudan. To reduce the incidences of child abuses, the project will empower frontline workers in child protection through a mix of interventions at family/community and institutional levels. This project will be implemented in Western Equatoria state specifically in Tambura and Ezo counties considered as severity 4 locations and in Central Equatoria State specifically in Juba, Lanya, kajo-keji and Yei Counties under severity 3 locations by the CPAoR. CCC will render CP servicers such as Case management, PSS, FTR, GBV services for minors for one year in these sites.

2 - Project activities

In order to address the above needs and achieve positive outcomes for children, the following activities will be undertaken: - Firstly, CCC will Provide FTR services for UASC identified in the targeted communities; CCC will identify, document and provide Family Tracing and Reunification (FTR) and follow up services for Unaccompanied and Separated Children (UASC). To ease the FTR complex processes, CCC will set up FTR system in the targeted communities by establishing and strengthening existing community-based CP mechanisms: build the capacity of response network (CP committees, staff and volunteers, community leaders and CBOs) to help prevent and identify family separations as well as facilitate family tracing and monitor reunification of children. CCC will conduct awareness raising sessions for children and caregivers in the targeted communities and orient other actors such as education, food security, and health on how to identify UASC during their respective activities. Lastly, CCC will participate in IRNA to determine the needs and UASC caseloads in the targeted project sites. Secondly, Provision of comprehensive case management services to UASC and OVC in the targeted communities; CCC will support extremely vulnerable children including UASC identified by the response network through comprehensive and age-specific case management services based on their needs. CCC Interim Care Centre (ICC) will remain operational in Juba providing accommodation, meals, medical care, clothing, and other assorted material assistance for the UASCs & OVCs identified or referred by other child protection partners. This will ensure the safety of children during transit for family reunification purposes and similarly, Foster Parents Association or group will be established in the targeted communities to be on standby for response as part of placement of UASC identified. Vulnerable children especially UASC, OVC and disabled with critical protection needs will be referred for specialized and essential services provided by other actors such as health and nutrition, education, food and livelihood. To ensure vulnerable children receive these services, CCC will work with other actors to set up ‘child friendly desks’ at registration and entry points during food distribution, health and nutrition and other services. In addition, CCC will participate in or establish case management referral pathways in Central and Western Equatoria states and case workers and volunteers will be trained on Case management. Thirdly, CCC will Provide Mental Health and Psychosocial Support Services through CFS and ECD activities in communities and schools; Structured community based psychosocial support activities through CFS and ECD activities will be provided for children of different age groups with the aim of facilitating their recovery, restore a sense of normalcy, and promote their cognitive, physical, social and emotional development. The CFS activities will mainly center on structured indoor and outdoor play and sport activities. Basic numeracy and literacy will be provided to both children and parents. In addition, children who require specialized services will be identified and referred using the established and updated referral pathways. In conjunction with CBCPCs, CCC will integrate risk reduction action and monitoring plans to address and monitor the specific child protection concerns and also conduct awareness raising session on child protection in the targeted communities. CCC will also mainstream child protection and psychosocial support activities in schools through capacity building events like training teachers and PTA/SMCs on PSS/PFA and organizing debates, music dance and drama activities with child protection risks mitigation messages. Child Rights Clubs (CRC) and Child Help Desks will be established in primary schools of the targeted communities to facilitate the identification of and response to child protection concerns.

3 - Project indicators

The indicators for provision of FTR services for UASC identified in the targeted communities include; # of UASC identified, registered and documented using the standard IA case management forms. #of children, parents who receive prevention messages on family separation and relevant child protection messages. # of frontline workers from other sectors trained on basic child protection including FTR. The indicators for the provision of comprehensive case management services to UASC and OVC in the targeted communities include; # of children registered and receiving case management services according to the vulnerability criteria. # of children with critical protection needs referred for specialized services. % of cases closed based on the case closure criteria. # of weekly case review meeting conducted with case workers. # of Case workers trained on Case Management (IA CM SOPs, coaching and supervision and ISP). # of newly identified UASC placed in family based care. # of selected and screened foster care provider trained on relevant CPiE topics. % of UASC children who received follow up visits per week. # of UASC re-unified with their primary caregiver or preferred guardian. The indicators for provision of Mental Health and Psychosocial Support Services through CFS and ECD activities in communities and schools include; # of children receiving focused and non-focused PSS activities in communities and schools. # of caregivers/foster families attending PSS/positive parenting sessions. # of adolescents trained on peer support and becoming community agents for change. # of Child/Adolescent Friendly Spaces (CFS) functional and maintained. # of community-based child protection committees/networks (CBCPC/N) formed and functional. # of Youth Clubs/after-school Clubs established and operational in the targeted community. # of CP Help Desks established in schools and communities to provide information on child protection and assist with referrals. # of children and adult reached with awareness raising sessions on child protection and PSS. # of teachers, facilitators, PTA/SMC members and social workers trained on CPiE including PSS/PFA and case management. # of complaint mechanisms and feedback mechanisms put in place.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

CCC will use feedback from the communities to make project decision to fit the needs of the targeted beneficiaries. Beneficiaries feedbacks for instance, about project design, implementation, will shape how the project will be delivered. For example, if beneficiaries complain that their source of livelihood is being affected by daily implementation of project activities such as PSS, CFS, awareness raising, then the project staff and management will device means of how to reduce the frequency of weekly activities. In addition, if the community decides that the staff and volunteer should be recruited from their locations to implement the project because there are competent people within the locality, then the entire field staff to be recruited will come from that specific location. This will enable community ownership of the project.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Individuals living with disabilities; To ensure feedback from People with disabilities, the project staff will consider the type of disability that the beneficiary has and adopt the appropriate strategy of collecting information. Therefore, the project staff will make sure the feedback session is organized somewhere safe and accessible, adapt questions that can be answered with simple response or gestures such as YES or NO or Pointing, have an interpreter with someone from the family or friend and use various means of communication such as images and pictures, visual materials, role play and drawings to explain question and receive feedbacks. Individuals who are not literate; To ensure feedback from individuals who are not literate, the project staff will make sure an interpreter from the family or friend is present during an activity. Local dialect/language per project site will be used to ensure feedback about services or project activities from all participants. In addition, the project staff will use emotional mapping strategy of using ‘story boards or feeling dice’ (with faces of ‘happy, sad and angry expressions’) to receive feedbacks about the experiences or activities. Women and girls; To ensure feedback from women and girls, the staff will organize focus group discussions or individual interviews in the women and girls friendly space. The WGF space provides a safe and private space for women and girls to freely express their feelings about an activity or experience about the project. The project also offers opportunities to Women and girls to freely drop-in to field offices in to give in their feedback about project.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

CCC will disseminate PSEA information to beneficiaries through organizing a training workshop for Training of Trainers (T.o.T) for Community Based Protection Committees comprising of community leaders such as chiefs, camp leaders, teachers, police, and women group. These leaders are usually trusted by communities in conveying messages on social problems such as PSEA. Focus group discussion (FDG) with women and children in the Women/Girls Friendly Space (WGFS) and Child/youth clubs and displaying PSEA posters with prevention and response messages in specific community gathering places are other strategies. All CCC staffs at the field and the main office, volunteers will be oriented and counter sign the PSEA code of conduct before any official employment as a preventative measure.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Provision of project information to the beneficiaries; At the beginning of the project, CCC will launch the child protection project to community representatives with the aim of explaining the objectives, approaches, staffing, duration, budget and the contact details. CCC will use notice boards in primary schools to share information about protection concern and school based CP referral pathways. Social events through music, dance and drama is also another way to be used to disseminate information on child protection. Posters with pictorial child protection preventative messages and referral pathways will be used in the communities during awareness raising due to the high level of illiteracy. Visibility items such as T-shirt, sign posts are ways CCC will adopt to share information about the project to the beneficiaries. Feedback or complaint Mechanisms; CCC will use both formal and informal mechanisms to receive feedbacks from the beneficiaries about the project. The formal level feedback will be collected through official consultation of beneficiary representatives such as camp leaders, chiefs, teachers, women leaders, and police among other community representatives through meetings, participatory monitoring, reflection and evaluation processes, and individual interviews. Suggestion boxes and child help desk will be introduced in the targeted community schools to enable children to give feedback to project staff on how to improve school outreach service. At informal level, beneficiaries (children, community members and leaders) can drop-in to CCC field office in Tambura, Ezo, Yei, Lanya, Kajo-kajo, Juba IDP camps 1&3 and Mangalla IDP camp or main office in Juba where they are able to give private, one-to-one feedback or raise specific concerns and challenges. This mechanism provides a safe and secure space where discussions on sensitive issues, such as abuse and violence, can take place in confidence.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

The risk of lack of access to / awareness of referral services for survivors of protection incidents will be mitigated through organizing monthly school campaigns to reach out many pupils with child protection messages. Mobile awareness raising sessions will be conducted in the IDP camps and in the host communities to reduce the risk of lack of access to awareness on child protection services. The risk that targeted project areas being contaminated with explosive hazards will be mitigated through integrating mine risk education information in child protection messages for children in schools and caregivers in the communities so that they are not exposed to explosives. The risks of women, girls and boys exposed to sexual violence, including sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA), when accessing humanitarian assistance will be mitigated through training staff on PSEA, all staff and contractors sign and adhere to PSEA code of conduct. Any staff or partner investigated and found guilty will be terminated and legal justice pursued against the culprit. Conducting a gender and diversity analysis in coordination with other humanitarian actors will mitigate the risk that marginalized groups, persons with disabilities and older persons, women, boys and girls, do not participate in consultations or decision-making processes for the project. Such analysis will allow the service providers to understand and respond to individuals and groups in the affected community, based on their specific risks, needs and concerns. Gender and diversity analysis will include the participation of persons with disabilities and older persons, women, boys and girls, which ensures protective and inclusive programming
