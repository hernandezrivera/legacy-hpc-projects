Project Summary

Contributing to the Education Sector objectives, the initiative aims to safeguard the right to quality and safe education for 17,000 (female: 9,350, male:7,650) displacement-affected refugee and host community adolescents and youth in Cox’s Bazar, with a specific focus on vulnerable groups aged 12 to 18. Through sector-endorsed curriculum frameworks, NRC will ensure that learners who have dropped out or experienced disruptions in their education can access equitable learning opportunities. Community engagement and teacher training will further enhance learning environments improving learning outcomes.
NRC will address the educational needs of over-aged learners by improving access to quality Myanmar Curriculum Accelerated Learning Programme (MC ALP) and foundational literacy and numeracy, offering tailored and age-appropriate services to facilitate their integration into the mainstream Myanmar curriculum. Community-Based Learning Facilities, home-based learning, and offline platforms will ensure access for the most vulnerable in hard-to-reach areas. Additionally, the programme will provide essential teaching and learning materials, conduct teacher training, engage with communities, and offer psychosocial support. Host community learners will be supported through remedial classes, offline library resources, and WASH interventions.
MC ALP will support over-aged learners who have missed years of education due to displacement, to bridge educational gaps and prepare them for mainstream secondary education. The Education Sector-endorsed fast-tracked curriculum condenses key learning milestones to enable learners to catch up with their peers. Building on NRC’s existing initiative, the foundational literacy and numeracy programme will expand to new areas, targeting the most vulnerable groups—young mothers, female-headed households, persons with disabilities, and other marginalised individuals who lack access to education. Using community-based offline learning hubs, the programme will deliver tailored activities that foster learning and engagement. This mobile approach overcomes barriers such as restricted movement, limited awareness of educational rights, and cultural norms. In partnership with education authorities, NRC will address learning gaps in host community schools through digital hubs offering a range of complementary services that support learners’ academic, emotional, and social development.
NRC’s classroom-based psychosocial support Better Learning Programme (BLP) will further equip learners with coping strategies to address psychosocial distress linked to forced displacement, isolation, and insecurity, ensuring they develop the resilience needed for academic success and long-term well-being. Life Skills will promote personal development, critical thinking, and cognitive skills enhancement, addressing key themes such as personal leadership, identity, and resilience building. Capacity-building for volunteer teachers on pedagogy, inclusive education, child protection, and psychosocial support will support the sustainability of these efforts. NRC’s Safe and Inclusive Programming (SIP) approach will reinforce protection and referral pathways, foster resilience, and integrate child protection practices.
As a member and leader of the Protection Consortium, NRC will collaborate with consortium partners to expand referral pathways, advance education advocacy efforts and promote disability inclusion, ensuring that learners receive tailored support for academic success. Gender-responsive programming will be promoted through sex-disaggregated learning centres, training and recruiting female teachers and awareness campaigns. Community education groups will be formed and capacitated to play a key role in engagement meetings and raising awareness about education activities. Crisis-sensitive planning, management, and coordination will be ensured in collaboration with other actors to deliver quality provision.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

The education needs of refugee adolescents and youth, along with vulnerable host community members in Bangladesh, remain critical and largely unmet. Representing one-third of the refugee population, young people face severe challenges due to prolonged displacement, limited age-appropriate opportunities, and restricted access to quality learning. Among adolescents, especially those aged 15 and above, access to education is extremely constrained, with only 20% reporting any form of learning engagement, according to ISNA 2024. The combined impact of exclusion from education and restrictive policies on income-generating activities has left 90% of youth without sustainable livelihood options, as highlighted in the youth-led assessment of 2023 by NRC. This gap in education opportunities heightens their risk of resorting to adverse coping mechanisms, including early marriage, human trafficking, substance abuse, and forced recruitment into armed groups, resulting in widespread psychosocial distress.Rohingya adolescent girls are particularly affected by cultural norms that undervalue their education, as well as strict movement restrictions after puberty. This has resulted in over 80% of girls aged 12 to 18 being out of school, according to MSNA 2023. The situation is compounded by a lack of gender-sensitive infrastructure and language barriers, as outlined in the JRP 2024, while young women in the host community also face limited opportunities due to conservative cultural expectations and economic constraints. Insufficient menstrual hygiene management resources and limited access to gender-specific psychosocial support further isolate these girls from educational opportunities, restricting their social and economic potential.Many adolescents and youth, having missed years of schooling in Myanmar, find themselves excluded from secondary school opportunities and lacking foundational competencies for further progression. Without age-appropriate bridging education programmes, these learners remain at risk of permanent educational exclusion, unable to transition into pathways that support meaningful future engagement. The shortage of qualified teachers and language barriers create additional challenges, restricting learning quality for older students.Refugee youth face high levels of trauma, fear, and social isolation, which hinder their social and emotional development. The absence of foundational life skills, including problem-solving, leadership, literacy, and numeracy, constrains their potential to thrive both academically and economically, as reported by stakeholders in the youth-led assessment of 2023 by NRC.Without access to suitable educational opportunities, youth face an uncertain future, with limited personal and academic growth that leaves them vulnerable to rights violations and perpetuates cycles of trauma, fear, and hopelessness. Conflict, violence, and forced migration have disrupted their education, employment prospects, and social connectedness, often pushing them into adult responsibilities prematurely. The lack of access to education directly impacts their transition into adulthood, reinforcing the strong link between educational deprivation and limited economic opportunities.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

NRC ensures that learners can claim their right to education regardless of gender, background, or abilities, with physical and emotional safety prioritised so they can learn and thrive. Protecting learners from harm or abuse is a vital part of NRC’s accountability. Physical and emotional safety is recognised as a prerequisite for learning, making it fundamental to NRC’s education response in crisis-affected settings. The project aligns with core protection objectives, incorporating preventive strategies to mitigate risks of negative coping mechanisms by promoting meaningful, structured engagement in safe and inclusive learning environments. NRC is committed to mainstreaming essential protection principles throughout the project lifecycle through the consistent integration of its Safe and Inclusive Programme (SIP) — focusing on safety, dignity, ‘do no harm,’ equitable access, accountability, and active participation. This includes the integration of inclusive approaches, prioritising individuals with disabilities, female learners, and gender-diverse groups to promote the participation of adolescent girls and other marginalised groups. A child- and adolescent-friendly Complaints and Feedback Mechanism (CFM) ensures that beneficiaries’ perspectives shape interventions and programme delivery. To enhance the protective elements of its programming, NRC places strong emphasis on capacity-building within communities, fostering active participation to create safer learning environments. The initiative also includes robust training for volunteers, particularly on the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA), Gender Policy, Code of Conduct, and Child Safeguarding, underscoring NRC’s commitment to ethical, safe, and transparent operations. This structured approach ensures that young learners access education in secure, inclusive settings, reinforcing NRC’s dedication to advancing protection and education outcomes in complex humanitarian contexts.

2.3 - Age & Disability

Vulnerable groups face significant barriers to accessing education, with restrictions impacting gender-diverse groups, persons with disabilities (PWD), and over-aged out-of-school learners. In alignment with NRC’s commitment to inclusive action, this initiative prioritises a holistic approach to age and disability inclusion, ensuring all learners, regardless of physical abilities or age, have equitable access to safe, quality education and support services. Recognising the compounded vulnerabilities faced by PWD, the project is designed to adapt learning environments, approaches, and materials to be accessible to all, integrating principles of the Age, Gender, and Diversity (AGD) approach. This includes modifications to learning spaces to accommodate those with mobility challenges and provision of assistive devices to support visual or auditory impairments, ensuring an inclusive environment that respects the dignity and rights of all learners. NRC’s home-based approach is designed to reach the most vulnerable and those denied access to education, including girls, PWD, and marginalised groups, ensuring the continuation of services throughout various levels of access restrictions. Training for volunteer teachers on inclusive teaching practices will further enhance access and retention. By focusing on age-appropriate, adaptive learning methodologies, the programme allows older adolescents who have missed years of education to re-engage through tailored pathways, bridging gaps in foundational skills. Through these efforts, NRC aims to mitigate barriers that prevent learners from reaching their full potential, contributing to resilience-building among youth who are often left behind due to age or disability. NRC incorporates sex, age, and disability-disaggregated data and uses the Washington Group Questions in its education activities and reporting, ensuring that programme impact is measured and that services are responsive to the specific needs of all learners.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

NRC prioritises accountability to affected populations across its programmes, ensuring that the voices of refugee and host community adolescents and youth influence programme design, implementation, and evaluation. As a key member, NRC actively participates in the Accountability to Affected Population Working Group (AAP WG), attending coordination meetings regularly. We are also part of a specialised working group focused on establishing minimum standards for CFRM.To operationalise this commitment, NRC conducted two youth-led assessments to ensure that their needs and priorities are integrated into programme design. As a co-lead of the Youth Working Group, NRC supported the inclusion of a dedicated youth assessment in the Inter-Sector Needs Assessment, amplifying young people’s voices and ensuring their perspectives are reflected in broader strategic planning.NRC’s Child and Adolescent-Friendly Community Feedback Mechanism (CFM) is an inclusive platform that enables target population to voice their concerns, provide feedback, and influence decision-making. The CFM integrates protection principles by ensuring accessibility, confidentiality, and response timeliness, allowing NRC to respond effectively.Community Education Support Groups (CESG) will engage youth and community leaders in participatory assessments, establishing channels of accountability that reflect local priorities and cultural contexts. A robust Monitoring, Evaluation, and Learning (MEL) system enables NRC to assess progress against intended outcomes, maintain high standards of quality, and implement tools that capture feedback. The system will gather valuable insights on effectiveness and identify areas for improvement, ensuring that interventions remain responsive to the evolving needs.By empowering project participants and establishing mechanisms for participation, NRC aims to build trust and enable young people to become active contributors in shaping educational and protective services.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

NRC is fully committed to integrating the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) across all levels of its programming, with a particular focus on creating safe learning spaces for Rohingya and host community youth. As a member of the PSEA network, NRC actively participates in the Cox’s Bazar-based PSEA network, attending coordination meetings regularly. We are also involved in a specialised working group dedicated to developing child-friendly PSEA. NRC aligns its messaging, uses IEC materials, and submits regular reports to enhance the safety and integrity of all NRC-supported environments. This project implements a comprehensive PSEA strategy, starting with mandatory training for all NRC staff, volunteers, and partners on ethical conduct, the Safeguarding policy, that includes PSEAH, and reporting procedures, in line with NRC’s Code of Conduct. It also includes awareness sessions for adolescents and youth, creating a safe environment where they can learn about their rights, the acceptable and unacceptable behaviour of NRC staff, volunteers, and partners, and the reporting channels available to them. Child-friendly reporting mechanisms are established to ensure accessibility and promote a culture of transparency, particularly within learning spaces. By embedding PSEA principles throughout programme activities, NRC upholds a zero-tolerance policy towards exploitation and abuse, fostering a foundation of trust, accountability, and respect for the rights of learners.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Recognising the increasing impact of climate change, especially on vulnerable populations in Cox’s Bazar, NRC’s programme integrates both climate adaptation and mitigation measures, with a particular focus on raising awareness among youth and equipping them with practical skills for resilience. This includes awareness-raising on environmental responsibility, climate risks, and sustainable practices, designed to promote climate-conscious attitudes and behaviours. NRC is committed to utilising eco-friendly, durable materials in learning facilities and promoting sustainable infrastructure that minimises environmental impact whenever appropriate. As part of its Life Skills programme, community-based projects led by youth will incorporate activities that contribute to the Climate Action strategy. Through these efforts, the programme will equip young people with the skills, knowledge, and attitudes necessary to lead advocacy initiatives and become active agents of environmental protection within their communities.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

NRC is an active member of the Emergency Response and Preparedness Working Group (ERPWG) and remains fully committed to contributing to interagency disaster preparedness and emergency response efforts. NRC’s emergency response team plays a pivotal role in providing capacity-building training for both staff and volunteers, equipping them with enhanced preparedness and response capabilities. The project incorporates Emergency Response and Preparedness (EPR) measures to ensure a rapid and coordinated response. A business continuity plan is in place, outlining organisational readiness to respond to critical disruptions and guiding the response team through essential steps to address the critical needs of people affected by natural and human-made crises. Furthermore, contingency funds are allocated to address the life-saving needs of affected individuals, allowing for immediate access to resources if demands increase. These EPR components collectively enhance the project’s resilience, enabling it to adapt and continue providing aid effectively, even in challenging or rapidly evolving situations.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

NRC's interventions are grounded in the principles of Positive Youth Development and Youth Well-being, designed to adopt a comprehensive approach that caters to the diverse needs of young people. Informed by NRC’s Youth Well-being Framework, the programme aims to create an enabling environment for personal, emotional, and social development through the provision of integrated services. Recognising adolescents and young people as essential agents of change within their communities, NRC is committed to advancing the five key actions outlined under the Compact for Young People. Furthermore, NRC’s emphasis on gender-responsive programming ensures that adolescent girls, who often face heightened restrictions, are actively involved and supported in leadership roles. By fostering a safe, inclusive environment, this initiative encourages youth to take ownership of their learning and development, bridging the gap between educational attainment and social contribution. In doing so, NRC reinforces the importance of youth agency, empowerment, and resilience-building, aligning with the broader humanitarian goal of nurturing future leaders and creating sustainable pathways for young people in displacement settings. Additionally, NRC co-leads a cross-sectoral Youth Working Group (YWG), a platform designed to elevate and address the needs and priorities of youth within humanitarian settings. This YWG is a collaborative effort involving various humanitarian stakeholders, including local and international NGOs, government representatives, youth-led organisations, and community groups, to ensure that youth voices are integral to decision-making processes. By co-leading this group, NRC can advocate for youth-centred approaches across multiple sectors, including education, protection, and health—ensuring that young people’s unique challenges and aspirations are systematically reflected in programme designs, policies, and service delivery frameworks.
