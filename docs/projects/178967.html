Project Summary

DRC is aiming to provide integrated protection services, to foster social cohesion and community participation, and inclusion. By which DRC interventions will assist in increasing access to life-saving emergency and life sustaining assistance, with the aim of maximizing the impact of humanitarian actions. DRC has the main office in Damascus and three working spaces across three governorates: Homs, Daraa, and Aleppo, while maintaining emergency response through Aleppo to Idleb and Hama. A total of 3,920 beneficiaries will be reached in different locations with a 3 and above severity scale where DRC will prevent, mitigate, and respond to acute protection risks aggravated by the COVID-19 pandemic, contributing to a strengthened protective environment for conflict and displacement affected individuals. DRC will achieve this through the following outcomes: 1. The well-being and coping capacities of vulnerable men, women, girls, and boys are enhanced through the provision of psychosocial support services. DRC has developed new PSS curricula to improve the well-being and coping capacities of men, women, girls, and boys, and with more age, gender, and diversity focus such as gender support groups, and stress management and empowerment for adolescents and children, as well as individual counseling for individuals requiring more targeted support.2. Men, women, girls, and boys are protected from the physical, mental, and social impacts of violence, abuse, exploitation, exposure to explosive hazards, and other protection threats through the provision of case management and Explosive Ordnance Risk Education.DRC will provide child protection, and human rights case management support to the most vulnerable men, women, girls, and boys that encounter protection threats such as violence, exploitation, abuse, neglect, psychological, and emotional harm, those at risk of eviction threats, with a view that severity and urgency of threats will be compounded by the effects of COVID-19 containment measures. DRC will also aim to reduce the impact of EO on civilian populations in Syria, particularly as communities resort to more risk-taking activities as economic pressure heightens as a result of COVID-19 and the economic crisis in Syria. DRC will maintain its inclusive, community-based approaches, implementing activities with physical distancing safeguards in community centres where persons of concern can seek assistance. DRC continues to advocate with the Government of Syria for the expansion of humanitarian access to underserved areas. For access at the local level, DRC engages with mukhtars, governors, line ministries, and SARC branches. DRC has engaged in discussions with the United Nations Mine Action Service (UNMAS) and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs (MoFA) for further work on the threat of explosive hazards in Syria.

4 - Needs

According to SYRIA 2021 Needs and Response Summary, 13.1 million Syrian people are in need of protection. Each year of the crisis has a compounding effect on protection needs and increases complexity creating a longer-lasting impact of unmet needs. Entering its tenth year, the impact of present and past hostilities on civilians remains the principal driver of humanitarian needs in Syria compounded by the covid-19 pandemic and increasing financial hardships. These economic hardships have been compounded by multiple shocks over the past 12 months, including the financial crisis and Covid-19 preventive measures. 6.7 million people remain internally displaced. According to SYRIA 2021 Needs and Response Summary, 27% of households report that children show signs of psychological distress. According to Syria HRP (December 2020), an estimated 2.5 million children face elevated protection risks. One in three children is displaced, with the physical and mental impact of displacement affecting individual growth and learning. According to the 2021 HNO, one in two people in Syria is threatened by EO. While data is still limited, the combination of several sources of information allows for an initial gender, age, and diversity analysis of casualty trends. Displaced persons are at increased risk, given the lack of knowledge they have of the area. According to different sources, children make up between 24% and 33% of known casualties. Children, especially boys, also engage in labor activities such as working on farmlands, scrap metal collection, and fruit picking, which increase their exposure. Agricultural land, roads, and private properties are the most contaminated types of land.

5 - Implementation of activities

DRC will provide structured and sustained PSS for children, child-led community-based protection initiatives, child protection case management including referral, and RE. DRC will conduct structured PSS activities such as under the Rainbow and Youth Program courses with vulnerable, mainly out-of-school children and adolescents with equal participation of girls and boys. DRC will implement child protection case management in the Community center for children at high risk of multiple intersecting protection concerns such as child marriage, risk of violence, and having profound psychosocial support needs due to experience of trauma. DRC will specifically target children and adolescents, boys and girls, including host communities, IDPs, returnees, and refugees. DRC will ensure services are accessible for children with disabilities, victims of explosive devices, unaccompanied and separated children, and marginalized groups. Where and when possible, DRC will provide awareness on covid-19 prevention in line with WHO regulations. DRC will implement Explosive Ordnance Risk Education activities addressing the threat posed by the presence of EO that is a designated priority for HMA interventions RE awareness-raising sessions will be participatory and educational sessions for groups of 5-25 participants, with the help of audio and visual aid to improve recognition of EO and identification of risk indicators and factors, and safe behaviors. DRC will approach individuals to become EO CFP through training in basic RE meant to prepare them to have the tools and techniques to share safety messages through their existing channels and beyond, as well as identify additional ways to function as role models of EO safe behavior.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

1. Child labor, including recruitment and use of children in armed conflict. Likelihood: High, Impact: High. Mitigation: DRC provides all children regular training to self-identify protection risks and collaboratively mitigate and identify solutions impacting them to ensure capacity is sustained beyond DRC presence.2. Girls and boys, including adolescents and children with disabilities, face a heightened risk of other forms of violence, abuse, neglect, and exploitation. Likelihood: medium. Impact: High. Mitigation: Through regular committee meetings and community campaigns, children will actively participate in addressing protection concerns that they have themselves identified and empower themselves to be active observers of the protection risks they face in their daily lives. Children will also be equipped with the skills to assess the threats and develop strategies to address them.3.Potential for ERW contamination in targeted locations in Syria, causing risk to beneficiaries and DRC staff. Likelihood: High. Impact: high. Mitigation: Training (through DRC’s internal risk education unit) of DRC staff delivering activities. Close liaison with area-based humanitarian coordination mechanisms and other actors active in the target areas to gain up-to-date information about possible risk4. COVID-19 includes enforcement of country-wide lockdowns that will hinder access to persons of concern and in-person services and that will result in a larger number of persons with heightened vulnerability. Likelihood: Medium, Impact: Medium. Mitigation: DRC has adapted service modalities so that services can be conducted remotely and will continue to generate protection analysis to monitor context and respond to needs in a timely way.

Risk: COVID 19 pandemic will Increase the prevalence of violence in the home or family, including intimate partner violence (IPV), the loss of employment, restricted access to essential services, and restrictions on freedom of movement can place significant added stress on a family unit and result in the use of negative coping mechanisms.Mitigation: support family members to ensure that COVID-19 prevention and control measures do not exacerbate harmful norms, provide them with psychosocial support, prepare to respond to possible surges in gender-based violence and sexual exploitation and abuse; and ensure that vulnerable populations are included in national surveillance, preparedness, and response plans and activities.DRC at the country level is regularly verifying that critical elements of prevention and response plans to sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) are in place and prepare for contingencies where they are significantly impacted by the pandemic through training (including refresher) on Code of Conduct, Child Safeguarding and PSEA policies; transparent response to SEA complaints; GBV mainstreaming in operational spaces/activities through Feedback and Complaints Reporting Mechanism to ensure beneficiaries and focus is given to facilitating access for women, girls, and other particularly vulnerable groups who face cultural and social barriers with regards to reporting violations knows how to how to report violations of rights, ethics, principles or standards safely.

8 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risk: Targeting of most vulnerable not possible due to externally imposed restrictions (e.g. access) Mitigation: Adherence to clear vulnerability criteria; geographical targeting of areas of high vulnerability (e.g. active hostilities, returnee communities, overburdened host communities); dialogue with authorities on adherence to humanitarian needs-based targeting. Assumption: Qualified, well-trained staff are available in areas of intervention Mitigation: Remuneration and non-cash benefits (e.g training) sufficient to facilitate the recruitment of staff in new areas; continual training and supervision ensured to retain and maintain quality. Risk: Humanitarian space is further squeezed with increased restrictions on access. Mitigation: Dialogue with authorities on the importance of humanitarian access and protection space; coordinated advocacy with DINGOs, Risk: significant changes in lines of control create an influx of IDPs into DRC areas of intervention, exceeding the capacity to respond. Mitigation: Endorsed Emergency Preparedness and Response Plan in place; an emergency roster of capacitated staff available.
