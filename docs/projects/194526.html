Project Summary

South Sudan remains impacted by fragility, economic stagnation and instability. Poverty is ubiquitous and is worsened by internal, external shocks. Inadequate access to continuous basic services, food insecurity, reported diseases outbreaks, alerts issued for Ebola, lingering pandemic (COVID-19) exacerbated the situation further. In the year 2023 an estimated 7.76 million people (62.7% of the population) are likely to face Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse food insecurity. A total of 2,175,546 children and women (1,469,919 in the host community, 315,503 returnees and 390,125 internally displaced people -IDPs) will require lifesaving nutrition care and treatment. These include 353, 606 children for severe acute malnutrition (SAM), 1,084,129 and 737,812 pregnant and lactating women (PLW) for moderate acute malnutrition (MAM). (October 2022 IPC Analysis; HNO-SS 2022).
In matters of underlying/underpinning causes or factors of malnutrition respectively, socio-cultural factors related to child feeding and maternal health (breast-feeding, food taboos) are widespread. Lack of education for mothers, unprecedented influx of displaced population, inaccessibility and inequity in the distribution of health care services) are pervasive. The country faces high levels of food insecurity where only too few children 6-23 months (4.6%) have access to minimum acceptable diet diversity (MADD) for them to grow and develop well. Inadequate access to safe clean water, hygiene and sanitation to prevent waterborne diseases, high morbidity rate among under-five children with 62.7% of children reported sick from one or more illness and 62% of the households could not access health care in past the 3 months due several reasons including distance, cost, insecurity, disability, and ill equipped and staffed health facilities (HNO,2022). This conjunction of factors accounts for the higher incidence of infant and Under Five (U5) malnutrition.
Five (U5) malnutrition.
In Bor South, Twic East and Pibor Counties of Jonglei State and Twic Counties of Warrap state, the 2022 FSNMS Suggests GAM rate are > than 15% and SAM > than 3% as per WFH-MUAC (far above W.H.O emergency threshold). In this proposed plan JAM will reach out to 184,688 individuals including persons leaving with disability (PWD) at a cost of USD 660,000. In line with Strategic objective 1, 2 and 3; JAM endeavors to contribute to decrease GAM among children under the age of 5 years and PLW in prioritized locations. This project is embedded to nutrition Sector Objective (SO)1,2,3 and 4; JAM will support Blanket Supplementary Feeding Program (BSFP) to 18,974 under 5 of age children and 15,811 PLW in Twic and Twic East. The project will strengthen care practices and services for maternal, infant and young child nutrition (MIYCN) by improving management of peer groups (MTMSG, FTFSG); actualizing of Positive Deviance Study (PD) enquiry finding, scaling-up SBCC to 27,394 primary caregivers of children aged 0-23 months and MIYCN counselling. The plan is to reach 38,809 boys and girls aged 6-59 months to receive Vitamin A, and 31,960 boys to be dewormed and GMP.
In SO2, 22,314 (girls and boys 6-59 months of age and 15,788 pregnant and lactating women (PLW) will be enrolled within the 39 static sites and 5 mobile units which will be established to support both (TSFP for 12,898 MAM Under 5 and 15,788 PLW) and 9,416 SAM in outpatient therapeutic program (OTP), Pibor. Up to 152 CNV will be recruited; demonstration and home gardens at nutrition sites and community level will be scaled-up. Improve on family MUAC, data reporting with integration to DHIS 2 will be supported. In SO4 and 70% (SAM) and 50% (MAM U5) caseload will undergo malaria testing and eligible cases treated. The plan is to distribute Long Lasting Treated Mosquito Nets (LLITNs), WASH and ECD kits to AM children and lastly linking the caregivers to FSL, Health, and Protection services.

1 - Needs

South Sudan (SS) continues to face overlapping crises including floods, conflict, and food insecurity – all contributing to persisting high levels of malnutrition. Food Security and Nutrition Monitoring System (FSNMS) Round and the 2022 SMART surveys show that the proportion of children 6-59 months suffering from acute malnutrition remains above the emergency threshold of 15 percent (very high acute malnutrition level- WHO classification) at 16.0% (15.4 -16.7) in lean season. Jonglei, State Counties like Bor South, Twic East and Pibor County; Twic in Warrap State are still facing high GAM rate classifying the state and the Counties at Very High and High in terms of Priority need and level of Vulnerability. The SMART Survey and FSNMS (Food Security and Nutrition Monitoring System) conducted in the year 2022, Suggests that Bor South, Twic East, Pibor County has high GAM of 22.3%, SAM 3.3%; Twic East GAM of 20.7%, SAM of 3.2%; Pibor GAM 18.5%, SAM 6.7%; and Twic GAM level of 18.3% and SAM 5.0% respectively. Far away above W.H.O emergency threshold. Conflict, inter-ethnic violence and communicable disease has had a devastating impact on Jonglei and Warrap State; Widespread Prolonged flood-related crop loss and the impact of COVID-19 – including 40% hyperinflation – means that the year 2022 is still a starving year. Upon secondary review of nutrition cluster (PIN) for the year 2023 – Twic, Twic East and Pibor has demonstrated increase burden for SAM, MAM, and BSFP for both Children under the age of 5 years, Pregnant and Lactating women and this includes persons leaving with disability among the mentioned group. Twic has burden of 48,886 (2022); Pibor has increased burden from 78,218 in 2021 to 89,339 in 2022; while Twic East County has a burden of 27,040. Although Bor South had a burden of 111,112 in the years 2021; there is still acute malnutrition need and burden for 81,768 in the year 2022. In, Pibor County the SAM burden has increased from 9,9195 in the year 2022 to 10,284 and this similar to MAM burden for Under 5 and PLW of 17,486 and 22,555 for the year 2022 respectively. Access to food remains to be a challenge in the County, as such, 99% of households reported using consumption-based coping strategies, with 72% reported begging or being generally exhausted to access food (REACH&FSLC, February 2021); and 86% adopting Emergency-level coping strategy, including selling land for food (IMC, SMART Survey March 2021).The under-five mortality rate remains high due to preventable diseases, such as diarrhea, malaria and pneumonia. The destruction of WASH facilities, inadequate hygiene and sanitation facilities and lack of accessibility to clean drinking water all contribute to poor health conditions and increase Malnutrition and this is worsened by poor coverage in health services. To undress the unmet need, funds are required; also there is need to establish static and mobile nutrition sites is key in provision of both nutrition specific and sensitivity intervention. The need to recruit staff, optimize the use of health and the 39+ nutrition sites as platforms to provide and scale up of prevention and lifesaving Maternal, Infant and Young Child Nutrition (MIYCN to address barriers, promote breastfeeding through counselling; Supplementation, deworming and treatment of Acute Malnutrition to both Under 5 and PLW through CMAM components, including BSFP (Blanket supplementary Feeding Program is paramount. The need to Strengthen inter-sectoral approach and collaboration is critical (HNO, 2022); this includes to enhance malaria screening and treatment; training and promote access to a backyard kitchen garden through integration with FSL; to promote and provide clean water and sanitation in the nutrition sites and caregivers; need for sensitization and awareness creation on hygiene promotions; to promoted child stimulation as part of early child development (ECD).

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

Maintaining staff and beneficiary safety is paramount; Using the safeguarding policies and procedures in place; the Gender and Protection and Accountability Coordinator, Sector leads will be superintending the protection of the target groups and ensure do no harm is correctly integrated. Project staff and each sector lead will be trained on protection, gender equality (mainstreaming), with a focus on the PSEA, child protection, and GBV. The project applies a strong community based participatory approaches from the formation of the project to the monitoring and evaluation stage. The integration of protection and gender staff in the project team will help the implementation of key activities to apply proper safe programming and Do No Harm approaches. In the nutrition component, community consultations with women, men, girls, and boys of all ages, Mother to Mother Support Group members and PLWD, will take part in safety audit to determine the service safety. Such platforms will also be used for awareness raising on available CRM reporting channels and referral mechanisms.Nutrition services will include confidential entry points and the facility staff will be trained to faster detect indicators of violence and to be able to manage SGBV cases. All the involved staff like CMAM Nurses, Nutrition Assistance, Nutrition officers; community workers such as the CNV, Lead Mothers, MIYCN will be trained to provide beneficiaries information; nutrition services, will help to manage SGBV cases and provide support to GBV victims through utilization of County Referral Pathway. The participation (consultation) of community members, especially women and girls, will ensure a gender-sensitive and culturally appropriate project approach. Sex, age, and disability disaggregated (SADDD) data will be collected by accessing facility registers such as mobility registers, program admission registers and referral slips. The raw data is then shared and uploaded on the nutrition management information system—standard systems within the respective clusters. These standard tools support the collection of disaggregated data which allows the team to monitor gender equality. For the PSEA, safeguarding and child protection, staff will share information using the organization Policy to the field focal person who will use the stipulated referral pathway and referral cards/ slips and other modalities to provide information in a confidential way; it is expected that the shared information through the referral pathway and communication mechanism will trigger support to the survivor and handling the perpetrator in a confidential manner to ensure do no harm.

Having accountability framework translate in complying to some principles of Core Humanitarian standards including Principle number 5. 8.11. Mitigation Measures.

The above risks were not selected due to organization structures that have been put in place to comply with the humanitarian principles mainly at the accountability sections, however the following mitigations will be put in Place:-Operational: The primary risk is one of accessibility, compounded by topographical challenges, flooding, and lack of access roads. To mitigate, project teams operating in Pibor County are divided between Pibor and Boma Field Offices to ensure reach and timely delivery of services. Motor boats and canoes are kept to reach flooded communities, while teams travel on foot to reach villages which have limited to no road access. To ensure availability of critical supplies all-year, they are prepositioned in sufficient quantity, when possible, during the dry season. If faced with supply depletion during the rainy season, necessary materials are airlifted using WFP/UNHAS support. Security: Insecurity in project areas can threaten the lives of program staff, limit access to the population, disrupt services and cause delay in overall implementation. To mitigate against this, JAM will work closely with relevant security teams (including with the UNMISS, UNDSS and government officials) to obtain most up-to-date information regarding project locations prior to scheduling project activities. Faced with active conflict, all personnel (and equipment, where possible) are relocated to secure UN compounds, or relocated to Juba where possible, with temporary shift in implementation approach of in consultation with the relevant clusters. At the start of the project; pre-implementation meeting will be conducted and the beneficiaries will not be required to offer anything in exchange of the project’s assistance. All staff sign the Code of Conduct, are trained and expected to adhere to the Safeguarding Policy thus holding them accountable. Complaints desks and accountability mechanisms will be set up, beneficiaries informed of their rights and how to report any violations.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

JAM plans to strengthen its Community Disaster Rapid Response mechanism through adaptation of previous lessons learnt and contribute to lower the flood related displacement in the proposed locations. In the year 2022, the use of previous lesson learnt; the updating of internal disaster related Contingency plan will be considered. In the previous lesson learnt- Participation of caregivers that includes men, women, mothers and elders was essential. The able household members, were engaged during early warning system activities for instance to establish dykes; moving house structures to hire ground; nutrition staff, volunteers and community workers followed the population to the higher ground and provided services on these places. Prepositioning of nutrition supplies and commodities in advance, prior to wet season, use of alternative means of movement like speed boats and satellite phones to monitored and collect data was considered. JAM endeavor to train the caregivers who are also community member on early warning system; through establishment of community DRR Committees, nutrition community committee members including MTMSG member will be advised to join and participate in CMDRR committees. The training will include fostering of these groups and making them functional and be able to use early warning systems to make decisions. The organization will work with incoordination with other partners that includes IOM, UNOPS who are supporting HH with children suffering from AM to develop community assets, that are in line to improving access to services during flood and reducing displacements. Establishment of nutrition sites will include, local authority engagement through discussion to enable the new structures constructed on higher ground. The annual review and operationalization of flood season contingency plan will be conducted; supplies and basic commodities will be facilitated in advance to ensure population are not displaced because of supplies and search for services. JAM also intend to work with other partners supporting CMDRR and support awareness raising; train caregivers, community members' local authority and nutrition staff; and support the community led initiative on development of CMDRR.

The nutrition staff will be encouraged to work alongside agricultural extension workers and FSL staff to achieve this part of capacity building, skills & knowledge development and community engagement. The project intends to integrate fish harvesting activities that can increase protein intakes and diet diversity. Nutrition activities will promote agricultural crops like vegetables, carbohydrates to nutrition program beneficiaries; Provide cooking demonstration using locally produced crops in nutrition programs and involving women and men and referring nutrition beneficiaries (families) to agriculture (FSL) program activities in Pibor County. Through nutrition education and behavior change communication it will sensitize the community to ensure that food production is consumed at the household level and thus assisting the Agriculture FSL sector in meeting nutrition objectives. The project will also refer beneficiaries to access foods that are energy, protein or micronutrient-rich, including fortified complementary foods. It is expected that through the integrated effort there will be decrease proportion of populations facing IPC 4 and 5.
