Project Summary

The proposed response plan seeks to contribute to food security and livelihoods of the persons in need (IPC3+) in Renk, Ulang, Nasir counties of Upper Nile state and Ayod, Bor South, Uror, Twic East, Duk and Nyirol counties in Jonglei state of South Sudan. Achievements will be through CO1: Conditional and conditional food assistance, CO2: Increased food production through in kind support of farming inputs, fishing kits, livestock restock treatment and vaccination, CO3: Capacity Building on Good Agronomic Practices for both trainers and farmers, training of community animal health workers, market support through livestock and seed fairs and strengthening livestock, fishery and farming value chains through investment of post-harvest handling equipment and technologies and anticipatory actions on disaster risk reduction, climate change adaptation and ecosystem management and restoration through tree planting and agroforestry, dike construction , dissemination of early warning signs among others.
The targeted beneficiaries are 20,000 households (individuals; 24% women, 16% men, 36% girls and 24% boys) consisting of 10,000 hosts communities, 6,000 returnees, 2,000 refugees and 2,000 IDPs. Beneficiary by type will be determined through baseline surveys.
Community based participatory approach will be adopted through Accountability to Affected Population (AAP) committees (consisting of RRC representatives, Boma chiefs, Women leaders, Youth Leaders, County Agricultural Department Representative) and through Project management committees. Indicators like household status (host, IDP, returnees and refugees), gender markers (targeted male and female) and special/minority groups like Child-headed, Elderly and Female-headed households under IPC 3+ will be screened before approval of the beneficiary list.
Continuous monitoring will be done throughout the project cycles to ensure quality project outcomes are realised within the allocated timeframe. Community representative committees (AAPs and PMCs) and donors will take part in the monitoring process and act as a platform for complaint-feedback mechanisms. This feedback will be included in the implementation and project designs where applicable.
Sustainability of the project will be realised through active involvement of the community stakeholders throughout the project cycle making them adopt and propagate the knowledge acquired.

1. Needs

According to IPC report (November 2023), an estimated 5.83 million people (46 percent of the population) are experiencing high levels of acute food insecurity classified as IPC Phase 3 or above (Crisis or worse), with 1.64 million people in IPC Phase 4 (Emergency). The reports projections suggests that between July 2023 and June 2024, an estimated 1.65 million children between 6-59 months are expected to suffer acute malnutrition including 480,000 million children expected to suffer Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) and 1.16 million expected to suffer Moderate Acute Malnutrition (MAM). 870,000 pregnant or breastfeeding women are expected to suffer acute malnutrition in this period. An estimated 35,000 people are classified in IPC Phase 5 (Catastrophe) in the Duk (3,000) and Nyirol (3,000) counties of Jonglei State; and the Rubkona County (15,000) of Unity State. An estimated 14,000 South Sudanese returnees who fled the ongoing conflict in Sudan are also classified in IPC Phase 5 (Catastrophe).The drivers of food insecurity include climatic shocks (floods and dry spells), conflicts and economic slowdown leading to depreciation of dollars against the South Sudanese Pounds. The onset of Sudan crisis has worsened the existing fragile condition resulting to more food insecurity caused by sudden population influx, increased demand for foods, breakdown of the supply chain and channelling of funds towards returnees and refugees. An estimated population of over 20,000 households have been displaced (IRNA August 2023) report from Ulang county, 2,713 households (16,278 individuals) reported to be returnees from Sudan coming through Ethiopia border 36% being refugees and 64% being refugees. Intra and intercommunal conflicts have led to loss of lives, destruction of properties including food items and displacement of the communities resulting to minimum agricultural production. Renk, Ulang and Nasir experienced flash floods which led to damaged agricultural fields and decimation of livestock numbers predisposing the community to food insecurity. Pests and diseases (locusts, birds, nematodes, and beetles. Common diseases identified are fungal and bacterial wilt) have been ranked among the main constraints to agricultural production in South Sudan leading decreased yields. Capacity strengthening on integrated pests and disease management will significantly contribute towards increased agricultural yields. Disease outbreaks among the livestock has been a major contributor of mortality and morbidity losses among the cattle keepers in South Sudan. Increased vectors prominent during rainy season, inadequate livestock extension services by the community-based animal health workers, lack of vaccines and basic infrastructure for vaccine storages at the county levels and livestock migration have been reported as the predisposing factors. Interventions through training of community-based animal health workers and livestock treatment and vaccination will contribute significantly to reduce the associated morbidity and mortality cases. Small holder farmers and fisher communities in South Sudan have had challenges of inaccessibility to farming inputs and tools such as seeds. Increased post-harvest losses along the agricultural value chains (planning, production, harvesting, storage, processing, and marketing) have been significant in the recent past with an estimated 40-50 percent (World Bank, 2020). Causes include poor storage systems, lack of skills and infrastructure for value addition, poor road conditions. Strengthening skills and investment on post-harvest infrastructure (processing equipment, adequate and efficient storage systems) and strengthening farmer organizations and cooperative movement would go a long in countering post-harvest losses.
