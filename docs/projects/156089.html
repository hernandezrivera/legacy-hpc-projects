Project Summary

In 2019, UNRWA will continue to provide formal and non-formal education services to up to 51,000 Palestine refugee children (an increase from 48,100 enrolled in 2018) in 103 schools located in Damascus, Rif Damascus, Aleppo, Hama, Homs, Latakia and Dera’a. A majority of schools still operate on a double shift basis. UNRWA will employ approximately 1,700 teachers including daily paid teachers to cover possible gaps created by the conflict.
The Agency will also continue its Education in Emergencies (EiE) interventions, including the provision of Summer Learning activities; the engagement of parents through awareness raising sessions on violence / Gender Based Violence (GBV), explosive remnants of war (ERWs), and career guidance for their children; the strengthening of the identification and management of Psychosocial Support (PSS) cases in schools; and continued capacity-building for education staff. Students in all UNRWA schools will benefit from group and individual psychosocial support counselling sessions guided by professional counsellors throughout the academic year, providing them with the necessary skills to cope with the anxiety and stress generated by the trauma of conflict.

School principals and teachers in UNRWA schools will receive training on safety and security topics with the aim of enhancing preparedness and response to security threats in schools, particularly those located in areas affected by conflict. Training topics will include evacuation procedures; dealing with ERWs; and tips on fire safety, in addition to live drills. Similar training sessions will also be provided to students, offering them basic survival skills and raising their awareness of security risks.
UNRWA will also focus its intervention on the resumption of educational services in newly accessible areas including in Yalda/Yarmouk, Sbeineh and Khan Eshieh camps in Damascus and Rural Damascus and in the South (Dera’a) provided that safe access is granted. Major construction, maintenance and rehabilitation works will be undertaken on UNRWA installations, with special focus on camps that witnessed major destruction, depending on the availability of funding, safety and security conditions, and access.

Needs

Eight years of conflict in Syria continue to have dramatic consequences for the people living in the country, including Palestine refugees. Violence, economic disruption and a contracting job market as well as a decrease in provision of public services and social security programmes have created and compounded vulnerabilities within the population. In the absence of a durable political settlement, intense and widespread hostilities have resulted in deaths and injuries, continued internal displacement, large-scale migration to other countries, lost livelihoods, mounting humanitarian needs and access restrictions hindering the emergency response, including in UNRWA areas of operation. In 2018, UNRWA estimates that 438,000 Palestine refugees remain in the country out of 560,000 individuals registered with the Agency in Syria. Displacement, loss of assets and livelihood and high levels of inflation have contributed to the increased vulnerability of civilians, including the Palestine refugee population. Many families have lost their main breadwinner, whether through death, incapacitation, detention or departure to another country in order to seek protection and asylum. Almost 60 per cent of Palestine refugees have been displaced at least once since the beginning of the conflict and, according to UNRWA estimates, more than 180,000 have had their homes destroyed or severely damaged due to hostilities. Yarmouk, Dera’a, and Ein El-Tal (Aleppo) camps, previously home to more than 30 per cent of the Palestine refugee population in Syria, have been almost completely destroyed. A vulnerability assessment conducted by UNRWA in December 2017 estimated that 90 per cent of Palestine refugee households in Syria live in absolute poverty (less than USD 2 person/day) prior to receiving UNRWA cash assistance. Palestine refugee children have faced significant challenges over the past eight years. Some have missed years of education due to prolonged displacement, and security issues and most suffer from psychosocial trauma. Ensuring that UNRWA classrooms provide a safe, inclusive and enabling environment for children is imperative and requires continued efforts to build the capacity of teachers and staff personnel alike on a range of topics related to education in emergency, protection, and safety and security. Moreover, the conflict has had a devastating impact on UNRWA educational facilities: recent assessment of UNRWA schools in Yarmouk and Dera’a reveal a great amount of destruction and damage. There is an imminent need to rehabilitate/reconstruct UNRWA facilities in camps and areas affected by conflict and provide children in those areas the opportunity to continue their education. The ability to do so remains contingent on access, availability of funds, and favorable safety/security conditions. Meanwhile, Palestine refugee children, as is the case with Syrian children, have been exposed to violence, destruction, displacement, and in certain cases to injury and death. This in turn has resulted in an increase in cases of anxiety, stress, and trauma, prompting the need for psychosocial support activities and interventions at the school level. Finally, stationaries, textbooks and school supplies are needed to ensure the delivery of quality education services to Palestine refugee children, particularly since the majority of families are struggling to make ends meet and to cover the costs of sending their children to school.

Implementation of activities

The Agency will achieve its objectives through the performance of the following activities: •Direct provision of educational services to up to 51,000 Palestine refugee students, through 103 schools (some in double shifts) including in newly accessible areas;•Provision of psychosocial support for students and staff by counsellors in all UNRWA schools;•Rehabilitation and maintenance of schools located in newly accessible areas and in areas severely affected by conflict (such as Yarmouk, Dera’a, and Ein El-Tal camps);•Provision of support and catch up classes to students, in addition to summer learning activities; •Delivery of training to school principals, teacher and students on safety and security and protection, particularly in areas affected by conflict

Protection risk: Violence against children in and around UNRWA schools. Likelihood: MediumImpact: MediumMitigating measures:1. UNRWA has developed a work plan that aims to address violence against children (AVAC) and to decrease violence in and around UNRWA installations, including education staff to children and peer to peer violence. 2. Self-care sessions are conducted with the UNRWA education staff and counselling and psychosocial support is provided to the students through UNRWA school counsellors and UNRWA PSS workers. 3. UNRWA addresses cases of violence against children from staff members using available investigative and sanctions regimes. Protection risk: Pressure from caregivers and the community to rely on potential negative coping mechanisms occasioned by the crisis like child marriage and child labour leading to drop out from school.Likelihood: LowMitigating measures: UNRWA conducts targeted activities and awareness campaigns on child labour and child marriage along with a variety of activities aimed at fostering a better understanding on the rights of children, including the right to education, and also aimed at reducing the longer term impact that the crisis has had on the mental health of children and the adult community members.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Improved access with localized conflict in certain areas of the country: Overall, humanitarian access is expected to improve with fewer Palestine refugees in hard-to-reach areas (13,500 as of end October 2018). At the same time, hostilities may continue in certain areas of the country. Should safe access resume for civilians in areas like Yarmouk and Dera’a camps, the Agency will focus on resuming its basic education services in these areas. Most Palestine Refugees in Syria will remain displaced: It is anticipated that the levels of displacement will remain high among Palestine refugees, as the housing stock in Yarmouk unofficial camp suffered significant destruction during the conflict, most recently in May 2018. The majority of the 160,000 former residents of Yarmouk are therefore expected to remain in displacement, often paying high rents, which further increases their vulnerability. A similar situation is expected in Dera’a and Ein El Tal camps which have sustained major destruction; these three camps used to provide housing for more than 30 per cent of Palestine refugees in Syria. Increase in spontaneous returns as more areas become accessible: As new areas become accessible to civilians, as observed for Sbeineh and Khan Eshieh camps in 2018, additional small scale spontaneous returns are anticipated, provided that safe access is granted for civilians and basic public services (water, electricity) are restored by the authorities. UNRWA will therefore seek to resume its education services in defined locations where feasible, to ensure that Palestine refugee school children are served. While a small number of spontaneous returns of PRS (Palestine refugees from Syria) from outside Syria were observed in 2017 and 2018 (about 2,000) and are expected to continue, most returns are expected to involve Palestine refugees displaced inside Syria. UNRWA continue to raise sufficient funds to sustain its operations in Syria: UNRWA will continue to mobilize resources and to advocate for the needs of Palestine refugees among donors, partners, and other relevant stakeholders. UNRWA will need an estimated US$ 220 million to respond to the emergency needs (health, education, protection, humanitarian assistance, WASH) of 445,000 Palestine refugees in Syria in 2019. UNRWA will mitigate the risk of limited funding by prioritizing key projects and interventions, and focusing on the needs of the most vulnerable.
