Project Summary

In a country of almost 20 million people where over 50% are either internally displaced or have sought refuge in other (mainly neighboring) countries, a large proportion of the over 5,500 Syrian (host) communities has seen the influx of IDPs place an enormous pressure on their infrastructure and services.
Communities, and in particular those groups most dramatically affected including IDPs and potential returnees, need to be assisted urgently to reinforce their resilience to address these shocks, and to accommodate the increasing pressures their growing community population will place on seriously depleted infrastructures and service capacities.
The ‘early recovery’ objective of this programme is exclusively focused on resilience building at the individual, household, local and community level. It is about providing Syrian communities with – still limited - support to regain control over their basic needs (housing, livelihoods, access to essential services) and reduce their dependency on humanitarian support.
This programme will target the following impact:
1- Provision of immediate response to collect and remove Solid Waste and Debris from existing as well as newly accessible areas;
2- Responding to the immediate basic needs of local communities by supporting the provision of rehabilitation of social services (health centers & schools) and basic infrastructure (sewage, water & electricity networks);
3- Exploring and implementing solar energy solutions (lighting, pumping & heating) in severely crisis affected communities;
Provision of emergency employment opportunities (cash for work) for affected and vulnerable populations (hosts, IDPs, youths and FHHs);
4- Support to sustainable livelihoods creation and rural recovery (revitalization of micro-small enterprises, employment creation through vocational and skills development and employment services, entrepreneurship development, short-term employment through cash-for-work for rehabilitation of rural agricultural areas distribution of tools and equipment for restarting rural livelihoods activities, linking to market access);
5- Support to persons with disabilities (PWDs) with a range of conditional cash-based interventions, physical rehabilitation, psycho-social support, and livelihoods creation.
6- Rehabilitation of critical local public service delivery capacities, such as (water, sanitation/sewage networks and power supply; waste collection; administrative services; etc.);
7- Strengthening the resilience of conflict-affected communities through improved community safety and enhanced social cohesion
8- Conflict affected women and youth are holistically supported by reducing gender inequality and youth marginality, and by promoting their empowerment to live a dignified life
Under this programme, 80 communities will be targeted among the 760 communities prioritized out of the total number of 5,628 Syrian communities.
Selection and prioritization of those communities has been based on a combination of the following criteria:
1- Areas of Possible Major Future Refugee and IDP Return :The initial areas targeted as main destinations for returning IDPs and refugees are Aleppo city; Rural Damascus; Ar-Raqqa; Deir-Ez-Zour and Daraa.
2- Communities Heavily Stressed because of large number of IDPs: There are currently 6.1 Million IDPs within Syria, or nearly one third of the actual population. They have placed a huge burden on their hosting communities.
3- Communities with Highest Severity Score (i.e. socio-economic needs): The 2015 Living Conditions Index, combined with the 2018 Severity Analysis, has allowed to calculate a compounded ‘Vulnerability Index’ which has made it possible to rank communities, localities, sub-districts, districts and governorates on the basis of the relative severity of their socio-economic needs.

Needs

The protracted crisis in Syria has depleted the capacity at all levels to address basic needs, resulting in the following situation:1. Poverty, at 13% before the crisis, now stands at 83%, 2. The unemployment rate was estimated to stand at 53 per cent, reaching as high as 75 per cent among youth (up to 24 years of age, while a sizeable reduction in the size of Syria’s current workforce, which stands at 51 per cent of precrisis levels is witnessed. 3. In the absence of livelihood opportunities, close to 20% of the Syrians have been forced to resort to negative coping strategies, including child labor; early marriage of their young children; and participation in the violence sector.4. 2017 Severity Analysis on issues that affect people’s resilience most (leaving aside equally critical issues related to governance and participation) such as jobs, access to resources, social services, availability of markets, water and electricity, damage to buildings; etc. put the severity scale at about 60%, but with a large number of the 281 sub-districts in Syria scoring even much higher levels of severity,5. A recent assessment on disability among adults in western Aleppo, Idleb and Ar-Raqqa governorates indicated an average prevalence rate of 30 per cent. An average of 45 per cent of surveyed persons injured during the Syria crisis are expected to sustain a permanent impairment (e.g. amputation, spinal cord injury, brain injury).6. 2018 figures also show that about 1.4 million mostly Internally Displaced and Refugees have returned, with UNHCR estimating that this figure could increase by year end.

Implementation of activities

In the communities selected for this programme, a mechanism for consultation and participation will be established as one of the first programme actions. This will be preceded or accompanied by a conflict-sensitive analysis, to make sure there is a full understanding of the issues to be taken into account when designing programme priorities and implementation options, so that conflict threats are not exacerbated, and community cohesion reinforced. As such, there will be a strong underpinning of ‘Do No Harm’ and ‘Do Good’ considerations. This will ensure that priorities supported under the programme will be identified and prioritized transparently and with the participation of the communities or their representatives. It will also allow to anchor on this mechanism – and its further community-driven implementation - the community strengthening and social cohesion activities that will be supported under this programme. As to project implementation, options will be prioritized that meet an objective, one, of drawing as much as possible on local – and preferably community-based – implementation arrangements, as far as they are inclusive (e.g. community groups; faith-based organizations; parents associations; farmer groups; youth clubs), and, two, draw as much as possible on local labor (with priority of recruitment among the most vulnerable or others to be prioritized for reasons decided and agreed by the community) so as to enhance the overall positive impact, in particular in terms of livelihoods, on those prioritized participants. An effort will also be made to encourage the participation of local informal and small businesses, to ensure the programme also provides a rapid (even if small) boost to local economic development. Finally, solid collaboration will be pursued with other UN Agencies and with non-governmental organizations.

Protection Risk Analysis

Risk 1: Resources and response capacities being limited and far below know needs, there will always be perception of biased targeting of beneficiaries or of biased selection of response activities, thereby creating or exacerbating tension; Mitigation measures: 1. Perform conflict analysis before planning the response;2. Ensure that a comprehensive needs analysis informs the response formulation, and that this is openly documented and made transparent;3. Make sure those in similar situation of need, at least within the geographical areas of response, area reached equally;4. Obtain validation of the prioritization and of the formulated response from a representative community group, or through well-selected key informants and/or participation mechanisms;5. Communicate the planned response, and the rationale behind, to the communities concerned;6. Efficiently deliver on the obligation of ‘Accountability to (Affected) Populations’, by keeping the communities concerned regularly informed of the project and its implementation;7. Make sure some process of receiving complaints and of responses to complaints is put in place;8. Have in reserve a strategy in case the planned response does unexpectedly create tension (e.g. add-on of other activities or change in activities, in consultation with concerned communities. Risk 2: Escalation of the security situation; Mitigation measures: 1. Focus on responses to the benefit of the entire community rather than individual beneficiaries (e.g. rehabilitation of social or community infrastructure, rather than e.g. individual housing support);2. Ensure there is a process established that allows to consult the communities on how to proceed with the proposed response in a deteriorating security environment.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1. Key stakeholders permit UNDP and partners to apply minimum targeting criteria outlined in this proposal ensuring impartial and needs-driven service delivery. Should UNDP and partners be unable to apply its procedures, activities may be delayed or frozen until needed permissions are negotiated.2. Areas of operation remain safe for staff and beneficiaries to access. Should life-threatening risks (such as ERW contamination, outbreak of active conflict, huge risk in V/PIED incidents, heavy IAF affecting local area) occur, activities may be frozen to safeguard lives. There is a risk that, for example, intensified conflict triggered by resurgence of ISIL remnants could affect the operating environment. in Deir-ez-Zor. 3. UNDP and (potentially) partners receive visas for key international staff involved in the implementation of this action. There is a risk that visas for key staff could be rejected, entailing remote management. 4. It is assumed that a significant devaluation of the USD against the Syrian pound does not occur; if this does, the ability of UNDP and partners to fund all target activities may be compromised. 5. Local communities actively participate to the assessment, needs prioritization, implementation and monitoring of the action and vulnerable groups are positively included in the community based mechanisms of this action.
