Project Summary

Due to a lack of access, transparence, the impossibility to monitor, and given that, according to international law, individuals under detention in a country must be provided the means to live with dignity by the government of this country, WFP, along with the partners of the Food Security Sector took the decision not to intervene in detention in order not to legitimize detention and provide assistance blindly where access and monitoring is impossible.
Consequently, alternative forms of assistance to vulnerable migrants have been developed and this project, targeting food insecure migrants outside detention centers, has been designed to support the food security and nutrition status of migrants while preserving them from any form of exposure, stigmatization and discriminations, ensuring their protection and promoting their inclusion within the local communities.
WFP and IOM have built its functioning partnership since November 2019 to provide food assistance to vulnerable migrants in Libya based on analyzed data. A total of 25,800 food boxes were distributed between November 2019 and May 2020. The most food insecure non-detained migrants were identified in close partnership with IOM Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM) Unit and Migrant Resource and Response Mechanism (MRRM) Unit.
Based on the built success, under the 2021 HRP - Food Security Sector, WFP is looking to work with IOM and local NGO partners to maximise the impacts, outputs and outcomes for people in need, while targeting the one-off life-saving assistance to 27,324 vulnerable and non-detained migrant individuals in urban settings.
Through its distribution cycles in April 2020-May 2020, WFP experienced in reaching vulnerable migrants under situations having the COVID-19 restrictions. Adaptation measures such as masks, disinfection, social distancing, temperature checks were systematically applied. These experience will be applied to this proposed project in 2021.
Beneficiaries will be disaggregated by Gender. The experience in WFP's food assistance to vulnerable Libyan in the past years indicates that the ratio of beneficiaries is 69% men / 31% women. For comparison, migrant population in Libya ratio is 89% men / 11% women. Women-headed households are proven to be more food insecure. Consequently the proportion of supported women is higher in this programme.
To enhance the quality and the impact of the assistance, building on the successful collaboration between IOM and WFP, including release of the WFP-IOM joint-publication on Migrant Food Security in Libya, WFP looks to strengthen the collaboration with IOM in joint analysis of IOM's face-to-face flow monitoring surveys on food security WFP’s web-based Migration Pulse surveys.

1 - Needs

Needs for food assistance to migrants in Libya based on analyzed data have gradually increased, as migrants are highly vulnerable to COVID-19 socio-economic consequences. The April 2020 report of the Joint Market Monitoring Initiative (JMMI) (conducted jointly by REACH, DRC, and WFP) shows that food prices in the country are significantly increasing since march, reaching unprecedented levels and more particularly in the South. The minimum expenditure basket (MEB) increased by 28.9% between March and April 2020, driven by an increase of food prices (+26.6%) and cooking fuel prices (+62.5%). In the south the median cost of MEB increase by 42.6%. This assessment provides information of the prices of key food commodities and their variation over time. This is put into perspective with other reports such as the Rapid Market Assessment, Protection Monitoring during COVID-19 crisis, and on occasion, Mobility tracking, impact on vulnerable populations on the move in Libya to measure the population’s access to food. The latter report highlights that the loss of work opportunities is the main cause of increasing vulnerability in Libya and exacerbates humanitarian needs. In 95 percent of the assessed locations, migrants who rely on daily labour opportunities were reported to have been negatively affected by COVID-19 induced slowdown in economic activities The IOM Migrant reports n° 28, 29 and 30 published between December 2019 and May 2020 also highlighted that foods is among the priority needs of migrants. The IOM Migrant Emergency Food Security Report, elaborated in collaboration with WFP, highlights that 32 percent of migrants suffer from inadequate food consumption and an additional 34 percent are considered marginally food insecure and at risk of food insecurity. Due to a lack of food or means to buy food, 65 percent of migrants surveyed had to resort to a stress, crisis or emergency livelihood coping strategy in the previous 30 days. For example, over a third of the migrants interviewed reported having to spend their savings to buy food (34%), one in five reported having to work in exchange for food (20%) and/or having to reduce their expenditure on essential non-food items (19%). The other most frequently adopted coping strategies included borrowing money to buy food (15%) and reducing health or medicine-related expenses (7%). These reports confirm the fragile food security and nutrition status of non-detained migrants and the relevance of such project. The joint analysis highlighted the strongest migration drivers as economic reasons, followed by insecurity, persecution and conflict, limited ability to meet food needs, environmental degradation and climate-related shocks. Also, the said analysis revealed that main challenges faced by migrants in Libya: related to security and finding opportunities to make a living, closely followed by high food prices. Over half of migrants reported to have been worried about not having enough food to eat. The slightly different vulnerability levels were identified: migrants who come from East African and South/Southeast Asian countries were found to be more vulnerable to food insecurity than other migrants. The analysis of migrants' needs and migration drivers could be made more useful for programme designing and re-designing if the same indicators are continuously monitored on a regular basis. This idea has been shared with otherhumanitarian actors and local donor representatives and accepted by them. Migrants and refugees have also been disproportionally affected by movement restrictions further affecting their access to markets and livelihoods, as most of them are employed in temporary or daily labour and can no longer afford basic needs goods.

2 - Activities

WFP in partnership with IOM will distribute monthly food rations to vulnerable migrants outside of detention centres. The distributions will be conducted through humanitarian protection lens, which means distributing food rations to targeted migrants through door-to-door or outreaches to safe community centres. Where the resources are available, WFP will mobilize ready-to-eat rations for the assistance to migrants outside of detention centres. As per the priority agreed on within the Food Security Sector, WFP plans to target the one-off food assistance at 27,324 migrants outside detention centres, with a priority in Benghazi, Ghat, Sebha, Tripoli and Zwara. WFP through IOM MRRM conduct regular mobile outreach to the migrant communities along the migratory routes in Libya to identify needs of migrant women, men, boys, and girls. While distributing the one-off food assistance, WFP will ensure that the practical programmatic control and quality assurance measures shall be put in place, which includes: i) WFP’s training of its Cooperating Partners and its own staff in cross-cutting issues such as gender, GBV, PSEA, Protection and Conflict Sensitivity; ii) WFP’s output monitoring through the verification of distribution reports and spot-checks and WFP’s outcome monitoring through annual post-distribution monitoring; iii) WFP’s distributions of leaflets and visibility materials to raise the awareness of participants and nonparticipants about the CFM. Based on the success gained through combining IOM's face-to-face Flow Monitoring Survey and WFP's web-based Migration Pulse survey for the past years, IOM and WFP plan to conduct additional data collection and qualitative research to further explore the relationship between factors and food insecurity. In parallel, IOM and WFP plan to examine protection concerns of potential food assistance programmes that would be inclusive of the migrant communities.

5 - Mitigation measures
5.1 - For each risk mentioned under 4 above, please describe the ways you plan to mitigate, prevent and/or address these.

4.1) WFP displays posters sharing the CFM number in all food distribution points and also shares this information verbally with beneficiaries during the distributions; 4.2) As part of its conflict-sensitive assistance, WFP conducts analysis and consultations with local authorities and IOM before expanding unconditional food assistance to any location. This includes ensuring access to all affectedcommunities and speaking to different groups to enable their participation. WFP also coordinates its food assistance to vulnerable Libyans with food assistance to vulnerable non-Libyans in the same geographical areas; 4.3) As part of its conflict-sensitive assistance, WFP analyzes before beginning food assistance in any location. This includes ensuring access to all affected communities and speaking to different groups to enable their participation; 4.4) COVID-19 guidelines are shared verbally andthrough posters throughout training venues. Masks and disinfection gels are provided at food distribution points, and waiting areas and food receipt points are re-arranged to facilitate social distancing; 4.6) Sensitive beneficiary data is only shared between WFP and its partners through secure means and never via email. Access to this data is strictly limited; 4.9) WFP utilizes IOM's established capacities such as DTM and MRRM to select the beneficiaries based on the vulnerability; 4.10) WFP consults a range of actors during the quick needs assessments, project implementation and quality control call phases. This includes interviews with locals from a variety of backgrounds; 4.11) WFP facilitates access to food assistance for people with disabilities where possible. This includes having signlanguage available or other support such as food deliveries by porters as needed.
