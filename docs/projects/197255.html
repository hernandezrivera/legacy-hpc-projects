Project Summary

In 2021, Street Child introduced Teaching at the Right Level to Cameroon through a short pilot programme for 500 children in Fako Division. The programme suits the context where children have missed up to 5 years of school. In under eight weeks, 44% of learners advanced at least one learning level in language and 57% of learners advanced at least 1 level. Our “We Go Sabi” project proposes to leverage the success of the first pilot and a fully funded project by TaRL Africa, to further utilise and adapt TaRL in a conflict-affected context where the approach has the unique potential to address an absence of schooling that has left severe gaps in foundational skills amongst school-aged children.
Education has been at the heart of the conflict in NWSW Cameroon, and over 90% of schools have closed for several years over the course of conflict.In 2020, only 23% of primary schools were operational (HNO, 2021). Since 2020 the space for education has opened up, and some children are slowly returning to learning in formal and community school settings.Though data on learning levels is not available, the majority of children are severely lacking in foundational learning skills after having missed up to five years of schooling. TaRL offers a simple, scalable and high-impact solution with the proven potential to address critical gaps in life-saving, life-sustaining learning for these children. Evidence from Street Child’s Assessment of Non-Formal Education in March 2021shows that an estimated 45% of children aged 5 to 16 across the region are learning in non-formal settings. 80% of facilitators in these settings have had no training at all, and most others have had some training based on the government curriculum but not on any emergency-adapted approaches to learning. There is currently no adapted curriculum or pedagogical approach being rolled out to these learning spaces, making it very challenging for teachers to meet the learning and psychosocial support needs of children who have experienced years of conflict. Furthermore, with these learning spaces largely run by communities without support from government or humanitarian actors, a lack of financial resources means teaching and learning materials are limited. The low-cost, localised approach to resourcing Teaching at the Right Learning is therefore of particular relevance in this context. Teachers and students in formal school settings also face barriers to effective teaching and learning. Teachers struggle to meet the varying ability levels in their classrooms, a key challenge given the high rates of displaced and conflict-affected children who are re-entering the formal school system after missing several years of schooling, and the varying levels of education these different children have had access to whilst out of formal school. As a result of many schools still being closed and high rates of displacement to urban centers, schools that are open face severe overcrowding, with pupil per classroom ratios of up to 200 children (HN0, 2021).The Teaching at the Right Level approach is a uniquely suitable solution, as its focus on foundational skills and flexible, individualized instruction are ideal in a context where differentials in disruption and dropout from learning require targeted, tailored teaching.
Street Child’s first pilot of TaRL showed the impact this approach can have in this context; in just 7 weeks, 57% of enrolled learners moved up one numeracy level and 44% of enrolled learners moved up one literacy level.However, this pilot was limited – both in terms of timeframe and level of coaching that could be provided to the teachers. We expect that the opportunity to implement TaRL more intensively over a longer period will have a significant impact for children and teachers, and also believe that there is strong potential for evaluation and evidence generation to engage the interest and investment of government and non-government stakeholders for scaling of the approach.

1 - Besoins / Needs

Cameroon is one of the world’s most neglected and underfunded crises (NRC, 2020), and as such donors who are prepared to invest in the response are seeking scalable, sustainable approaches – a gap which TaRL has the proven potential to fill. The proposed project offers an outstanding opportunity for evaluation and evidence generation that supports scaling at the regional and national levels – through humanitarian actors in the immediate term, and through the government in the longer term. At the national level, there is also significant potential to prove Teaching at the Right Level as an appropriate approach for addressing learning loss at scale. Though the conflict context in the NWSW is unique, children in other regions of Cameroon face similar barriers to quality learning. In the Far North region, impacted by the Boko Haram conflict, 37% of children have missed out on school due to displacement, and 25% of in-school children are over-age for their grade level (NRC, 2018). In the East and Adamoua regions, 54% of refugee children from CAR are out of school (HNO, 2021). Considering the scale of needs and differences in context across the country, a partnership with TARL Africa offers an excellent opportunity to learn from the Teaching at the Right Level communities of practice – in particular, through Teaching at the Right Level partners in neighbouring Nigeria, and partners who have adopted the model for refugee learners. The latter includes learning from Young African Refugees for Integral Development (YARID) – who have enhanced and expanded their approach for refugee learners in Uganda with support from Street Child and TARL Africa. At the regional level, where 45% of children are estimated to be attending some form of learning in community spaces, there is a tremendous need for foundational approaches in non-formal learning spaces. This is of particular interest to members of the NWSW Education Cluster, particularly for local actors able to take TaRL to hard-to-reach areas where there are no alternative learning opportunities. Street Childs experience in hard-to-reach areas and in building capacity of local partners positions us to be able to cascade the approach and lessons learned to other cluster members.
