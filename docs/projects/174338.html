Project Summary

TWB will provide language services and communication support across sectors for more effective communication with communities, with a particular focus on the development of a common multilingual feedback mechanism. The project's primary target for 2021 is that 20% of the affected population report receiving information in a preferred language other than Hausa. TWB also aims to ensure that, by the end of 2021, there is consensus on a sustainable and effective inter-agency accountability mechanism.

1 - Needs

Language barriers continue to hamper humanitarian communication with vulnerable people in northeast Nigeria. The 2019 & 2020 Multi-Sector Needs Assessment (MSNA) conducted by REACH, data from IOM’s Displacement Tracking Matrix, and focused research by Translators without Borders highlight the critical gaps listed below. Communicating in Hausa alone is not enough. The 2019 MSNA data shows that Hausa is the primary language for a minority of people surveyed (31%), and a large proportion of the population don't understand it well. Internally displaced people speak more than 30 different primary languages; among them, Kanuri, Fulfulde, Bura, Shuwa Arabic, and Marghi are the most common. Humanitarian information provision, data collection and feedback mechanisms must cater to a wider range of languages spoken in their targeted area. Both language and format matter. Across the three states, 59% of 2020 MSNA respondents said they prefer to receive information in a language other than Hausa. Low literacy levels also mean that some people―particularly women—will not understand any written information. Clear, accurate content and mostly verbal mother tongue communication are critical to reach everyone, including the most vulnerable and less literate groups. Widespread communication gaps compromise accountability. A reliance on spoken Hausa and written English (including for suggestion boxes) makes it difficult for all but the most educated and native Hausa speakers to engage in real dialogue with humanitarians. Young women and older people in particular are not confident that their feedback is appropriately received and addressed. In addition, people are not comfortable discussing misconduct by aid workers and other sensitive issues in person. The result is less effective, inclusive, and transparent community feedback mechanisms. Community members report engagement and mutual trust would be improved if they could communicate in local languages, including via remote communication channels (e.g. hotlines, chatbots). COVID-19 prevention and protection measures are proving to be less accessible amongst speakers of marginalized languages. Households who preferred a language other than Hausa were 7% more likely to report a family member living with a vulnerability, 9% less likely to wash their hands regularly, and 10% less likely to call a dedicated COVID-19 phone number if they are sick. Complicated and technical terminology is making clear communication especially difficult, whether in relation to COVID-19 or other outbreaks affecting vulnerable populations in Northeast Nigeria.

2 - Activities

Responding successfully to humanitarian needs in the Northeast, and implementing functional feedback mechanisms, requires that evidence-based communication strategies become an integral part of humanitarian plans. TWB will lead efforts to coordinate and promote CwC across the response and to design a common feedback mechanism. Language services for two-way communication - The information needs of affected populations in Northeast Nigeria are evident: populations require clear, prioritised information in languages that enable them to make the best decisions for themselves and their communities. Without clear, trusted, timely and actionable information, every other aspect of the humanitarian response is compromised. To date in 2020, TWB has translated nearly 2 million words and 3,000 pieces of content for 38 organizations in the response. In 2021, TWB will continue to adapt and localize these language services in the following ways: Translation: TWB Nigeria employs language specialists who provide translation, including in audio format, in nine languages selected to meet the needs of partners and affected communities. Communication training: TWB will provide sector-specific language awareness training to field workers and volunteers, training in the basics of translation, as well as an introduction to humanitarian interpreting. Visual and pictorial support: TWB will support partners with graphics and design, ensuring that people at all literacy levels have access to clear, useful information. Plain language editing: TWB will continue to offer plain language training to help partners develop messages that meet their audiences’ needs. Assessment, consultation and development of common CFM - As the leading CwC actor on the AAP WG advisory committee, TWB will spearhead a sub-group for CwC, emphasising field-level coordination in the production of effective two-way communication strategies and materials. In its first year, the CWC sub-group will act as an exploratory committee to build consensus around the design of a platform that will consolidate and analyse a common feedback mechanism and host multilingual, plain language, community-facing information products produced by the response. TWB’s process for coordinating and co-designing a common feedback mechanism will include: A comprehensive assessment of existing CFMs/agencies/referral pathways and CwC materials produced across the response. Community consultations to assess what is needed, what is successful, what has failed, what will be used and preferred by members of communities. This will include all marginalised and vulnerable groups. Inter-agency and sector consultation to align INGO, NGO, CSO and UN agency, sector and government objectives and outputs and to meet requirements outlined in the HCT Community Engagement Strategy. Development of formal SOPs and standard guidance for information sharing and closing-the-loop. Will include categorization of complaints, reporting, response times, processing, feedback, agency responsibilities, language and translation issues, etc CwC subgroup’s exploratory committee agrees on the design of a common digital hub and data mechanism for the collection, filtering, analysis, aggregation and reporting on all input from CFMs. Setting up a common platform will involve considerable technical expertise and financial commitment to enable the establishment, refinement and operation among humanitarian actors. Multi-year funding will be needed, from more than one donor, to enable the collective mechanism to contribute to the humanitarian response. TWB’s aim in 2021 is to ensure consensus on a sustainable and effective inter-agency accountability mechanism that will carry the response into the implementation of a successful common feedback mechanism in 2022.

8.6 - Please describe briefly the modalities your project will put in place to ensure protection mainstreaming (particularly referring to the above questions).

The links between language and ethnicity require careful attention to the potential implications of gathering and sharing information on the languages people speak. Conflict sensitivity and protection risk will be an explicit consideration in TWB’s research and mapping activities, as well as in the provision of language services, with a risk analysis to be conducted in collaboration with protection sector partners and others across the response. Training on the basics of interpreting and language awareness for humanitarian protection staff and volunteers will include an explicit focus on both gender- and conflict-sensitivity. TWB will consult with protection sector partners and draw on research findings to develop these aspects of both training programs for the context of northeast Nigeria.

9.5 - How will your project ensure feedback is collected from the most vulnerable community members?

Through TWB’s audio mechanisms, we are able to create the same information for all nine language groups that we serve. We place a particular emphasis on making information, communication and messaging available – and in a language that is well-understood – to those who are less mobile, visually impaired, illiterate, very young or elderly. During community consultations and frequent monitoring activities, TWB will reach out to vulnerable members of the community for specific feedback and information to ensure their concerns are incorporated into all relevant activities.
