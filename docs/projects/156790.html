Project Summary

PIN will target the most vulnerable individuals/HH notably IDPs, the most vulnerable among hosting populations, people in-hard-to-reach areas, people with chronic illness, people with disabilities and people facing extreme socio-economic hardship- all possibly facing further various level of protection risks and vulnerabilities which can lead them to resort to harmful coping strategies. This is achieved through protection-sensitive programme design, targeting, and implementation, to ensure an inclusive approach to programming; notably intended to reach female-headed houses, which are often isolated. Food assistance in its various modalities are in themselves protection mechanisms, supporting vulnerable households to meet urgent needs and preventing the resort to negative coping strategies. Women are routinely identified as heads of households for the purposes of receiving assistance, to promote their participation in decision-making regarding utilization of assistance given.
PIN will monitor the conflict and population movement to be able to assist newly displaced persons in the timeliest manner with appropriate response both across North East and North West Syria. A specific attention will be pay to IDPs as being 6.1 million in-country presently living in camps, host communities, last resort and makeshift settlements, resulting from both newly or protracted displacement.
The activities fall both into emergency and life-sustaining response to address food and basic needs of target beneficiaries. Ready to Eat Rations (RTERs) distributed within 96 hours of displacement is complement by Multi-Purpose Cash Assistance to cover full monthly Survival Monthly Expenditure Basket (SMEB) up to 6 weeks.
The second component is regular monthly food assistance as a regard of life-sustaining strategy. PIN aims to provide for up to 9-month consecutive food assistance when possible. These activities will cover up to 78/80% of the monthly SMEB, the remaining is aimed to be provided by beneficiaries themselves through their own means. Yet particular attention should be brought in situation where assistance is the sole source of income of target HH as PIN monitoring activities as underlined, for them to be kept them away from harmful coping strategies.
In the first days of displacement, rapid assessments are carried out by PIN and in coordination and consultation with other response actors, local councils, and/or camp management committees, as applicable and in line with internal SOPs and SPHERE standards.
Likewise, this emergency response is complemented by early recovery assistance covering WASH, Livelihoods, Education in host communities receiving IDPs, in order to maximise ‘do no harm’ considerations and ensure that vulnerable host populations also receive appropriate assistance. When possible beneficiaries will be directed from emergency to regular food assistance and early recovery.
Constant M&E monitoring act as activities backbone through beneficiary selection and verification, need assessment, market monitoring, post-implementation (such as distribution) monitoring, routine monitoring, Complaint and Response Mechanism (CRM).
It should be noted that activities are designed to be in line with the relevant coordination system such as Sphere standards and meet FSL cluster recommendations (length of assistance, contents of kits, value of cash/vouchers etc.).
Lastly, all activities will be carried out in coordination with local councils (LC), ensuring to share information on needs and response gaps at the village level as well as involved in beneficiary selection process; local associations, and IDP representatives at village level will be considered, if any.

Needs

According to the Humanitarian Needs Overview 2018, there are 6.1 million IDPs living inside Syria with approximately 6,550 displaced each day notably suffering from extensive displacements and eroded family and community support. HNO data highlightsthat 13.1 million people across Syria are in urgent need of assistance, including 6.5 million people food insecure, whilst a further 4 million people are at risk of becoming acutely food insecure. Overall, 10.5 million is the total figure brought up by HRP 2018 forthe number of people being in need of urgent life-saving and life-sustaining food, agriculture and livelihood assistance. The dire humanitarian needs are expected to remain high over the course of 2019 as part of the unpredictable ongoing conflict. Specificsectors, regardless of the current level of hostilities, will be further damaged by the conflict such as infrastructures, availability of services, livelihood opportunities. Livelihood and basics services being the two most rated needs among this population according to Humanitarian Needs Assessment Programme (HNAP) for Syria 2018. To address this PIN is planning to implement multi-sectoral programming, part of a broader response plan, under which PIN is combining cash, in-kind and NFI/ Winterization assistance to vulnerable populations affected by conflict. Households across all target areas have been found to be resorting to negative coping strategies in both NWS and NES, with 54% of interviewees with borderline food consumption scores and over one third of respondents with ‘poor’ food consumption scores. 80% of respondents said that they were purchasing food on credit at least once in the week preceding the questionnaire, while 71% said they were limiting portion sizes at mealtimes. Vulnerable households have entered into chronic cycles of debt – with 80% of pre-assistance interviewees in NWS and 73% in NES relying on vendors giving items on credit as a key food sourcing strategy. This has led to high levels of dependence upon local vendors, who in times of political uncertainty have been found to withdraw offers of selling on credit and begin to collect on outstanding payments. Dependency on humanitarian assistance is equally pronounced. Amongst PIN beneficiaries in 2018 who had received three months of food assistance, 89% of food voucher beneficiaries in NWS and 81% of beneficiaries in NES said that they would face difficulty meeting their food needs during the next three months, if they were not to receive further assistance. This would suggest that despite provision of assistance for three months, target groups are nevertheless struggling to meet basic needs. A number of at-risk groups with specific needs were highlighted during needs assessments, including Female-headed HH (FHH), children, disabled and elderly groups. FHH were consistently found to be most vulnerable, with baseline data highlighting that 82% FHH in NWS had no income, compared to 44% of male headed households (44%). This is resulting in more severe resort to negative coping strategies amongst FHH, which has a corresponding negative impact on children. Baseline data in NWS in 2018 highlighted that 45% of FHH had out-of-school-children whose education had been disrupted due to the need to earn money to support the basic needs of the HH, compared to 24% of male headed HH. Child labour was mentioned by KIIs in 92% of villages during a needs assessment in NWS, followed by child marriage and family separation, as the most pressing concerns in the community. High protection risks for children were echoed in assessments in NES around Raqqa, where early marriage was mentioned as an increasing trend in communities without access to sufficient income to cover basic food needs. FGDs and KIIs in NES and NWS highlighted that disabled and elderly people experienced significant barriers to accessing both assistance and markets.

Implementation of activities

Objective: Improve the food security status of assessed food insecure people through emergency life-saving and regular life sustaining food assistance. Result: life-saving- the most vulnerable households affected by sudden onset emergencies are enabled to meet their basic food needs. Activities: RTERs are employed in response to sudden onset emergencies in support of affected HH, including newly displaced households within 96 hours of displacement, provided as part of a first line response package, alongside with other complementary assistance such as MPCAs within 10 days after the RTERs distribution. RTERs aim to meet at least 2100kcal/person/day, 100% of kcal needs for 5 days while one-time emergency MPCGs are currently valued between $120 and $130 USD-sufficient to cover monthly 100% SMEB for a month. The combination of multiple rapid response modalities gives beneficiaries enough resources to meet their food, hygiene, and other survival needs during the first one month, up to six weeks displacement, depending on family size. Result: life sustaining- the most vulnerable households are enabled to meet their basic food needs without recourse to negative coping strategies. Activities: PIN will provide monthly assistance for food insecure HH through MPCA, cash for work and flour distribution. For vouchers, PIN aims to target HH with 3-month cycles support to provide up to a total of 9-month continuous assistance. Current projections demonstrate an expected value of approximately $90 per month, equivalent to at least 2,100/ person/ day. PIN is considering on conducting a pilot on electronic vouchers in spring 2019. Cash for Work (CfW) will be used to provide food support to vulnerable HH with labour capacity, whilst providing vital support for basic rehabilitation to critical infrastructure and restoring dignity while supporting broader community restoration. Electricity, WASH, road, and public facilities have sustained heavy damage as a result of the chronic impacts of the conflict. Lastly, support to bakeries to produce subsidized bread will be provided in areas concentrating the highest number of IDPs and vulnerability, to promote access to affordable bread to communities with the highest levels of need in areas overlapping with areas targeted by PIN under emergency one-off food assistance. Output: life sustaining- the most vulnerable HH are enabled to increase food production through home-gardening, increasing income generating activities and food diversity available on markets. Activities: PIN will support low income small-scale rural farmers whose capacity to engage in agriculture is critically undermined due to poor purchasing power in the post-conflict reality. A specific focus will be towards supporting female-headed households. This programme is designed to strengthen the food security of local communities, increase self-sufficiency in accessing food, and maximize horticultural production of beneficiaries while supporting the local economy through a market driven approach. PIN will provide agriculture vouchers to HH for provision of seeds, tools and other agricultural commodities whose value is set at $100 according to market value and based on the vegetable seed and inputs required for a land size of approximately 300 m² to 1 dunum. Vouchers are also more sensitive in terms of the technical feasibility as the farmers can rely on their experience and existing knowledge in the selection of suitable crops. Lettuce, radish, parsley, and arugula are consumed daily and are important to diversify nutritious sources, enhancing nutrition especially for elderly, children, pregnant and lactating women. In addition, they offer the advantage of being easily planted, harvest and familiar to the Syrian population as endemic to the area. PIN also plan for a series of technical training. PIN will target locations where PIN has provided monthly food assistance in order to graduate some households to develop resilience within households

Protection Risk Analysis

PIN programming in Northern Syria are inherently driven by protection considerations. Within implementation and regarding the specific activities related to the Food Security sector, the following protection risks may arise with the following measures to be taken:• Risk: Lack of freedom of movement due to security and protection concerns, possibly resulting in lack of opportunities to spend cash/travel Probability: Medium Mitigation: Alternative modalities; door-to-door distribution; resort to local network (cash delivery with Hawala etc.)• Risk: Unequal repartition within the HH, disagreements about cash spending/tension within HH. Probability: Medium Mitigation: community based social dynamics knowledge; PDM; monitoring by female aid workers; restricted CBR• Risk: Tensions between host communities/IDPs. Probability: High Mitigation: awareness-community based social dynamics knowledge; awareness and transparency about targeting criteria; community based beneficiary selection• Risk: Cash diverted by influencing stakeholders-LCs, vendors etc Probability: High Mitigation: Reporting system: CRM, whistleblowing policy, internal monitoring, advocacy, awareness; switch modalities • Risk: Cash programming creating increased demand/raise in price for specific commodities Probability: Medium Mitigation: Market assessment and monitoring • Risk: Specific vulnerable groups/individuals are excluded from assistance. Probability: High Mitigation: Vulnerability scoring criteria for beneficiary selection assessment; CRM; participatory approach and consultation with local stakeholders to crosscheck data; community stakeholder meetings• Risk: Beneficiaries attending distribution are subject to harassment, unsafe environment for specific vulnerable groups. Probability: High Mitigation: Door-to-door distribution; gender separated queue lines; gender awareness, CRM, all staff trained on PSEA and code of conduct

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks and mitigating measures: 1. Inaccessibility to target areas due to insecurity, large scale military offensives, or change in territorial control with safety and security of staff/beneficiaries being compromised.PIN would triangulated information with available sources, use rigorous monitoring mechanism, suspend; relocate activities to areas where access is upheld; shift program resources to other areas; switch to remote management; potentially through pre-identified local partners. 2. Borders are temporarily closed. PIN would suspend activities; advocate with UNOCHA and local authorities to facilitate access and operations; purchase locally if possible; eventually resort to remote cash-based programming in extreme cases. 3. Legal/administrative constraints – including refusal of required operational permissions and visas – delay or prevent aid delivery in countries from where cross-border operations are conducted.PIN would adopt a phased approaches to withdrawal. 4. Inflation of the Syrian pound / Exchange rate fluctuations / Local markets are not able to meet the increased demand for goods use available in-kind commodities through food basket/bread/RTER provisions; switch allocations to increase in-kind assistance provision; reshape programming by revising targets in coordination with ECHO; switch to food basket distributions with items procured in neighbouring countries. 5. Restricted cash liquidity inside Syria/international bank transfers rejections.Cash modalities would be suspended whilst the finance department assess and monitor market situation/development; switch to in-kind modalities; envisage back up CTA. 6.Syria offices/warehouses/assets are raided. PIN would temporarily suspend activities; carry out internal/external investigations; advocate for dialogue with relevant actors; inform relevant counter-terrorismagencies/authorities/partners of the incident; seek to relocate the intervention to other areas. 7. Local Councils or other relevant local stakeholders stop collaborating with partners or fail to apply agreed methodologyPIN would temporarily suspend activities; engage dialogue to re-sensitize on humanitarian principles of engagement/PIN policies; relocate resources to other target areas. 8. Aid is perceived as politically motivated and/or PIN are subject to intimidation from military groups or local authorities.Activities would be suspended; re-sensitization/dialogue; reallocate funding to alternative areas. 9. Aid is diverted for personal or political gains. PIN mitigates aid diversion through a comprehensive set of rigorous management policiesPIN would suspend activities; carry out pertinent investigations; advocate for dialogue for return/replacement of diverted aid; re-sensitize concerned actors on humanitarian role of PIN where possible; reallocate funding to alternative target areas.
