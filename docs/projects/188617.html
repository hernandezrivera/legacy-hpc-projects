Project Summary

The effects of climate change are being felt in Syria, during the 2020/2021 agricultural season there was a significant delay in the onset of rainfall by at least 2 months across Syria combined with an earlier than usual cessation of rainfall, with the last rains being received in mid-April 2021. The erratic rainfall during the 2020/21 winter season, accompanied by higher than average temperatures, resulted in drought-like conditions and significant water scarcity in Syria, further worsening food insecurity prevalence.
The water scarcity, damaged irrigation infrastructure and the low rainfall level has forced most farmers, to rely on wells to cover part of their irrigation needs. This will have negative impact on groundwater decline and depletion, leading to high pumping costs. The low production of wheat and other crops during the 2021 harvest season has resulted in a significant gap in supply and demand, mainly for wheat and other crops such as legumes. Agro-climatic conditions, particularly rainfall distribution and amount, are projected to be worse in the 2021 – 2022 agricultural season, especially in northern and southern Syria.
The limited natural resources in Syria, and the exacerbation of the impact of climatic changes, especially drought, led to a decline in the production of crops and livestock, an increase in the vulnerability and food insecurity of farming households. The effects of climate change call for urgent actions, measures and approaches that will help farmers sustainably increase their agricultural productivity and incomes, adapt and build resilience to climate change and greenhouse gas emissions, where possible.
In this context, the proposed project targeting 163,333 households (equivalent to 980,000 people) is based on two main outputs:
Output 1: Awareness raising of farmers’ on the risks of climate change and adoption of climate-smart agriculture (CSA) approaches enhanced. Output 2: TOT for agricultural extension technicians to conduct farmer field schools (FFS) and train them on climate-smart agriculture approaches.
This project will help farmers rationalize the use of natural resources in a sustainable manner, as well as adopting climate-smart agriculture (CSA) approaches that will contribute to improving local food production at the household and national levels as well, and alleviating losses due to extreme climatic condition.

4 - Needs

Agriculture remains a key economic sector in Syria and a major source of livelihoods for most crisis-affected families. Crop production being sensitive to weather shocks and climate volatility is being affected negatively by climate change and there is urgent need to scale-up and enhance adaptive actions. Many key strategic crops in Syria contribute significantly to the countries food basket and nutritional requirements. For example, Syria is currently a wheat deficit country, meaning that the country does not produce enough to meet its needs. This has resulted in wheat being imported into the Syrian Arab Republic mainly through its seaports and possibly through land border ports. The wheat deficit and crop production losses that have affected other crops is due to various challenges impacting the respective production segment of the various crop value chains. These include the high cost of inputs and unavailability of quality seeds, fertilizers, herbicides, pesticides, fuel and key among these is the erratic rainfall and the increased water scarcity for both rainfed and irrigated crops and climate change.Climate change remains a contributing factor to the worsening food insecurity. The overall estimated Sector PIN for 2022 – of food insecure and those at risk of food insecurity – is estimated at 13.9 million, with 12 million facing acute food insecurity and 1.9 million people at risk of sliding into food insecurity. 9 - Protection Risk Analysis

Beneficiaries being targeted as a result of being selected for project/Risk of community tension arising from beneficiaries selection. Mitigation measures: Inclusion of different categories of affected population (for example IDPs and host communities; returnees and refugees; MWBG.The design of the project has taken into account protection elements, in line with the do-no-harm principles. Measures will be taken to limit the risk of beneficiaries being targeted because of the being selected for the project. Women and youth will be prioritized, inputs and trainings will take account of the different requirements of women and men, boys and girls. Returnees will also be targeted so that re-settlement will be aided. Where IDPs are selected, representatives from the host communities will be included as well, to limit possible friction between the two groups. The implementation of field activities will be in line with the principles and guidelines on protection developed by the Protection Cluster. An accountability to affected population system including beneficiaries’ participation and appropriate information and feedback/complaint mechanism will also be put in place to provide communities with pathways to express their concerns and grievances. This will be used to make adjustments and improvement on the implementation of activities. Special attention will be provided to returnees to help them in their re-settlement and resuming their production activities.
