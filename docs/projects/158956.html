Project Summary

This project is a roll over from 2019 HRP project HPSE19-EDU;PRO-150670-1. The proposed project is in line with the EIEWG education 2019 framework objectives 1 and 2 and the protection objective related to legal aid in West Bank. The project aims to provide protection of vulnerable children affected by conflict and other situations of violence, particularly those at risk of denied access to educational services and individual and mass forcible transfer in the West Bank. The project will target 61 most vulnerable Ministry of Education and Higher Education (MoEHE) schools supporting children access to a protective and enabling educational experience and will be four-fold: 1) provision of psycho-educational support through training of teachers on how to manage a classroom with children who have gone through stressful and traumatic events, how to better support under-achievers and training of counsellors to support students with chronic nightmares that severely affect their school functioning thought the NRC flagship ‘Better Learning Program’ (BLP); 2) provision of emergency school kits to support crisis-disaster risk reduction in tandem with capacity building; 3) delivery of legal information sessions through awareness raising of students, school staff and parents about legal rights and protection as well as response to incidents/violations through documentation, including response and referral mechanism to document interferences/impediments to the delivery of education in schools, and strengthen the legal response of the ‘Alert Mechanism’; and 4) advocacy pursuing enhanced protection from violations of the right to education.

1 - Needs

Fifty-one years of occupation of Palestinians and eleven years of siege imposed on Gazahave led to increasingly disintegration of the Palestinian territory and of its people.According to CAAC data between Jan to June 2018, 13,539 children aged between 3 to 17were affected by incidents of grave violations. This marked an increase compared tosame period in 2017 when 10,615 were registered. In 2017, 170 education-relatedincidents were documented by the UN. All - except 7 - incidents took place in the WestBank (WB). In particular, the right to education was significantly hampered by clashes inand around schools, search and arrest operations, restrictions of access to schools, settlerviolence related incidents against schools, arrest and intimidation of school children andteachers. 165 incidents of interference with education by IF were documented, affectingover 27,000 students. There has been to date, no research that has explicitly linked thegrowing rights-based violations, to key learning outcomes. In the recent study (AnnStrømmesen, 2018) carried out in four most vulnerable schools, it was found that almosthalf of the students expressed challenges with social relationships and/orcommunication, and finding difficulties in talking to others. Stress and anger constitutedthe largest emotional subgroups while another characteristic found was the feeling oflack of knowledge, resulting in students feeling helpless. The protracted conflict has longlastingnegative effects on the psychosocial well-being of children and their caregivers.Teachers and counsellors often lack the skills and response methodology to identify thesechildren and respond in an appropriate manner. Teaching methods continue to rely onmemorization and note learning, 60% remain unqualified (EDPS 2014-2019). In the ‘NRCImproved Education’ evaluation (Sue Nicholson 2018) whilst 97% girls stated their teacherwas kind and helpful only 40% of boys did, however, 79% girls said their teacher wassometimes angry and punished children a lot compared to 38% boys. 60% stating theyhad problems getting to school on time with more girls (52%) than boys (34%) saying theyexperienced problems. In the BLP evaluation (Ritesh Shah 2017) it was reported that 36%of counsellors suffered from sleep disturbances, over 70% of them suffered fromconsistent feelings of sadness and 51% felt helpless. The findings of the NRC assessmentcarried out in Nov. 2017 show that in 21 schools 93% needed continued support: i) 90% ofschool principals reported that students experienced trauma, 93% reported that there werestudents underachieving, specifically (24%) underachievers and (17%) traumatizedchildren. All recommended to continue providing legal awareness sessions and support inthe activation of the referral system. Although 96% of the schools' counsellors receivedthe BLP training, further follow-up and consolidation of the BLP methodology andoverseeing of the techniques was identified as a need, 87% reported the lack of schoolmaterials. The recent After Action Review (M. Cluttenberck, 2018) considers theeffectiveness of the ‘Alert Mechanism (AM) for legal response to attacks againsteducation’, looking at the need for it, beneficiary buy-in, relationship with other responsemechanisms, and the sustainability of the mechanism. Main needs highlighted: i)consideration of better ways to provide legal and social protection to encourage victims topursue legal responses; ii) development of a single coordinated response mechanismbetween MoEHE, UNICEF, NRC and SCI; iii) greater referral of freedom of movement andsettler violence cases to NRC; iv) hold workshop involving key legal and educationalactors to coordinate roles; v) Development of a joint legal/advocacy/protection/educationstrategy amongst key stakeholders to focus on certain patterns of education-relatedviolations; and vi) focus group discussions with parents/teachers to discuss theeffectiveness of the AM.

9 - Protection mainstreaming
9.1 - Do No Harm

NRC recognises that safeguarding of children and vulnerable adults engaged in their programmes is a fundamental responsibility. All staff must sign and are trained on NRC’s Code of Conduct, and are now required to sign and be trained on the organisation’s new Child Safeguarding Policy. The training for Education staff and staff with regular contact with children includes content on child rights, understanding child abuse and sexual abuse, reviews behaviour and conduct required from staff and how to receive disclosures and report and refer cases. BLP content is also designed to promote safe and inclusive learning environments for children and should support staff and teachers’ understanding of child protection. NRC Action trusts working on incorporating protection principles and prompting meaningful access, safety, and dignity in the proposed humanitarian aid. At the very beginning of the action in the needs assessment phase, the NRC will identify the people at risk, kind of risks they face and expected to face, and why they face such kind of risks, as well as activating a structured referral system who people who seek more help and support.The NRC action follows the participatory approach, where beneficiaries will be included from the early beginning of the action, participating in the needs assessment, monitoring and evaluation phase, and in identifying the area of improvement. Moreover, all beneficiaries will have the same chance of receiving the service (e.g. school children will receive the service at a voluntary manner and with a respected informed consent from their parents). Both teachers, and counselors as a main beneficiaries in NRC action will participate in a voluntary manner, and due to their interest. A follow up mechanism will be shared with all NRC suggested beneficiaries, where open channels of communication will be offered by NRC (e.g. Generic emails, field visits….etc) with all beneficiaries that guarantee the confidentiality.

9.2 - Equal and impartial access to aid

The NRC action focuses on three dimensions of intervention: i) a general classroom-based intervention approach targeted at all children in target schools, its suitable for all children in any context, as its designed to reach larger numbers of children through classroom delivery. This dimension is designed to ensure and guarantee the equal chances of access for all children both males and females and their specific needs. ii) The second dimension offers a special kind of support to children who need additional support, to support resilience a mong them for the early recovery or protracted displacement when conditions for learning have stabilised. iii) The third dimension is a specialised, clinical one designed to address nightmares, which many children experience as a chronic symptoms of traumatic stress. The equal chances of both males and females, boys and girls, special need or without special need children are affordable based on the real needs, beneficiaries interest and potential. As well as without any kind of discrepancies among the beneficiaries either they are children or adult. The NRC action welcomes all children being part of the service delivered regardless of the differences among them based on inclusion approach.
