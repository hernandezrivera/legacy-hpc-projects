Needs

Decades of conflict and violence in Iraq have severely affected provision of critical water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) services and resulted in large-scale damage of water and sanitation infrastructure. In this context, potential outbreak of waterborne diseases remain a significant concern. Prolonged exposure to poor water quality, inadequate sanitation facilities and services, and insufficient awareness of good health and hygiene practices all contribute to placing vulnerable populations, among them children and women, at increased risk of disease, including Acute Watery Diarrhoea (AWD) and potential outbreaks of cholera, which is endemic in Iraq with the last major outbreak in 2015. Need for support for increasing access to and improving quality and sustainability of WASH services and facilities will continue into 2019. Based on the Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO) 2019, over 2.2 million people across Iraq remain in critical need of sustained, equitable access to safe and appropriate WASH services. Of these, more than half (52 per cent) are women, nearly half (47 per cent are children), and 4 per cent are elderly. Protracted displacement from homes and places of origin can increase levels of vulnerability found in a population. Since 2014, more than 5 million people have been displaced in Iraq at one point or another, often fleeing intense armed conflict to live in formal camps, informal settlements, temporary shelter such as schools or mosques, or in host communities. Despite a decrease in armed violence in the country in 2018, there remain 1,866,594 people internally displaced in Iraq (International Organisation for Migration, Displacement Tracking Matrix, October 2018). While more than 4 million people have reportedly returned from their displacement locations to their places of origin (IOM, DTM, October 2018), one of the main constraints preventing internally displaced people (IDP) from returning to their areas of origin are the unavailability of quality water and sanitation services (Iraq CCCM Returns Monitoring Analysis, 3 September 2018). Further, in 2018 an acute concern has been the increasing water scarcity in Iraq since water levels in both of the main water sources, namely the Tigris and the Euphrates rivers, have been decreasing at an unprecedented rate. This is caused by changes in the country’s climatic and meteorological conditions, with increases in average temperatures compounded by lower average rainfall throughout the year, and water damming actions by neighbouring countries. In addition, these environmental and geo-political elements are exacerbated by continuing poor water resource management in Iraq. In recent years much of Iraq’s health and education infrastructure has suffered damage or neglect caused by armed conflict, use of schools as temporary shelter for IDPs, neglect, or lack of investment in maintaining or improving facilities. Many schools and health facilities, especially in areas of return that saw active armed conflict between 2014 and 2017, rely on public water supply, provided through sub-standard or damaged, infrastructure for meeting their drinking water needs. Poor community sewer services leave these facilities with the only option of on-site sanitation through latrines linked to cesspools or septic tanks threatening the surrounding environment; most septic tanks are ‘soakaways’ that do not treat sewage, either as closed systems that require disposal, or open systems which allow sewer water to be disposed into ground water. For children in schools, a shortage of fixtures, inferior quality construction of facilities, improper drainage arrangements, lack of hand-washing facilities along with poor hygiene practices, and lack of special designs for children with disabilities are amongst main constraints to access to appropriate facilities.

Mitigation Measures

WASH humanitarian response aim at obtaining full respect for the rights of the targeted populations, where key protection principles will be considered into the humanitarian response, UNICEF will mainstream protection ensuring that WASH services are based on the needs considered equity, equality and privacy. UNICEF in coordination with partners will build partner capacity to mainstream protection activities, in coordination with the Protection Cluster. WASH field teams will receive protection training and context-specific tools will be disseminated. Specific vulnerable groups may struggle to access WASH services. WASH will ensure all services are more accessible by consultatively addressing the specific needs of the elderly, people living with disabilities, and other vulnerable groups. UNICEF in coordination with WASH partners will ensure WASH services follow a ‘do no harm’ approach by systematically analysing the benefits of the response versus its potential negative impacts and mitigate these risks accordingly. Manage information in a sensitive manner so that the security of informants or others who may be identifiable is not jeopardized and support the efforts of the affected population and local communities to find security and restore dignity. For instance, WASH will ensure facilities consistently meet quality standards in terms of lighting, privacy and gender segregation. It will also receive feedback on protections issues from partners and users through WASH committees, community-based groups, and local representatives. UNICEF will train male and female service providers, hygiene promoters and WASH committees to ensure decision-making, monitoring and care for installed infrastructure is a shared responsibility. In additional to using available complaints mechanisms and referral of protection issues, focused group sessions and feedback from especially vulnerable interest groups of women. UNICEF will ensure providing equitable and impartial access to assistance based on need and guard against any form of direct or indirect discrimination along with paying special attention to individuals and marginalize groups. UNICEF will promote respect for human rights, ensuring accountability for affected people by set-up appropriate mechanisms through which they can measure the adequacy of interventions, address concerns and complaints., claim their rights and access remedies from relevant authorities; to obtain information on their entitlements and secure the documentation needed to demonstrate their entitlements; and to recover by providing psychosocial and community support; ensure consultation with the target population at all stages, and the participation of all in the design and targeting of interventions, vulnerable and marginalized groups UNICEF will ensure that locations of WASH facilities are safe, well-lit and secured by a secondary enclosure to ensure privacy (adequate and separate space for women/girls, people with disabilities), WASH facilities are adapted and designed for safe use by all sections of the population, including children, people with disabilities, the elderly and pregnant, potential health hazards for the target population associated with the contamination of water due to the destruction or damage of water infrastructure, poor maintenance or repair…etc

How is your project accountable to affected population

UNICEF is accountable to women, boys, girls and men whose lives it aims to improve and places this responsibility at the core of its humanitarian policy. Two-way dialogue between UNICEF staff and affected populations is facilitated to ensure dissemination and collection of key information so to ensure that programmes continue to deliver results for affected populations. The UNICEF-led WASH Service Centres (WSC) initiative works through local NGO partners, in some cases with staff from the IDP population. Throughout 2018, the initiative has expanded to a network of 18 WSCs each covering specific geographic areas in conflict- and displacement-affected governorates, facilitating a stronger feedback and complaints system for IDPs on services provided. This mechanism has significantly enhanced UNICEF’s capacity to involve, and to be accountable to, affected populations in Iraq. One of the key element of the success of the WSC initiative is the response capacity in a difficult environment, where WSC staff are IDPs living in the camp or in nearby host communities. They are familiar with the challenges faced in the local area, and have already gained the trust and built relationships with the people in the area of response. Participation by IDPs in the WSC model facilitates an active role in decision-making processes that affect them and their communities, making WSCs key partners in follow up and feedback for in service delivery.
