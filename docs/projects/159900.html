Project Summary

This project seeks to build on gains made through programming in 2015–2019, meeting the needs of approximately 111,180 conflict and famine-affected individuals (53,366 males and 57,814 females) in South Sudan through an integrated Nutrition, Agriculture and Food Security, and Water, Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) response.
The areas of South Sudan targeted for this project include Mayendit, Aweil East, Aweil North (NBeG), Abiemnhom and Mayom Counties. Under Agriculture and Food Security programming, Agricultural Training Groups (ATGs) will be formed, seeds and tools will be distributed to beneficiaries and fishing kits with training on best practices will be provided to improve future food security. Households (HHs) with children under five years of age (U5) (who are more susceptible to water-related diseases and disproportionately affected by malnutrition) will be specifically targeted for Infant and Young Child Feeding (IYCF) trainings to improve their nutritional wellbeing. Nutrition training and cooking demonstrations will also be provided to ATGs and Care Group members to help mitigate the underlying causes of undernutrition, and complement Agriculture and Food Security, and WASH activities. The Care Group methodology will be used to cascade nutrition behavior change messaging and activities to pregnant women and mothers of children under two years of age including Women of Reproductive Age (WRA). Key decision makers in the communities will be mobilized and sensitized on maternal nutrition, optimal IYCF practices, HH dietary diversity, and gender empowerment. To increase adequate access to clean water, hand pumps will be rehabilitated, a manual drill will be utilized to drill additional wells, and existing water points will be upgraded to solarized systems to enhance water yields for increased beneficiary coverage. In addition, water-management committees (WMCs) will be formed and hand pump mechanics will be trained in a bid to foster community ownership and sustainability of the water infrastructures. With regard to efforts to improve health and hygiene practices, communal and HH latrines, and gender-segregated bathing shelters will be constructed, and trainings will be conducted on better health and hygiene practices.

1 - Needs

It is estimated that 5.8 million South Sudanese will require food and livelihoods support in 2019. According to the January 2019 IPC analysis report, 57% of the population of South Sudan is facing at least crisis levels (Phase 3) of acute food insecurity – a 9% increase in comparison with the same period in 2018. With the anticipated lean period, it is expected that the food insecurity situation will deteriorate further, affecting approximately 6.87 million people, including 1.91 million people at the emergency (Phase 4) level. Aweil North, Aweil West and Mayendit Counties are expected to transition into emergency levels of food insecurity while Abiemnhom and Mayom Counties are anticipated to remain in the crisis phase in the coming months. In reference to the baseline survey conducted by SP in 2018, the food security situation in Abiemnhom, Mayom, Mayendit and NBeG still faces a great challenge as the majority of HHs had a poor FCS. Compared to 2017, food insecurity worsened in NBeG and Mayendit Counties in 2018. Only 20% of HHs in Mayendit were considered food secure compared to 41% in 2017, another 45% were borderline, and 36% were severely food insecure. In NBeG, 39% of HHs were food secure in comparison with 47% in 2017, 33% were borderline and 28% were severely food insecure. Results for Abiemnhom and Mayom showed that 61% of HHs were food secure, compared to 50% in 2017, 32% were borderline, and 7% were severely food insecure, indicating a slight improvement in the overall food security situation in the two counties in 2018 compared to 2017. Even though most HHs participated in planting crops, many of them continued to struggle with food insecurity. On average, the communities had 10 Months of Adequate Household Food Provisioning (MAHFP), slightly above the minimum of nine months. This was attributed largely to General Food Distribution (GFD) rations and rotational open operation of the market in the region, depending on the security situation. However, respondents indicated that food from their own production lasted only two to less than six months, and, on average, planted only two feddans, implying that most of the HHs experience food shortage during the lean months of April-August before they start harvesting what they planted. In addition, during Focus Group Discussions (FGDs), many women cited limited male support in cultivation and scarcity of seeds (both staple and vegetable) to cultivate. Nonetheless, the same survey revealed that community members have learned basic agronomy practices through mentorship programs between lead and follower farmers over the past years of OFDA supported programming. However, there is still a need for strengthening agronomy training, community follow-up and sensitization for sustainable uptake of improved agronomic practices coupled with diversification of crop varieties, in an effort to increase food production.

2 - Activities

According to the 2018 baseline assessment, fishing remains one of the most viable livelihood options for communities located close to water bodies. However, the fishing industry, with its immense potential, has yet to be exploited by the populations in the intervention locations; only 40% of HHs in Mayendit practiced fishing, and this percentage was even lower in Abiemnhom (27%) and Mayom (19%). Focus Group Discussions conducted by SP during the baseline study revealed that, despite knowing different fishing methods, many HHs did not practice fishing, citing inadequate fishing equipment and lack of a market for the fish during the rainy seasons, in addition to long transportation distances, as major impediments. Therefore, Agriculture and Food Security intervention aims to improve the protection and rehabilitation of livelihoods by increasing access to quality seeds and tools and improving crop management through agronomy training to increase food production. Proposed activities will reflect the preference of targeted participants and will be implemented in a manner that will, as much as possible, contribute to environmental sustainability. Activities will expand to new areas in Abiemnhom County and maintain the 2018 areas of Mayom, Mayendit, Aweil East, and Aweil North, serving 7,600 HHs.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

Samaritan’s Purse is anticipating that the emergency in the five counties will most likely fluctuate during the life of this project. According to the UNOCHA 2019 Humanitarian Response Plan assumptions, in the short-term, displacement will remain both a driver and a result of vulnerability, although the United Nations Country Team is actively exploring likely scenarios for refugees’ and IDPs´ responses to the revitalized peace process and the accelerated momentum gained through the October 2018 national peace celebrations. Initially, people who have been displaced may return temporarily or seasonally to farm; to evaluate their options regarding housing, land and property and access to livelihoods, services and available support; and to assess their confidence in safety and security. Some of the early returns may not be sustainable due to insecurity and unmet assistance and protection needs, particularly related to housing, land and property. Against this background, there is a strong probability that displacement will continue in South Sudan including Mayom, Abiemnhom, Mayendit, Aweil East, and Aweil North Counties. The IDPs will most likely remain displaced until permanent peace is established.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

Samaritan’s Purse implements project activities and coordinates closely with the Agriculture and Food Security cluster. A participatory approach is used to work with community leadership and local government in the targeted counties, as part of the design and implementation of all project activities in an effort to promote ownership and enhance sustainability.

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?
6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

Activities proposed under the Agriculture and Food Security Sector will improve food security among IDPs and communities in targeted areas through increased and diversified food production and livelihood opportunities.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

The program in the proposed areas is designed to be a temporary intervention and focuses on emergency response. However, there are several IDP host communities within Mayendit, Abiemnhom, and Mayom, who will remain within the counties once IDPs return to their homes. Samaritan’s Purse’s primary mechanism for transition is, therefore, to work with community leaders and local government to ensure local ownership of project activities and that water and sanitation infrastructures are managed well even after the project ends.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

Community participation at all stages of the project cycle will be encouraged and ensured through community meetings with local leaders (village elders, WMCs and health committees). This will ensure that beneficiaries provide feedback on their level of satisfaction with the quality and effectiveness of the assistance received and project activities. Samaritan’s Purse will use qualitative methods during bi-annual assessments to understand the community’s opinions, feelings, beliefs and perspectives on how to improve the program. Each sector will develop a dissemination strategy for sharing lessons learned internally as well as externally. Program Managers will also forward evaluation findings to local authorities and beneficiaries’ representatives such as Lead Farmers and Lead Mothers to enable findings to be synthesized and linked to program planning adjustments. For all these purposes, the evaluation findings and lessons will be presented in a brief, reader-friendly summary format tailored for the targeted groups. Stakeholder´s workshops will also be held during the program opening and closing to discuss the project plans, roles of partners and expectations. At these workshops, stakeholders (particularly beneficiaries and staff) will be consulted about the standards adopted by projects being undertaken in their areas and the mechanisms available for addressing any concerns that they may have. Throughout the project progress, participants will be updated on the status of the project, and any problems will be solved together. Program Managers and beneficiaries (together with their leaders such as Lead Farmers and Lead Mothers) will meet quarterly to discuss the progress of the project. A well-developed documentation system is in place to allow the program team to follow-up on emerging cases and track action points. Samaritan’s Purse has a policy and system of addressing complaints which enables beneficiaries and staff to report complaints and seek redress safely.
