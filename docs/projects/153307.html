Project Summary

CARE seeks to implement GBV prevention, response and risk mitigation activities by providing quality and timely age appropriate case management and psychosocial support to GBV survivors in Koch, Uror, Maiwut, Abiemnom, Pibor, Torit, Lafon, Ikotos in Jonglei, Unity, Upper Nile and Eastern Equatoria States. This project is designed to improve responses to cases of GBV, enhance the ability of survivors to recover from GBV, and make progress towards changing the social norms that perpetuate GBV, with the intent of fully mitigating the effects of GBV and preventing future cases. It will provide GBV response services and address critical gaps in information and knowledge among women and girls on GBV response, which limit access to services and heighten GBV risk. CARE will encourage health-seeking behaviours among GBV survivors, facilitating referrals, and creating supportive structures to prevent GBV. CARE will work in collaboration with local partners and will establish and operationalize women and girls’ safe spaces and provide periodic individual and group psycho social support for women and girls. This will include dialogue sessions, psychosocial support through group counselling sessions, skills building, production of reusable sanitary pads using locally available materials, alternative fuel strategies through production of fuel efficient stoves, information sessions on pertinent issues to women girls on access to available services, sexual and reproductive health issues on safe deliveries, family planning and post abortion care, personal hygiene, menstrual hygiene management, decision making and peer to peer support. This project will support the healing process of GBV survivors by providing community based case management and psychosocial support services and engage men and boys in community dialogues and awareness raising sessions on GBV, its effect and consequences and their roles in prevention. Protection interventions will be integrated within CARE’s health, nutrition and FSL programmes to compliment holistic assistance to communities through static and mobile services. CARE has experienced increased uptake of protection services through mobile services as people felt comfortable to seek help close to their homes. CARE will ensure commitment to diversity and gender equity as CARE’s core business is premised on women and girls’ empowerment and therefore will strive to periodically carry out gender and protection analysis including assessments of vulnerabilities related to age, sex and diversity, and will give priority to vulnerable population. CARE will ensure protection from sexual exploitation and abuse for beneficiaries through awareness and training to CARE’s staff, partners, volunteers, vendors, contractors and awareness to ensure beneficiaries are aware of PSEA and reporting mechanisms. As part of CARE’s conviction for effective humanitarian programmes, addressing the humanitarian needs of women and girls remains an integral part of any response in South Sudan. In many instances, women play multiple roles in relief and recovery activities, as participants, organizers and leaders. CARE takes a deliberate focus on women by recognizing that disaster and conflict increase women’s vulnerability to poverty, violence and other human rights violations. To this end, CARE will work with women’s rights organizations to build their capacity to take leadership of, participate and be represented in humanitarian coordination. The project will ensure that women are engaged and supported to become first responders within their communities. CARE will work to advance women’s rights, voices and leadership through increasing their knowledge and their participation in humanitarian coordination. This will contribute to women’s protection and empowerment and will transform women’s position in emergency response and resilience, while helping to address the core issues that affect communities in emergencies.

Needs

From the outbreak of conflict in December 2013, nearly 7.5 million people are in need of humanitarian assistance and protection across the country. The number of people uprooted since the start of the conflict has reached more than 4 million including 1.9 million internally displaced people, with up to 85 percent estimated to be women, girls and children. The humanitarian and protection needs are compounded by high rates of malnutrition and severe levels of food insecurity. More than half of women aged 15–24 have suffered some form of gender-based violence. According to a 2017 study on what works to prevent violence against women and girls’ study by GWI, IRC and CARE, GBV is pervasive in these conflict zones with up to 65% of women and girls experiencing physical and/or sexual violence in their lifetime. These are among the highest rates in the world. Women and girls have experienced either physical or sexual violence from a partner or non-partner in the course of their lifetime. The Gender-based Violence Information Management System data for 2017 recorded 95% of reported GBV survivors were women and girls (South Sudan GBV Sub Cluster Strategy, 2018). A 2017 CARE study on trends of change in a turbulent context; social norms and barriers analysis for women and girls in Jonglei and Eastern Equatoria, indicates that about 52% of girls in South Sudan are estimated to be married by the age of 18. In Jonglei, marriage related conversations begins either at menstruation even earlier at 7 or 8 years of age. A 2017 IOM survey found that in one out of two households (50% of households surveyed) a woman or girl had experienced some form of GBV in the past 12 months. The deepening economic crisis have forced women and girls to risk their lives to fend for their families including resorting to negative coping mechanisms such as sex for food “transactional sex” (CARE; HCT Protection Strategy Baseline Assessment Survey (2016). The continued practice of dowry payments perpetuates the belief that girls are both a source of wealth for their families and property of their prospective (CARE, 2017). There is a complex relationship between gender roles, social norms, food insecurity and GBV and a persisting phenomenon of ‘hyper-masculinity’ amongst men often exacerbated by the conflict of risk and insecurity. This reinforces harmful power differentials between men and women (and children), often leading to GBV and other forms of violence. Patriarchal societal norms that promote gender inequality (including men minimising the occurrence of violence, and both men and women accepting the use of violence) are common, (CARE, 2017). While the crisis has dramatically increased the challenges that households face, the division of labour has not shifted, but rather has strengthened with women facing immense additional burdens when trying to feed, clothe, and protect their families. Women and girls are seen as assets in obtaining bride price, a social function closely linked to income security and male dominance and status while boys are seen as heirs to the family wealth, property and family name (CARE, 2016). Evidence gathered by CARE, GWI & IRC on Lessons from South Sudan in a September, 2018 study on intersections of violence against women and girls with state-building and peace-building suggests that war and conflict also increase other forms of VAWG – including forced marriage and that other forms of endemic violence, such as intimate partners violence are more prevalent during conflict compared to conflict-related sexual violence. It also confirms that violence against women and girls, gender inequality and conflict are often mutually reinforcing and that during times of conflict, gender inequality remains the core cause of GBV. Despite the prevalence of GBV, there is still insufficient services to respond to the needs of survivors compounded with limited knowledge on availability of services.

Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

CARE considers the following factors that might bar access;• Inclusion and exclusion errors leading to tensions in the communities• Shift of power dynamics within the households• Increasing exposure to exploitation by employers, clients, suppliers• Inciting backlash from family or community members when women start earning money• Increasing vulnerabilities of theft and violence due to greater assets and wealth• Reinforcing inequalities by continuing to limit choices to more vulnerable groups• Causing false expectations and dependence on short term surge of cash or assets• Increasing costs and drawing on resources, such as providing small livestock without fodder or veterinary care• Exacerbating tensions between host community and affected communityCARE’s integrated approach programming begins with an analysis of target beneficiaries to understand the barriers. Our analysis takes into account a protection issues and concerns to including internal and external shocks that are often the immediate barriers CARE’s program design is consequently built on a model that is focused on sustainability by designing interventions that address the multi-dimensional (individual, relational, and structural) barriers to achieving gender equality. A priority is placed on understanding the impact that gender has ensuring that program interventions do not over-burden women, but rather, advance gender equality. CARE’s approach to protection often includes selecting a broad range of collectives, such as self-help groups to facilitate the engagement of a broad base of vulnerable beneficiaries. These fora serve as a common platform to disseminate information on protection, FSL, health and nutrition. The intent is to promote behavior change at the HHs and community levels so that a broad base of vulnerable beneficiaries are able to access and control various resources and activities within households and with formal institutions. The result is an inclusive and sustainable process.

Project activities address inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

CARE tackles inequality and incorporates protection threats throughout all stages of its response cycle. As a Protection cluster co-lead in Eastern Equitoria and SAG member of the GBV sub - cluster nationally, CARE is ideally placed upon alert, to raise, consult and resolve any issues with the protection cluster to ensure an intervention will not cause harm. Regular protection assessments are conducted for all of CARE’s projects in close coordination with other partners and stakeholders and enforced by protection staff involvement during project implementation where possible. In the first stage of assessment, partners review the context for conflict sensitive and issues, and referral pathways, in order to improve understanding of the context dynamics and therefore decrease harm on the community. CARE’s field assessments begin with safety audits to understand risks of every gender. Both the assessment and response stages of CARE projects incorporate a high level of community engagement, capacity building, and feedback mechanisms in order to ensure all people, especially marginalized groups, have an active role in the way CARE projects are carried out. With evidence about particular vulnerabilities, CARE takes special measures to support vulnerable beneficiaries. Where possible, integrated thematic outreach and protection messaging is carried out.

Does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

In all its operational areas, CARE is guided by the principles of fairness, high quality of work, respect, focus on community engagement and interaction, and integrity that have acknowledged by the communities and beneficiaries it serves. Strong community acceptance and buy in is secured during initial project design consultations, project inception during and after project implementation. Aside from the sporadic insecurity incidents of which community members and beneficiaries / CARE vice versa regularly warn of the threats and risks access is generally not a problem for CARE and beneficiaries since they are regularly consulted on appropriate site selection, developing beneficiary targeting criteria & selection and good open lines of communication have been established which ensure that only vulnerable beneficiaries are targeted and benefit. From previous experience, in new communities CARE is more than often invited to work with them, as opposed to the need to negotiate access into them. Regular joint supervision field visits are purposed and conducted in consultation with stakeholders and partners to monitor access and quality of services being proffered to beneficiaries.

Does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

CARE maintains a security team at country office whose responsibility is to conduct security risk assessments before project roll out, during implementation and after in all its operational sites. CARE works to obtain required security clearance before traveling to the proposed project locations in light of the context. In case of any impending or actualized insecurity incidents CARE endeavours to keep our beneficiaries, cluster members and other stakeholders duly informed of any associated changes. Before, during and after project implementation, CARE undertakes significant safety and security briefing for beneficiaries on the relevant context specific safety and security risks that face them. From experience, it is noted that communities and beneficiaries are very aware of the different risks and appropriate safety and security measure they can take. In some cases, as a result of the close collaboration, they do have prior information of such incidences and inform our staffs who then implement precautionary/ remedial measures. Continuous conducted safety and security trainings have ensured that staff have a growing security awareness.

Does the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

Yes

How the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

CARE bases its humanitarian response on impartial assessment of needs, vulnerabilities and capacities to identify the most appropriate response. These assessments provide CARE with an understanding of the humanitarian situation and analysis of threats to life, dignity, health and livelihoods, as well as people’s ability to help themselves. And further help project teams determine, in consultation with the relevant authorities, organisations and the affected population, what CARE's response should be and whom it should target. Considerable emphasis is placed on beneficiary targeting criteria and selection based on disaggregated data by sex and age to ensure that women, girls, boys and men are targeted appropriately. Some groups will be at greater risk because of their age, gender, disability, social status, ethnicity or religion. CARE through these assessments seeks to understand these distinctions and tailor its response accordingly. Our action endeavours to reflect impartial assessment, and the right of those affected by an emergency to the minimum conditions they need to live in dignity. It aims to reinforce local capacities for self-help by vulnerable and at risk beneficiaries, communities and the response of partners and authorities. Ultimately, CARE endeavors by building resilience to minimize communities’ future risk of disaster.
