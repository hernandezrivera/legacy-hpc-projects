Project Summary

The project involves provision of training, mentoring and livelihood kits to conflict, drought and flood affected communities in Greater Upper Nile (Pigi Canal, Old Fangak, Ulang) and Western Equatoria ( Mundri East, Mundri West and Mvolo). The support will target 10000 returnees, internally displaced persons and vulnerable host households ( 60000 individuals) in Western Equatoria ( 4000 households) and 6000 households in Greater Upper Nile. The intervention is designed to bridge the trajectory from relief to early recovery through revitalization of existing and establishment of new agriculture activities. Supported with training on agriculture and protection, the project seeks to strengthen beneficiaries' resilience to cope with natural ( droughts, floods) and man made disasters (conflict) thereby, reducing their exposure to risk and negative coping strategies. Project activities have been designed to mitigate risks to beneficiaries by ensuring that input distribution is done on time and at various distribution sites. The project will seek to integrate livelihood and education through promotion of the establishment of school based vegetable gardens.

Needs

FH conducted a needs assessment in its current project areas in Fangak and Ulang with a focus on FSL, Nutrition, WASH and Protection. The findings for the October, 2018 assessment included the following:Findings Food Security and Livelihood The predominant livelihood is agriculture (76%) while other sources of livelihood in the project target area include fishing, livestock, and wage labour. The main source of food is dependent on one’s ability to grow food which has largely not been possible due to displacement. FEWSNET updates for Upper Nile and Jonglei States recorded emergency outcomes from June to September 2018 and this has been affirmed by the Food Security and Nutrition Monitoring System (December 2017) whose finding states that the country is facing one of the worst food security situation since 2010. The worsening food security indicators have been as a result of insecurity whereby 86% reported that it was unsafe to plant, while 71% of respondents reported that fighting destroyed crops. This has also had an impact on the market trends in addition to the increase in fuel and inflation rate of 89.47% - as at September 2018, according to online sources. The assessment also positively identified specific categories of the population who face extreme difficulties in accessing food. The level of difficulty varied; women (34%), the elderly (24%), orphans (20%), widows (12%), persons with disabilities (PWD) (8%) as well as children (3%).Reduced access to food resulted in settlements reporting increasingly severe consumption coping strategies and health concerns. For instance, only 1% of assessed settlements in Ulang reported that they were able to consume three meals per day while 32% reported that they could only consume one meal per day in Fangak. Crop destruction has left settlements to deal with a potential lack of food especially during the lean season. A common observation amongst these communities was the glaring limited knowledge on sustainable farming knowledge thus, there is an urgent need to strengthen their capacities with knowledge along the whole production chain from seedbed preparation to harvesting and storage. Importantly, the study noted the need to strengthen their knowledge in the relevant crop, vegetable and fishing value chains.The study also noted the need for investing in contemporary thematic areas such as protection, gender and information dissemination. Overall, the communities require further information on disaster preparedness and positive coping mechanisms. Furthermore, the study noted the need to integrate protection and mainstream gender into agricultural activities as a way of enhancing sustainable production.

Activities

Food Security and Livelihood Activities1. Community mobilisation and sensitisation: As one way of ensuring awareness and ownership of the program, communities will, at the start of program implementation, be sensitised on the proposed goal, activities, implementation strategy and expected outputs. 2. Community-based targeting criteria validation: Beneficiary selection will be community-driven using an agreed criterion. 3. Registration and verification of beneficiaries: The community-based registration process will be carried out by FH staff members and community led committees. 4. Distribution of seeds and fishing kits: FH will distribute seeds (crops and vegetables) to 10000 vulnerable households for both the main and dry season planting. 5. Crops, Vegetables and Fishing training: FH will engage with beneficiaries from the point of distribution through the entire lifecycle of the crops through trainings in basic agricultural practices and to follow-up with the beneficiaries. Trainings will be implemented using different approaches like demonstration plots and topics including, basic agronomic training consisting of seed selection, drying, intercropping, proper seeding rate, row planting, weeding, timely harvesting, soil conservation, food storage and kitchen/vegetable gardening. 6. Training on fishing will include net braiding, fishing techniques, fish processing and marketing Community-based facilitators will be used to cascade trainings who will include field demonstrations of the techniques to allow for proper feedback and monitoring of practice. 7. In order to encourage maximum food utilization, training on food storage, preservation and preparation will be conducted. 8. FH will provide trainings to 20% of the beneficiaries with agriculture training to increase agricultural gains from the farm inputs. These farmers will then be expected to reach out to at least five beneficiary farmers to share the learnings. Fall Army Worm (FAW) Responses Training and Awareness will also be conducted.9. FH will coordinate and work with other agencies and partners in South Sudan and key elements of our strategy include:• Collaborate with FAO and WFP initiatives to further train FH agricultural extension workers on the identification, monitoring and surveillance of the FAWs in the target areas. • Train farmers on the identification of FAW and control measures.

Does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

Yes

How does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

The information dissemination strategies adopted for this project will include:(i) From FH to community members through formal gatherings such as input distribution sites. In particular, pre distribution meetings are conducted as a mechanism for disseminating information. (ii) Through school children. Announcements are made at school and children cascade information especially on distribution dates and times.(iii) Through local leadership - an important constituent of the programme will be the involvement of local leadership - both traditional ( chiefs etc) and non traditional ( county commissioners and RRC).(iv) Songs, dance and drama will be the vehicles through which information on gender and protection issues are communicated at community gatherings.(v) Information, education and communication materials such as banners, T shirts and fliers ( in vernacular) will be used to disseminate project strategies, goals and particular themes(vi) A hotline will be activated to disseminate from the beneficiaries to FH(vii) FH field staff will also be a conduit for information dissemination and overall coordination.

Are messages context-specific or/and tailored?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Messaging will be project specific and context specific. For example, information being dissemniated to children will be child friendly to arouse their interest and ease of understanding. Illiterate community members will be communicated with through messages that are in vernacular whilst government officials will primarily be communicated with through the English language in formal meetings primarily at their offices.

Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse

Yes

Briefly describe how

Part of FH global's standard operating procedures include a comprehensive package of training on sexual abuse and exploitation. This training is further cascaded to office bearers in the field as well as to community leaders as a commitment to safeguarding these issues as well as to mainstream them in daily operations. Specific community members will be trained to safeguard and monitor implementation of protection issues and serve as primary focal persons in the field. Training on referral mechanisms for the same is a key component of FH's strategy.Protection from sexual abuse and exploitation among FH staff is further reinforced through provisions in its polices such as:• Protection Policy (from sexual abuse and exploitation, includes children and women)• Global Security Policy• Whistleblower Policy (Wrongful Conduct Reporting)• Employee Conduct Policy• Ethics Policy• Conflict of Interest Policy• Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) Policy

Nature of the consultations

Yes

Briefly describe the nature of the consultations

Consultations with communities started with the needs assessment that was conducted. This brought together participation of school children, beneficiaries ( IDPs, refugees, returnees and host communities); and their local leadership. In an endeavor to leverage on knowledge and expertise from other implementing partners in the counties, NGO staff were also consulted. Furthermore, non traditional stakeholders in food security and livelihoods, such as law enforcement officials and state gender focal persons were also consulted to further understand protection and gender safeguarding issues.

Feedback /complaint mechanisms?

Yes

Briefly describe the mechanisms

FH engages affected populations to share their perspectives, feedback and complaints. It puts particular emphasis on appropriate and effective systems to communicate, receive, process, respond to and learn from complaints about policy breaches and stakeholder dissatisfaction. Some of the mechanisms that FH has adopted include:(i) National hotline - this is a toll free telephone line that is ahred with all stakeholders for information sharing(ii) Suggestion boxes - these are sealed boxes that are set up in communities for feedbacking purposes.(iii) Monthly progress and review meetings - these are organised with county officials to share progress, lessons learnt, challenges as well as to brainstorm on strategies and solutions to the problems.(iv) Focal persons - these are trained volunteers who act as focal persons for gathering information in the field and sharing with FH staff for actioning.

Community Engagement?

Yes

Briefly describe how

FH has adopted participatory monitoring and evaluation as a strategy for community involvement. It believes that participatory M & E is not just a matter of using participatory techniques within a conventional monitoring and evaluation setting. It is about radically rethinking who initiates and undertakes the process, and who learns or benefits from the findings. As such, communities are engaged in all stages of the implementation cycle starting with registration of beneficiaries whereupon communities are involved in identifying the criteria for inclusion and exclusion. They also participate in beneficiary verification and validation. Specific committees are selected and empowered to see this through. As a practice, FH engages local community volunteers as enumerators in data collection processes - specific activities they participate in include post distribution monitoring; baseline and mid term evaluations. Further, communities participate in data entry and preliminary analyses. Communities are also engaged in community feedback mechanisms as custodians of suggestion boxes as well as instigators of solutions to identified issues.

Protection Risk Analysis?

Yes

Briefly describe how

FH has adopted an approach that is shifting from protection mainstreaming to the 'do no harm' approach that seeks to identify intervention specific protection risks and the strategies for addressing them. Findings from a Food Security and Livelihoods Protection Risk Analysis study conducted in Greater Upper Nile, Western Equatoria and Jonglei states revealed that most of the risks associated with FH interventions are related to distances beneficiaries and staff move for input distribution; the length of time it takes to distribute the items and the considerations for school children to attend school, among others. As a result, FH has decentralised its distribution sites to boma level; has set up a standard of 4 daytime hours for distribution of inputs as well revised its security policies to ensure that vehicles and staff are secure by 6pm, among other provisions.Protection of beneficiaries from sexual abuse will include staff training and community awareness on FH code of conduct and protection from sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA), accessible complaint mechanisms in the local language and accessible to the local population. Additionally, in consultation with women, FH will anticipate and address any negative effects that the distribution of food and inputs to women and the issuance of the same entitlements in women’s names may have on the community and intra-household relationships.

Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Cognizant of the ethnic sensitivities associated with South Sudan, FH takes into account the different barriers that specific population groups face. As a result, project activities such as input distributions and public consultations are structured to ensure that particular population groups such as IDPs and refugees also benefit, the same way returnees and host communities benefit. Representatives of special interest groups such as the disabled are consulted and where possible, coopted into coordination structures to avoid exclusion errors.

Does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Project monitoring activities have been designed to take account of various access issues. These issues are monitored through verification; observations and structured processes like post distribution monitoring surveys that track (i) the time taken to distribute inputs, (ii) the distance travelled by recipients of development aid (iii) how the inputs / food distributed was utilised and (iv) appropriateness and effectiveness of the items distributed. Regular joint field monitoring visits are organised to track progress and address access issues. Based on these, informed decisions are made.

Does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

FH has established a safety and security department that has been instrumental in undertaking security and access assessments for both staff and beneficiaries on a regular basis. These have informed the design of strategies including development of journey management plans that take into effect, potential safety risks ( political, environmental or otherwise) for staff and beneficiaries alike. As a result, contigency planning has become a key feature in FH programme planning.

Does the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

Yes

How the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

FH endeavors to uphold the dignity of its stakeholders and beneficiaries in particular during its implementation process. This is done not only as a way to uplifting their self esteem, but as a standard global practice. Special needs groups such as the disabled are given preferential treatment in distribution processes whilst the elderly are also considered first before the able bodied. FH does not segregate on literacy grounds. As such, both the literate and illiterate form a single line during distribution processes. Those that can sign for their inputs received will sign and those that cannot will use thumb signing. Furthermore, FH endeavours to synchronize distribution processes ( time, duration, and sites) are conducted on dignified sites like church buildings, clinics and schools. Where project activities are conducted, FH will ensure that ablution facilities and mobile water tanks are available to all beneficiaries
