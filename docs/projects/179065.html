Project Summary

Multiple conflict-related protection risks persist and have been further compounded by the financial crisis and COVID-19 outbreak which has eroded family coping strategies and forced girls and boys who would previously have been in school into harmful practices, including begging, child labour and child marriage. In December 2020, communities reported weather-related challenges, seasonality of production and the increased prices of fertilizers as key barriers to income generation. At the same time, neither the host communities, nor the IDP camp residents reported ongoing livelihood assistance in their respective areas. 88% and 73% of interviewed household reported insufficient income and lack of employment opportunities matching people’s skills. These barriers were coupled with the worsening economic situation resulting in increasing food prices, and the rising cost of the survival minimum expenditure basket (SMEB) (REACH, HSOS Dec 2020). In NES, for example, both IDPs and host communities currently rely heavily on intermittent unskilled daily wage labor with low wages. A high number of adolescents and youth have missed critical years of schooling and lack basic literacy and numeracy skills which are critical for the work-force and entrepreneurship. As a result, many have turned to negative coping mechanisms, such as child labor and skipping meals (WFP Food Security Update, March 2020).
Over the last year in the face of the COVID-19 pandemic, food prices across Syria have hugely increased and the value of the Syrian Pound has plummeted. The depreciation of the Syrian pound (SYP) spiraled spiking at a record low of SYP 4,700/USD 1 on 17 March 2021 before stabilizing around SYP 3,700/USD 1 by late March 2021. As a result, it is expected that the food security situation and nutrition status of the most vulnerable will worsen in the coming months (UNICEF Whole of Syria Situation Analysis, March 2021).
To address these needs, Save the Children will target conflict-affected boys and girls and their families with monthly food baskets. SC will aim to meet the nutritional needs of households as much as possible through providing monthly food baskets which contain 1550 Kcal per person per day (nutritional contribution: 59% of energy to be contributed by carbohydrates, 12% from proteins and 29% from fat). According to the FSL Working Group, there was significant funding gap in NES in food assistance starting from April 2021. After the coordinated effort by the FSL Working Group with all the FSL actors in NES, Save the Children was requested to cover for Tel Samen camp for the duration of April to December 2021. In addition to this, Save the Children will provide monthly food baskets as emergency support to the vulnerable people identified in other locations as well. The most appropriate modality (in-kind support, cash or voucher assistance) will be selected based on the targeted locations, and in consultations with the beneficiaries and local stakeholders. The distributions in camps are made in-kind from the distribution point or tent-to-tent based on the procedure set by the Camp Administration. The food baskets will be distributed targeting the most vulnerable, food insecure households in areas with low service coverage, and the targeted locations will be identified in close coordination with the FSL WG. The targeting criteria will be adapted to the local context in order to identify the most vulnerable food insecure households in the area. In Tel Samen, the distribution is blanket distribution. For other emergency food support, the criteria are child-headed household, single women and people with disabilities. If there is only one person in the family, half basket is provided for a month, 2-6 family members one basket is provided for a month and more than 7 members 2 baskets are provided for a month.
It is estimated that the project will reach 20,000 individuals.

4 - Needs

In February 2021, the populations in NES were reported to face major challenges in meeting their daily needs. Increasing prices and lack of rainfalls have further compounded the already dire situation. High cost of food, water, rent, health services and operating generations for electricity were particularly problematic. Accessing sufficient, quality food has been challenging for many as the high cost of food items, particularly the high costs of appropriate formulas for infants, let to numerous coping strategies such as borrowing money to buy food, using cash to buy food which was intended to purchase something else, and resorting to eating lower quality food. Child labor, early marriage, and sending children to beg were highly prevalent in the assessed communities (REACH: HSOS NES, February 2021).In NWS, the depreciation of the SYP further decreased households' purchasing power and affected their ability to secure sufficient food quantities. In March 2021, the SYP value hit a historical low at 4,760 SYP/USD. Livelihoods and food assistance were the top priorities by the majority of key information, at 67% and 42% respectively. Only 3% of residents were able to access livelihood assistance, while food assistance reached 58% of the respondents. IDPs reported similarly high needs in livelihoods (62%) and food assistance (52%) support, while access to these services remained low among the displaced populations – at 2% for livelihoods and 64% for food assistance. 84% of key informants cited the high costs of food as a common challenge, coupled with 94% of household reporting insufficient income and 54% reporting general lack of employment opportunities (REACH: HSOS NWS, March 2021).

5 - Implementation of activities

A monitoring plan is developed during the inception phase of the project in coordination with the project team to ensure that the plan is both feasible and aligned with the implementation plan. Data will be collected on a monthly basis by the field teams on activity reporting and monitoring (e.g. training reports, activity reports, attendance lists). FDGs will be held regularly with children and caregivers taking part in project activities to ensure that children’s voices feed into programming. Participants will be a mix of women and men, girls and boys. In addition, qualitative data will be routinely collected through monitoring visits. Project quality will be monitored through quality benchmarks. A range of mechanisms will be applied to ensure accountability to the affected population. Information about the project will be made available to target communities through appropriate media. SC will also create opportunities for communities, including children, to participate in programming decisions and provide feedback throughout the project cycle.

7.1 - Please list any risks related to sexual exploitation and abuse that have been identified during the project design phase and any risk mitigation and prevention measures that have been adopted and built into the project

SC is invested in the Centrality of Protection and is developing an action plan that focuses on capacity building of staff and partners, embedding of conflict sensitivity and protection analysis, strengthening safe programming and child safeguarding approaches, improving risk management and integrating protection into advocacy work. In addition, SC integrates protection mainstreaming across its programs, and these principles guide the implementation team to ensure safe and quality programming. Building on the foundation of all staff members being trained on Child Safeguarding, protection against sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA) and Safe Programming, SC is also expanding capacity building of staff in conflict sensitivity, protection analysis and participatory approaches. A child safeguarding risk assessment, gender and GBV audit, and conflict sensitivity analysis are conducted for each program. These identify gaps, inform the project design, and mitigate protection risks for children and their families while accessing SC facilities and using SC services. In addition, SC has prioritized more sustained capacity-building for staff related to disability and disability inclusion, as well as on gender mainstreaming and GBV.

8 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Unstable security situation: conflict escalation, poor acceptance from local stakeholders and/or change in lines of control can prevent access to different program areas, either temporarily or permanently. Inappropriate project design can exacerbate tensions between communities and lead to unintended negative or harmful outcomes during implementation including suspension of activities. In order to manage and mitigate this risk, SC regularly monitors the local context, and SC employs security focal points. SC staff complete mandatory security trainings. Context-specific Safety and Security Management Plans are regularly reviewed and updated.Failure of money transfer mechanisms or change in banking regulation restricts movement of funds; and currency fluctuations: SC may be unable to deliver program activities due to lack of funds or program staff may be put at risk due to a lack of personal funds to hibernate or due to deterioration in local acceptance as a result of unpaid balances to other staff, suppliers etc. SC mitigates this risk by using multiple money transfer channels and agents who have strong reputations and have been able to deliver funds in almost all circumstances inside Syria. All SC agreements are denominated in donor currency.Risks due to the COVID-19 pandemic: The situation may result in risks to children and their caregivers due to lack of access to programming sites; further exposure to protection concerns, including violence; and health risks to beneficiaries and SC staff members. Relevant programme adaptations have been developed and are currently in place. SC has also introduced Standard Operation Procedures for staff working remotely and to identify COVID-19 focal points in each programme area.
