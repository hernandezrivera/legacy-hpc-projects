Project Summary

IOM will continue to offer holistic support predominantly to internally displaced persons (IDPs) including individuals, families and other vulnerable people in camps and return areas in Ninewa and Dohuk Governorates, Kirkuk and Diyala, particularly those returning to their original communities or integrating in the communities of displacement. IOM is planning a wide variety of protection and Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) activities to support vulnerable populations, at all layers of intervention under the Health and Protection Clusters. IOM will provide activities in line with the Protection Cluster objectives 1 -Provide specialized protection assistance, and community-based support, to conflict-affected individuals, thereby enabling them to secure their rights, access humanitarian assistance and public services, and overcome barriers to achievement of durable solutions; 2 -Support and advocate with authorities, humanitarian and recovery actors to address protection issues in accordance with protection norms and applicable legal standards, thereby contributing to a favorable protection environment; and 3 - Improve response timeliness through preparedness: to prevent further degradation of humanitarian needs.
Focused-non-specialized and community-based activities will help beneficiaries to feel safer and calmer, reactivate or strengthen their social networks, and regain hope in the future. This includes case management, emotional supportive sessions, social gatherings, skills training, educational activities and guided group discussions. Vulnerable populations will gain tools to cope with displacement and be more resilient, being active members of their recovery. Services will be offered though static community centers and mobile teams, including outreach visits in both modalities. Referrals will be done to specialized services. Services will be carried out in line with the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) and global cluster guidelines and in coordination with the Clusters. MHPSS activities will be implemented through Psychosocial Staff based at the centers, with the support of community based Focal Points and volunteers. The project will engage qualified IOM national staff, including psychologists, social workers and educators, some of them belonging to the minorities. They would be directly supervised by international psychologist, psychiatrist and peaceful co-existence experts. Activities related to peaceful co-existence, including awareness and community-based activities, will primarily focus on supporting IDPs in areas of displacement.
For sustainability and preparedness, capacity building for humanitarian and development partners and government staff will be provided to enhance their capacity to provide MHPSS services, and IASC guidelines. Capacity building is provided though classroom base training, coaching and supervision. To improve timeliness and response, there will be a focus on improving referral pathways, access to hotlines and a big emphasis on the prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA), through internal and external trainings, awareness raising and speaking directly to beneficiaries. IOM interventions are designed to support the coping mechanisms and resilience of affected communities.
Specify governorates and districts for GBV
IOM protection is currently conducting protection scoping missions to determine gaps in GBV programming. As part of this process, IOM will work closely with the GBV and protection clusters, and other I/NGOs and local stakeholders. Possibilities include IOM Mosul or Falluja Community Resource Centers (CRCs) (note these are different to the IOM Community Centres); Sinjar; and scaling up of the Dohuk response. Currently GBV programming is in Ninewa: Haj Ali IDP camp and Dohuk: city and Sharya IDP camp. GBV awareness raising activities take place throughout Iraq, mainly in Erbil, Dohuk, Sulaymania and Baghdad, in and out of camps.

Needs

The project responds to needs arising from the multiple waves of conflict experienced in Iraq, resulting in millions of people experiencing distressful events, disruption of social networks, loss of property and harsh living conditions. As of November 2018, Iraq is still experiencing the consequences of what is arguably theworst violent conflict and displacement crisis in the country’s troubled history, with 1,890,696 IDPs across the country, with approx. 21% female headed households (MCNA in camp IDPs, September 2018). 4,075,350 displaced people have returned to areas of origin, many of them still facing fear of violence, discrimination, lack of services and destruction of property, with approx. 13% female headed households (MCNA non-displaced, September 2018). Members of various communities, mostly minorities, have experienced displacement and extreme events. Women, men, boys and girls have experienced different protection and MHPSS related risks, including forced recruitment for boys, and sexual exploitation and violence for women and girls. These experiences have affected them psychologically, especially people who survived sexual exploitation, violence, abuses and slavery. The protracted conflict has also resulted in many individuals remaining physically and mentally traumatized or disabled. This includes women and girls subjected to GBV violations – including domestic violence, sexual violence, exploitation and abuse – which have been reported in both camps and informal settlements (HNO, 2019). Stigma and fear of reporting and understanding of what constitutes GBV can also contributed to feelings of shame, anxiety and withdrawing from families or communities.In and outside of camps in Dohuk, there are reports of early marriage for girls, as well as domestic violence cases in some camps in Dohuk, and increased rates of suicide. Critically, there are currently limited services for survivors. There is a need for actors in camps to raise awareness on these issues and continue to monitor, collect information and report on the situation. In Sinjar, the biggest gaps appear to be in protection, Gender Based Violence (GBV) and psychosocial and psychological service provision and access to livelihood opportunities, with considerable protection needs among the IDP population and very few protection actors present. A 2016 study among 668 women and girls who escaped ISIS reported that only one was receiving ongoing care while all of them reported struggling with various forms of breakdowns and trauma (insomnia, anxiety, flashbacks) (HRW 2016). In 2018, a gap was identified in a settlement on Mt. Sinjar whereby 2000 adult women did not have access to sanitary hygiene products. IOM distributed 2000 dignity kits to the area. In 2019, in coordination with the Clusters, IOM will seek to identify gaps and locations that can benefit from an emergency distribution of dignity kits. While most of children have access to education, there appears to be a significant gap in child protection, psychosocial and recreational activities for children. In camps, there are 4% households with children showing signs of psychosocial distress, such as behavior change since the conflict began. Outside of camps, this figure is at 9%. There are 3% of households with at least one married child (aged 12 – 17) in camps, and 1% outside of camps.As per general statistics on mental health after emergencies, approximately 3-4% of the population present severe disorders and need advanced services, 15%-20% suffer mild or moderate mental health disorders and a large part of the population experience normal distress or other psychological reactions. Gaps in the provision of MHPSS services have a strong impact in the ability of IDPs and host community to function and be active members in the society. IOM and the Department of Health (DoH) has identified a strong need for MHPSS activities to provide IDPs and returnees with an enabling and safe environment in which they can identify the main factors of personal uneasiness and stress, to express and elaborate on grievances and painful experiences that become triggers of tension, and to engage in meaningful communication and interaction with peers and members of their communities through selected activities in a safe social space.Sources: IOM Protection Observations and Field Reports 2018; IOM DTM 2017-2018; BCF Protection Reports to IOM from Mount Sinjar 2018; 2019 Humanitarian Needs Overview; 2019 Oxfam and UN Women Iraq Gender Equality Profile; Human Rights Watch. Mitigation Measures

Mitigation measures1. Gender, protection, conflict analysis as part of assessment processes, and include stakeholder and power mapping; adhere to WHO ethical and safety principles for research sexual violence. Project staff trained on gender and inclusion of vulnerable groups awareness and specific actions required to ensure their safe participation and ongoing assessment of power dynamics. Transparent and inclusive dialogue with communities, including community leaders and local government authorities to address issues of concern around inclusion. Adapt activities based on ongoing monitoring/ lessons learned from community engagement. Ongoing participatory GBV safety assessments to be conducted with community members and project staff to design locally relevant risk-reduction strategies (with girls and women and boys and men). Strategies to ensure boys and men are involved and supportive of women and girl’s involvement. Promote ongoing trust-building and dialogue particularly through MHPSS activities regarding the importance of women and girl’s participation and ensuring their safety.2. Design assessments and activities specifically to capture the different needs and barriers to assistance for women, girls, boys and men of all ages, abilities and ethnicities. Consult with women and girls in gender safe spaces. Utilize global level best practices and guidelines such as Universal design and Minimum Standards for Age and Disability Inclusion in Humanitarian Action. Provide training for staff and partners on gender and inclusion in assessments and project processes. Provide feedback to communities on complaints received and ensure discussion on how to address different needs. IOM will use findings and analysis from IOM’s Thematic Report to inform gender mainstreaming initiatives across IOM HRP projects, such as those in shelter responses in coordination with the relevant sectoral clusters (Shelter/WASH/etc). Protection mainstreaming will also be supported through establishing a mechanism whereby selected protection focal points who can access hard to reach areas will be capacitated to identify, monitor, report and respond to protection issues identified throughout the response thereby minimizing harm and enhancing protection of the most vulnerable populations.3. Procedures are in place for GBV data protection and confidentiality; train specialist staff on GBV guiding principles; conduct ongoing technical monitoring. Training encompasses non-specialist project staff, partners and community members on gender, inclusion and stigma reduction, GBV disclosure and limitations. Disciplinary action against staff/partners not following GBV guiding principles. Implementation of IOM PSEA response. Coordination with camp management and the Protection Cluster to report occurrences and take appropriated measures. 4. IOM has over 1300 staff operating directly in all governorates within Iraq, providing significant access to at-risk populations, including in areas of high insecurity. IOM has established relationships in GoI and KRG with key government focal points to ensure necessary bureaucratic processes for support. IOM can rely on existing access and coordination arrangements in all governorates to support access to beneficiaries. IOM operates within the UN Safety and Security (UNDSS) procedures and is therefore supported to access a range of insecure areas. The project is designed to be flexible and adaptable to the changeable context within Iraq and will be able to utilise the extensive access network of IOM staff across the country to support access to at-risk populations. Personnel is trained on escalation of violent situations and follows security regulations. A security guard will be engaged in community centers at risk of possible incidents.5. Training material will be developed to align with the context and experience, ensuring that it is interactive and relevant to the experience of participants.

How is your project accountable to affected population

IOM will promote accountability mechanisms for PSEA, including reporting tools and complaint mechanisms for beneficiaries. IOM has a mandatory Instruction: Policy and Procedures for Preventing and Responding to Sexual Exploitation and Abuse, which binds staff members to standards of behavior when working with beneficiaries, including during emergency response. Sexual exploitation and abuse of affected populations constitutes gross misconduct and is grounds for disciplinary action, including summary dismissal and referral for criminal prosecution. In addition to IOM's PSEA Instruction and the Standards of Conduct, IOM has also made PSEA commitments that include inter-agency coordination to prevent and address SEA incidents that may occur. IOM Iraq has a PSEA focal point who is responsible to advise staff and, if SEA instance is brought to her attention, to channel the information to IOM HQ through relevant channels. IOM participates in the IDP Call Center, who receive information from beneficiaries, and submits to the Protection Cluster who will request investigation and feedback from IOM. IOM teams continuously assess the needs of beneficiaries including IDPs in camps,through regular activities and discussing programming with beneficiaries. Assessmentshave been conducted in Mosul and Tel Afar and are undergoing in Hamdaniya, Dohuk,and Sinjar, which will be used to inform programming. IOM staff guarantees an adequateinformation sharing system through constant presence in the field and daily direct contactwith beneficiaries, to collect and address their needs or complaints and adapt to theevolving surrounding situation. Though daily community-based activities focus groupdiscussions (FGD) and other services, beneficiaries are encouraged to share theirfeedback. A M&E plan is in place, including surveys to measure impact and satisfaction,most significant change interviews for selected beneficiaries, monthly narrative reports,evaluation forms for events and trainings and pre and post-tests for trainings to beupdated depending on the training content. FGDs gather feedback from beneficiaries andthe psychometric tools PHQ-9, SDQ and SQR-20 measure progress in case managementand are included in case management files. FGDs determine the current protectionsituation and perceptions of safety. Communicating with Communities (CwC) activities have been mainstreamed throughout the project. IOM performs consistent assessments through communication with the clusters, I/NGOs and local stakeholders. IOM will utilize global and Iraq specific tools and resources to ensure appropriate and effective protectionmainstreaming of its emergency activities. Protection mainstreamingwill include developing tools to support safe responses to protection risks and concernswithin IOM programming, and include protection indicators into assessments, M&E to enhance protection mainstreaming throughout the response. This includes internal response mechanisms for protection concerns, Rapid Shelter Assessment forms, which incorporate specific gender and protection indicators andpromote the active participation of girls, boys, women and men in providing feedback ontheir specific protection needs. Tools will be responsive to the needs highlighted during the crisis and as IOM is participating in the Protection Cluster’s preparedness planning, tools will be developed in coordination with Cluster partners and priorities. Protection focal points can report and respond to protection issues to minimize harm and enhance protection of the most vulnerable populations. IOM collects age and gender disaggregated data for all activities, striving for gender balance. It includes demographical information to ensure equal participation of different communities and including of vulnerable populations. IOM strive to hire gender balanced teams when possible.
