Project Summary

The Food Security Sector (FSS) plays an essential role in enhancing the nutrition and health of Rohingya refugees and host communities in Teknaf and Ukhiya Upazilas, Cox’s Bazar. In the face of the global climate crisis and increasingly variable local climate conditions, an effective food security programme is key to reducing climate vulnerabilities, minimising protection risks, and fostering peace and stability in the region.
In Cox's Bazar, BRAC’s Food Security Sector (FSS) takes a comprehensive humanitarian approach that integrates developmental strategies, addressing the crisis's protracted nature. In alignment with sectoral objective 2, BRAC aims to strengthen food security resilience for 20,075 Rohingya refugees across 22 camps through climate-adaptive food production. The initiative will engage beneficiaries in activities such as homestead gardening, pond fish aquaculture (community approach), seedling and sapling cultivation, kitchen waste composting, and mushroom production to enhance food security and nutrition, especially for vulnerable women and persons with disabilities. These community-driven interventions will also help strengthen social bonds and foster positive relationships within the Rohingya community.
Supporting sectoral objective 3, BRAC will work to enhance food security for 7,210 individuals in six unions of Ukhiya and Teknaf Sub-districts within nearby host communities. Planned initiatives include climate-smart agricultural practices, agro-processing, poultry and livestock rearing, market linkage development, and the creation of resilient income opportunities, alongside disaster preparedness and early response efforts.
Building on its experience since 2017, BRAC will emphasise sectoral integration and localisation by strengthening the capacities of local NGOs, CBOs, youth clubs, and government stakeholders through targeted training and support in implementing core activities. Additionally, BRAC will prioritise land stabilisation, environmental restoration, and local climate awareness to safeguard natural resources and ecosystems. The Food Security programme will implement a contingency plan to mitigate environmental risks, restore agricultural assets, and empower affected households and individuals to rebuild their food security and livelihoods.
BRAC is committed to ensuring that 95% of project participants are adult women from both Rohingya and host communities, with an emphasis on supporting widows, female-headed households, transgender individuals, members of LGBTQ+ communities, people with chronic illnesses, survivors of GBV/SGBV, and persons with disabilities of all ages. This inclusive approach aims to provide equitable support and build resilience among the most vulnerable community members.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

Food security for both Rohingya refugees and host communities in Cox's Bazar is an escalating concern, particularly in the current global context. With limited opportunities for self-reliance or income generation, refugees are heavily dependent on humanitarian assistance, while nearly all households have unmet multi-sectoral needs, especially in food security and livelihoods (J-MSNA, 2021). Food access remains a priority, as reported by 75% of Rohingya and 33% of host community households (J-MSNA, 2023).The FAO’s 2023 Resilience Index Measurement Analysis (RIMA) indicated that only 48% of Rohingya households have an acceptable Food Consumption Score (FCS), while 50% have borderline consumption, and 3% have poor consumption. Among host community households, 68% show acceptable consumption patterns, while 31% are borderline, and 1% have poor consumption (RIMA-III, 2024). Since the Rohingya influx began in 2017, the host community has encountered increasing economic pressures, with heightened competition for resources, particularly in the food labour market. Many local farmers, foragers, and fishing communities—who were already living below the poverty line—have lost their livelihoods, making it challenging to meet basic needs.The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) analysis highlights chronic food insecurity in Cox’s Bazar, where 70% of the population experiences some level of food insecurity, with 25% moderately and 10% severely food insecure. Among the Forcibly Displaced Myanmar Nationals (FDMN), an estimated 20% are in IPC Phase 4, while 60% are in IPC Phase 3 or above. In the host community, 5% of the population is in IPC Phase 4, and 40% are in IPC Phase 3 or above. High inflation, income reductions, and repeated climate shocks are exacerbating acute food insecurity for the poorest households, necessitating urgent action to protect livelihoods and bridge food consumption gaps (IPC, April 2024).Local food security is further strained by infectious diseases, such as dengue and conjunctivitis, and rising commodity prices driven by global crises, which have pushed many households deeper into poverty. Alternative income opportunities are essential to reduce vulnerability in the host community. Current food allocations for refugees are based on family size rather than the specific needs of individuals, particularly children, pregnant and lactating women, and persons with disabilities. Additionally, both communities have entrenched patriarchal structures that limit women’s participation in income generation, leadership, and decision-making. As a result, women, children, and other vulnerable groups often face higher rates of nutritional deficiency and related health issues compared to men. In both communities, cultural norms place men at the top of the consumption hierarchy, often leading to inadequate food intake, low dietary diversity, and malnutrition among women, children, and especially pregnant and lactating mothers.BRAC recognises the urgent need to address these gender disparities in food security and is committed to applying a practical, gender-sensitive approach across all project interventions. BRAC will ensure that agricultural input distribution support is tailored to meet the diverse needs of different genders and age groups. This includes prioritising additional support for pregnant and lactating women, children, female-headed households, and persons with disabilities. BRAC will also focus on empowering women by facilitating their involvement in community decision-making and income-generating activities, recognising that women's active participation can significantly enhance family welfare.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

As a member of the PSEA network, BRAC upholds robust policies that prioritise protection and safeguarding, with a strong emphasis on the 'Do No Harm' principle to prevent any unintended negative impacts of its programmes. BRAC ensures meaningful access, participation, and empowerment for all beneficiaries, embedding accountability at every level of programme design, planning, and implementation. Additionally, BRAC enforces a comprehensive safeguarding policy, including specific measures for child safeguarding. In all its programmes and enterprises, BRAC places participants’ and employees’ safety as a primary consideration from the outset of design. Preventing sexual harassment, child and adolescent abuse, intimidation, violence, bullying, humiliation, discrimination, neglect, and exploitation are integrated into BRAC’s practices as thoroughly as possible. Where programme design and implementation alone cannot fully mitigate risks, BRAC has established additional safeguarding mechanisms. These include the careful selection and training of personnel, ongoing supervision and monitoring, the establishment of multiple reporting channels, and effective procedures for investigation and accountability. This project will be no exception; it will fully align with BRAC’s commitment to protection mainstreaming. All activities will incorporate BRAC’s protection and safeguarding standards, ensuring that interventions are not only effective but also safe, respectful, and empowering for all participants, particularly the most vulnerable. Through these aligned practices, BRAC aims to create a supportive environment that upholds dignity and security at every stage of the project.

2.3 - Age & Disability

The project team will implement periodic, community-based, inclusive methods, such as Participatory Rural Appraisal (PRA) and gender analysis, using sex, age, and disability-disaggregated data to assess and address food security needs. This approach will identify the capacities, barriers, and specific food and nutrition requirements of diverse groups, with a focus on vulnerable populations, including women, children, the elderly, persons with disabilities, and female-headed households. To tackle the unique challenges faced by vulnerable groups—especially women, girls, the elderly, LGBTQ individuals, and persons with disabilities—the project team will actively engage male community members, sensitising them through male leaders and volunteers to foster supportive environments. BRAC will also connect vulnerable individuals to essential protection measures through its programmes, ensuring equal representation and prioritising those most at risk in the community. This comprehensive approach aims to build inclusive, resilient support networks that protect and empower vulnerable members of both Rohingya and host communities.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

The Food Security project team will empower beneficiaries to meaningfully participate in decision-making through inclusive, non-discriminatory processes that honour the diversity within the community. To strengthen transparency and mutual accountability, the project will establish individual feedback channels as well as innovative joint feedback systems, creating a positive impact by empowering beneficiaries to voice their insights and concerns. Project information will be communicated clearly and transparently to all beneficiaries. Committed to inclusivity, the project will ensure that persons with disabilities and individuals with specific needs are actively engaged in programme activities. This includes their involvement in the planning and design phases of activities and ongoing feedback opportunities to maintain transparency, relevance, and responsiveness throughout the project. Through these practices, the project aims to create an environment where all participants, particularly the most vulnerable, have a meaningful role in shaping programme outcomes.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

In BRAC’s Food Security Sector (FSS) project, Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) is a core principle integrated into all stages of emergency preparedness and response. The project aims to enhance food security and resilience for both Rohingya refugees and host communities in Cox’s Bazar by engaging vulnerable groups, particularly women and persons with disabilities, in climate-adaptive food production activities. BRAC ensures that these communities are well informed about their rights and responsibilities, available services, and accessible channels for reporting any concerns related to sexual exploitation and abuse.The project establishes effective and secure feedback mechanisms for reporting any incidents, and ensuring confidentiality and safety. By prioritizing the protection of the most vulnerable, such as female-headed households, survivors of gender-based violence, and LGBTQ+ individuals, BRAC ensures equitable support. This comprehensive approach, linking food security, climate resilience, and PSEA, not only strengthens community bonds but also safeguards individuals from exploitation and abuse, reinforcing BRAC’s commitment to creating a secure and inclusive environment for all. BRAC shows zero tolerance against sexual exploitation and abuse. BRAC, within its policy emphasized on PSEA being a mainstreaming agenda.Building on its 2024 successes, BRAC will continue to prioritize safeguarding and PSEA in 2025. This includes training sessions for all staff, volunteers, vendors, suppliers, and service providers (including drivers and guards), awareness sessions for programme participants, and standing agenda of PSEA and Safeguarding in staff meetings. A robust response mechanism will remain in place to support survivors maintaining a survivor-centred approach, and CFRM will be maintained. Additionally, SEA risk assessments and dissemination of awareness materials will be ongoing.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

The proposed project aims to tackle land stabilization and environmental restoration in host and Rohingya communities. It will introduce climate-smart agriculture to enhance soil carbon absorption, reduce greenhouse gas emissions, mitigate the adverse impacts of climate change, and enhance agricultural production. Within these communities, BRAC has an established Emergency Operation Centre (EOC) and Emergency Response Team (ERT) structure, featuring representatives from all sectors to promptly respond during emergencies.Moreover, BRAC's Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) programme delivers crucial training in Disaster Risk Management and Emergency Response to project staff, including community volunteers, in order to enable them to respond effectively to emergency crises. As part of their skill development, first aid training is also provided to all staff and volunteers.In addition, BRAC has implemented an Environmental and Social Safeguard Framework (ESSF) that takes a scientific precautionary approach to project design and implementation. BRAC's interventions in the Rohingya context are proactive in anticipating and addressing potential harm before it materializes. In situations of threat of serious or irreversible damage, the absence of scientific certainty will not be used to avoid cost-effective measures to prevent environmental degradation in the project implementation process.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

BRAC designed the project by considering the emergency preparedness and response plan having a contingency fund. Every year, natural calamities like torrential rainfall, cyclones, and flash floods damage homestead gardens. To re-establish the garden after such damages, the project provides different types of input support, such as vegetable seeds, manure, sack bags, fencing nets, and seedlings. Moreover, during different training and awareness sessions, the beneficiaries are sensitised on emergency response operation procedures.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

The project is designed to actively engage youth (ages 15-24) by establishing 25 youth clubs, each with 5 members, comprising promising and skilled vegetable gardeners from various camps. These clubs will serve as hubs for innovation, with members acting as model farmers within their communities. Other beneficiaries will participate in cross-visits to the club members’ gardens, allowing them to observe and learn about best practices in vegetable gardening. The youth clubs will participate in various community-empowering activities, including:•Vegetable Seed Production: Cultivating high-quality seeds to enhance local vegetable yields.•Vegetable Aggregation: Collecting produce for efficient distribution and sale.•Transitioning Homestead Gardeners to Commercial Farmers: Supporting gardeners in scaling up to commercial levels.•Strengthening Market Linkages: Connecting with local markets to ensure fair pricing and sustainable sales channels.•Community Promotion: Raising awareness of sustainable gardening practices within their communities.•Post-Harvest Management: Teaching efficient handling and storage techniques to reduce spoilage and maintain quality.Through these activities, youth clubs will not only enhance food security but also foster a resilient, self-sufficient agricultural network within the camps.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

The project aims to enhance self-reliance and resilience among Rohingya beneficiaries through capacity-building training and inputs for year-round homestead vegetable gardening. Participants will acquire best agricultural practices and improved production techniques, thereby boosting food security and dietary diversity. Surplus vegetables can be sold in local markets, fostering economic independence. Additionally, beneficiaries will engage in seedling and seed production, as well as composting, to further enhance their agricultural skills. Marginalized gardeners will be encouraged to explore commercial farming initiatives, promoting sustainable livelihoods. Ultimately, the project aspires to cultivate a resilient community capable of meeting its food and economic needs.
