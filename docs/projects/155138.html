Project Summary

SC's EiE Response will provide safe, protective, inclusive and age appropriate learning for 14,729 children aged 3-18 years (58% girls and 42% boys) and ensure refugee and host community children have optimal access to education. In 2019, SC will further consolidate its existing EiE presence in 15 camps across Ukhia and Teknaf. This programming is strategically integrated within SC’s larger humanitarian response in order to provide children with access to essential services. Teachers will be trained to identify and refer children to SC's wider child protection, psychosocial support, health, and nutrition response activities. SC will be mainstreaming wider inclusion practices across all sectors on issues related to Gender Based Violence and Disability. Activities will lead to improved gender equitable, gender sensitive, GBV preventative, GBV responsive, and disability sensitive programming. This included conducting an intra-agency assessment looking at inclusion needs in SC’s shelter, health and education programmes. To ensure gender equity, SC’s response will include targeting adolescent girls as they face a distinct set of barriers in accessing education. SC is also committed to ensuring that at least 50% of teachers are female and that 50% of teachers are from the Rohingya population. To mitigate the risk of reinforcing existing inequalities between women, men, girls and boys, or the risk of creating new gender inequalities, the initiative will seek to engage female and male stakeholders in a gender-responsive and culturally sensitive manner and ensure alignment with SC’s Child Safeguarding Policy and Do No Harm principle.

Needs

Since 25 August 2017, over 700,000 Rohingya refugees have been forced across Myanmar border into Bangladesh to escape widespread violence in Rakhine State. This new influx of refugees joins an existing population of Rohingya that crossed into Bangladesh over the last few decades, escaping violence and persecution. Cox’s Bazar now hosts over 900,000 refugees, and 58% are under the age of 18. It is estimated that approximately half of refugee children have not participated in any form of learning prior to and since arriving in Bangladesh. ACCESS TO EDUCATION: In the last year, education enrolment and attendance remains low, especially at pre-school level (aged 3-5)- at 45% of boys and girls. Only 4% of adolescent girls (aged 15-18) girls attending the Learning Centres (LCs) compared to 14% of adolescent boys, (JENA, 2017). Top barriers to accessing education, identified by parents, include: distance to facilities and safety for children aged 3-5; responsibilities in the household for children aged 6-14; lack of adequate learning materials, and inadequate WASH facilities. Enrolment data collected by the Education Sector shows older children less likely to access education, with upped primary age groups (11-14 years) showing the lowest enrolment rates across all learning centres, and within this, a decrease in gender parity among children enrolled in this shift. Furthermore, while the response as focussed on children aged 3-14 years, 92% of adolescents and youth remain without access to learning opportunities. The physical space for education services is currently constricting due to the limited availably of land and existing LCs are at risk of landslides and flooding caused by the annual monsoon and cyclone season. Due to restriction allowing on the use of temporary materials for construction, such as bamboo, most LCs are already in need of rehabilitation and this is predicted to incur significantly during the recurrent monsoon and cyclone season (April-October). Land scarcity is a key issue severely hampering the response, and requires innovation approaches to learning provision and meet the vast education needs. QUALITY EDUCATION: In Rohingya refugee response, recruitment of qualified, experienced and well-trained teachers is a constant barrier to providing quality education to all children. In addition to inadequate knowledge and training, teachers have also been traumatized by this crisis and need support to recover and remain motivated to teach. The recruitment and retention of Bangladeshi teachers has been hindered by a lack of guiding curricular and harsh physical landscape (JENA, 2017). While there has been progress on standardizing curricular for students, learning outcomes are unlikely to improve without provision of quality continuous Teacher Professional Development (TPD). ADDITIONAL VULNERABILIITIES: Given the complex reality on the ground, despite joint humanitarian efforts, about three quarters of Rohingya children still do not have access to education. Without access to education, vulnerable children are at significantly higher risk of negative coping mechanisms and life-threatening conditions. Currently, the congested and chaotic living conditions in camps and host communities pose an increased risk of separation, exploitation, abuse and injuries, especially as children are sometimes unattended whilst caregivers are busy finding suitable places for living, collecting relief, and participating in daily labour. Children living in insecure, overcrowded spaces with one or no caregivers face risks related to SGBV and trafficking into prostitution, particularly for adolescent girls. Adolescent girls are particularly susceptible to early or forced marriage and sexual abuse, while boys face heightened risks of child labour, exploitation and human trafficking.
