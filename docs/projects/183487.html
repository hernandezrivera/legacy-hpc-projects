1 - Needs

A protracted protection crisis continues in Palestine, which remains largely attributable to Israel’s ongoing occupation, now in its 54th year, violations of international humanitarian and human rights law (IHL/IHRL) and continuing Palestinian divide. According to the UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA), between 1 January 2021 and 19 September 2021, Israel seized or demolished 675 structures (including 184-donor-funded structures), resulting in the forced displacement of 164 households that consist of 963 people (including 542 children) and otherwise affecting 1,197 households that consist of 7,556 people (including 3,686 children). 85% of these seizures / demolitions took place in Area C, while the remaining 15% were executed in East Jerusalem. Moreover, 89% of all demolitions in East Jerusalem were executed in the “Holy Basin”. Additionally, many livelihood, education and health structures were demolished. In fact, in July 2021 alone, a mobile clinic was demolished in Um Qusa in Hebron, a school was demolished in Shuafat in East Jerusalem, and an agricultural reservoir was demolished in Furush Beit Dajan in the Jordan Valley. If demolitions continue at this rate, we will witness the highest annual figures since 2016. More alarming are the new trends in the West Bank vis-à-vis demolitions: an increase in use of seizures as opposed to demolitions, new building permit regulations limiting access to legal remedies, and mass demolitions of entire communities in Area C (Humsa Al Bqai, Al Qaboon); and an increase in self-demolitions in East Jerusalem and key public interest cases mitigating mass evictions/demolitions coming to an end (Sheikh Jarrah, Silwan, Al Walaja). In addition, resuming land settlement/registration process in East Jerusalem and Area C and allocation of millions of dollars by the Israeli Government to monitor Palestinian structures in Area C will be of particular concern in the years to come. Settler violence and land takeover has been exacerbated by the fact that there has been no international protective presence in the West Bank since March 2020 due to the pandemic. Between 1 January 2020 and 19 September 2021, there were 413 Palestinian injuries and 4 casualties as a result of incidents of Israeli settler violence. The majority of injuries resulted from teargas inhalation, followed by rubber bullets, physical assaults and live ammunition, while all four fatalities resulted from live ammunition. The most affected area by settler violence was East Jerusalem, followed by the Hebron Governorate (Hebron H2 and South Hebron Hills), Ramallah Governorate (Northeast Ramallah), Nablus Governorate (Southeast Nablus) and Tubas Governorate (Northern Jordan Valley). These areas will likely remain the focus of settler violence and settler takeover of land for the remainder of 2021, as well as in 2022. The new trends in the land grab is best demonstrated in the case of the Evyatar Outpost on Jabal Sbeih in southeast Nablus, where a settlement consisting of paved roads, electric and water infrastructure, and tens of prefabricated structures was erected in one day. This is a stark contrast to the makeshift outposts established by the hilltop youth or herding outposts that we have seen in recent years, and could pave the way as a model for future settler takeover. Moreover, to appease settlers in the absence of de-jure annexation, the Government of Israel has been expediting settlement expansion plans, such as the E1 Spatial Plans, and settlement infrastructure projects that will further fragment the West Bank, segregate the transportation system, and pave the way for annexation, all while putting more families at risk of dispossession, displacement, loss of shelter, loss of livelihood, poverty and food insecurity. These infrastructure projects include, but are not limited to: the Huwarra Bypass Road in the northern West Bank, the Fabric of Life Road in the Jerusalem Periphery, the American Road in East Jerusalem, the expansion of the Tunnel Checkpoint west of Bethlehem, widening Route 60 in the southern West Bank, and the widening of Route 90 throughout the entire length of the Jordan Valley. As regards residency rights, the law on family unification has been renewed annually since 2003. It expired on 6 July 2021 and was not renewed for political reasons. This created a possibility for ICLA to start filing for residency status on behalf of tens of thousands of people. As the applications are piling-up in the Ministry of Interior (MoI), Israel’s Minister of Interior has given an unlawful directive to the staff not to process any such applications, and stated that if the law is not renewed, she would advocate for the application of the basic law of immigration to such cases. In either case, there is a need for legal action on the public interest level. Moreover, we expect that within the framework of a political agreement within the ruling coalition in Israel, that the MoI may use its discretionary power to make positive practical concessions for West Bank ID holders with pending family unification applications, such as granting them the right to acquire driving licenses, and this would naturally require individual legal follow-up. Finally, West Bank ID Holders have been unable to obtain family unification for their expat spouses for more than a decade now. This creates a situation of uncertainty and instability within these households, which is conducive of emigration. In 2020, NGOs secured several blanket extensions for expats who were residing with their spouses / families in the West Bank on B2 Tourist Visas. The last blanket extension expired on 31 December 2020. Therefore, these expats were given an ultimatum to either leave the country or remain illegally. Those who left are now facing difficulties in coming back to the country, as they are not given a special permission to return if coming from red or orange designated countries.

2 - Activities

In 2022, ICLA will target 17,583 Palestinians identiﬁed as affected by conflict-related violations and protection risks such as conflict-related violence, risk of forcible transfer, restrictions on freedom of movement and access to services, including livelihoods and settler violence. ICLA will prioritize communities living in Area C, Hebron (H2), Nablus, Bethlehem and Ramallah governorates, Jerusalem periphery and East Jerusalem. Tailored services will aim to reach people in financial hardship, especially women and children, IDPs and UNRWA refugees, Bedouin / herding communities and people with disabilities, as well as Palestinian workers and small-business owners at heightened risk of negative impact of discriminatory policies and practices. ICLA will continue to provide: Awareness-raising: ICLA will proactively identify the beneficiary needs and address obstacles in accessing justice or essential services through community outreach, service-mapping and information dissemination. In order to ensure community engagement and awareness on protective mechanisms and legal aid ICLA will increase internal knowledge and skills base required to support beneficiaries with comprehensive solutions to their issues. ICLA will also seek to engage volunteers (University law students/graduates and diverse representatives from beneficiary communities) in the design, implementation and monitoring of ICLA programme activities (e.g. assessments, reports, referrals) in cooperation with partner organisations and institutions. In addition, Mobile Legal Aid Clinic (MLAC) for Wall-Affected Communities will be further pursued through partners and MLAC modality for other hard-to-reach areas (e.g. Seam Zone, remote villages/communities) will be explored. Legal Counselling and Assistance: ICLA will continue to provide legal counselling (including due diligence, risk-assessment and mitigation, individual and community legal advice, supported referrals), as well as legal assistance, including paralegal services, accompaniment, follow up or court representation in order to ensure the best possible individual legal protection outcomes (e.g. ensuring temporary/permanent protection from evictions/demolitions, obtaining residency and family unification, filing complaints re: settler violence, access to socio-economic rights, particularly of women, people with disability and other vulnerable groups, etc.). Policy Change: ICLA will continue to use a range of complementary approaches and strategies to expose, challenge or change unjust laws and policy, including legal research and analysis of domestic laws and international humanitarian and human rights laws, case-based advocacy, data collection, collation, analysis and dissemination, and public interest cases (PIC) in order to secure individual/HH/community protection and/or temporary solution. In addition, advocacy activities will include: • Policy-Level Advocacy: seeks to engage and inform the international community in an effort to have them influence the GoI for legal reform. These advocacy efforts include the provision of situation reports, policy briefs, factsheets, bi-monthly newsletters, diplomatic briefings, field trips and press releases. • PICs: The project will lobby the UN, EU and Third States to pressure the GoI to refrain from enacting and upholding discriminatory laws and obtain favourable resolutions to PICs. In supporting PICs under this Component, the project will strengthen the protection of IHL and IHRL by generating public debate on, and creating public awareness about, systemic and widespread violations of rights and freedoms. • UN Mechanisms: The project will engage UN and human rights mechanisms, including the Security Council, General Assembly, Secretariat, UNHRC and human rights treaty bodies. • Targeted Research and Analysis: The project will generate evidence-based content with a cogent rights-based legal analysis to raise awareness amongst duty bearers and third parties about serious IHL and IHRL violations, including research studies, legal analysis briefs, timely updates on key legal developments and beneficiary stories. This research will be linked to project advocacy initiatives and shared with partners through the Legal Task Force (LTF). • Strategic Engagement and Coordination: Enhanced coordination is central to advocacy. It will involve efforts to bring civil society, human rights defenders and lawyers together to discuss legal strategies, for example, by facilitating brainstorming sessions on key cases and new laws. The NRC co-chaired LTF will be an excellent forum to create linkages between the project and other stakeholders lobbying for legal reform. Stakeholders Capacity Building and Empowerment: ICLA will continue to provide support to national capacities through training, mentoring, secondment, facilitating knowledge network, and provision of material and technical assistance to improve quality of legal interventions. ICLA will also continue to lead Legal Task Force (LTF) and coordinate activities of legal aid actors by engaging them through regular meetings, workshops, exchange of legal briefs/alerts, research and policy documents, etc. In addition, ICLA will intensify joint response with the West Bank Protection Consortium (WBPC), ensure representation in other coordination forums (East Jerusalem and Area C Task Force, Education Cluster, Protection Cluster, Child Protection Working Group) and proactively seek to engage with other stakeholders in order to improve non-legal forms of protection and assist in addressing the socio-economic needs in targeted areas. In order to ensure an integrated holistic approach that supports and amplifies legal aid, ICLA is working within elaborated referral system jointly with other NRC core competences, West Bank Protection Consortium partners, and OCHA, as well as with local authorities and village councils.

In 2022 the ambition is to drive change by considering specific opportunities for gender equality. NRC has already partnered up with local organisations working on empowering women to access their socio-economic rights. Through focus group discussions, workshops, learning events with female lawyers, universities and institutions working on family protection and other cross-cutting areas of law, the programme will embed gender considerations and contribute to changing the legal landscape. Other initiatives include commissioning an IHRL/IHL legal expert opinion on the legality and differential impact of Israeli HLP/residency violations on women and/or a study on women’s awareness of their HLP rights and how involved and empowered they are in joint decision making on issues that affect them (demolitions, evictions, loss of livelihoods opportunities). In 2022, NRC ICLA will work with the Protection and Education Clusters to secure that collective awareness-raising activities are integrated into the PSEA plan to ensure appropriate and consistent PSEA messaging to communities as in community engagement initiatives such as posters and flyers. In addition, ICLA will work jointly with NRC Education programme on incorporating awareness-raising, sensitization and capacity building for parents, teaching and non-teaching staff at school level.

9. - Feedback and complaints mechanisms

Yes

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

NRC has developed a standard complaint and feedback mechanism across the organization with available complaint box, email, field visit and phone line. The CFM is used to improve the organizational accountability and increase trust of the external stakeholders (beneficiaries, authorities, partners etc). The CFM offers beneficiaries the opportunity to approach NRC to ask questions and receive response, increase their understanding of the programs, reduce potential tensions and develop beneficiaries trust in the organization. Furthermore, the CFM is used to identify coverage gaps and program quality issues to improve programming by helping to identify issues and risks earlier and address them in a timely manner before they come larger or difficult and expensive to manage or resolve. One of the articles that NRC’s partners should agree upon before projects start implementation is the “Code of Conduct for non-staff representing NRC or accompanying NRC on missions”, as sexual relationships between NRC personnel and beneficiaries of assistance undermine the credibility and integrity of the work of NRC and are prohibited. Yearly due diligence of every NRC partner assesses PSEA policies and capacities. All ICLA staff and ICLA partners’ staff are required to take an online course/attend training on PSEA. In achieving accountability and transparency, NRC’s complaints and feedback policy is complementary to NRC’s Protection Policy, Code of Conduct, Child Safe Guarding Policy, Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse documents, Anti-Corruption Policy and the Security and Risk Management Policy.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

In order to prioritize safety and dignity of Palestinians at risk or affected by forcible transfer over other considerations, ICLA will conduct protection risk assessments (Do no Harm), adopt conflict and context sensitive approach in dealing with beneficiaries, ensure informed beneficiary consent and acting on instructions of beneficiaries, securing confidentiality and data protection. In addition, ICLA will use additional protection technical expertise to supplement its interventions; coordinate with specialised protection actors to assist with conducting protection risk assessment of ICLA beneficiaries, carry out combined ICLA/protection campaign around protection issues; in collaboration with gender / child protection actors, conduct joint legal and protection research around a particular protection issue or risk in order to inform programming or recruit staff with expert protection skills in NRC. For example, although the legal framework might enshrine certain rights and entitlements that NRC would ideally like to pursue (e.g. strategic litigation), NRC will not do so, if the process of accessing justice were assessed as likely to do harm to the claimants. . Another example concerns the provision of legal aid prior to delivery of humanitarian assistance and includes: 1. a community protection analysis to generate legal vulnerability profiling, which in turn informs the design of the humanitarian interventions including community-tailored legal awareness sessions. 2. Next, legal advice is provided with respect to specific list of activities aimed to be delivered within a specific community. In some cases, the legal advice would be to suspend the implementation of the project as it might pose a risk on the community or it could have a negative effect on pending legal procedures.NRC’s Protection Policy, Gender Policy, Code of Conduct and Child Safeguarding Policy and guidance notes and tools including Core Competency Quality Assessment tools and the Protection Resource Pack all contribute to reinforcing and providing guidance on how to action the protection policy. NRC’s Code of Conduct holds staff, partners and associates (e.g. contractors) accountable, while projecting a common set of standards to the beneficiaries being served. PSAE policies and related mechanisms are included in the project design (see below). At the community level, ICLA will support multi-sectoral, sustainable, and integrated programming with other humanitarian actors (e.g. through West Bank Protection Consortium), which will allow people to preserve or increase their resources and opportunities, thus making them more resilient, and more likely to find durable solutions. ICLA will ensure community engagement on different levels of project cycle management and echoing community feedback. ICLA will conduct needs assessments, service mapping and where relevant, refer beneficiaries to other NRC core competencies or other partners providing specific services. NRC ICLA will support OCHA in the assessment, dissemination of information and implement referral pathways in order to ensure coordinated prevention and response to emergency. ICLA will actively and regularly participate in the Protection Cluster, lead Legal Task Force and collaborate with other Clusters and actors (e.g. UNRWA) on awareness raising, sensitisation, outreach and information dissemination activities to ensure that people have meaningful access to information and feedback mechanisms. NRC Advocacy unit will seek to maintain the humanitarian situation in West Bank on the international agenda in an effort to shape priorities and address the impact of the GoI policies through an evidence-based approach with the consent of the individuals involved, while upholding the do no harm principle.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

NRC ICLA programme is designed to ensure that the most vulnerable Palestinians in hard-to-reach areas have access to basic rights and humanitarian assistance, reduce their vulnerability and build resilience. Information dissemination and counselling: ICLA’s main priorities will be to empower women, men, girls and boys to have equal access to information and services. ICLA will develop information and communication products to access basic services, rights and entitlements (e.g. online, social media, WhatsApp etc. methodologies to overcome Covid-19 restrictions). Accurate, timely and relevant legal information and awareness-raising messages will be disseminated through outreach activities, including face-to-face information and counselling by trained field staff. ICLA will also identify and train members of the communities to disseminate information, recognise protection concerns and refer cases to specialised service providers. Legal assistance including representation: ICLA will use judicial and administrative remedies to support access to justice for the most vulnerable including women, children and people with disabilities. Approaches and procedures will be selected on a case-by-case basis to allow for a customised response that meets the specific needs and interests of the beneficiary(s). In this respect, carefully selected and trained fe/male officers may provide legal information and advice, facilitate negotiations, and if relevant, provide legal representation. Legal assistance will be delivered through ICLA’s legal aid clinics including mobile legal aid clinic to ensure widest possible outreach for people with limited mobility. ICLA’s Mobile Legal Aid Clinic is specifically designed to reach marginalized communities. Flexible and creative approaches will be used to address barriers to access to justice such as health, education, costs, distance, etc. Capacity Building: ICLA will also provide technical support (sensitisation, training, orientation, etc.) to local NGOs, youth and women leaders and community representatives, as well as humanitarian actors with a view to strengthening their capacity to ensure protection for IDPs/refugees and support host communities. Particular emphasis will be placed on IHL/IHRL, rules and regulations about the rights and issues of women and other vulnerable groups. Subject to needs assessment, ICLA may also provide financial and organisational support to implementing partner to strengthen their capacity and ensure sustainability of the intervention. Advocacy: Advocacy seeks to achieve a policy change. It aims for the GoI to align its policies and practices in the OPTs with its obligations as the occupying power under IHL and IHRL. The project will apply an IHRL framework to advance a rights-based discourse that both emphasises the duty bearers’ responsibilities under IHL and highlights violations through accessible and compelling rights-based messaging. ICLA recognises the fundamental need to address the different impacts of conflict and disasters on and promote the potential for positive transformation of gender norms. With regard to the situation in the West Bank, women are disproportionately affected by the loss of homes resulting from demolitions and forced evictions and often face more dire consequences due to the denial of residency rights, such as their inability to enter the workforce due to movement restrictions. Jerusalem residency rights convey access to social welfare entitlements available to all of the city’s residents. The provision of legal aid can serve to alleviate the psychosocial impact of the threat of displacement by reassuring the entire family that the demolition or eviction is being challenged, providing at least temporary protection at no cost to the family.
