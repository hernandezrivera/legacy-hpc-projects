Project Summary

A comprehensive mine action intervention which seeks to prevent injuries and save lives for individuals still living in conflict-affected communities as well as returning IDPs; allow contaminated land and infrastructure to be put back into productive use; and build capacity of state institutions to reduce civilian casualty rates through the provision of training and equipment.

Needs

Since spring 2014, DRC/DDG has recorded over 1,500 casualties as a result of landmines and other explosive remnants of war (ERW). According to the most recent Landmine Monitor report, Ukraine ranks number 5 in the world for casualties from such devices. It is also a little-known fact that Ukraine recorded the highest number of anti-vehicle mine accidents globally in 2016. As other fierce conflicts around the globe have shifted attention away from Ukraine, and whilst the protracted nature of the conflict in the east has increasingly become a fact of life for so many Ukrainians, the situation faced by those in the Donbas has a tendency to be overlooked. Since 2016, DRC/DDG has conducted non-technical survey, coordinating closely with other international organizations, such as the HALO Trust, collectively mapping approximately 15km2 of hazardous area. Owing to difficulty accessing areas within 15km of the contact line, a comprehensive survey within this ‘buffer zone’ is yet to be undertaken. The majority of contamination is expected to be within the buffer zone and as yet, remains to be surveyed and quantified. The most vulnerable are those who remain in affected areas for economic reasons, due to poor health or age or because they are financially dependent on remaining family members. This makes contamination with mines/ERW/IEDs a serious protection concern. 65% of the civilian accidents that DRC/DDG has logged have been as a result of carrying out simple livelihoods activities such as grazing animals, hunting / fishing, foraging and collecting wood. 15% of accidents have been as a result of handling ERW, particularly amongst teenage boys in the 12-17 age range, therefore this group will receive more focused, targeted MRE. Contamination from mines and other ERW not only stands to erode the resilience of the most vulnerable people left behind in conflict-affected areas, it also poses a serious and widespread obstruction to recovery and reconstruction efforts. For instance, the settlement of Talakivka, near Mariupol, has seen no less than seven separate tractor accidents as a result of anti-vehicle mines since 2014, essentially rendering most of the agricultural land in the area unusable. The fact that there were six accidents following the initial accident points to the pressure on the land and civilians’ urgency to put it back into use in order to generate desperately-needed income. Despite wide-reaching mine risk education (MRE) campaigns run by DRC/DDG, as well as several other organizations, a recent survey by the UN Education Cluster revealed that 73% of schools surveyed indicated a continued need for MRE whilst 62 schools indicated the presence of UXO in the immediate vicinity of the school. Finally, more direct support is needed for victims of mine/ERM incidents and their families. Since the start of the conflict, for the period of June 2014 – May 2018, a total of 778 accidents were recorded by the DDG in Ukraine, from open sources. Of the 1,487 casualties, 108 were children (61 accidents), of whom 96 child-victims (54 accidents) were in Luhansk and Donetsk regions (62% in NGCA). On 5 April 2018, the Ukrainian Parliament adopted a resolution establishing a status of ‘child victim of military actions and armed conflicts’. The resolution explains all the legal procedures and conditions which make a child eligible for this status. Yet, as of today, no governmental support – financial or otherwise – has been officially prescribed for child survivors of military actions and armed conflicts in Ukraine. To summarize: The main needs of the mine action sector can be summarized as follows: • Significant expansion of humanitarian mine clearance capacity• Continues support for MRE• Continues support for non-technical survey• New, specialized attention to mine/ERW accident victims and their families• Support for national capacity in terms of data, management, standards, and equipment

Activities & outcomes

A1: Mine risk education (direct and training-of-trainers): Increase safety and security of school-aged children, conflict affected communities, and IDPs. Personnel from schools and communities within the target area are trained to provide MRE, creating a more sustainable capacity. A2: Land release: Land and infrastructure is cleared of landmines and ERW. A3: Non-technical survey: Eastern Ukraine continues to be systematically surveyed in order to accurately quantify the scale and scope of the threat. Hazardous areas are mapped, analyzed and prioritized according to humanitarian impact. A4: Advocacy interventions at national level: Government of Ukraine is encouraged to bring about much-needed legislative reform in the demining sector. A5: Mine/ERW victim assistance (MVA): Adult and child victims and their families are assisted, including through individual case management provided by DRC-DDG. (Packaged with this activity are 1) a situation analysis in relation to mine victims and mapping of available services is conducted in order to contribute to improved service provision; and 2) strengthening protective environment for child mine victims through community support networks and awareness raising to support inclusion.) A6: Support for National Capacity: Equipment vital to the safe and efficient clearance of landmines is procured on behalf of national partners (in DRC-DDG’s case this would be the State Emergency Services)

Indicators & targets

Gender: According to DRC-DDG data, over 80% of landmine victims are male. Within this 80% it is approximately an even split between the age groups of 12-17 and 18-35. Interventions for MRE, for example, would need to factor this into account by targeting the male population at risk within these age ranges disproportionately to other groups. Similarly, DRC-DDG will look to address the perception amongst beneficiaries and staff that demining is a ‘male job’. Training and employing suitable female candidates for these types of roles has proven to be an effective measure in combating these sterotypes. DRC-DDG will therefore look to actively encourage the employment of female deminers under the project. Accountability: DRC-DDG maintains a firm commitment to the principle of ‘Accountability to Affected Population’ (AAP) and uses the AAP Framework as a basis for its approach to AAP best practice in Ukraine.
