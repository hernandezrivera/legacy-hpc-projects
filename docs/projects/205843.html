Project Summary

HCO with more than 10 years of experience in providing protection-related through mobile and static interventions in partnership with various stakeholders across South Sudan, current funding from UNHCR contributes 20% of the projected emergency budget. For smooth implementation, HCO will be supported by the Protection Cluster, Child Protection AoR, CMTF/UASC Working Group, GBV Sub Cluster, EORE Working Group, MHPSS Working Group and the State Working Groups to deliver the following outputs to address the identified needs in the targeted:
Provision of case management services to children including cash-based initiatives; Provision of family tracing and reunification for Unaccompanied and Separated children; Provision of community-based mental health and psychosocial support to children, adolescents and caregivers;
Conducting awareness-raising for women, men, girls and boys on GBV risks, available response services and timely reporting; Supporting vulnerable women and girls with cash and voucher assistance; Distribution of dignity kits to women and adolescent girls; Supporting individuals with specialized GBV response; Developing and strengthening functional GBV referral pathways in areas of operation;
Monitoring people upon arrival to SSD; Conducting protection awareness-raising sessions for individuals with protection concerns or specific needs; Identifying and referring individuals with protection concerns or specific needs; Conducting protection interventions intended for durable solutions (other than CP, HLP and GBV) including legal aid, access to documentation, reintegration and other protection support that can also be cash-base
Conducting EORE for affected individuals;
The centrality of protection will ensure that the rights of beneficiaries are respected. Through advocacy for the full respect of children’s rights and compliance with international law that supports a stronger protective environment. Activities like awareness raising on the rights of children and human rights will be aimed at the promotion of the beneficiaries’ rights. Provision of immediate and sustained support to those harmed by violations, including referral to additional services as appropriate, and taking reasonable steps to ensure affected populations are not subjected to further violence, coercion or deprivation. All beneficiaries will be able to access solutions (such as legal actions at local, national or international levels) and claim legal rights (such as inheritance or restitution).
Using simple, accessible, child-friendly, anonymous programme feedback and reporting mechanisms at each site. Affected populations must be aware of how to use mechanisms & what response they should expect when raising a concern or reporting an incident. Friendly feedback and complaint mechanisms e.g. suggestion boxes, on one, and focus group discussions will be explored in all our target areas.
Through regular field visits will ensure open meetings with the communities of the affected populations, hence beneficiaries will be given a chance to express themselves safely. Reports will be collected from community structures hence strengthening linkages of feedback and complaints. HCO will conduct and participate in joint or coordinated needs assessments, monitoring activities & share information about project plans with relevant stakeholders. Including local authorities in joint meetings so that their time is not overloaded juggling competing demands from different actors. Challenges will be discussed together, and seek joint solutions.
Reporting of PSEA-related incidents will be mandatory with regular training on child safeguarding and PSEA, do no harm, psychological first aid, and risk assessment.
Marginalized groups, persons with disabilities & older persons will be consulted, and their contribution & participation will lead to decision-making. The Project will identify and address barriers to participation faced by people with or without disabilities.

1. Needs

Eleven years after independence and four years after the signing of the revitalized peace agreement, people in South Sudan continue to face deteriorating humanitarian conditions. The situation is worsened by endemic inter communal violence, conflict, access constrains and operational interference, public health challenges, outbreak of climatic socks resulting in extraordinary flooding and localized drought. These in turn have a severe impact on people’s livelihoods; hamper access to education and water, sanitation, hygiene and health services. Protection concerns remain high, especially for women and girls, men and boys. People affected by violence have limited access to formal justice as well as access to basic needs such as food. The humanitarian community in South Sudan estimated that 9.4 million people will be in need of humanitarian assistance in 2023, a staggering 76 per cent of South Sudan’s population, increased by 500,000 people from 2022 and 148,000 returnees from abroad remained displaced within the country border unable to reach their home.According to HNO, South Sudan continues to face multiple compounding shocks and stressors. Insecurity, sub-national violence, regional and national macroeconomic challenges – intensified by the global economic down turn, the impact of climate change and the ongoing Sudan conflict – all adversely affected the food security of South Sudanese families, as of 16 October, 317,993 people have been recorded crossing into South Sudan from Sudan since the onset of the conflict in April. Overall, half of the arrivals are female and about half are children below the age of 18. South Sudanese returnees account for 88.5 per cent of all arrivals. New arrivals continue to report harassment, intimidation, torture and extortion of money during their journey to South Sudan, including by security personnel along the route, the proportion of refugees among new arrivals from Sudan is increasing, with Sudanese refugees accounting for 40 per cent of new arrivals since the beginning of October, Some 37,066 refugees and asylum-seekers have sought asylum in South Sudan, with 61 per cent biometrically registered. More than 9,643 refugees and asylum-seekers are in Aweil relocated from Abyei Administrative Area and 8,600 in Juba. In the Abyei Administrative Area, intercommoned violence and tensions continue to affect the ability of humanitarians to provide services and partners continues to report an increased influx of people from Sudan to South Sudan in search of safety and humanitarian services, including in-kind cash assistance and health services, presence of UXOS, land disputes among returnees and host community. Women and girls live in proper conditions and some lost their dignity due to lack of hygiene kits. Partners continue to roll out Phase II of the response, which includes the provision of services to returnees in their final destinations, such as nutrition treatment and food assistance by cash based assistance and in-kind food as an alternative in places where cash is not viable. In Western Bahr el Ghazal, there is high response need for returnees from Sudan.Physical assault, threat, intimidation, harassment, looting/theft of relief supplies and robbery incidents remained the main concerns in Western Bahr el Ghazal state. On 16 September, Eight out of 30 incidents, mostly violence/threats, were authored by community members – indicating their aggressive reactions to decreasing and suspension of humanitarian services despite heightened needs in the country. Over 43,848 cases (20,633 girls and 23,215 boys) as unaccompanied, separated or missing and at-risk most vulnerable children have been registered as per to UASC report for caseload summary of 31st October 2023, while 17,291 children (8,088 girls and 9,203 boys) as active caseload. Due to the above needs and protracted protection issues, there is a need for continued Humanitarian Response in the targeted counties in South Sudan
