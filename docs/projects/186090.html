Project Summary

An estimated 2,045,461 million people (1,791,557 million children and 253,904 caregivers) in north east Nigeria are in urgent need of child protection services; this includes an estimated 22,394 children and 3,174 caregivers living with disabilities.
Street Child will implement protection support and expansion activities for 109,820 conflict-affected children in the BAY States, of which 55% will be girls. Project activities will include targeted case management and psychosocial support services, as well as strengthening and development of community-based protection mechanisms and structures, aimed primarily at contributing to preexisting gaps in high priority LGAs.
The most high-risk children such as unaccompanied and separated children (UASC), children associated with armed forces and armed groups (CAAFAG), and child survivors or children at risk of gender based violence (GBV) will be particularly prioritized for support and holistic, integrated protection intervention. The project aims to both support urgent and immediate child protection needs, while also working with parents, caregivers, communities and other stakeholders to jointly identify and address root causes of child protection risks in an accessible and relevant way.
Within the scope of this project, the following LGAs have been identified for intervention:
Adamawa LGAs: Mubi North, Mubi South, Michika and Madagali.
Borno LGAs: MMC, Jere, Gwoza, Damboa, Monguno, Biu, Mobbar, Dikwa and Bama.
Yobe LGAs: Gujba and Gulani.

1 - Needs

Child protection needs in northeast Nigeria remain substantial and urgent/emergency in nature. The Child Protection Sub-Sector (CPSS) identified in mid-2021 that 1.7 million children (41% girls, 59% boys) were living in areas where there was little to no child protection infrastructure - either specialized practitioner-based or community-based - available. Over 6,000 children (50% girls) have been abducted, recruited, or made victims of armed groups since the start of the conflict while over 15,000 (50+% girls) have been affected by child protection risks. The conflict in the region, characterized by regular raiding of civilian settlements and extensive abductions by armed groups, continues to affect children and subject them to extreme acute and chronic protection risks, while ongoing an ongoing Cholera outbreak and seasonal flooding also contribute to protection concerns. Child protection risks, which already stem from multiple root causes, have been amplified in the past year due to the economic and social consequences of the COVID-19 pandemic which put children at greater likelihood of recruitment and child labor (in the case of boys) and early marriage or GBV (in the case of girls). Adolescents are more likely than children to be recruited into armed groups or face early marriage, pregnancy, and reproductive health issues. Furthermore, mass defections and surrenders of OAG members have occurred since factional infighting led to the death of Shekau in May 2021. Among these defections are 6,200 CAAFAG as of October 2021 who will be presenting with considerable acute protection concerns and require urgent MHPSS intervention. The Borno State Government resettlement plan has been accelerated in late 2021 with the stated closure of all IDP camps in the state and return of all IDPs to their placers of origin, which will further destabilize the child protection environment as at-risk families return to areas with critically low protection mechanisms in place. Programs will need to take into account the diverse gender and disability identities of women, men, girls and boys. Key needs of the affected population are virtually unchanged from previous HRPs. In particular, case management of children facing acute risks - for instance UASC and CAFAAG, remains a considerable gap within the response. Improved FTR, inter-sectoral referrals and pathway strengthening, and alternative care provision are needed - including strengthening of coordination mechanisms to better support timeliness and effectiveness particularly for children living with vulnerable caregivers. Approximately 111,000 parents and caregivers require support to provide appropriate standard of care for children, and avoid negative coping mechanisms to address overarching contextual problems, with female-, child- and disability-headed households among the most vulnerable. Street Child's integrated approach of working holistically with families on child protection, education and livelihoods and seeking to build capacity of caregivers to provide for their children is therefore relevant and timely. The severity of needs is highest in Borno state, with the needs in 12 LGAs rated as 'severe'. This includes the LGAs of MMC, Jere, Gwoza, Damboa, Biu and Bama. In Yobe state, 2 LGAs are rated as having 'severe' needs, including Gujba. In Adamawa state, the needs in Michika LGA are rated as 'severe'. A final key need is the limited institutional and technical capacity of national child protection partners, particularly in providing quality case management services and timely referrals. Linked to this, there is a need to strengthen field-level coordination and build LGA-level capacity.

2 - Activities

Street Child will provide psychosocial support (PSS) to 90,000 children through after-school recreational activities and peer clubs facilitated by trained PSS facilitators. The activities will be tailored to the different gendered and age-specific needs of different children and will address topics such as health and hygiene, Covid 19, GBV prevention and child rights. Caregivers will also be engaged in PSS activities to promote positive parenting skills. Child Help Desks will run alongside PSS activities, providing more tailored support for children identified as at-risk and supported by trained clinical psychologists. Community members will be trained as PSS facilitators to build community capacity and resilience and improve sustainability. 1,270 children affected by protection risks (including GBV survivors, CAAFAG and UASC) will be targeted for tailored case management, including a tailored care plan and regular follow up from dedicated case workers. Street Child's services include mental health and psychosocial support, family tracing and reunification (FTR) for UASC, socio-economic reintegration of CAAFAG, the provision of protection kits and GBV case management. Across all PSS activities, an integrated referral pathway will provide specialised services including medical support and legal support, and conduct and follow-up for all referrals made. Capacity building to local partners and actors will include the strengthening of field-level referral pathways and developing LGA-capacity to monitor and respond to needs. In addition to traditional case management services, CAAFAG will receive tailored socio-economic reintegration support, including vocational skills training and resettlement grants or education and life skills training, as appropriate. Street Child will also work with communities through adolescent and community activities to advocate for reintegration and acceptance of CAAFAG, and to reduce stigmatisation. As well as providing FTR services to UASC, a focus will be on improving care providing by alternative or interim caregivers, including foster parents. Positive parenting skills training will target these caregivers (both male and female), and the situation of children in alternative care arrangements will be monitored at least once a month, with the aim of identifying any challenges and providing foster parents with support as needed. Key actors and stakeholders (such as MoWASD) will be engaged to screen and monitor foster parents as well as supported to develop sector-wide guidelines on alternative care, building capacity and strengthening the system as a whole. Parents and caregivers of the most at-risk children will also be supported with livelihood activities, which aim to build sustainable income-generating opportunities and ensure that increased household income improves the wellbeing of their children (including through enrolment in school). In all target communities, Street Child will build community-based protection and response mechanisms, establishing or strengthening community-based child protection committees (CBCPCs) and mapping referral pathways to empower community members to be able to identify child protection concerns and respond. CBCPCs will also be the entry point for working with communities for disaster risk reduction (DRR), identifying and mitigating major risks and dangers to children's safety and security. Street Child is committed to localisation and will work through local partners to implement project activities and provide tailored mentoring and capacity building to strengthen technical and organisational capacity. Furthermore, Street Child will provide technical support to the Ministry of Women Affairs and Social Development (MoWASD) to develop a guideline on alternative care for Borno State. The guidance will improve the quality of services and implementation of minimum standards for working with children who require alternative care.

7.1.d - In which LGA are you planning to undertake Nexus activities?

The Nigerian Federal and relevant State Governments are in the process of declaring an end to the humanitarian emergency and a transition into an early recovery - development programme. To this end, Street Child will likewise focus its action across both sides of the nexus across all LGAs in which it will be undertaking activities. In order to implement both response and prevention measures, Street Child will deploy a holistic and integrated programme that supports families and caregivers with means to address root causes of protection violations - for instance, livelihood support and positive parenting sessions that can minimize negative coping mechanisms - alongside acute and emergency MHPSS interventions for survivors of protection breaches. Capacities of communities and local stakeholders will be strengthened in prevention and response, to improve community resilience and the sustainability of the intervention as well as supporting national ownership of protection structures. Nexus activities will therefore be undertaken in Gujba, Gulani, Mubi, Michika, Madagali, MMC, Jere, Gwoza, Damboa, Monguno, Biu, Mobbar, Dikwa and Bama.

8.6 - Please describe briefly the modalities your project will put in place to ensure protection mainstreaming (particularly referring to the above questions).

The prioritisation of protection, safety, dignity, accountability and minimising unintended negative impacts ensure that the communities where Street Child works can access meaningful, dignified support. Street Child is committed to centring and mainstreaming protection throughout its activities and projects, and has pre-emptively identified potential protection risks within the project as well as taking active steps to integrate mitigation and management strategies. Protection reporting, response and prevention mechanisms are integrated into the design, and protection mainstreaming indicators are included within the monitoring and evaluation framework. At the beneficiary selection stage, Street Child will use tested vulnerability assessment tools and clear selection process to ensure that beneficiaries are selected based on need and through predetermined and clearly communicated criteria of vulnerability. In order to ensure participation and minimise inter-group tensions, targets have been set to include different communities (IDP, host community and returnee) as well as both men and women. Community leaders and members will be informed of the selection process and criteria during the inception stages, and continuous sensitisation of the selection criteria will be used to ensure that possible intra- and inter-group tensions are minimised. Sensitisation on these topics through regular community visits and feedback sessions has proven successful during past projects, where dissatisfaction about not being included in the project has been reduced by clearly explaining the aims of the project and how beneficiaries were selected. As part of our commitment to accountability and protection, Street Child commits to work towards the elimination of exploitation and abuse in the communities where we work and in our engagement with vulnerable populations. Street Child has a clear safeguarding policy in place which applies to all personnel and affiliates, including implementing partners and community members who are affiliated to our work. All Street Child staff, partner staff, and project-affiliated community members such as volunteers and committee members receive comprehensive training of the Street Child safeguarding policy. This comprehensive approach, which is also intended to be rolled out to vendors, ensures that all actors within SC projects are operating under the same rules and procedures and creates a harmonised safeguarding environment.In addition to training key community members on the Street Child safeguarding policy, wider community outreach and sensitisation is carried out via hosting of community forums and meetings, or use of visual aids including posters and murals which offer accessible and culturally relevant and appropriate key messages to empower community members on understanding and reporting concerns. Finally, Street Child is an active participant of the PSEA working group in Borno, as an extra level of accountability, and follows the best practices and lessons learned shared through this working group.

9.5 - How will your project ensure feedback is collected from the most vulnerable community members?

Proven feedback mechanisms, which are already widely used and understood by stakeholders and beneficiaries in northeast Nigeria, will be utilised at all stages of the project cycle. At the planning stage, a needs assessment will be conducted in targeted communities and utilise focus group discussions (FGDs) to collect direct feedback from a diverse range of stakeholder constituencies, including gender, age, disability, and other group markers. FGDs will take place at both baseline and endline assessment, for community members to give feedback on the project life cycle and to allow SC to measure community satisfaction. In all target communities, a dedicated complaints mechanism officer (CRM) will be identified and made responsible for collection of feedback and coordinating the sharing and actioning of feedback between Street Child, partners, and community members. The CRM officer will hold weekly helpdesk sessions in each community for any beneficiaries who are unable or unwilling to use other feedback mechanisms. A complaints register, featuring details of complaint and actions taken, will be maintained. Outside of individual feedback, Street Child or project partner staff will attend weekly coordination meetings with community coalitions and structures, for instance CBCPCs with an equal representation of women and men, to collect feedback and share progress relating to specific child protection concerns. For community members who want to give feedback anonymously, anonymous complaint boxes will be placed in each community and checked weekly. A toll-free line will also be advertised where community members can call at any time to provide feedback or share urgent concerns, for instance acute safeguarding concerns. All feedback received will be fed into review sessions and management decisions, allowing activities to be adapted to respond where appropriate in line with Street Child's commitment to accountability and evidence-based learning.
