Project Summary

The proposed project aims to contribute to provide safe access in areas with high rates of returns in Morobo, Lainya and Mundri East Counties. Support for Peace and Education Development Programme (SPEDP) has been implementing Dan Church Aid funded mine action project titled “Saving lives and building safer communities through sustainable risk education, livelihood and cash intervention in Magwi County, Eastern Equatoria State of South Sudan” since 2020. In alignment with the Humanitarian Response Plan 2022, the SPEDP Strategy 2021 – 2025, and United Nations Strategy on Mine Action 2019-2023, SPEDP will mobilise Explosive Ordnance Risk Education (EORE) team to facilitate EORE in Morobo, Lainya and Mundri East Counties, reflecting the needs and severity in line with Mine Action Sub Cluster. SPEDP maintains GBV, Mine Action, Education, Health, and FSL in these areas for a multi-sectoral life-saving intervention.
SPEDP will provide lifesaving EORE to 35,697 individuals including 5,000 girls, 10,697 boys, 7,500 women and 12,500 men. The affected population at the county level will be further disaggregated into the following three groups: internally displaced people (IDP), refugee returnees and host community. The project will form and train an integrated Community Liaison Team (CLT) to provide EORE to vulnerable community members, targeting IDP, returnees, and host communities. The trained CLT members will provide safety briefings to returnees and provide EORE in Morobo, Lainya and Mundri East Counties where a large numbers of IDP and returnees who are likely to return to their home villages. EORE alerts communities on how to identify, avoid and report explosive hazards they may encounter to reduce their threat of injury or death.
The project will focus on the two main approaches with key EORE messages in all areas of operation. These will namely be primary and secondary approaches. Primary approaches will be used for groups identified as the most-at-risk in the community – those living in or close to contaminated areas or other suspected hazardous areas. They will benefit through direct presentations. Secondary approaches will involve the most influential stakeholders. These are people regarded as very close to their population, who act as agencies of change in their community. Religious leaders, teachers, group leaders or representatives fall into this category. They will be educated in Explosive Ordnance Risks to pass on other members of the population who might not be reached directly with the EORE messages.
The project will implement the following EORE activities: Training of EORE CLT teams; EORE assessment; community engagement / mobilization of women, girls, boys, and men; leaflet provision of integrated EORE and community liaison, linked with child protection and education to particularly reach girls and boys in and out of school; use of approved / standard EORE materials and age-appropriate/gender-sensitive means of communication; and advocacy to greater prioritize EORE as a life-saving humanitarian intervention, especially for children. EORE provides community members with the means to keep themselves and their families safe and to report potentially hazardous items to keep their communities safe. Training to CLT members gives the communities the means to continue to spread safe behavior messages even after the project is over. Through this integrated approach of EORE, and training CLT members, the project can sustainably prevent and mitigate protection risks.
The project outcome will enable beneficiaries obtain solutions to their displacement, choose places to live or stay for safe, dignified voluntary returns and build resilience to acute shocks and chronic stresses and support coping capacities and livelihoods within Morobo, Lainya and Mundri East Counties.

1 - Needs

An estimated 8 million people live in counties impacted by landmines and Explosives Remnants of War (ERW) in South Sudan. The United Nations Mine Action Service (UNMAS) estimates the total contamination area with ERW to be some 18.36 km2. From March to June 2021, UNMAS found and destroyed 50 anti-personnel mines and six UXOs, clearing 226,050 m2 in total. Much of the contamination straddles a primary return route for the 56,000 refugees in the Democratic Republic of Congo and 920,000 refugees in Uganda (UNMAS 2021). As of January 2021, there were approximately 350 hazardous areas recorded in the Information Management System for Mine Action (IMSMA) database, which are either suspected or confirmed to be contaminated by landmines or ERW. In the past five years, a plurality of accidents (38%) took place while playing or doing recreational activities, while 15% of accidents took place while traveling. During January – December 2020, 42 people were killed or injured by landmines or ERW, including 51 boys, 5 girls, and 1 woman (IMSMA 2021). In 2022, more than 650,000 people are estimated to be at risk of injury or death from landmines and ERW. There are 6 counties with catastrophic, 24 counties with high and severe needs for Mine Action (MA) challenges mitigation. The counties with the highest level of recorded contamination are, at the extreme level, Juba, Terekaka, Yei, Magwi, Torit and Canal/Pigi, and at the severe level, Lainya, Morobo, Akobo, Maban and Mundri East. The Mine Action (MA) Sub-Cluster will target over 221,000 people in 2022. Altogether 327,000 children affected by MA challenges, 215,000 IDP and returnees among MA people in need. 18 million m² of land is suspected of being contaminated with landmine and ERW. Most of ERW contamination straddles return routes and block access to crucial agricultural land, livelihood activities, shelter, education and health facilities (MA Sub-Cluster). The MA sub-cluster will focus on surveying and clearing explosive hazards, as well as providing explosive ordnance risk education (EORE) to host communities, displaced people, returnees and refugees. EORE will instruct women, men, girls and boys on how to identify, avoid and report explosive hazards. Special emphasis will be given to children, as they make up the majority of accident victims and survivors, as well as displaced people and returnees due to their unfamiliarity with the terrain (2022-2023 HRP Protection and Sub-Clusters Response Plan). The legacy of a protracted and multi-dimensional conflict has resulted in civilians living in close proximity to landmines and ERW. Specifically, there are three counties: Morobo, Lainya and Mundri East, with the higher record of confirmed hazardous areas. These explosive hazards threaten the lives of civilians, particularly those fleeing active armed clashes. It further inhibits the return of displaced persons, the resumption of livelihoods, and the delivery of emergency and life-saving assistance. A multi-sector needs assessment conducted by the SPEDP in March 2022 covering Morobo, Lainya and Mundri East Counties indicated that landmines and ERW impact far higher numbers by restricting freedom of movement, including access to key socio-economic assets providing healthcare, education, and food production and livelihoods opportunities. The assessment reveals that the ERW threaten the lives of civilians, particularly those fleeing active armed clashes. Because of this threat to physical safety, explosive hazards reduce the ability of community members to access land, services such as schools and clinics, and natural resources such as natural water sources, wood for charcoal, and land for grazing. The presence of explosive hazards present barriers to freedom of movement and access to resources and services. ERW affect all members of the community: women, girls, boys, and men. However, men and boys are at an elevated risk of being harmed by mines and ERW.

2 - Project activities

Explosive Ordnance Risk Education (EORE) is a life-saving intervention, especially for children and other vulnerable groups. The dangers posed by remnants of war has prompted SPEDP to develop specifically targeted EORE interventions. SPEDP has undertaken EORE in heavily affected Payams of Magwi County since 2020. This project aims to prevent and mitigate against the physical risks posed by landmines and ERW through enhancing preparedness and resilience through EORE messaging. SPEDP will provide EORE to 35,697 individuals including 5,000 girls, 10,697 boys, 7,500 women and 12,500 men for IDP, returnees and host communities in Morobo, Lainya and Mundri East Counties. The project will conduct the following activities: Activity 1: Provide staff with refresher EORE training, incorporating COVID-19 protocols. Project staff will conduct information sessions to increase community awareness on mine and ERW safety. Staff teams will focus on the primary and secondary approaches to deliver EORE messages. Primary approaches will be used for the most-at-risk who are living in or close to contaminated areas or other suspected hazardous areas. They will benefit through direct presentations. Secondary approaches will involve religious leaders, teachers, group leaders who act as agencies of change. They will be educated in Explosive Ordnance Risks to pass on other community members who lack access to EORE messages. Activity 2: Conduct EORE in schools and other community based centers for children and community members living in high mine risk areas. The project will conduct safety briefings on mine and ERW safety in schools. Activity 3: Provide community based psychosocial support activities and case management for girls and boys in extremely vulnerable situations. SPEDP provisions survivor centered psychosocial support including individual and group PSS services and referrals to appropriate service institutions. Activity 4: Develop and disseminate EORE learning materials targeting at-risk women, men, boys and girls within displaced and host communities to promote greater awareness and safer practices in relation to landmine. The project will promote safe behaviour among women, girls, boys, and men, to reduce the risk of injuries and death caused by mines. The project will develop and disseminate leaflet of integrated EORE and community liaison, linked with child protection and education to particularly reach girls and boys in and out of school. The project will use of standard EORE materials and age-appropriate and gender-sensitive means of communication. Activity 5: Formation of CLTs and mobilise CLTs to conduct focus group discussions with community leaders, youth, and women to determine what types of contamination different members of the community encounter. CLTs will facilitate EORE Sessions to at-risk populations (IDP, host communities and returnees) that will be (disaggregated by sex and age) benefiting from EORE activities. Activity 6: Provision of critical assistance to address the priority needs of targeted people among vulnerable women, men, girls and boys in hard to reach and / or priority geographical areas. SPEDP provides protective livelihood programming and activities such as kitchen gardening, baking, basket weaving and other skills building activities based on women and girl’s preferences. Activity 7: Train CLTs to reinforce EORE messages and provide safety briefings to returnees and newcomers to the community. Training to CLT members gives the communities the means to continue to spread safe behavior messages. CLTs will provide EORE tailored to the needs of the audience (IDP, returnees, host communities, children, men and women to receive targeted messaging and teaching methods). Activity 8: Collect data related to landmines and ERW and provide regular updates on weekly and monthly progress and obstacle during the project to UNMAS and NMAA. The project will periodically conduct EORE assessment.

The surrounding communities will be informed and made aware of the status of the hazardous area. Measuring the impact of the MA intervention will be done by a robust MEAL plan developed to measure both outcome and outputs of the project in a quantitative and qualitative way. The measure of outcome results will include the collection of a baseline, to have a base against which to measure the success of the intervention. Additionally, indicators measuring overall satisfaction of beneficiary will be measured on the regular basis through PDM exercise and quality surveys performed quarterly in all locations.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

SPEDP adopts an age and gender diversity (AGD) mainstreaming approach across all its programmes, meaning that it gives full consideration to AGD in the design, implementation, and monitoring of its activities. The project engages with community leaders, religious leaders, women, men, youth, elderly, and persons with disabilities to solicit their feedback on the project to ensure that activities are tailored and meet the needs of different groups. SPEDP also considers physical access issues for those with disabilities when selecting an activity site. SPEDP will actively seek the views of the targeted beneficiaries to improve EORE, ensuring that feedback and complaints mechanisms are streamlined, appropriate and robust enough to deal with (communicate, receive, process, respond to and learn from) complaints about breaches in policy and stakeholder dissatisfaction. Lessons learned will be used to improve on the project service delivery. SPEDP will ensure that the beneficiaries’ feedback is incorporated into the project by conducting EORE. It is at this stage where beneficiaries are able to express their concerns or fears of explosive ordnance as well report previously unknown hazards around their communities. EORE will provide substantive education to avoid making the community stay in constant fear of the environment. With the understanding that most families are headed by women – and it is them who daily experience difficulties in accessing food, water, education, livelihoods and recreation, this project will administer psychosocial support to the vulnerable population. This will include Psychological First Aid as well as other forms of non-formal psychosocial support. The project will network with other protection sub clusters such as Child Protection and GBV. Even as SPEDP increases the capacity of communities to identify, report suspected ERWs, SPEDP will strengthen and establish community networks for sustainability of the project.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Community Feedback and Response Mechanism (CFRM) will be set up and made functional at community levels. The CFRM will deliver a mechanism that enables the beneficiaries and community members to provide feedback and seek responses in relation to activities of this response and other humanitarian actors in their communities, in a manner that is safe, non-threatening and accessible. SPEDP will measure the adequacy of interventions and address concerns and complaints of most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls). Access barriers could be at following level i.e. within IDP, refugee returnees and host communities and age and sex. The CLT members are specifically trained to speak to diverse audiences and do not only consist of both males and females, but are always from various regions and tribes within South Sudan to uphold the community’s cultural relevance and norms. This ensures continuity amongst the beneficiaries and guarantees that the vulnerable are able to express their views/opinions. Delivery of interventions will consider individuals living with disability, gender and age appropriate as highlighted in the delivery of EORE. SPEDP’s CLT visits individual households to provide EORE and to find out about contamination the area. This also gives community members and opportunity to provide feedback or express concerns. Because visits are made to individual households, vulnerable individuals who are not able to access community and public services are able still able to be reached with EORE, and it also allows them an opportunity to interface with SPEDP CLT and provide feedback if necessary.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

SPEDP has robust safeguarding policies (which include PSEA, code of conduct, health and safety, confidentiality, do no harm principle and unacceptable behaviour protocols) and procedures in place that govern all aspects of SPEDP’s activities. The project will ensure that all stakeholders adhere to work proactively in preventing and protecting affected persons from any form of sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA). SPEDP conducts regular awareness raising with beneficiaries on PSEA, including reporting mechanisms, as well as provides information on PSEA to beneficiaries in multiple formats. In line with the Secretary General’s bulleting of 57/306 (15 April 2003), SPEDP is committed to Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse. The project will provide trainings and orientations to project staff on Programme, Participation, Protection, and Policy (P4) and PSEA policy. It will focus on the five working principles: 1) rights of all, 2) participation and access to decision-making, 3) non-discrimination and equal access, 4) accountability and access to rule of law, and 5) transparency and access to information. The other factors such as cultural, gender norms, values and beliefs will take into account while developing behavioral change communication messages. Similarly, SPEDP will facilitate beneficiaries (particularly girls) to report cases of GBV and sexual harassment from relatives or strangers, which will be documented and referred back to the line cluster partners. SPEDP believes that everyone, regardless of age, gender identity, disability, sexual orientation or ethnic origin has the right to be protected from all forms of harm, abuse, neglect and exploitation. SPEDP takes all reasonable steps to prevent harm, particularly sexual exploitation, abuse and harassment from occurring. SPEDP endeavours to protect people, especially vulnerable adults and children, from that harm. SPEDP responds appropriately when harm does occur. More specifically, SPEDP addresses safeguarding using the three pillars of prevention, reporting and response. SPEDP offers support to survivors of harm caused by staff or associated personnel.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Community engagement modalities will form a critical part of all protection interventions, incorporating such approaches as media campaigns, community consultations on feedback, complain boxes, hotlines and institutionalization of complaint and feedback mechanisms. Efforts will be made to ensure safe and accessible reporting on Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA), quality and accessible survivor assistance and accountability to affected populations (AAP). A complaint and feedback mechanism will be established in the project field offices in order to register complaints from community and give immediate feedback to the complainers if it is a case that can be handled at field level but if not, the case will be referred to Juba. The project will utilize Community Based Response Mechanism (CBRM) as a community management mechanism that delivers collaborative livelihoods and income generating opportunities as a means of building peace and stability. The CBRM will address issues that may arise in a credible manner. CBRM will also facilitate community-based dispute resolution process to receive feedback, deal with complaints or disputes. The project is expected to receive and proactively respond to the issues and concerns. SPEDP is committed to accountability to affected populations by integrating feedback and accountability mechanisms into EORE strategies. Community members will be included in assessment, design and implementation of the project. SPEDP has IEC materials available in multiple formats that are displayed/shared in order to raise awareness on the feedback and complaints mechanisms. The project will ensure accessible and timely information to affected populations on organizational procedures, structures and processes that affect them to ensure that they can make informed decisions and choices, and facilitate a dialogue between SPEDP the targeted populations. The beneficiaries and the local community representative will be given opportunity to provide feedbacks regarding activity performance and the overall impact of the intervention.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

Women are disproportionately harmed by flooding and related displacement. Women typically bear greater responsibility for tasks that are made more difficult by flooding, including sourcing food and water. Displaced women are also disadvantaged in many humanitarian recovery efforts, and may face greater difficulty in accessing assistance. Displacement are often conducive to gender based violence. The affected population are critically in need of access to safe drinking water, anti-malarial and other basic drugs, and plastic sheets to be used as temporary shelters for families with damaged or destroyed houses. It is likely that the degraded or lost crops and other sources of livelihoods will cause longer-term humanitarian needs and reduce food security in 2022. There is a need to provide immediate support to the flood affected communities. Shelter and essential household items (HHIs), Health, Water, Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH), Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR), Protection, Gender and Inclusion (PGI) activities are required for an integrated response. SPEDP will refer the flood affected communities to access to safe drinking water, anti-malarial and other basic drugs, and plastic sheets to be used as temporary shelters for families with damaged or destroyed houses. SPEDP will also provision psychosocial first aid to the affected, family reunification, linkages to needed and available services and ensuring there is mainstreaming of protection services into other sectors such as WASH, Health and Livelihoods. Mainstreaming will be done using the minimum standards for protection, gender and inclusion in emergencies.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

The project contributes to improved food security through the release of safe land for productive use, and thereby indirectly has an expected positive effect on GAM rates. In addition, opening safe access to humanitarian services will further benefit the targeted communities. The project enables safe and equitable access to life-saving services (ie food distribution sites, water points, markets, etc.) as well as release safe land for cultivation purposes. The release of cleared land allows the targeted communities to use it for productive purposes. This means that local food production is increased. At the same time, released land provides space for safe and sustainable returns from neighbouring countries. Returnees will require access to land and food to settle. As this will initially increase the burden on the host community, it is important to provide opportunities for food production, preventing an increase in the population facing IPC 3, 4 and 5.
