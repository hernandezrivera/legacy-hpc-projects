Project Summary

Building on the rehabilitation and re-equipment of PHC facilities in Medair’s health programming, integrated preventative and curative nutrition services will be provided at each facility and wherever possible in the community through community health workers (CHWs). Facility based activities will include malnutrition screening for CU5 and PLW, treatment of severe and moderate acute malnutrition, provision of micronutrient supplements for CU5 and PLW and iron supplementation for pregnant and postnatal women. IYCF counselling including breastfeeding support and promotion of appropriate complementary feeding will also be provided, both in health facilities and in communities. Relevant clinic staff will receive both formal and on the job training. Therapeutic food and supplements will be provided through a well-established partnership with UNICEF and WFP.
CHWs will work in pairs in the clinic catchment area, delivering targeted relevant nutrition messages to households and providing malnutrition screening to CU5 and PLWs. They will also deliver health, hygiene and disability messages and act as a link to the PHC clinic, referring any malnutrition cases identified. In line with MoH guidance the CHWs are currently given a performance related quarterly gift in kind incentive worth up to approximately 55 USD; in the future Medair hopes to be able to provide voucher or cash incentives of similar value. No other cash or voucher assistance is planned as part of this project.
Medair will target locations which are newly accessibly, hard to reach or have high severity needs. Planned locations include Deir-ez-Zor, rural Aleppo, rural Homs, southern Idleb and Daraa and Quneitra. Medair has established access and initiated multisectoral programming in these areas. In addition possible expansion of activities into Raqqa and/or Hasakeh is being actively explored.

4 - Needs

Continued displacement, economic hardship, restricted food availability and lack of awareness all contribute to ongoing nutritional needs in Syria. Chronic malnutrition and stunting is a significant problem, with a national prevalence of 12.6% in children and higher rates in NES, Idlib (20%) and Aleppo (25%). (HNO draft, 2020) While GAM rates in CU5 were below emergency thresholds at 1.7% (SAM 0.4%, MAM 1.3%) among PLWs GAM rates have increased to 4%. There is significant variation at governorate, district and subdistrict level. Deir-ez-Zor had the highest GAM rates in children under 5 at 2.7%. There is insufficient access, not only to curative interventions for acute malnutrition, but also to preventative interventions for malnutrition. A key factor in acute and chronic malnutrition is poor IYCF practices: a nationwide SMART survey in 2019 showed exclusive breastfeeding rates among children under 6 months old of only 28%. In rural Dar’a, Medair found women usually returned to manual work 4-6 weeks after giving birth due to acute economic pressures, and therefore did not breastfeed due to separation from their infants for 6-8 hours/day. Use of inappropriate breast milk substitutes e.g. sugar water was widespread (FGD, 2019). Early initiation of breastfeeding in Aleppo city was only 57% despite 89% of deliveries taking place in a health facility (Medair LQAS survey, 2019), suggesting additional training of health staff is needed. Coverage of IYCF services is largely through awareness raising rather than the needed specialized services. The capacity of health and nutrition practitioners in specialized IYCF skills needs to be strengthened on a national scale. More than 30% of pregnant women and 41% of children aged 6-23 months have anaemia which may also indicate other micronutrient deficiencies.Contributing factors include poor dietary diversity, limited access to health care and negative coping measures such as child marriage. (HNO 2020, draft)

5 - Implementation of activities

Integrated nutrition activities will be implemented in PHCCs already rehabilitated and reequipped in Medair’s health programme. Activities include screening and treatment of SAM and MAM in all CU5 and PLW, IYCF counselling of PLWs and provision of appropriate micronutrients to CU5 and PLWs. Cases of severe malnutrition with complications will be referred appropriately. Clinic staff receive an initial 5 day IYCF/CMAM training and on the job training. Provision of key messages and counselling in clinics and community outreach areas will promote appropriate IYCF behaviours, particularly breastfeeding and adequate, timely complementary feeding.Therapeutic supplements are expected to be supplied as GIK from UNICEF/WFP . Monthly supervision visits will monitor quality of implementation and counselling and data on outcomes. If outcomes are below SPHERE standards action will be taken. PHCCs are fully handed over to MOH after 12 months of support by Medair. Through the CHP, CHWs will provide house to house malnutrition screening and IYCF promotion. Malnutrition cases identified are referred to the PHCC. About 20 CHWs/clinic are recruited (predominantly female)working in pairs in the catchment area, conducting 15-20 HH visits/week. In line with MOH guidance, CHWs will not receive incentives, but a quarterly gift worth around 60 USD. CHWs will receive a 5 day training, followed by bimonthly refreshers. Topics include IYCF, malnutrition screening, communication skills, first aid and disability. Reporting data will be reviewed weekly and monthly.All activities will be closely coordinated with MoH/UNICEF/WFP. Medair/DoH are planning monthly joint supervision visits to the supported PHCCs, dependent on DoH availability and security clearance. Wherever permitted community members will be consulted during assessments. Beneficiary feedback will be sought through exit interviews if permitted and complaints boxes. Monitoring visits by UNICEF/WFP will be supported.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

- Confidentiality of sensitive beneficiary data is breached, risking stigmatisation of individuals/HHs: awareness raising among staff of data protection principles; clinic staff to ensure privacy during consultations- Health facilities are perceived to be inaccessible to particular ethnic groups due to location or staff ethnic background: Ensure local context well understood prior to clinic selection, consult community members wherever possible. Staff training on humanitarian principles if potential issues identified.- Inequity of access to health facilities between men and women: Awareness of wider community context e.g. transport, power availability, main market/shop times. Ensure health facilities and service providers available at times that suit women as well as men.- Inequity of access to nutrition services between boys and girls: Training for clinic staff and CHWs. Monitor data for adverse trends. - Head of household or PLWs unwilling to be screened, access services or receive visits: CHWs are predominantly female (85%) to achieve acceptance for HH visits. CHWs receive training to effectively communicate with Head of House.- GBV towards CHWs: Always make HH visits in pairs.- GBV disclosed during consultations is not handled appropriately: train clinic staff and CHWs on dealing sensitively with reports of GBV and identify referral pathways for each location- PSEA (actual or alleged) by clinic staff or CHWs: provide training on Medair’s PSEA policy and reporting mechanisms to clinic staff and CHWs, AAP mechanisms including complaints box and exit interviews. -Inequity of access to health facilities for people with disabilities: accessibility adaptations e.g. ramps to be installed during rehabilitation; disability awareness and inclusion training to all staff and CHWs -Nutrition supplies are diverted or sold: stock checks during supervision visits including reconciling amounts with treatment records. Delivery in tranches.

MITIGATIONS-Conduct robust, regular context analysis as part of security management.-Keep security planning up to date and train a crisis management team for each location.-Remain flexible in project locations if access to a particular community or programme site is refused.-Proactive coordination with Ministries and SARC, other agencies and UN to avoid duplication.-Maintain good contact with SARC, Ministries, local authorities, communities, UN agencies and other stakeholders.-Train all new staff on procurement guidelines, conduct systematic internal and external audits to assess adherence, spot checks for fraudulent activity.
