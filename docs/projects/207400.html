Project Summary

The International Rescue Committee (IRC) in Bangladesh plays a crucial role in the healthcare sector of Cox's Bazar, particularly within the Rohingya response. The IRC is committed to delivering essential health services with a focus on Maternal, Newborn, Child, and Adolescent Health (MNCAH), Sexual and Reproductive Health (SRH), including adolescent SRH, Non-Communicable Diseases (NCD), including Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS), and Communicable Diseases (CD).
IRC will operate four Primary Health Care Centers (PHCs) located in camps 6, 11, 22, and 25 and one BEmONC Center situated in camp 2E PHC. The 24/7 PHC services provided by our four PHCs adhere to the health sector-approved Minimum Package of Essential Health Services (MPEHS) for Rohingya people. In camps 6 and 11 PHCs, IRC will maintain a coordinated approach in collaboration with SRC/BDRCS. In camp 2E PHC, we will work closely with BDRCS/SRC to deliver comprehensive SRH services (ANC, PNC, FP, MRM, STI) treatment and PAC) including normal vaginal delivery (NVD) services. The IRC will dedicate its efforts to strengthening the management of NCDs in its PHCs to address the increasing NCD health service requirements of the population. These facilities will also be complemented by comprehensive diagnostic laboratories, enabling proper diagnosis and reducing the need for unnecessary referrals to secondary or tertiary care facilities. IRC will also pilot of the World Health Organization (WHO) framework for meaningful engagement of people living with NCDs, specifically focusing on diabetes and hypertension. This includes the implementation of self-care management practices in humanitarian settings and the utilization of the Health sector and WHO NCD-facility based monitoring guidance.
The IRC is committed to continuing its community health initiatives, coordinating with the Community Health Working Group (CHWG) within Rohingya camps, with a particular focus on camps 11 and 25 and plans to expand to camp 22. Our goal is to empower Rohingya individuals by increasing their knowledge of health-seeking behaviors, with a particular emphasis on MNCH, SRH, and NCDs.
IRC will continue its support to Referral Hubs (RH) in 10 camps, including 6, 7, 8E, 8W, 11, 19, 20, 20E, 26, and 27, which ensure referral and transportation support for patients with emergency obstetric conditions and other medical emergencies. This assistance facilitates access to PHC/BEmONC facilities through community health volunteers and deployed ambulances, working closely with PHCs, BEmONC facilities, and Community Health Workers (CHWs) in the respective camps.
In line with Emergency Preparedness and Response (EPR) guidelines, the IRC will sustain four surge Mobile Medical Teams (MMTs) in camps 6, 11, 22, and 25. These MMTs will be ready to respond to natural disasters and artificial hazards prevalent in Cox's Bazar, including fires, monsoon rains, cyclones, floods, and epidemics, through training and simulations. We ensure timely and regular reporting of health services to DHIS2, 4Ws, and the Early Warning, Alert, and Response System (EWARS). We also address gender norms to influence behavior and encourage male engagement in MNCH, family planning (FP), and SRH. We will consider the diverse needs of children, adolescents, youth, and the elderly, incorporating various factors such as gender, age, disability, and ethnicity, and employ Sex, Age, and Disability Disaggregated Data (SADDD) for program quality improvement. Furthermore, the IRC actively promotes Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) by building the capacity of project staff members, producing community-facing content, and assisting in establishing Complaints and Feedback Response Mechanisms (CFRM) while analyzing feedback for program development.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

Approximately 931,574 Rohingya refugees are residing in the Cox's Bazar district within the 33 densely populated refugee camps. The total population in need, including affected host communities, amounts to 1.4 million individuals. Among this population, 59% of Rohingya and 55% of host community members have experienced health problems and require access to healthcare services. Among those in need of healthcare, 12% are from the host community, and 11% are refugees, indicating unmet healthcare needs (JMSAN 2024). In 2022, a total of 5.5 million outpatient department (OPD) consultations were provided, equating to a utilization rate of 3.92 consultations per person per year. Common health issues among Rohingyas include skin diseases (21%), Acute Respiratory Infections (ARIs) that are non-pneumonia/Upper Respiratory Tract Infections (URTI) (18%), and other acute conditions (10%) (Health Sector Bulletin, August 2023). From January to September 2023, a total of 138,865 consultations were sought for hypertension, and 119,794 consultations were conducted for diabetes (HS biweekly meeting presentation, Nov 23) indicating increase NCD disease burden within Rohingya community. There is a pressing concern regarding the increasing maternal and newborn mortality resulting from preventable causes. The maternal mortality rate in the camps remains a concern , with a rate of 319 deaths per 100,000 live births reported by WHO in February 2023 (compared to a baseline rate of 326/100,000 live births). As of October 2023, there have been 68 maternal deaths observed, and from January to June 2023, a total of 410 perinatal deaths occurred (MPMSR Dashboard), which is higher when compared to the average population in Bangladesh. Quality of care within health facilities has become a priority needs to avert some of these deaths. Furthermore, challenges in relaying in informal healthcare providers and the availability of emergency transportation from homes to nearby health facilities, particularly at night, which is crucial for reducing maternal and neonatal mortalities and addressing other medical emergencies (MPMSR Dashboard, Barua M. et al., 2020). Communities generally lack awareness about Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs) and their effects on reproductive health. An increase in the uptake of short-acting contraceptives (oral pills and injectables) over long-acting and reversible contraceptives (LARCs) has been observed. Although there has been some improvement in LARC uptake, the rate remains low at only 3% (1% IUD and 2% Implant) in 2022 (SRH WG 2022). The preferred family planning method is the oral contraceptive pill (61%), followed by injectable contraceptives (27%) and condoms (9%). A recent study reported that 60% of participants were unaware that there was no physical harm associated with using LARCs, 40% felt ashamed and afraid to discuss family planning matters with their husbands, and 58% believed that a couple should continue having children until a son is born (Azad MAK, et al., 2022). Furthermore, child marriage is prevalent in the Rohingya camps and is rooted in socio-cultural and religious beliefs regarding readiness for marriage (Melnikas AJ, et al., 2020). This has significant consequences for maternal morbidity and mortality. It's worth noting that 76% of women and girls fear being identified as survivors of Sexual and Gender-Based Violence (SGBV) when seeking services. It has also been reported that incidents of rape, including marital rape, have contributed to an increase in unwanted pregnancies and unsafe abortions, resulting in severe emotional and physical consequences for survivors (IRC safety audit, 2021).

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

The health facilities managed by IRC (located in camps 6, 11, 22, and 25) have already received recommendations from the health sector based on a recent rationalization of health facilities. In camp 2E PHC, IRC will provide BEmONC services in coordination with BDRCS/SRC, which aligns with the health sector's recommendations. A majority portion of funds for camps 6, 11, and 2E has been secured until August 2024, while partial gaps remain. Camp 25 and camp 22 have funding confirmed until February and March 2023, respectively. These health facilities have already integrated Emergency Preparedness and Response (EPR) services, and camp 25 PHCC has also integrated rehabilitation services with Humanity & Inclusion (HI). Eye care services have already been integrated into camp 6 PHCC, and efforts will be made to integrate them into other PHCCs. IRC has secured partial funds for all these health facilities, and it is highly anticipated that donors supporting these interventions will continue their funding into the year 2024 and beyond. To ensure the continuity of NCD services, IRC will onboard private donors to address the funding gaps for NCDs.Maternal and newborn mortality remains a significant concern, and ensuring their safe transportation to healthcare facilities is of paramount importance across Rohingya Response. Funding for Referral Hubs in seven camps has been confirmed till February 2023, with a strong likelihood of continued support for the next few years. Additional funds for three Referral Hubs are expected to be secured for FY 2024. These hubs are well integrated with EPR, and efforts are underway to integrate them with Gender-Based Violence (GBV) referrals.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

The proposed project will focus on integrated protection intervention. The IRC will ensure access to protection mechanisms to increase capacity to stay safe, exercise basic rights, and access essential services. IRC will ensure integration within the protection (CP, GP, and GBV) sector. Particular attention should be dedicated to all mechanisms to protect refugees from Sexual Abuse and Exploitation by from anyone including humanitarian workers. The IRC will ensure staff are trained on humanitarian code of conduct, Child Safeguarding, and Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse and Harassment (PSEAH).All part of the health facilities will be cover with adequate lighting with required security guards to make sure all parts of the health facilities are safe for every client and the staff working in these health facilities. There will be separate space (e.g. rooms and screening process) to isolate any client of contagious diseases to reduce the transmission of CD diseases to other clients as pert of IRC’s “No Harm” policy. Health facilities will have adequate medical waste management system (e.g. waste segregation, incinerator) to avoid causing infection and or contaminating the environment including water.

3.3 - Age & Disability

The project conducts an analysis of the risks and needs of clients with a focus on age diversity. Services are tailored to address specific needs, including comprehensive assessments for individuals aged 40 and above, providing the highest level of sensitivity for children aged 0 to 5, and offering separate content and services for adolescent boys and girls, taking cultural sensitivity into account. Child safeguarding is a top organizational priority, and it falls under the mandatory reporting protocol. Both preventive and responsive mechanisms are in place to ensure safeguarding awareness, including robust sessions with staff and incentive workers who have direct contact with clients on a regular basis. We will consider the diverse needs of children, adolescents, youth, and the elderly, taking into account various factors such as gender, age, disability, and ethnicity. We will also employ Sex, Age, and Disability Disaggregated Data (SADDD) to improve program quality. IRC will provide orientation for healthcare staff on disability and assess health facilities to address infrastructure needs for people with disabilities.

3.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

The IRC is fully committed to prioritizing the individuals we serve – our clients – at the core of our humanitarian response, ensuring that we are responsive to their aspirations and perspectives. This approach enhances the effectiveness, relevance, and appropriateness of our services and assistance while also making our decisions and actions more accountable to our clients, affected communities, and other stakeholders.As part of our Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) efforts, IRC will continue to operate Help Desks at Primary Health Care Centers (PHCCs) in five different camp locations, including camps 2E, 6, 11, 22, and 25. Additionally, IRC has set up Suggestion Boxes and Hotline Numbers to receive complaints and feedback from our clients. Moreover, we will conduct one-on-one discussions, focus group discussions (FGDs), post-distribution monitoring, and "client satisfaction surveys" to gauge the level of satisfaction within the community.

3.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Due to its geographical location in the deltaic plain, Bangladesh ranks 13th globally as one of the most highly disaster-prone countries. The coastal areas, such as Cox's Bazar, are particularly vulnerable to various natural disasters, including cyclones, floods, storm surges, riverbank erosion, landslides, and heavy rainfall. These events are associated with heightened health risks, including drowning, casualties, and deaths from landslides and flooding. They also contribute to an upsurge of epidemic-prone diseases, disrupting the normal health service delivery system.Furthermore, mass casualty management is a critical aspect of emergency health service delivery during disasters. Challenges in this area include a lack of human resources, emergency medical supplies, communication infrastructure, and coordination with both internal and external actors. In response to context-specific natural hazards and past disaster impacts, Disaster Risk Management (DRM) measures have been implemented to reduce the risk of negative effects on life and the environment.The project has established an information management system to ensure the timely dissemination of early warnings. It also includes multi-hazard preparedness messages aimed at increasing awareness among the target population. Additionally, efforts have been made to enhance the knowledge and skills of frontline emergency health service providers, volunteers, and community members to effectively implement disaster risk reduction interventions.The identification of natural hazard risks is facilitated through health facility assessments and periodic community health risk assessments. An emergency response plan has been developed as part of these efforts. Advocacy with relevant actors is conducted, and emergency health services are provided during disasters in accordance with the ISCG Multi-Hazard Response Plan, which includes health sector specific preparedness and response guidance.
