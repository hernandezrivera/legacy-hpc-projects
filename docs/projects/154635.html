Project Summary

The project will contribute to filling in a gap of services in the protection sector particularly SGBV. This will be achieved through multi-sectoral SGBV prevention and response programming in four key areas of intervention (health, psycho-social services, protection and livelihood). The project will directly contribute to lessening the impacts of the challenges identified in Section A. The project will also explore working with local faith leaders to promote awareness on SGBV and battle social norms that harm survivors and further stigmatizes them.
The project is designed to contribute to the goals and objectives as set by the Iraqi Government and Humanitarian community in Humanitarian Response Plan 2018. The project is also in line with the IRW’s commitment to gender equality, women’s rights, and supporting the basic needs of the hardest hit by conflict by addressing gender rights to protection and their equal access to SGBV resources (including physical and mental health) from a rights-based approach.
The project has three main components including i)Sexual and reproductive health, ii) gender empowerment and iii) community outreach and sensitization.


i) Sexual and Reproductive health aims to:
• Increase the coverage and referral systems of basic and comprehensive emergency obstetric and neonatal care services.
• Increase the coverage of family planning services for populations affected by the emergency.
• Awareness raising on sexual and reproductive health.

ii) Gender empowerment will be strengthened through a multi-sectoral response to gender-based violence (health, psychosocial and livelihoods) aiming to:
• Develop skills of health service providers and social workers on GBV prevention and response through a multi-sectoral training package
• Institutionalize community-based service mapping and effective referral systems for GBV
• Support the provision of quality psycho-social services, life skills training, provision of dignity kits and referrals in Women and girls space centres
• Integrate GBV prevention and response into RH services through training of RH providers in caring for GBV survivors including CMR
• Distribute Dignity Kits as an entry point for awareness raising on GBV services
iii) The third component will increase public awareness of the detrimental impacts of gender-based violence on families and communities, including child and forced marriage, domestic violence, honour killings and temporary marriages.
• Community centres will be established
• Community outreach through awareness campaigns will be conducted targeting boys, girls, women and men.
In December 2017 the Iraqi government has announced that the Iraqi land is completely liberated from ISIS. Hence the government started encouraging the IDPs who are living in IDP camps to go back to their origins in Anbar, Mosul, SalahEl Den, and Kirkuk governorates. The government started encouraging IDPs to go back to their origin in a very quick manner. This immature return has caused second and third state of displacement. As a result of the engorgement of the return of IDPs to their origin, the IDPs camps in Baghdad, Anbar, and some camps in Kirkuk have been closed and families were evicted from camps. Most of the IDPs who could not go back to their origin have resided in out-of-camp areas in Baghdad and Anbar.
Due to the above mentioned changes in the context of Iraq, Islamic Relief Iraq will adjust the response plan of this project and focus on out-of-camps areas in Anbar governorate. This project will establish a total of 10 women space centres (5 in Ramadi and 5 in Falluja) in off-camp areas, the established women centers will be operated by social workers who will deliver protection and PSS sessions, in addition rto Case Management Officer who will be responsible for case management, address service mapping, and develop referral pathway for survivors among girls and women.

Needs

Sexual violence is a major concern despite it being highly underreported due to a number of serious factors, including: risks, threats and potential trauma faced by those who come forward; significant gaps in available services; restrictions on movement; shame, fear, stigma and discrimination; risks of retaliations and “honor killings”; impunity for perpetrators; and the existence of mandatory reporting of sexual violence by government health staff. Feedback from communities and service providers indicate an increase of sexual violence within communities, directly related to the crisis and mass displacement. However, when reporting of GBV does happen, it is untimely, with only 9% of GBV cases reporting within three days of the incident. Timely reporting is critical for addressing the mental and physical health needs, including preventing long-lasting psychological effects of trauma and providing lifesaving HIV post-exposure prophylaxis ad other immediate medical care. Gaps in humanitarian aid have increased risks of violence and heightened the likelihood of women and girls being forced to engage in negative coping strategies (like survival sex or early and forced marriage) to meet basic needs. Displaced women, especially widows and female-headed households, are particularly vulnerable to GBV.In 2018, Islamic Relief Worldwide, Iraq has conducted need assessment off-camps areas in Anbar and Baghdad, The following summarizes the situation of sexual and gender based violence: Domestic Violence:Actors of all types across Iraq consistently and overwhelmingly identified domestic violence (intimate partner violence (IPV), and other GBV within the home as the number one priority GBV issue. Iraq had a high level of domestic violence before the conflict, with 46% of married women exposed to at least one form of spousal abuse. Overcrowded accommodation, financial strain, shifts in traditional gender roles, insecurity and other factors directly related to displacement are contributing to high levels of stress within households, which in turn is manifesting as physical, verbal and emotional abuse targeted at women, girls and boys either due to their perceived weaker role within the household, or to enforce compliance with expected gender roles. Deeply entrenched cultural norms strongly deter women from reporting incidents, considering fear of persecution that can result in death, stigma, discrimination and shame. So-called “honor killing,” or the murder of a family member due to the perpetrators' belief that the victim has brought shame or dishonor upon the family or has violated the principles of a community or a religion, often by engaging in some form of extra-marital engagement, is a common fear and prevents survivors from reporting incidents. Forced Marriage including Child Marriage: Many of the identified priority protection issues follow a similar pattern, with pre-crisis major areas of concern being exacerbated and increased as a result of the crisis. Prior to the crisis, 21% of girls aged 15-19 were married, and 5.5% of girls under the age of 15 were married despite that the legal age to marry is 18.9 years. The conflict has increased the level of threat, both real and perceived, to women and girls while simultaneously deepening men’s social role as ‘protector.’ This combination has contributed to marriage being seen as a means to protect young girls and women, increasing rates of forced and child marriage. Child marriage is likewise being used to cope with diminishing resources, especially in prolonged displacement, through reducing the household size and gaining monetary compensation from the groom. Additionally, many families are afraid to send their girls to school for security concerns as adolescent girls are particularly vulnerable to multiple forms of violence and have limited support systems and access to information and services.Sexual Violence Harassment and Exploitation: Uprooted from the protection of familiar communities and challenged to meet basic needs, displaced women, girls and boys face increased GBV through public harassment and exploitation. Prior to the crisis, 19.5% of women 15-54 years old were exposed to violence in the street during a one-year period, 18.9% were exposed to violence in the market, and 10.5% on transport . Children have also reported harassment on the way to and during school to be a cause of dropping out. Lack of social and Economic Empowerment: Survivors’ need for empowerment is commonly mentioned particularly in relation to the vulnerability caused by economic dependency. Women’s limited livelihood options and reliance on male family members raise their susceptibility to exploitation and denial of essential resources and increase vulnerability to other forms of GBV. Protection Risk Analysis

Risk:1. There is a risk of tension may occur between the returnees and local communities. 2. There is a risk of sexual exploitation and abuse. 3. 2. Security Situation deteriorated in the targeted area

Mitigation Measures

Mitigation Mechanism: 1. To mitigate this risk, the project team will induct the targeted communities on the project beneficiaries selection criteria and ensure that the formed community committees are well oriented on the targeted beneficiaries selection criteria, so the community committees will be be involved to induct the targeted communities accordingly. In addition, this project will also target local communities in the targeted areas based on the specified criteria under this project. 2. To mitigate this risk, Islamic Relief Iraq will ensure well-reference mechanism is done when project staff are recruited. The hired staff under this project will be introduced to internal policies and procedures including harassment policies, safeguarding policy, sexual exploitation. 3. Regular security update will be maintained by the project team and security focal persons involving the clusters, INSO, and the local authorities. If security situation get deteriorated in the targeted project area due to any reason, Islamic Relief Iraq will avoid areas under conflict and will concentrate on comparatively safer areas for project activities implementation and might propose change the targeted area in case of inaccessibility.

How is your project accountable to affected population

Islamic Relief is committed to ensure that its program interventions are appropriate and designed in a way which positively contributes in lives of women, men, boys, and girls in the targeted communities. To abide by this commitment, Islamic Relief has a Monitoring, Evaluation and Accountability (MEA) system which aims at improving quality of our interventions and document key learning, at the same time MEA provide timely information to program staff and senior management to make critical and strategic decisions regarding our program interventions.The project Coordinator will take the lead role in conducting a series of community mobilization sessions at targeted locations in form of public gatherings where project goal, objectives and funded activities will clearly be outlined. The project M&E Coordinator and Project Coordinator will also be responsible for complains-feedback mechanisms whereby project beneficiaries, local authorities and other key community key people will freely contact them (through a shared and displaced phone number and complaint box ). All complains will be registered, classified (based on the seriousness of the complaint), relevant departments/sectors consulted and timely feedback relayed back to the complainant/community leaders. The project will also provide opportunities for women, men, girls and boys to engage directly in the project implementation thus stakeholders will be encouraged to raise their concerns.
