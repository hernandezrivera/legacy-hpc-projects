Project Summary

Aligned with the Decade on Ecological Restoration, FAO will continue working to restore socio-ecological systems throughout Cox’s Bazar. Local communities and authorities will be at the center of the programme to build consensus on sustainable use and management of natural resources while safeguarding the ecosystem, current, and future. Climate change, environmental degradation, and high population density weakens ecosystems and puts pressure on natural resources, leaving communities more vulnerable.
In the camp area, which was a forest reserve area prior to the influx, 40% of the shelters are considered not safe considering landslides and erosion. According to a recent study conducted by FAO, the main influencing factors for landslides in the wider Cox’s Bazar area are hill-cutting and deforestation. FAO will promote disaster risk reduction through scaling-up piloted landslide early warning system in the camp area under ECHO PPP. In this project community landslide response teams will be trained in preparedness, awareness and search and rescue in the most landslide prone camps. In addition one person per camp will be capacitated to collect necessary inventory data during the monsoon season to validate the early warning system through cash-for-work modality (5500 cash-for-work days), and act to increase landslide awareness in their communities. To more-over reduce the number of shelters in landslide risk-zone, biological land stabilization and reforestation plantations will be done in accordance with guidelines developed by FAO and successfully piloted in the camp areas. Final selection of cash-for-work beneficiaries and plantation sites will be selected based on landslide susceptibility map for camps.
To increase ecosystem health and the services, such as protection from natural hazards, provided to the camp population, FAO will continue to provide technical support and planting materials for reforestation and rehabilitation of degraded land, as well as streambank rehabilitation and roadside plantation for reduced erosion, in addition to slope stabilization. A landscape approach is used, where watersheds are considered as the functional unit for planning when designing interventions such as roadside and riparian plantations. All plantation activities will be conducted through cash-for-work modality.
All camp dwellers are considered in-direct beneficiaries, as healthier ecosystems and reduced risk of natural hazard, including protection of common infrastructure will benefit them. In restoration projects impact is usually calculated based on hectares of plantation. Considering all plantation and fencing activities will target a total of 250 hectares, a total of 40 000 person days are needed for plantation and maintenance. The cash-for-work beneficiaries, in addition to trainings, are considered as the direct beneficiaries of this project.
FAO will also continue its support to partners in monitoring and implementation of overall ecosystem restoration and conservation of biological diversity through development of specifications and guidelines. Already camp specific land stabilization and reforestation guidelines have been developed, and piloted in the camps. FAO are also (SAFEPLUS2) developing multi-sectoral joint monitoring platform for environmental and socio-ecological assessments, under this proposed project training and capacity sharing will be done with relevant stakeholder in the camps to further develop and contextualize the framework.
FAO use conflict sensitive approaches in all its programming to ensure project interventions are done in consensus with communities. The key to this approach is building trust with all stakeholders, and with this trust enabling solutions that are accepted by all. Trainings will be conducted with site-planning and implementation partners using conflict sensitive natural resource management methods developed by FAO, with the aim to make sure all plantations do-no-harm.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

The climate and topography of Cox’s Bazar, being located on the Bay of Bengal, creates an environment where communities are exposed to multiple natural hazards and experience extreme and recurring weather events including cyclones, landslides and flash floods. Hazards that are exaggerated in frequency and magnitude by on-going climate change and environmental degradation, putting further pressure on the overall socio-ecological system. For instance, the expansion of agricultural land, illegal logging and cutting of hills for settlements has caused deforestation, soil erosion and stream siltation, and it is increasing the risk of hazards. Between 2010 and 2020 Cox’s Bazar lost almost 500 hectares of tree cover, equivalent to over 250 kilotons of CO₂ emissions, and the total area of humid primary forest decreased by 30 percent (Global Forest Watch, 2021, https://www.globalforestwatch.org/). In response to the Rohingya refugee influx, the Government of Bangladesh allocated over 2 500 hectares of protected forest land for the construction of shelters. The need for cooking fuel, from both host communities and Rohingya refugees, expanded the area of forest degradation (at different scales) to over 7 000 hectares (Mahamud, R. et, al. 2018. Technical specification for land stabilization and greening the camp in Cox’s Bazar south forest division. Bangladesh). Studies indicate that the extraction of forest resources increased the demand-supply gaps of biomass by 10 000 tons per year (IOM and FAO 2017. Assessment of fuel wood supply and demand in displacement settings and surrounding areas in Cox’s Bazaar District). Under SAFEPLUS1, a total area of 500 hectares was taking under restoration plantations. One of the main difficulties for restoration inside the camps was due to limitations of available land for plantation, why there is an important need to coordinate and plan together with site-planning and management for further restoration activities. According to a recent study from Shelter/NFI sector (2022), 40% of shelters were not on safe sites from soil erosion and landslides, where Camp 20 (27%) had the lowest proportion of shelters located on safe sites. Site safety from soil erosion and landslides was measured on the basis of whether the slopes along shelters were protected (by terracing, bamboo/sand bag retaining walls, planting stabilization and/or drainage to prevent erosion). The study indicates the shelters by camps which provides an overview of the camp-wise vulnerability. In addition to that study landslide susceptibility maps have been produced for camp area where vulnerable areas have been identified based on digital elevation model, which also indicate vulnerability in almost all camps (Landslide susceptibility map, NATHAZ, 2022). The piloted landslide early warning system developed by FAO is an online based model where warning is triggered by rainfall and risk classification from landslide susceptibility map verified by field inventory and demographic data. During the latest cyclone that hit Bangladesh, Sitrang, a total of 7 slope failures were reported. The cyclone also damaged 73 learning facilities due to heavy rain, winds and landslides in 15 camps (11 in Ukhiya and 4 in Teknaf). This indicates the urgent need to better understand landslide dynamics in Camp context, to be able to increase preparedness and awareness to reduce risk both from impact (i.e. biological land stabilization), and capacity building. According to the ISCG guidance note on mainstreaming AAP (2022), there is a strong need for strengthening community participation and engagement in decision-making, in the camps. In addition pressure on natural resources in the congested camps creates an environment where trade-offs are inevitable and conflicts over resources such as land and water, arise. This leaves an urgent need for conflict sensitive programming, building trust and enabling the weakest stakeholders.

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

FAO is a specialized agency of the UN supporting member states to obtain sustainable food production, food security, nutrition, and DRR and environmental restoration. In Bangladesh, FAO has been has operated since 1972. In Cox’s Bazar FAO is jointly leading the technical part of the FSS sector and EETWG with other UN agencies since 2017. According to the strategy of the FSS sector and EETWG, FAO is ensuring knowledge-based interventions to restore the fragile socio-ecological systems in collaboration with all key stakeholders (community, development banks, donors, government, private sectors, research institutions, sectors, cooperating partners, and so on). FAO’s intervention includes technical guidance and capacity strengthening, synergies, monitoring, research, advocacy and communication, and mainstreaming climate actions in environmental restoration, DRR, and livelihoods. As per the current setup of sector interventions, EETWG will play an advisory role amongst all sectors e.g. - SMSD, WASH, SHELTER/NFI, and FAO will play an active advisory role by supporting these sectors with all above-mentioned interventions and coordination framework. Under the JRP 2023-2025 of SMSD, FAO has identified much-needed natural resources management and DRR activities, which will compliment ongoing activities and provide technical backstopping. FAO has submitted two JRP proposals, one for FSS, where on-farm activities for the refugee camps are included, and both on-farm and off-farm activities are included for host communities. The other one is this proposal for SMSD, where off-farm, i.e. natural resource management and disaster risk reduction activities are proposed. FAO Cox’s Bazar is working with a programmatic approach, meaning that we use a landscape perspective working for socio-ecological restoration. This allows us build synergize between projects, sectors and stakeholders.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection

FAO integrates “Do No Harm” approach in designing, developing, implementing and monitoring programme interventions in Cox’s Bazar to ensure that all activities target and benefit the most vulnerable individuals, enhancing safety and dignity. In addition, FAO follows PSEA guideline and internal referral pathway to report sexual exploitation and abuse and other forms of sexual violence. Also ensuring gender, age, and disabilities are taken into account and acknowledging the fact that climate change and natural disasters will affect different societal groups differently. All targeted beneficiaries are systematically registered, where analysis of household resilience including gender, age and access to productive assets (using the FAO RIMA tool) will inform the intervention design. For example, landslide early warning, search and rescue, anticipatory action and preparedness, age will play an important role in an individual’s vulnerability, and the possibility of evacuation.

3.2 - Gender

The project will strive to provide equal opportunities for women and men to be part of project activities and the 40 percent quota will be introduced for women to ensure gender parity. The female-headed households for Rohingya people will be prioritized in selecting targeted households. Field workers will be trained and regularly apprised to be more responsive to female needs. This is done practically through training for field staff in conflict sensitive programming that provides methods for assessing and addressing power dynamics in interventions.

3.3 - Age including child safeguarding

Children who come into contact with FAO as a result of the project or programme activities must be safeguarded to the maximum possible extent from deliberate or inadvertent actions and failings that place them at risk of child abuse, sexual exploitation, injury and any other harm. FAO has zero tolerance for such behavior and pursues rigorous policies to prevent and respond to these issues.

3.4 - Diversity

The project strictly follows FAO Policy on Indigenous and tribal peoples. In the project activities this is integrated through conflict sensative programing, again acknowledging and assessing power dynamics ans strengthening weaker stakeholders. Gender-diverse groups, LGBTQI, marginalized ethnic and religious groups are acknowledged following the above mentioned policy, strictly respecting all groups.

3.5 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

FAO enforces a policy of AAP, which is aligned with the ISCG AAP guidance. Regarding “Participation, Engagement, and Inclusion and Information, Communication, and Transparency” project design follows a participatory approach and training for field staff in conflict sensitive programming, provides methods for assessing and addressing power dynamics in interventions, including acknowledging the personal interest of field staff. Regarding Complaint and Feedback Response a dedicated hotline number is set up where the beneficiary can directly send complaints and feedback to FAO. The implementing partner will sensitize the beneficiary about the AAP and provide the hotline number, when delivering services at the field level/conducting trainings. Complaints through the hotline number will immediately go to FAO MEAL team for investigation to verify the complaint and report. The investigation and reporting will be done by maintaining a high level of confidentiality.
