Project Summary

The goal of this project is to reduce protection risks and vulnerability for persons living in Northwest Syria through the provision of integrated services following protection-centered and community-based approaches. The project aims to improve the protection environment in Northwest Syria for at-risk populations through 1) results-oriented protection monitoring and inter-agency taskforce coordination that promotes continued analysis of protection risks for cluster-level advocacy and response as well as inform humanitarian actors’ protection mainstreaming; 2) provision of critical information that enables displaced populations to obtain referrals for services. 3) specialized Psycho social support for men and other vulnerable groups

4 - Needs

As the Syrian conflict heads into its eighth year, 13.1 million people are in need of humanitarian assistance, with over 5.6 million living in acute need. Individuals in Syria face increased protection risks in their daily lives while the capacity to positively cope with these risks continues to be stretched. Indiscriminate and targeted attacks on civilian areas and facilities, grave rights violations, denial of humanitarian access, and violations of international law persist. Violence at household (HH) and community levels continue, and vulnerabilities based on gender, age, disability and other factors often intersect. Humanitarian actors remotely managing emergency programming in conflict-affected northern Syria face significant access challenges, resulting in unclear understanding of contextual protection issues. IRC protection monitoring, both through regular monitoring and rapid protection assessments in emergencies, can provide timely and accurate information about protection risks and concerns to generate actionable recommendations for humanitarian stakeholders. In NWS, in addition to the HNO data, the three protection field teams collect both qualitative and quantitative data which informs the PMTF bi-monthly protection needs analyses, in conjunction with UNHCR data. IRC uses a mixed methodology to select participants for household (HH)-level assessments with IRC’s protection monitors visiting every fifth house within the location of focus and IRC assessors conducting visits where recommended, based on vulnerability, by members of the community (i.e. utilizing the “snowball” methodology). IRC’s protection needs assessors also conduct Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) and KIIs to gather additional qualitative information on key thematic issues. Due to the limited sample size, data gathered cannot be considered statistically representative, but is meant to highlight some of the main protection issues affecting the population in surveyed areas in NWS.

5 - Implementation of activities

As the Syrian conflict heads into its tenth year, 13.1 million people are in need of humanitarian assistance, with over 5.6 million living in acute need. Individuals in Syria face increased protection risks in their daily lives while the capacity to positively cope with these risks continues to be stretched. Indiscriminate and targeted attacks on civilian areas and facilities, grave rights violations, denial of humanitarian access, and violations of international law persist. Violence at household (HH) and community levels continue, and vulnerabilities based on gender, age, disability and other factors often intersect. Humanitarian actors remotely managing emergency programming in conflict-affected northern Syria face significant access challenges, resulting in unclear understanding of contextual protection issues. The 2019 HNO, PMTF and IRC’s PM reports show violations of international humanitarian law are widespread. Forced and multiple displacements, family separation and the loss/lack of documentation result in negative coping strategies, weakened support structures, and a high risk protection environment. In 48% of communities assessed (HNO), at least four protection issues were reported. Reliance on humanitarian assistance remains a significant coping mechanism (45% of assessed communities), indicating efforts are needed to deliver humanitarian assistance in an inclusive way. IRC’s RNA (May 2019, Idleb) showed surveyed HHs on average had been displaced five times since the start of the conflict; 16.4% of the HHs surveyed reported they had been displaced 10 or more times.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

Protection is a core objective of all humanitarian work in crisis affected areas. Given that affected populations heavily depend on humanitarian services for survival, it is crucial that services be delivered in a safe and dignified manner. Due to access constraints in Syria, many humanitarian actors provide remote service delivery from Turkey and Jordan. It requires actors to carefully consider protection-specific needs and how their humanitarian interventions do not cause harm to beneficiaries, including the prevention of exploitation and abuse. Services will be open to Syrian locals, IDPs, and returnees, but will be targeted based on need and vulnerability, prioritizing women, children, PWD, and the elderly. The PM team will collect data directly from vulnerable communities to allow IRC to understand beneficiary needs, gaps in service provision, and context specific protection risks related to aid provision, deliberately ensuring that all vulnerable groups can access services. The Humanitarian Access Unit (HAU), work closely with program leads and field management to identify risks and trends that may impact IRC to manage, mitigate and prepare contingency plans. The Syria country program developed a Security Management Plan (SMP) and Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) for northwest Syria to guide IRC operations and staff, to enable them to provide lifesaving assistance using the safest methods for IRC staff and beneficiaries. The HAU, keep monitoring the conflict situation and provide analysis and scenarios for potential risks to inform the contingency planning through Bi-weekly situation updates, quarterly humanitarian access updates and the daily security situation reports including one-on-one meetings for specific risks management. These practices give the IRC the ability to ensure the safety and security of staff, beneficiaries and assets including the IRC obligation in regard to duty of care and staff welfare.
