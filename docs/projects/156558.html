Project Summary

Food insecurity is affecting an estimated 6.5 million Syrians and an additional 2.5 million people are at risk of food insecurity. The number of negative Coping strategies adopted by the population are increasing – including reducing quality of food consumed and number of daily meals; more extreme coping strategies include taking sending children to work, early marriage of girls and transactional sex.
The project adheres to the Sector Objective 1 by implementing two activities: (1) Newly affected people who have immediate food needs for 1 week will receive a minimum of 2100 kcal per person per day of ready to eat food in the form of Ready to Eat Rations (target of 3,500 households); (2) The most food insecure IDPs and returnees will receive a minimum of 1,600 Kcal per person per day as a monthly food ration for the family (target of 3,500 households). The amount of humanitarian food assistance provided will adhere to sector recommendations. Activities will also be implemented aligned with the Sector Objective 2 aim to reduce host communities’ dependence on food aid. Distribution of emergency seeds (vegetable, wheat and barley; cultivated in home-gardens, greenhouses or open fields), tools and water delivery inputs, together with technical capacity building will be implemented to improve the household food security status. The distributed vegetable seeds can be cultivated in three growing seasons (late summer, winter and spring) to allow households to have access to year-round harvested vegetables. The seed wheat will allow harvesting of wheat to produce flour; and the harvested seed barley to be used as a fodder resource for livestock farmers. The activities implemented under this objective include: (1) Distribution of agricultural inputs, such as seeds, fertiliser, pesticide and equipment, and related training (target of 3,500 households); (2) Support to asset building and asset protection (small livestock and animal feed distribution) including winterisation activities, and related training (target of 3,500 households); and (3) Emergency livestock treatment and training for veterinary services (including community animal health worker) (target 100 para-vets). The Action adheres to the sector defined indicators.
Protection issues will be addressed in all activities through identification of the risk – for example, relating to beneficiary selection, inclusion of women in selection processes, feedback mechanisms, distribution processes, potential tension between male and female beneficiaries, and diversion of aid. Mitigation measures have been given for each identified risk – with particular focus on inclusion of women at all stages of the humanitarian action. Gender receives particular attention with inclusion of women at the project design (needs analysis), design of the humanitarian assistance, participation and monitoring phases.

Needs

There are 13.1 million people requiring humanitarian assistance, including 1,7 million people in hard-to-reach areas, where they are exposed to grave protection threats. 5.2 million are facing acute need. Food insecurity is affecting an estimated 6.5 million Syrians and an additional 2.5 million people are at risk of food insecurity. The main drivers to food insecurity are prolonged displacements, loss and lack of livelihoods and reduced production capacity which in turn results in limited physical and financial access to food, high prices and inflation contributing to reduced purchasing power and continuous depletion of livelihoods assets of the most vulnerable populations. 40% of Syrians spend more than 65% of their expenditure on food; 57.2% of food insecure households reported to have outstanding debt and 65% of food insecure people are adopting crisis or emergency negative coping strategies . 2018 witnessed the lowest wheat and barley production since 1989, with only 30% of production compared to pre-conflict averages (4.1 million mt/year pre-conflict vs 1.2 million mt/year in 2018 ). The destruction, damage and lack of maintenance to irrigation infrastructures amplified the impact of the erratic rainfall on crop conditions and performance. Furthermore, agricultural production continued to be seriously hampered by insecurity that constrains access to fields, and expensive and insufficiently available inputs (fuel, seeds, and fertilizers). In Hassakeh traditionally contributing between 50 to 75% of national wheat production, the low wheat crop production could contribute to shortages of bread as well as price increases. This could have national implications on food consumption scores and could lead to increased recourse to coping strategies. Overall, HHs are resorting to harmful coping strategies that disproportionately affect children, girls, women and young adults: cutting back food consumption, spending savings and accumulating debt; child labor, early marriage for girls and engagement with armed groups for boys as young as 12. AAH has been assessing several communities in Rural Damascus and Dar’a where agriculture is the main source of income of the population, but production has reduced significantly, with constrained access and availability of food in the market. Women interviewed reported having issue to breastfeed due to poor food diet during and after the pregnancy, with most of the women depending only on powder milk to feed their children.Livestock production levels have fallen dramatically, indicated by a 45% reduction in the number of sheep, 30% reduction for goats and 40% reduction for cattle when comparing the 2010 MAAR livestock census with current figures. The main contributary challenge is the high cost of fodder and insufficient coverage by veterinary services. This reduction in livestock assets has significantly affected livelihoods leading to implementation of irreversible coping strategies.

Implementation of activities

Food aid will be ideally distributed in the first days of the displacement as a one-off action. The main target group for the provision of supplementary food will be recently displaced households and returnees. Because they have left behind their belongings, livelihoods and sources of income, and having little savings or other but limited forms of portable wealth, they are not able to meet their basic needs in terms of food intake. AAH will respond to the needs of recently affected IDPs and returnees through two appropriate modalities depending on vulnerability criteria and contextual needs:(1) Newly affected people who have immediate food needs for 1 week will receive a minimum of 2100 kcal per person per day of ready to eat food in the form of Ready to Eat Rations. (2) The most food insecure IDPs and returnees will receive a minimum of 1,600 Kcal per person per day as a monthly food ration for the family. The Ready to Eat Rations or food parcels will be delivered by SARC and/or other local partners, and complemented with targeted nutritional support for specific groups, starting with pregnant and lactating women. To reduce host communities’ dependence on food aid, they will be specifically targeted with activities to restore the means to produce home-grown food of adequate nutritional value. Distribution of emergency seeds (vegetable, wheat and barley; cultivated in home-gardens, greenhouses or open fields), tools and water delivery inputs, together with technical capacity building will be implemented to improve the household food security status. Gaps in accessing food will be mitigated by providing basic vegetable production kits depending on the three cultivation seasons (late summer, winter and spring) ensuring vegetables produced by beneficiaries are available throughout the year; home gardens or greenhouses will produce food of high nutritional value sufficient to meet the nutritional demands of households, and to protect and rebuild their livelihoods. AAH will provide certified and drought resistant seed wheat to ensure vulnerable households have a self-produced source of wheat flour for baking bread; and seed barley, from which the harvested grain will support vulnerable households to maintain flock numbers. The seed wheat and barley will be procured and distributed to beneficiaries in July/August 2019 to be harvested in June/July 2020. The harvested barley will ensure a self-produced nutritional resource, thus obviating the need to purchase expensive (and sometime unavailable) fodder from outside suppliers. The harvested wheat will ensure a self-produced source of wheat flour for baking bread. The inputs will be procured from the General Organization for Seeds Multiplication (GOSM) to ensure locally sourced and successfully tested. This response will target the most vulnerable households, which have safe access to a backyard garden area 200 to 500 square meters for home gardens activities; or a separate group of vulnerable households who have access to 10 dunums (1 hectare) of cultivated land for seed wheat and barley activities. 80% of livestock production costs is attributable to purchase of the livestock ration. In an emergency context, distribution of fodder (available from the General Organisation for Fodder <GoF>), will ensure livestock farmers will be able to maintain breeding animals in the flock thus avoid the negative coping strategy of disposing of their prime producing livestock asset. Fodder distribution will be complemented with self-production of barley as a source of feed as described above. Linked with improvements in livestock rations, provision of veterinary care through training of para-vets will address the lack of veterinary expertise required by livestock farmer for emergency events, and access to prophylactic vaccines and routine visits.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risk 1: The security situation in targeted areas is unstable and AAH has no access to implement the activities; there are additional unforeseen administrative procedures imposed, and restrictions on access to beneficiaries. Likelihood: medium. Impact: highMitigation: AAH works and coordinates with authorities and Ministries to maintain access to target areas and beneficiaries, and the capacity to implement activities. AAH will reach out to local authorities and line ministries with trust building activities to further develop relationships and capacity to avoid or mitigate restrictions in AAH capacity to operate in Northern and Southern Syria. Advocacy and coordination with the humanitarian community and humanitarian diplomacy activities on the local level are also considered to contribute to this mitigation measure. Risk 2: Substantive devaluation of the EURO or USD against the Syrian pound; International bank transfers to Syria are fluid and timely.Likelihood: medium. Impact: highMitigation: The eventuality of significant changes in the value of the Syrian currency will be closely monitored and, if materialized, timely communicated to donors and options discussed. Risk 3: Aid diversion, fraud, and corruptionLikelihood: medium. Impact: highMitigation: diversification of supplier, triangulation of information, standard monitoring procedures in place, security plan and risks assessments tables updated and followed. Risk 4: Information gathering activities hampered by suspicion or misunderstandingLikelihood: low. Impact: mediumMitigation: Transparency, trust-building, developing relationships with municipalities, district authorities and facilities managers
