Project Summary

Please provide a short summary of the intervention, describing its purpose and target
In Ninewa, and Kirkuk Governorate large scale destruction of infrastructures, looting of supplies and equipment, contamination of lands and large-scale displacements of population have led to mass disruption of livelihoods amongst IDP’s, returnees and non-displaced host community (FAO, 2017). Currently, an estimated 6.5 million people are in urgent need of humanitarian assistance, this includes displaced people in camps and out-of-camps locations, poor communities hosting large numbers of displaced people and returnees who are in very high and high severity hotspots. Out of these people, the HRP 2019 will target 1.75 million people with humanitarian assistance, including livelihood support for their survival (HNO/HRP, 2019).
Tearfund (TF) will provide emergency asset replacement, asset recovery and/or small grants in Tel Afar, and Hawiga. TF aim is to restore income generating activities to households who have been affected by conflict, including business mentoring and support. And to promote social cohesion through support of community savings groups for vulnerable families having difficulties in their return process.

Needs

In Ninewa and Kirkuk, displacement, collapsed markets, looting of supplies, stores and equipment and destruction of natural resources (e.g. mined/contaminated soil, destroyed water supply) have led to mass disruption of livelihoods. IDP, non-displaced and returnee Kirkuk, and Ninewa populations reported during cash programming expending all savings whilst under ISIS control, selling or losing productive assets and using negative coping mechanisms to survive. Across crisis affected areas, the lack of safe and lucrative livelihoods opportunities increases economic dependence on external sources, reducing households (HHs) ability to meet their basic needs. Ninewa governorate: In Ninewa, there is a mix of (semi-)urban (public sector, local market places, small scale business) and rural (predominantly agricultural and daily labour) livelihoods. Rapid Needs Assessment (RNA) in Tel Afar (March, 2018) show that 95% of the HHs have outstanding loans or debts with friends or family that are on average far greater than their current savings or income; 62% of the HHs in Tel Afar share concerns of people in the community not having enough income, money or resources to live, only 12% of assessed households capable to access previous livelihoods. Income generation is ranked among the top priorities for all HHs across Ninewa; unemployment among HHs is high, for males 61% in Tel Afar and fewer female HH members are looking for employment respectively 20% and 17%.Most markets are functioning in Ninewa and basic goods are again available after the markets collapsed or closed during the crisis; the majority of HHs are depending on humanitarian aid to survive. Consequently, HHs are not able to regularly contribute to the market economy as nobody has the capital to invest, and markets are far below the potential they had before the crises. FDGs and KIIs in similar contest like Bashiqa indicate that access to social networks is the most valuable and important asset to revive businesses and help restore livelihood opportunities. When asked to identify the greatest obstacle, difficulty in finding financial resources is identified as most problematic. Banks, micro-finance institutions or other formal institutions to provide loans were very few to non-existent. Following local culture and traditions, households (HHs) instead relied on family and friends who were willing to provide financial capital to build livelihoods or to help overcome shocks during crises. Kirkuk governorate: Kirkuk Prior to ISIS control, 98% of HHs had their capacity to meet their basic needs, dropping to just 7% under ISIS control as 84% did not have any earnings during this time (HI Socio-Economic KAP survey, August 2017). TF FGD conducted in May 2018 in 2 small market towns in Hawiga district indicate that prior to ISIS, over 90% of people had a livelihood pre-crisis (farming, government employment, retail or casual labour), dropping to 10-25% post-crisis. Average income has dropped from 3-5 million IQD a month to 130,000-150,000 IQD; a thirty-fold reduction. People expressed a strong desire for livelihoods, particularly young people, in order to bring in much needed revenue. The main barrier was consistently reported as cash, due to lack of capital to repurchase damaged or sold assets. Other barriers mentioned were lack of electricity and the difficulty in obtaining equipment; both financial and to transport it through checkpoints. With the lack of income, people are dependent on relatives for either aid or loans; with over half the community reportedly reliant on relatives. Hawiga town market has started to revive in the past few months, but many pre-crisis small businesses owners have been unable to restart their businesses due to lack of liquidity.

Activities

Activity 1: Asset replacement grants in cash and/or in kind: Rapid market- and community capacity assessments are planned to update existing labour market analysis done in 2018, as it’s a rapid evolving context. The aim is to identify key obstacles to restarting livelihood with a focus on most vulnerable groups like female headed households and/or minority groups; most suitable business recovery sectors (avoidance of exaggerating local markets) 299 business grants (of which 79 business grants by TF as implementing partner for Dorcas Aid International) for vulnerable households interested to (re)start their business are planned through provision of grants and/or asset replacement. A high focus will be put on relative young beneficiaries in the age of 18-40 years old. Potential candidates are interviewed on their entrepreneurial abilities, motivation and vulnerability to target most promising and needy families. Expected business support in urban areas are in Tel Afar and Hawiga town. A public information campaign, feedback from Advisory Committees and beneficiary feedback mechanisms ensures transparency and equal chances. Successful candidates are invited to BDT and complete a business plan upon which the grant can be awarded. BDT topics include market analysis, marketing, logistics and budgeting to distinguish between business income generation vs. home consumption. An average grant of 2,000 USD and max. 3,000 USD is provided in cash and/or equal value of productive assets. Beneficiaries are expected to investment up to 15% of the grant value in cash or in-kind (however in some fragile communities this % may be lower) the remaining 85% will be matched by TF. Beneficiaries sign a grant agreement with instalment plan upon which the grant will be released to enable monitoring and provide technical support. Technical support will be provided to all business grant holders over a time span of 4-8 weeks as part of the business start-up time. Technical support is accomplished through facilitation of voluntary training; these will be based on gaps in knowledge as identified through monitoring by project staff, e.g. finance/debts management, sourcing suppliers, mental health awareness sessions to cope with stress of running a business. Specific agricultural support can take place through technical training service providers or partnering with other agencies. To promote learning, community members with experience and willingness to participate will be encouraged (voluntarily) to share knowledge and experiences.

Protection Risk Analysis

Health and security considerations: Safety, health and security in the workplace is important to running a business, and for those involved in CfW/apprenticeships and project staff. Specific identified risks are contaminated agricultural land, unexploded ordinances (UXOs) or remnants of war that might cause harm and can also be found in collapsed structures or rubble in case of CfW cleaning up actions. Gender and age considerations: Though legally there are no restrictions for women to work, cultural and religious boundaries have limited their income generating options. 39% of respondents in Bashiqa mentioned social and cultural norms as obstructing women’s access to the labour market (NRC, 2018). Based on TF livelihood applications in Bashiqa, woman activities are livestock rearing (e.g. milk from cows), seasonal wage labour (e.g. olive picking) and home-based bakeries and tailoring/selling clothes for woman and children. This information was confirmed by triangulation in Kirkuk assessments as opportunities for female employment in Hawiga. Home-based businesses are more difficult to run explained by a need to balance roles of caretaker and income generator, this is especially true for female-headed households. Female-headed households are being recognized as highly vulnerable and between 10-25% of the households in Ninewa Plains are assessed to be female headed (REACH, MCNA, 2018). Risk of (S)GBV and mental health disorders: The long-term issue of GBV in Iraq has been exacerbated by the conflict. Mental health conditions as a result of conflict and GBV continues to be a significant and urgent need in Ninewa and Kirkuk. The World Health Organization (2015) estimates that after an acute onset of major emergency, on average about 1 in 6 people will suffer a mild to moderate mental disorder and about 1 in 30 people will have a severe mental disorder. For Iraq, prevalence of mental health disorders remains high while service provision remains low. One known barrier to seek assistance is lack of income generation and lack of opportunities causes stress in families. In relation to the proposed project activities, anecdotally, for female relatives of male participations in whose household’s income generation is a stress that triggers GBV, when opportunities are provided the risk of GBV can be reduced. However, providing cash and income opportunities to female participants in the project may also make them targets. Risk of social exclusion and/or conflict: Previously-displaced persons may be more vulnerable in terms of needs, but those that did not flee may face increased vulnerable situation as a result of perceived connection with the suspected perpetrators. These issues can influence project activities when selecting beneficiaries for projects. In addition, vulnerable groups may not have social integration and acceptance in the community to apply for the project at all, e.g. IDPs in host communities. As previously mentioned, the project target locations are very diverse with different ethnic and religious groups, balance among these communities in proportion and size may have shifted significantly due to displacement or/and impact of the conflict. Negative drivers of the conflict, favouritism in business practices and employment are expected issues that can as well interfere with the project selection of beneficiaries. Risk of fraud/extortion/looting: Distribution of cash or cash equivalents always includes risks of financial corruption such as fraud, bribery, extortion and theft. Another risk related to project activities is markets becoming inaccessible and out of stock of essential items, fraudulent activities are being carried out by Hawala or Zain agents, community or project staff or even attract attacks on distribution sites or increase insecurity in and around distributions.

Mitigation Measures

Health and safety: The project will adhere to agencies/government regulations. Preventive precautions will be taken; e.g. provision of uniforms, hand gloves, protecting boots as needed, security training and orientation for post-conflict destruction removal, or in cleaning campaigns. Provision of first aid kits and health referral pathways. Coordination with closest security actors and demining agencies is sought to prevent unnecessary risks. First aid kits will be present on-site and training venues will be assessed for suitability like sufficient lighting, water and seating places, safe access routes, separate restrooms etc. In the CfW/apprenticeships salary a standard 10% is added for health insurance; and business grant holders are trained to include insurances as well. Gender & age: Proportionate participation by men and women will be aimed for in staff recruitment, FDGs, beneficiary selection and through female feedback groups to advise how to increase female participation and a gender sensitive approach. To the extent local cultural practices allow, examples of women businesses are displayed to increase community acceptance. The minimum requirement for grants distribution is 18 years old, a session on Child Rights will be provided for CfW/apprenticeships as needed; Iraqi law allows children to work at 15 years old for a few hours a day as long as it doesn’t interfere with school, however this information is not always known within families. Within the business grant agreements, a protection clause is signed by grant holders to provide safe work, fair wages, no child labour, anti-trafficking and against the exploitation/harassment of women in the workplace. GBV and mental health: The project ensures reduction of GBV related risks for female participants as a result of participation in the project. Awareness sessions will be included in training, e.g. mental health awareness, human rights, benefits of female employment and GBV. In CfW/apprenticeships beneficiaries that are transiting to/from training or to receive cash are monitored closely and feedback to project staff is available. Consistent with safeguarding policy for vulnerable adults and children, project staff will be trained on PSEA (Protection Against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse), proper referral mechanisms, including designated protection focal points to facilitate referrals. Social exclusion and/or conflict: FDGs with identified minority groups are planned to identify the communities’ definition of wealth groups and changed power relations after the crisis and displacement. Hence possible risks of conflict mapping will be done in advance (including possible friction between IDPs, returnees and vulnerable non-displaced host community) to avoid aggravation of conflict and/or social exclusion. Community decision makers and gate keepers are informed on humanitarian standards and impartiality. Fraud/extortion/looting: Markets will be monitored for fluctuations. Large provision of cash will be kept limited and confidential. Only limited number of senior staff members will have access to all beneficiary details, these lists will never be shared with third parties without data protection policies in place. Mobile money transfer services can be used to decrease visibility of transactions, avoid need to carry a lot of money and/or store it at home. Spot checks will be conducted by the MEAL team and beneficiary accountability officer. Misconduct and whistle-blowing: All staff follow a mandatory (online) course including fraud policy, but also covering Safeguarding, Code of Conduct, Conflict of Interest, Anti Bribery and Corruption etc. These policies will be posted in every public space, at every training for transparency. TF has whistle-blowing mechanisms that can be used by staff to report any behaviours or actions by other staff members. All staff complete training to understand the Whistle-blowing structure and process for their organization.

How is your project accountable to affected population

Understanding the local context: In the initial assessment and analysis of community’s needs, FGDs are carried out with women, youth and different ethnic/religious groups to get a better understanding of their vulnerabilities and abilities. Key Informant Interviews are held with existing business owners and agricultural entrepreneurs to better understand the impact of the crisis on their activities and their ability to meet basic needs for their families and to identify bottlenecks for successful re-activation of livelihoods. Community-based advisory committees: Based on the SOP’s for business incubation and CfW, the communities will be asked to form an Advisory Committees to provide feedback on vulnerability criteria, advise on a transparent selection process and coordinate announcement of the final results of the business application / CfW selection process. The Advisory Committee remains active to support in community liaison and provide valuable feedback to the project teams in addition to existing beneficiary accountability mechanisms. Roles and responsibilities of the Advisory Committees are outlined in an agreement with the organisation including specific timings of meetings, responsibilities and mutual expectations. Public information campaign: In any registration process of the project; application for grants/asset replacement, CfW activities and/or free ongoing trainings, information will be decimated through Advisory Committees with agreed messages. Moreover, posters, leaflets and targeted text messages through mobile phones will be used of means to inform communities about upcoming activities, locations, times, etc. Registration and feedback boxes will be put down at project sites and, if needed, Q&A sessions will be arranged where project staff will be available to respond to any questions or concerns that may arise during the implementation of the project. Last but not least, a feedback hotline is established and portrayed in all project locations which is accessible five days per week. SOP will be established for the feedback received by TF and it will outline the anonymity (if possible) and confidentiality of information received. It also outlines the information-flow from moment of receipt, to internal analysis, feedback to the sender and/or action taken as response. The hotline is hosted by staff not directly involved in the day-to-day running of the project and the hotline “responder” will be trained on the principles of anonymity and confidentiality. Beneficiary accountability officer: A dedicated beneficiary accountability officer will carry out spot checks and provide input to the project teams to ensure inclusion of beneficiary feedback into project design details and decisions. Beneficiary communities and individuals can contact the free hotline number to report issues and complaints related to the project activities; with each call reviewed and responded with recommendations to improve the project systems or to indicate any action to be taken. Monitoring Evaluation Accountability and Learning (MEAL): As part of the project cycle the teams will be supported by a system of monitoring, evaluation, accountability and learning through the MEAL team who operates independently to the project team. The MEAL team will take responsibility to manage beneficiary database to cross-check transparency and accountability of vulnerability assessments and registration process. The MEAL team will facilitate a midterm lessons learned workshop will be conducted to reflect and learn from the first project cycle. Relevant findings, learnings and/or success cases of will be shared with communities. Additionally, an external evaluation will be conducted in the final month of the project to assess the impact of the project.
