Project Summary

Children represent 54 per cent of the Rohingya refugee population in Cox’s Bazar (CXB) District (435, 679 children according to UNHCR-RRRC Family Counting data). Of these, 266,416 have been identified by the as children in need of immediate assistance by the Child Protection Sub-Sector.
The protection environment has become more complex with deteriorating security environment, which in 2019 particularly affected women’s and girls’ safe access to services, and conflicts with host communities. Based on UNHCR August 2019 settlement profiles, risk related to kidnapping, trafficking and violence were the three most perceived risks after natural hazards. UASC are at heightened risk of child trafficking, abuse and exploitation. Girls are of particular concern to protection actors as girls as disproportionately vulnerable to abuse, violence, sexual exploitation, and neglect. Boys have limited access to GBV services and have been found to be particularly vulnerable to child labour including worst forms of child labour. Other known prevalent risk factors for boys and girls include child marriage, child pregnancy and motherhood, domestic violence, as well as challenges to access to services for children with disabilities.
The project will be implemented through partnerships with established child protection agencies, which will improve access to quality child protection services through case management, Multipurpose Children and Adolescent Hubs and community based child protection structures. In line with the CPSS strategy of putting the community at the centre of the response, focus will be placed on scaling up and strengthening community child protection mechanisms such as community volunteers involved in prevention and response activities. Further engagement with influential community leaders as well as parents/ caregivers will be done to enhance community participation, ownership and sustainability of the programmes.
Specialised services will be provided for children at a higher risk of abuse, exploitation, neglect and violence taking into account Age, Gender and Diversity. Through targeted activities in existing safe spaces, the resilience and development of boys and girls especially adolescents will be strengthened; and specialized psychosocial support will be given to those in need, including psychologically distressed children. Community and child participation is key to protection of children and this project will provide capacity building through trainings and coaching to identified child protection structures as well as other stakeholders. Appropriate child friendly feedback mechanisms will be utilised throughout the project cycle in line with AAP principles.
All activities will be coordinated through the identified child protection focal point agency in each camp and UNHCR’s active participation in and contributions to the Child Protection Sub-sector and Protection Sector coordination mechanisms and initiatives; and will be implemented with a multi-sectoral approach to ensure mainstreaming of child protection in all areas of the humanitarian response.
Some of the outcomes of the project include, but not limited to:
1. Increase in number of adolescents who participate in targeted programmes.
2. Reduction in physical violence or psychological aggression experienced by children in homes, safe spaces and community.
3. Girls and boys protected against harmful practices
4. Children have safe access to community spaces for socialising, play, learning, etc
5. Children have non-discriminatory access to national child protection and social services
6. Children with specific needs receive Age, Gender and Diversity appropriate services.

Needs

Children represent 54 per cent of the Rohingya refugee population in Cox’s Bazar (CXB) District (435, 679 children according to UNHCR-RRRC Family Counting data). Of these, 266,416 have been identified by the as children in need of immediate assistance by the Child Protection Sub-Sector. The protection environment has become more complex with deteriorating security environment, which in 2019 particularly affected women’s and girls’ safe access to services, and conflicts with host communities. Based on UNHCR August 2019 settlement profiles, risk related to kidnapping, trafficking and violence were the three most perceived risks after natural hazards. Persons with disabilities are also an underserved population. According to the 2019 MSNA, interviewed households reported that 10% of them had at least one family member requiring assistance to complete daily activities, and 27% of them unable to access services. During the UNHCR Child Friendly Space (CFS) assessment conducted in June, it was noted that participation of children with disabilities was very low, compounded by the fact that there were limited structured activities that integrated the needs of these children. Response to these children’s needs has mainly been through mobile services leading to further isolation of the children. In addition to dealing with the emotional distress of being separated from their primary caregivers, many UASC are at heightened risk of child trafficking, abuse and exploitation. Girls, who make the majority of UASC, are particularly vulnerable to abuse, violence, sexual exploitation, and neglect while boys are particularly at risk of labour exploitation. Other known prevalent risk factors for boys and girls include child marriage, child pregnancy, child marriage, domestic violence, physical and mental disability. Through community consultations, LGBTI children were also identified as a particularly vulnerable group having a high risk of exposure to violence and with a corresponding limited access to services. Due to their invisibility, accurate numbers are yet to be verified. According to the Multi-Sector Needs Assessment commissioned by UNHCR (Sep 2018), only a very small number of adolescents (e.g. just 3% of 15-17 year olds, of which girls were only 1%) were attending Child Friendly Spaces (CFS). With limited access to education and/or livelihood opportunities adolescent boys and girls are at a high risk of child marriage and child labour: many families expect girls to marry, and boys to start earning money once they turn 12-14 years of age. In August 2019, the MSNA child labour statistics showed that 3% of 5-17 year old children were engaged in child labour, with 51% of those exposed to the worst forms of child labour. With 14% of the population between the ages of 12-17, a lot more has to be done to deliver protection to adolescents and to positively engage them through participation, lifeskills, social cohesion and resilience-building. Advocacy with the Government to allow refugee children have access to national services in accordance with the national child policy and applicable laws ongoing.

Activities or outputs

In coordination with its partners and the CPSS, UNHCR’s child protection interventions will address violence, exploitation, abuse and neglect through both preventive and remedial actions in the best interests of the child. 1. The community is at the centre of UNHCR’s protection strategy. UNHCR and its partners will engage with parents, girls and boys as well as key community representatives to strengthen the protection environment of children and adolescents to reduce potential risks to children especially adolescent through outreach, parenting sessions and strengthening community participation in case management. By strengthening CBCPMs, the community will have ownership of CP protective structures which in turn will ensure their sustainability. 2. Specialised case management services, taking into account age, gender and diversity, will be provided for girls and boys at risk by supervision of appropriate alternative family or community-based for UASC; Best Interests Determination (BID) for complex cases; multi-sectoral services for GBV survivors and LGBTI.3. The capacity of social workers will continue to be built through training, mentoring and coaching. Local NGOs and governance structures will receive regular technical support in CP in line with agreed standards developed by the CPSS including the CPMS, IASC guidelines and UNHCR Framework for the Protection of Children. UNHCR will continue to build capacity of local NGOs and encourage partnerships between local and international NGOs.4. Multipurpose Children and Adolescent Hubs will enable children and adolescents to actively engage in age-appropriate recreational and psycho-social activities in a safe space facilitated by trained staff and with the close involvement of Community-Based Child Protection Committees and Child Clubs. Identified CP cases will be referred to specialized services. Information on gender equality/risk, life skills and reproductive health will be provided. Mobile services will continue so as to reach the most vulnerable and children with disability. 5. UNHCR and its partners will coordinate their activities through the interagency CP Focal Point system, and coordination at camp and CXB level, through the use of the existing referral pathways. The project also includes a multi-sectoral approach to response activities with other Sectors and UNHCR units to ensure CP mainstreaming.6. UNHCR has internal SOPs, office focal points and PSEA is embedded in every training provided to partners. PSEA and other complaints are received by UNHCR from the community and through partners and third parties. All UNHCR staff undertake a mandatory training on PSEA and staff, including partners, sign the UNHCR Code of Conduct. The Code of Conduct is displayed in the CFS’s and includes a referral mechanism for complaints.7. UNHCR and its partners will pilot CP socio-recreational activities as well as mainstreaming CP in activities implemented by UNHCR that are already operational in the host community. Partners will continue to collaborate with the Department of Social Services. Referral pathways for identified host community children at risk will be developed and strengthened in coordination with the CPSS. 8. Lost Child and Caregiver Meeting Points will continue to be supported. Facilities used by partners will continuously be evaluated to ensure that they are safe. Strengthened community structures will also work to prevent temporary separations in case of emergencies. UNHCR and its partners will continue ‘greening’ projects where children at the hubs plant and care for vegetation.9. UNHCR and its partners will continue to tap into community structures to respond to emergencies. To ensure risk mitigation for children in need, UNHCR and its partners will continue to support the CPSS in strengthening of emergency response plans.
