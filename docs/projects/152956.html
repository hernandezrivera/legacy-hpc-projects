Project Summary

This project addresses the urgent need for functional and physical rehabilitation and psychosocial support in South Sudan through a flexible response. Humanity & Inclusion understands that the needs in areas affected by the protracted crisis are alarming, but it also acknowledges that there are still unmet needs in protracted situations, which may grow more acute as the situation protracts. This project will, therefore, be a flexible intervention model to address these needs through both a static and mobile response. To that end the project is designed to target Jonglei, Unity, Central Equatoria, Eastern Equatoria, Western Equatoria, and Western Bahrel Ghazal in its interventions. HI already has a static presence (2016-2019) in the 3 Equatoria, frequent mobile missions in Unity and recent (2016) static presence in Jonglei.
The action targets the most vulnerable persons at risk of exclusion (including people with disabilities, people with injuries and chronic diseases, people experiencing psychosocial distress or mental health troubles, children, older persons), who are often invisible to mainstream relief organizations due to the specificity of their needs. By identifying specific vulnerabilities and providing targeted assistance to these vulnerable individuals through the deployment of a team of specialists providing rehabilitation and psychosocial support to the conflict-affected locations, the action will reduce vulnerability and will mitigate the impact of the conflict. It will do this by facilitating access to lifesaving services, improving functional capacities and independence, strengthening positive coping mechanisms, rebuilding social ties, promoting wellbeing, reducing the chances of psychological distress, and eventually improving the dignity of the targeted beneficiaries.
This project will contribute to the South Sudan HRP 2019 and the specific Health Cluster objectives through providing rehabilitation services to 13802 women, men, boys, and girls with disabilities and by providing mental health and psychosocial support to 20705 people in need of such services, including staff of humanitarian agencies who have gone through traumatizing incidences while on duty. Through identifying and assessing persons with disabilities for rehabilitation and mobility devices and services, this project will contribute to the Health Cluster objective 1, indicator 3 (and HRP objective 1) by ensuring that all persons, regardless of mobility challenges can reach lifesaving services as well as OPD services. Once assessed, persons in need of rehabilitation will have a treatment plan created for them and then implemented to include basic therapeutic activities, physio therapy, and occupational therapy. Caregivers will also be attended to as they are supported to care for their relation with a disability through basic disability education, device maintenance, and mental health care.
This project will also contribute to the Health Cluster objective 4, indicators 1 and 2 (and HRP objective 3) through providing access to mental health and psychosocial services to strengthen the resilience of communities and by training health care workers on providing MHPSS services to persons with disabilities, on basic MHPSS and PFA, and training them on how to work with survivors of SGBV. Though the mental health of all persons will be focused on in this project, a special consideration will be given to the mental health of persons with disabilities and their caregivers. This will happen through referrals to the PSS team by the rehabilitation team of those with disabilities who are provided with rehab services and in need of PSS services.Those in need of individual and family counseling will also receive that support. Additionally women SGBV survivors will receive special counseling sessions in safe houses and safe spaces and there will be collaboration with protection partners on mapping SGBV services and referrals.

Needs

Globally, of the more than 130 million people currently in need of humanitarian assistance, at least 15% of them are people with disabilities (WHO, Jan 2018). According to the Under-Secretary-General for Humanitarian Affairs and Emergency Relief Coordinator, Mark Lowcock, of the more than 30 million displaced people in 2017, a third of them were people with disabilities (Global Disability Summit, July 2018).In South Sudan, the lack of infrastructure and continuing conflict in 2017 had terrible consequences for the lives of the most vulnerable in South Sudan. Persons with disabilities, persons affected by long and chronic disease, persons suffering from mental health issues, and the elderly suffer from even greater distress. They faced greater risks of being caught in fighting, being often left behind by their families. And survivors face great challenges in getting necessary humanitarian assistance, their access to humanitarian services being limited due to their disability. (HRW, May 2017; HI/IOM Assessment of Bentiu POC Site, December 2017).This marginalization of persons with disabilities in South Sudan has continued into 2018 as is evidenced by continued reports of abuse and neglect from both the HI teams on the ground, as well as through media reports of persons with disabilities unable to access basic services and lack of knowledge for caregivers on how to take care of people with disabilities (AFP, May 2018; Juba Monitor, July 2018) HI already has a static presence (2016-2019) in the 3 Equatorias and Jonglei with frequent mobile missions in Unity. In Juba POC and other IDP sites, HI identified 656 (1% of the IDPs) new persons with specific needs in the first half of 2018. This is on top of the more than 2,471 persons with specific needs identified in the IDP sites in 2017. Also in the first half of 2018, in Bentiu collective sites, HIs Flying Team supported 1,547 IDPs with disabilities and their caregivers who were living in dire conditions and had limited access to services (FSL, WASH, NFI). HI teams remain overwhelmed by the number of persons with specific needs and persons living with disabilities affected by the humanitarian crisis. On top of this, as IDPs have few of their basic needs met, communities and social bonds are impacted by the conflict, and solidarity is difficult to maintain. These key factors, added to the fear of the conflict and overall terrible living conditions, contribute to a high level of stress. HI’s teams report critical levels of mental health issues in their interventions as MHPSS services remain limited throughout the country.MHPSS assessments conducted by HI in Mundri (July 2018) and Bentiu (September 2018) revealed high levels of stress and trauma among beneficiaries with a request for counselling and PSS group activities. As regards the psychological impact, the following main challenges were identified: beneficiaries (especially women) live with fear and overthink their situation leading to frustration and hopelessness over their life. Mobilizing early and adequate MHPSS can prevent distress and suffering from developing into something more severe. Every person has strengths and abilities to help them cope with life challenges. Some people are particularly vulnerable in a crisis situation and may need extra help. This includes people who may be at risk or need additional support because of their age or because they have a mental or physical disability, or because they belong to groups who may be marginalized. Mentally healthy people generally are able to react in positive ways to events, and to bounce back or take positive steps to improve their situation.HI activities in South Sudan aim at reducing vulnerabilities both by ensuring a better capacity to cope with the situation for persons with mental health issues, and by improving functional capacities of persons with disabilities in line with Health Cluster policy.

Activities

HIs work will fit within Cluster objectives 1 and 4 using elements of PHCC and Mobile team packages.Cluster Objective 1, Improve access and scale-up responsiveness to integrated essential healthcare needs of the vulnerable populations focusing on major causes of morbidity and mortality PHCCHI mobile responses in Unity State have also demonstrated a need to establish a static base there due to the sheer number of persons in need of rehabilitation services. Thus a static rehabilitation center will be established in Rubkona. Identification and assessment, followed by physiotherapy and occupational therapy care to persons with disabilities or to persons with temporary injuries/impairments or chronic illness will be provided. Assistive devices will be distributed to persons in need. Patients and caregivers will receive trainings on use and maintenance of devices and basic therapeutic exercises 3,802 IDPs will be supported. Psychosocial support and physical rehabilitation services will be provided to IDPs in stable sites where there will be a focus on persons with specific needs (people with disabilities, children at risk, unaccompanied or separated children, women at risk of SGBV, single parents, pregnant or lactating women, persons with serious medical conditions, elderly people) through tailor-made comprehensive PSS services to strengthen existing coping mechanisms. 10,352 (6550 PSS, 3802 rehab) IDPs will be supported through this activity. Mobile rehabilitationA rehabilitation team will be deployed following new crisis to implement emergency support to persons with disabilities and elderly people. Persons with disabilities will be identified and assessed by the team and a treatment plan will be proposed adequately to the persons’ needs. Basic therapeutic exercises, physiotherapy, occupational therapy sessions will be provided, as much as education sessions for caregivers. Mobility devices will be distributed and trainings on maintenance of these devices will be taught. Clients requiring further support will be referred to local clinical and rehabilitation actors. Awareness training sessions on disability related issues and the need for inclusion will be conducted, as much as trainings on basic rehabilitation procedures to health workers. 10,000 persons including IDPs and staff of humanitarian agencies will be reached through this activity. PSS teams will be deployed following new crisis to implement emergency activities to prevent and assess the development of mental health related issues. A particular attention will be given to the situation of persons with disabilities and persons with special needs who will be supported by/referred from the HI rehabilitation team. Following emergencies, the PSS mobile team will provide group activities in the communities to help reduce stress and strengthen coping mechanisms. Patients suffering from distress will receive individual counselling and families will receive sensitization on how to deal with mental health issues. 10253 persons are targeted for this activity. PHCC and MobileHI will support humanitarian workers to provide PSS services by providing capacity building on PFA and developing awareness on mental health issues. PSS through stress management sessions and individual counseling will be provided to humanitarian staff confronted by mental health related issues to help them deal with mental health cases, to help them develop their resilience and to help them prevent secondary traumatization. HI will train 3450 health staff workers to basics of MHPSS and PFA.PHCCPsychosocial support will also be provided to survivors of sexual and gender based violence, both from IDP and host communities as well as humanitarian agencies .3253 survivors of SGBV will be supported.

Does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

Yes

How does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

When there is an intervention in a particular location, HI works with partners to explain the assistance to be provided and then prepares beneficiaries and let them know of the team’s presence for service delivery. This is done by following the system set up by the CCCM. This is to first discuss with CCCM the activities and needs of HI in the area. The CCCM then connects HI to community representatives and leaders where they are also provided with this information. These representatives then take the messages to the community through the use of pictograms and other information. Community mobilizers are also used to assist the HI team in recording beneficiaries information and in doing crowd control at service access sites. Later HI’s community mobilizers are involved to deliver information about services available for the target population(clients). For example, awareness raising through focus group discussions and social media concerning issues like disability and the psychosocial wellbeing of a person in the family and community.We understand the importance of being consistent in communicating what the intervention might be and ask if it is acceptable to the beneficiary, also we try not to make any definite promises for intervention just in case something does not happen and we are unable to go back to the location to provide the needed services.

Are messages context-specific or/and tailored?

Yes

Briefly describe how

The project has messages that are within context and specific and tailored to project activities.For example, the criteria for prioritization for services has been summarized into a pictogram, as most beneficiaries and even community mobilisers are unable to read, and the pictograms are also useful for persons with hearing impairments. An audio version of HI messages are communicated through the use of a public address system(megaphones) at field level. In terms of gender and age-groups, there are also messages passed out to the beneficiaries depending on their specific needs. For example, messages are diseminated that address the specific needs of women, men, boys, and girls.

Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse

Yes

Briefly describe how

HI has a code of conduct and PSEA policy that all staff must understand and agree to. In the field to protect against SEA, HI works in teams and one person alone does not enter into the home of a beneficiary. The team also obtains at least a verbal consent before touching a beneficiary. Channels of reporting a PSEA concern are shared with staff and confidentiality is encouraged.

Nature of the consultations

Yes

Briefly describe the nature of the consultations

Though all consultations begin with community leaders, HI also consults community members comprised of women, men, children, and people with disabilities (including disabled peoples organizations) for project activities. One area where HI can expand this is to also include more government officials and other humanitarian organizations into the discussions.

Feedback /complaint mechanisms?

Yes

Briefly describe the mechanisms

Yes, but this is limited. For example, the project has a built-in mechanism for feedback through activity evaluation forms that are filled only during workshops/trainings and feedback community meetings. However, there is no concrete lay out of when, how and who is to receive the feedback. There is also no regular complaint mechanism (for example beneficiary satisfaction) about rehabilitation and PSS project services provided by the project, except when success stories are collected.

Community Engagement?

Yes

Briefly describe how

All monitoring and evaluation centers on beneficiary feedback surveys whereby beneficiaries who have received HI services are asked questions about the services they received. These questions focus on improvement in quality of life as well as satisfaction with HI services.

Protection Risk Analysis?

Yes

Briefly describe how

There was a protection risk analysis that was conducted and it was realized that women, men, boys, and girls with disability are vulnerable to certain protection risks including SGBV and lack of access to basic services such as health and food distribution. There are also protection risks considered that are produced due to the stigma surrounding disability and mental health problems.

Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Barriers to access include lack of ramps in buildings with steps, physically inaccessible meeting venues, doors that are too heavy for people with disabilities to open, or doors that are too narrow for wheelchairs to access, inadequate signage to guide persons who have visual impairments, and the need to travel for long distances with no transport means. These are some of barriers among others which are existing and which are addressed in the project through ensuring that services are located in physically accessible places. Attitudinal barriers such as the stigma that having a disability or mental health problem can produce, are also addressed through basic awareness on mental health, disability, and inclusion.

Addresses inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

People with disabilities and those with mental health problems often report they are targeted with protection concerns such as lack of access to services, robbery, SGBV, and social ostracization. This project aims to reduce the stigma surrounding disability and mental health concerns and raise the ability of people with disabilities and mental health problems to have greater self-sufficiency and self-esteem. This will allow beneficiaries greater access to services and supplies to ensure their protection from lack of access to services. Working with communities and the humanitarian actors will also help to reduce protection concerns and increase access to services by stressing the importance of inclusion and the importance of people with disabilities to society and the importance of considering their needs in a humanitarian response.

Project activities address inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Specifically the activities will help to reduce protection threats. For example providing mental health services will increase peoples’ ability to cope with stresses and traumas they may experience which can reduce instances of domestic violence including SGBV as well as increase their personal health. The rehabilitation services will increase peoples’ ability to become independent in their daily life and also allow them to access other services they need for survival such as food, water, NFI, shelter, etc. Reducing the dependnanc eon caregivers will also improve the mental health of caregivers as they will also have greater autonomy in their life.

Does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

The project monitoring and evaluation measures access of beneficiaries informally through observation by project team members in the field and then later formally through beneficiary feedback surveys during monitoring and evaluation missions. Concerns that are raised in the form of (reports, feedbacks) from community representatives of the affected population during the time of services are also recorded and evidence is gathered to take the concern to the relevant partner.

Does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

During the monitoring feedback missions beneficiaries are asked about their feelings of safety post treatment versus pre treatment. This will be based on several factors. For example, during HI activities, persons with visual impairments and those who may have greater vulnerability are provided with appropriate company to ensure safety entering and leaving the activity areas. Additionally to ensure beneficiary safety, all beneficiary information is kept confidential in the HI database with restricted access, and only shared upon permission of the beneficiary with other partners.

Does the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

Yes

How the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

Project beneficiaries of all ages and gender express their frustrations due to the fact that some family or community members assume that they are not human beings and thereby they are not considered in decision making, and they are also isolated from public activities. During HI activities, respect of dignity is provided to the beneficiaries by gathering beneficiaries and their caregivers together for experience sharing and to facilitate access to their needs. As a result, the voice of the beneficiairies is being heard and at least recognised by the public and concerned authorities, families, and communities bringing the recognition that a person with a disability or mental health problem has rights like any other person in society. Monitoring this comes in two forms. The first is through HI staff observations including the number of beneficiaries included in social activities and the increase of beneficiaries participation in certain sectors including health and education. The second method is during monitoring missions to project locations. Questions pertaining to the sustainability of these actions to improve dignity and the maintenance of dignity are also asked of project beneficiaries and their caregivers.
