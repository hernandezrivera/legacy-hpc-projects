Project Summary

In line with commitments to the global Call to Action on Protection Against Gender-Based Violence in Emergencies, IOM’s GBV in Crises (GBViC) Operational Framework and in accordance with the IASC Guidelines for Integrating GBV Interventions in Humanitarian Action (2015), IOM will continue to develop its comprehensive GBV intervention focused on three key pillars: 1) Survivor-centered support, 2) Risk mitigation and 3) Addressing root causes that perpetuate GBV.
In line with the 2020 JRP’s three strategic objectives and two of the GBV sector’s objectives, most of the activities described in this proposal will be jointly implemented by IOM and its local partner, PULSE Bangladesh. IOM and PULSE teams will work alongside each other, through Women and Girls’ Safe Spaces (WGSS) and the emergency transitional shelter and at the community level through ten facilities across nine camps. IOM directly implements case management and supports the day-to-day operations of the WGSS. PULSE teams work as activity animators, community mobilisers and also manage IOM’s emergency transitional shelter for GBV and trafficking survivors.
The WGSS provide non-stigmatizing case management entry points where women and girls can safely access individual case management and life saving services, including temporary accommodation through an emergency transitional shelter, as well as streamlined referral pathways for health, legal, MHPSS and self-reliance opportunities for women and girls. To mitigate risks, prevent GBV and empower women and girls, the WGSS provides age-appropriate and structured psychosocial and educational activities, life-skills and vocational learning, forums for information sharing and learning, including targeted awareness on SRH and radio listening groups, amidst a wide-range of recreational activities. Through close linkages with IOM general protection, child protection and counter trafficking teams, women and girls are empowered to manage their own safety and protection.
IOM will also focus on diversity of need, mapping health posts and confidential spaces to ensure non-stigmatising access for male and LGBTI survivors, persons with disabilities and child survivors as needed. IOM caseworkers will strengthen counselling support, referral and follow up of such cases working through an integrated approach with IOM Health teams. IOM GBV will refer identified cases – for example general protection cases, child victims of trafficking, other child protection cases, in need of specialized services to IOM’s integrated protection specialists or other protection partners.
IOM will scale up coaching and capacity building to PULSE, particularly in regards to shelter management, in line with its localization strategy whilst providing specialized services, capacity development and systems strengthening in GBV and Counter Trafficking (58% by PULSE, 42% by IOM). IOM and PULSE will also scale up their work with their community groups, advocates and volunteers going into 2020 to ensure sustainability and a community-based approach.
IOM and PULSE will focus on GBV mainstreaming across sectors and within emergency preparedness and response efforts. IOM GBV Caseworkers will continue to support as part of interagency response structures and Mobile Medical Teams. IOM is as an active contributor to the GBV Sub-Sector and its related task teams.
IOM is an active member of the GBV subsector and related task teams, including: GBV Case Management, GBV IMS, Male Engagement, Women’s Leadership and GIHA. IOM also co-chairs the Counter Trafficking Technical Working Group and the PSEA Network. IOM senior GBV staff also support interagency training on PSEA and SEA case management as a pool of facilitators working to support the PSEA Network in Cox’s Bazar to ensure capacity building in quality survivor-centred direct assistance.

Needs

IOM works off the assumption that GBV is occurring in all crises and that it is threatening crisis-affected population everywhere IOM operates: Reliable prevalence data on the scope of GBV in crises remain difficult to obtain, due to insecurity, service gaps, and lack of safety or access to service points for survivors. The Policy and Advocacy Task Team of the GBV Area of Responsibility, of which IOM is member of, has informed that since the influx of August 2017, there have been widespread reports of multiple-perpetrator rape and sexual assault, in Myanmar, during flight, and during displacement. Within host communities, a Violence Against Women report indicated that 42.5 per cent of women surveyed in the Chittagong division (includes Cox’s Bazar) had experienced physical violence at some point in their lives. Within both populations, women and girls are among the most vulnerable and are exposed to high levels of violence, sexual harassment and exploitation and harmful social and cultural practices and norms. According to the latest GBV Information Management System (GBV IMS) reports (2018-2019), GBV types reported include: rape, physical assault, sexual violence, forced marriage, denial of resources, and psychological abuse. According to 2019 GBVIMS community consultations, many GBV incidents are also unreported due to lack of protection of survivors, fear of reprisals and impunity for perpetrators, social stigma, perceptions and cultural norms around sexual violence and community pressure over survivors. The Aug. 2019 ISCG Multi Sectoral Needs Assessment found that 57 per cent of the refugee households were unable to identify any of the GBV service points, while 85 per cent mentioned Majhis their preferred point of referral for cases of sexual violence. FGDs mention that pressure and fear of parents and community leaders as well as the low participation of women and girls in making decisions at household level constitute barriers for survivors. Risks of forced marriage with the perpetrator and not being protected against reprisals are commonly cited concerns. Social stigma and perceptions about rape survivors, the community leaders’ interference in handling rape cases, lack of protection of survivors, insufficient awareness of consequences of rape, benefits of services and service accessibility could considerably delay survivors’ decision making in reporting and accessing services on a timely manner. Nearly half of the reported GBV cases were perpetrated during the night since last year. A recent assessment revealed that 25 per cent of female respondents noted latrines as one of the areas they do not feel safe and 50 per cent said lack of light at night to safely access latrines/bathing facilities was key. In the same assessment, female respondents also noted water points, markets, distribution points, and their own shelters as among areas where they do not feel safe. Regular risk mapping with communities has confirmed these risks are particularly acute in areas with pre-existing protection concerns such as Teknaf and in congested makeshift camps.Inadequate or un-available services for Rohingya male survivors of sexual violence have also been identified a crucial gap in the response, building upon the findings of the 2018 Women’s Refugee Commission report. Likewise, GBV service gaps have been identified for groups with other diversity of need including persons with disabilities and LGBTI groups such as the Hijra (third gender) community. There is a need to reinforce community-based approaches to be able to effectively prevent GBV, address stigma and support survivors’ access to specialized services in the Rohingya and host communities. Harmful social norms, gender roles and negative self-reliance coping mechanisms such as early marriage require social change interventions that are holistic, community-led and evidence-based.

Activities or outputs

Indicator 5.4: IOM will ensure technical expertise and quality survivor-centred care working closely with IOM Health and Protection teams to ensure diversity of need. Integrated capacity building and coordination as well as a focus on supervision skills and training for local organisations and national authorities on GBV will be emphasized. Technical training for caseworkers and health workers on key groups such as male survivors, LGBTI groups and persons with disability will cascade beyond IOM teams into field training in line with interagency guidelines and assessment tools (pre/post tests) and service audits. IOM and PULSE will strengthen referral pathways working with humanitarian stakeholders, government authorities and community groups in the camps and host communities and will also orient health service providers in handling different types of GBV cases and will work with communities to better understand health-seeking behaviours of men and boys. Indicators 5.1 and 5.2: to ensure sustainability and localization, IOM GBV will scale up work with its community groups, Community Advocates and local partner to transition day-to-day management of key WGSS activities including: group psychosocial support activities, life skills training, structured GBV prevention activities, and radio listening group facilitation to its community groups. Child friendly corners will be integrated in the WGSS to improve women’s access, participation and leadership in WGSS management. IOM and PULSE will play a supportive role in mobilizing resources to support activities and capacity building to community groups and advocates in the camps and host community and will scale up its work with community volunteers as front liners to ensure awareness on GBV, safe referrals and dialogue on GBV prevention in line with appropriate social change curriculum PULSE and IOM will focus on capacity development of men, women, boys and girls from 100 sub-blocks to broadcast the information related to GBV services that are available, contribute to GBV sensitization and structured social change. This approach focuses on supporting community members to lead, inspire and influence positive change in their own lives and the life of others by challenging the social norms that perpetuate violence and power imbalances in communities. This will allow IOM to better work with communities to address root causes of gender inequality and affect holistic social change of harmful behaviours and practices related to GBV. Indicator 5.3: Skills development and empowerment of women and girls will be become more focused and structured. Participants who have graduated from skills-building classes and groups will be engaged as peer trainers and supported with coaching and capacity building in facilitation, communication and management skills. IOM will identify opportunities to affect power imbalances identifying opportunities for women’s participation and engagement through multi-stakeholder approaches and a do-no-harm approach. IOM will scale up several self-reliance pilot projects (Women’s Participation Project and Women’s Income Generation Support) with IOM and partners across other areas of operation. IOM will replicate these pilot interventions, sharing lessons learned and working with partners beyond IOM’s areas of intervention. IOM will work closely with relevant sectors share key lessons learned and facilitate workshops for other humanitarian stakeholders to ensure coordinated programming and long term strategies for participation and empowerment throughout the response, particularly around inclusive emergency preparedness and response planning. All data collected will be disaggregated by sex, age, and diversity in order to adjust and target programming as needed.
