Recent studies indicate a high prevalence of GBV in South Sudan (IRC, 2017; IOM, 2017). The needs for GBV services are high, yet availability of multi-sectoral GBV services across the country is low, and access remains a challenge for women, especially in rural communities. Areas of return and locations of displacement outside camps are often underserved by services, which heighten risks for women and girls.
The prevalence of GBV is underpinned by a patriarchal social system reinforced by other harmful gender norms. The conflict and economic collapse have affect men’s ability to provide for and protect their families. For those unable to live up to idealized norms of masculinity, acts of violence and crime are used to express their authority and control. Youth gangs, blamed for increase in crime including GBV, have increase in urban centres of Wau and Juba, as traditional leadership has been eroded. There is a need to engage men and boys, particularly disenfranchised youth in being part of the solution to prevent GBV, particularly in transitional and recovery locations.
IOM works to ensure a consistent and robust approach to addressing gender-based violence in crises, utilizing IOM’s institutional framework (2018). This project will contribute significantly to protection and gender equality and includes conscious strategies for inclusion of marginalised groups. IOM will contribute to Protection Cluster Objectives PO1 and PO2 through the following activities in Juba, Wau, Twic (Warrap) and Magwi:
• GBV response: Providing psychosocial support and case management, Establish and operate women and girls friendly spaces (WGFSs), awareness raising on available GBV services, build capacity of multi-sectoral service providers, provide dignity kits to strengthen protection outcomes.
• GBV prevention: Conduct GBV behaviour change and harmful social norms programming utilizing EMAP, empower women and girls through targeted livelihoods, leadership and life-skills activities.
• GBV risk mitigation: Build skills and attitudes among WASH, livelihoods and peacebuilding actors on risk mitigation, women’s participation and to address the gender gap in humanitarian employment opportunities.

1 - Needs

Recent studies indicate a high prevalence of GBV; 65% of women and girls experience some form of sexual or physical violence in conflict-affected areas of South Sudan (IRC, 2017); with a further study finding that 75% women had experienced intimate partner violence within the last one year (IOM, 2017). The needs for GBV services are high, yet availability of multi-sectoral GBV services across the country is low, and access remains a challenge for women, especially in rural communities. Recent IOM GBV assessments and coordination activities through the GBV sub-cluster identified service gaps for case management and psychosocial support (PSS), and referrals to other response services such as the Juba teaching hospital in the outskirts of Jubek state (Luri county). Similarly, in Wau in Western Bahr-El-Ghazal, several communities including Hai Masna are outside close service coverage, and both clinical management of rape, GBV case management and PSS are inconsistent in Magwi (Eastern Equitoria), and there is a significant gap in Twic (Warrap). There is a need to provide quality case management and PSS, improve referrals and build capacity of multi-sectoral services including working with health actors and the police to support recovery of GBV survivors in these areas. In the context of protracted conflict, displacement, and the dire economic and food security situation, individuals and families have resorted to risky coping mechanisms, such as increased forced and early marriages (Oxfam, 2019) and survival sex (SHIA network, 2019, Luedke, 2019). Areas of return and locations of displacement outside camps are often underserved by services, especially water and sanitation, which heighten risks for women and girls who are predominately responsible for water collection. Perceptions of women collecting water show only 34% of women always felt safe, and 20% rarely felt safe, with Juba, Ayod and Mayom as the highest risk areas (IOM, 2018). It is critical to engage women and girls, community leaders and humanitarian actors in GBV risk mitigation and women’s participation, to support women’s income generation interventions and provide support and information on options and lifeskills to reduce these risks through women and girls friendly spaces (WGFS). The prevalence of violence against women and girls is structural, underpinned by a patriarchal social system, connected to the political economy of bridewealth (Luedke, 2019) , and further reinforced by other harmful gender norms. An IOM study to found widespread normalization of harmful gender norms at the root of GBV perpetration(IOM-NSMC, 2018). The conflict and economic collapse has affect men’s ability to provide for and protect their families. This has contributed to a loss of identity for those who cannot live up to idealized norms of masculinity, leading to acts of violence and crime to express authority and control (Birchall, 2019). Traditional leadership has been eroded, and in some locations replaced by armed and criminalized groups of youth who are blamed for rising crime, including GBV (SIHA network, 2019). Particularly in Wau Town and Juba, there is a need to engage men and boys, particularly disenfranchised youth as being part of the solution to prevent GBV, especially in transitional and recovery locations.

2 - Activities

IOM works to ensure a consistent and robust approach to addressing GBV, utilizing IOM’s institutional framework on GBV in crisis (2018). This project will contribute to the Protection Cluster Objectives PO1 and PO2 through the following activities: • Provide psychosocial support and case management services for GBV survivors in Luri county (Juba), Hai Masna (Wau), Magwi and Twic. This includes providing technical capacity building on GBV case management, counselling for GBV survivors, caring for child survivors with IPs and the GBV sub-cluster TRGs.• Establish and operate women and girl’s friendly spaces, at least one in each location, to improve access to services, conduct lifeskills and income generation activities and provide group psychosocial support for women and girls of different ages.• Conduct GBV awareness raising in target communities on GBV consequences, available services and initiate community awareness on GBV issues (IPV, child marriage, sexual and economic violence). This includes ongoing engagement of gender norms and orientation of community workers and activists on facilitation skills and the use of IEC materials. • Build technical capacity of multi-sectoral service providers (health, psychosocial and security actors) to improve the quality of GBV response services based on survivor-centered principles and to support GBV survivors of trafficking.• Provision of dignity kits for IDPs, school girls and vulnerable women in target communities.• Conduct GBV behaviour change and harmful social norms programming utilizing EMAP, community action groups and working in schools in Juba and Wau. This includes providing technical capacity for partner CSOs/NNGOs on GBV primary prevention.• Build capacity among WASH, livelihoods and peacebuilding actors at program and community level on GBV risk mitigation and women’s participation, including advocacy and capacity building to address the gender gap in humanitarian staffing. This includes coordinating the WASH cluster AAP technical working group and being the GBV sub-cluster focal point to the WASH cluster.

6.6 - Will beneficiaries' rights be legally recognised?

Yes, GBV is a human rights violation; and various forms are legally recognized as a criminal offense in the penal code (2008) such as rape, and child marriage is criminalized in the Child Act (20018), although provisions on domestic violence are currently weak and a significant gap in South Sudan law. GBV survivors are entitled to support to recover, and as such this is a right that’s legally recognized and beneficiaries will be informed of their rights such as services being free of charge.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

Yes, IOM and partner GBV programming ensures that IDPs are engaged in all stages of the project design, including community consultations, safety audits, CBFM. IOM builds the capacity of IPs to conduct monitoring of interventions and collects community feedback through surveys, semi-structured interviews, focus group discussions, community meetings and verbal reporting, and ensures that all the groups in the targeted community have opportunities to voice their opinions on assessing the results the intervention. Separate consultations based on age/gender/diversity, including displaced populations, are conducted to ensure different marginalized groups opinions and contributions are included in program design and implementation.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

Yes, IOM GBV program operates from the national level in coordination and collaboration with the MoGCSW, as well as at State level (where existing). County and community level government and community systems are also engaged and, where possible, strengthened in IOM’s GBV programming, especially related to local government implementing responsibilities related to CEDAW and engagement on social norms change to promote gender equality. IOM GBV team also works with community-based structures related to community protection and WASH service provision to address risks related to GBV in the community.

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

Yes, the project contributes to the Protection Cluster HRP objectives and the GBV sub-cluster strategic plan (2018 – 2020). Furthermore, this project supports GBV survivor’s rights to recovery and prevention of violence against women and girls, which are national objectives related to CEDAW implementation. In addition, the project supports the implementation of the Kampala Convention which requires that the Government provides assistances to IDPs. Furthermore, the project supports the implementation of the National Action Plan on Child Marriage.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

The protection situation is varied across the country; however, the protection environment is not stable enough as the country is in a critical period of political transition for the implementation of the R-ARCSS. Humanitarian needs also remain high, as significant displacement, weak infrastructure and service coverage. GBV services and programming capacities continue to be weak among indigenous organisations and the GoSS. Despite some high-profile gains in justice on sexual violence, institutional capacity for justice and political will to address accountability on conflict related sexual violence (CRSV) remains elusive.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

Yes, IOM GBV program has a strong partnership and capacity building component, with an aim to engage and capacitate national and local CSOs/NGOs on GBV prevention and response. IOM engages in capacity assessment of local partners, defines a technical and organizational capacity building plan and engages in an intensive mentoring approach with partners. Emergency GBV programming at community level has a tendency to be organization rather than community lead in initial phases, however, in Juba, IOM and partners have built strong community engagement and the start of GBV activism through the EMAP and social norms work which has enabled a transition to a community-lead approach. IOM intends to replicate this in other areas, as communities start to recovery from conflict and displacement. IOM’s support for WGFS is in locations where semi or permanent structures can be built, with community ownership over the activities and space a core component of the approach.

8.18 - Please indicate how the risks selected above will be mitigated.

Women and girls with disabilities and from marginalized communities are at higher risk of GBV. IOM works with partners and GBV sub-cluster to identify marginalized groups during GBV assessments and address their barriers to participation in activity set-up. Furthermore, based on identification of communication channels, IOM and partners conduct regular consultations to address access and complaints and provide feedback. IOM staff or interpreters are used to access minority groups, and are oriented on working with people with different impairments. IOM is currently working to strengthen recruitment of people with disabilities as staff and volunteers. IOM works with partners to provide services for GBV survivors, which would be at high risk of retaliation and stigma if there are unsafe practices related to data protection and management. IOM implements an institutional data protection policy and follows GBV case management data protection protocols including capacity building and monitoring implementing partners on their application. GBV survivors are in a very vulnerable situation, and frequently do not feel entitled or in a position to complain. Despite this, IOM and partner’s staff and volunteers are oriented to provide information that complaints and feedback is important and share avenues for complaints. Dignity kit post-distribution monitoring is conducted, as well as satisfaction surveys with persons engaging in GBV program activities. Operating women and girls friendly spaces can be a high risk activity. However, IOM and partners abide by the UNICEF WGFS guideline which stipulates development of community buy-in to establish these spaces and to engage the community on protection of these spaces. There are safety risks for GBV case workers as a result of supporting GBV survivors, the project will mitigate these risks by ensuring that case workers go to the communities with other outreach staff, meet clients in a neutral space and engage community leaders.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

IOM direct implementation and support to national partners (IPs) promotes inclusion, feedback and ownership of program activities. IOM provides regular capacity building, and training and mentoring to promote accountability to affected populations through the following means: 1) IOM works with IPs to gather information of preferred communication channels of age and gender groups, with separate discussions with adult women and men (ages 25 – 59, 60+), girls/young women and boys/young men (ages 15-24) at schools and in communities. Consultations are held to engage people with disabilities, elderly and persons from minority ethnic groups, including by conducting door-to-door visits for those with limited mobility. Based on these findings, communication and feedback channels are tailored, with information provided through community meetings, and in separate age/gender/diversity groups. 2) Community-Feedback Mechanism (CFM) avenues established in IOM and IP operational areas include communicating directly through staff/volunteers, hotline and proactive FGDs conducted by IOM M&E team. IOM developed a CFM flow chart to ensure that IOM and IP staff and volunteers are able to handle sensitive and non-sensitive program complaints/feedback in a safe and ethical manner. 3) IOM and IPs are developing pictorial IEC materials for low literacy populations and local staff/volunteers who speak native languages; pre-testing with the community, and where possible supporting the community to develop context-specific IEC. IOM utilizes an “Inclusive checklist” to ensure IEC materials are accessible for people with different impairments and staff are trained to adapt their communication methods for inclusivity. 4) IOM has a robust PSEA system, and conducts regular training for IOM PSEA focal points and IP partner staff and vendors to support improved PSEA systems among partners. IOM is an active member of the PSEA task force at national level, and co-leading in Wau and in Abeyi.
