Project Summary

The specific objective of the project is to improve access to specific services for the most vulnerable people in Ninewa, Kirkuk, Anbar, Diyala and Baghdad Governorates.
In direct relation with the priority needs identified, the project will provide both an immediate and long term response. This will be delivered through a package that focuses on the availability, accessibility and quality of health care services through direct service provision and capacity building with a view of addressing the short term and long term needs for Iraq. The project will target IDPs, returnees and host communities identified as being vulnerable within this context including persons with disabilities, children, and youth.
Specifically, Handicap International (HI) plans to:
 address the lack of availability of health services through the provision of physical and functional rehabilitation services for IDPs, Returnees and host communities combined with PSS services for the respective patients and their relatives.
 address the lack of accessibility to health services through promoting existing services to mitigate the barriers of non-information; support the local health services to be able to address the specific needs of vulnerable populations; provide cash assistance for high cost of transport for IDPs, returnees and host communities; support existing health structures in the accessibility for people with a physical impairment to their services.
 work to Improve the quality of existing functional and physical rehabilitation services for IDPs, returnees and host communities with a focus on training of staff in health centers;
The modalities for the intervention will depend on the nature of the activity and the level of access (including security considerations). Furthermore the intervention will follow a comprehensive approach as it will benefit from other HI interventions to ensure multiple yet integrated activities (including risk education, MHPSS, physical rehabilitation, and inclusive access to service) in order to maximize the intervention for beneficiaries. Finally, inclusion mainstreaming will be applied throughout the intervention with a specific focus on Gender, Age and Disability.

Needs

After over three years of spheres of control wielded by the “Islamic State" group in several parts of Iraq with intensive battles to retake territory from the group, on 21 November 2017 the Iraqi Prime Minister declared the group to be militarily defeated. This has come at the heels of 25,986 civilian deaths, 45,429 civilians injured (as reported by UNAMI from June 2014), extensive infrastructural damage in retaken areas, and mass displacements of affected populations. According to IOM, as of the 31st October 2018, there were 1,866,594 IDPs in Iraq and 4,113,624 returnees. The conflict in Iraq is resulting in large numbers of people sustaining injuries that often result in permanent disability. According to the Action on Armed Violence, there were over 50,000 civilian deaths and injuries from explosive violence in Iraq (2011-2017). In 2017 alone there were 8,896 deaths and injuries from explosive violence. Furthermore, persons with disabilities in Iraq tend to be invisible in their communities due to the inaccessible environment, lack of inclusive services, lack of awareness at community level etc. Conflict/disaster situations push them further to the margins as living conditions worsen. The families of persons with disabilities also face greater difficulties addressing their basic needs. In such a situation, the time and attention dedicated by family/caretaker to a family member with a disability decreases with regard to other household priorities. Persons with disabilities and their families are part of the most vulnerable groups and too often their specific needs are ignored by relief and rehabilitation projects. People with specific needs often face additional barriers, such as lack of information on available services, financial issues, or geographical barriers.The capacities of the Ministry of Health and Directorates of Health to provide health care services continue to be overwhelmed by the rapidly rising numbers and demands of displaced and returned individuals. Funding uncertainty faced by the government contributes to the inadequate level and quality of health care services in Iraq. In recent years, rehabilitation services have been neglected and received little support, the buildings have often fallen into disrepair and physiotherapy equipment is either lacking, malfunctioning, missing and/or destroyed. A lack of adequate staff and skills on advanced rehabilitation care techniques, acute shortages of medical consumables and assistive devices due to disrupted supply chain and poor monitoring, follow-up and patient referral mechanisms for persons requiring rehabilitation are among the gaps identified by Handicap International (HI). Thus, apart from direct provision of psychosocial support (PSS) services, HI is planning to support health facilities with training for staff. More generally, HI is aiming to contribute to improved access to healthcare, with a specific focus on physiotherapy (PT) and MHPSS services by working both at community and governmental levels through awareness-raising. Within the country, there are very few actors to address gaps in physiotherapy and Inclusive services (acknowledged by the World Health Organisation (WHO) and Health Cluster). Moreover, an assessment completed by REACH in September 2018 highlighted that of those who reported chronic health conditions, 69% reported barriers to accessing health care services. The top barrier reported for accessing health care for returnees in Iraq was the high cost of services. Furthermore, the most common challenges observed by HI includes the difficulties faced by persons with disabilities in terms of mobility (e.g. cost of transportation to the rehabilitation center, cost of assistive devices and other prohibitive costs involved in the rehabilitation process such as accommodation, food supplies, child care), additional limitations to mobility as a result of insecurity and contamination from Explosive Remnants of War (ERW)s and barriers to access related to the lack of information on the existing services. In an emergency, trauma is widespread. 71% of persons with specific needs identified by HI throughout its projects show concerning signs of psychological distress and difficulties, with considerable impact on daily lives. They report experiencing or exhibiting symptoms such as hopelessness, isolation, persistent existential concerns of safety, loss of social role in the new environment; and depressive signs such as anxiety, disturbed sleep, loss of appetite, and loss of motivation for recovery from injury. In general there is a lack of trained non-specialized mental health care providers to provide quality care. Mental health is not adequately considered and routine reporting at health facilities and PHC facilities does not include classification of mental illness. There is a lack of coordination on MHPSS assessments and availability of MHPSS information that helps to improve programming and support to affected populations.

Activities

In response to the assessed needs, Handicap International proposes to continue its ongoing response through addressing availability, accessibility and quality of health services in Iraq whilst ensuring inclusion (gender, age and disability) mainstreaming is applied in a way that is contextually appropriate. In addition, over the course of the intervention, HI will adapt its service provision and its modalities of intervention to best adapt to the changing context, in order to continue to meet critical needs of the most vulnerable people. Handicap International will address the lack of availability of health services through:a) Provision of physical and functional rehabilitation services for IDPs, Returnees and host communities combined with PSS services for the respective patients and their relatives Secondly, Handicap International will address the lack of accessibility to health services through: a) The promotion of existing services to mitigate the barriers of non-informationb) Support to the local health services to be able to access and address the specific needs of vulnerable populations where access to humanitarian assistance is limited;c) Provide cash assistance to address the high needs related to the cost of transport for IDPs, returnees and host communities. Finally, Handicap International will work to Improve the quality of functional and physical rehabilitation services for IDPs, returnees and host communities with a focus on hard-to-reach areas and training of staff in health centers;a) Support with capacity building of existing health structures through:- Training and coaching of medical and rehabilitation staff on PSS concepts and practices to promote a holistic approach and enhance rehabilitation outcomes- HI will develop a comprehensive capacity building plan aiming at reinforcing the rehabilitation service and promoting the early rehabilitation in hospital. This will target the hospital as a whole and more specifically the rehabilitation service, the other medical staff (nurses, doctors) and the non-health staff (administrative, supply…).- The capacity building also includes the Training of Trainers (ToT) on awareness on the benefit of rehabilitation and on inclusion for the health and non-health people at the community level. Through the capacity building focus, the transfer of skills and knowledge, the work to increase accessibility and quality of existing structures, HI will work to ensure the sustainability of the activities.In addition, HI is a member of the physical rehabilitation working group and will continue to work with them to prioritise and map which health structures we are providing support to. Handicap International, globally and in Iraq, is committed to complying, to the extent possible, with core commitments to Accountability to Affected Populations as agreed upon by the IASC. All HI interventions require beneficiary consent and participation, and operations staff members are required to solicit feedback and complaints.

Protection Risk Analysis

- Risk of increased harm or distress from interventions such as mental health and psychosocial support involving highly sensitive issues without appropriate referral / follow up services and procedures in place. - The risk of non-meaningful or non-adapted services preventing access for individuals and groups who may be particularly vulnerable, such as children, women, persons with disabilities, ethnic minorities and populations with perceived affiliations. - Due to the nature of this work, the activities will involve working with those who are at a heightened risk of human rights violations- The risk that HI staff and beneficiaries may be vulnerable to harassment, sexual assault and abuse- HI staff and beneficiaries not understanding HI’s approach to protection against sexual exploitation and abuse or understand how to raise concerns to allegations related to a safeguarding issue. - Working with children who may have already have experienced previous traumatic incidences - Risk of violence or harm due to a change in the context including unrest or political tension- Risks posed by existing Explosive remnants of war, Improvised explosive devises and mines to both HI staff and populations drawn towards locations of service provision

Mitigation Measures

Protection policies in HIThe principles of equality, non-discrimination and protection are outlined in HI’s Gender Policy, HI’s Child Protection Policy and HI’s Protection of Beneficiaries from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) Policy, which every HI employee reads and signs at the beginning of their assignment. In Iraq, HI designs a feedback mechanism for receiving complaints. HI also conducts awareness workshops and training sessions for all staff on PSEA in order to prevent incidents and to raise awareness on the complaint mechanisms. Finally, HI staffs received protection training on GBV, protection principles, humanitarian principles. Do No HarmHI’s activities are designed and implemented so as to not cause harm and to be consistent with core humanitarian principles. A do-no-harm approach seeks to mitigate any negative impact of external assistance, and ensure that the intervention does not reinforce or exacerbate the conflict and associated tensions. This is bound up with conflict sensitive programming, which entails close analysis and understanding of intergroup relationships within an operational context, as well as any potential interaction between the intervention and these dynamics, in order to avoid any negative impacts. Inclusion of Persons with DisabilityIn Iraq, persons with disabilities are disproportionately affected in situations of risk and humanitarian emergencies, and face multiple barriers in accessing protection and humanitarian assistance, including relief and recovery support. They are also particularly exposed to targeted violence, exploitation and abuse, including sexual and gender-based violence. HI has been working with LNGOs, INGOs and the government in order to address these barriers faced by different groups and also build the capacity of actors to promote an inclusive response for the affected communities at both displacement and return situations through awareness raising, training and coaching. Using the findings identified by HI in surveys, HI will aim at tackling existing barriers to increase level of access to those services. By doing so, HI expects to ensure meaningful access to all planned activities. Gender and Age MainstreamingHI is committed to equal access for people of all genders and a gender-tailored, conflict sensitive response that caters to the different needs and vulnerabilities of all people. The project design seeks to ensure access to services for women, men, boys, and girls of all ages through integrating gender mainstreaming at every stage of the project cycle. Among the steps that HI takes include, but are not limited to, the following:• Gender Sensitive Recruitment and Gender Balanced Teams: HI’s Human Resources seeks to recruit, to the extent possible, gender-balanced teams for Risk Education, Psychosocial Support, and Physical Rehabilitation support. Moreover, all HI staff are required to sign and comply with the PSEA policy. • Gender and Age Disaggregated Data Collection and Decision-Making: HI’s operational and monitoring teams collect data disaggregated by age and gender at every stage of the project, to ensure that the different needs of men, women, boys, and girls inform all decision-making. • Gender and age tailored programming: HI undertakes all measures, to the extent possible, to reach all beneficiaries through its project design and implementation process, based on its contextual experience. ERW/IED/Mine contaminationConsidering the level of contamination in HI areas of intervention, HI, as part of its comprehensive approach, combine to the extent possible its health programing with its protection programing related to Arms Risk Reduction and aiming at reducing the impact of the Conventional Weapon and Improvised Explosive Devices for conflict-affected populations by promoting safety messaging through Risk Education and land release. HI provides regular Mine Risk Education session to all HI staffs and other service provider staff at request.

How is your project accountable to affected population

HI's monitoring evaluation accountability and learning (MEAL) practices are framed by HI's global quality policy and tools and HI Iraq MEAL strategy. HI Iraq understands accountability as giving HI beneficiaries, HI staff and partners the opportunity to understand and/or influence the key decisions which are made during HI actions. HI Iraq accountability commitments are the following;• Transparency - Information sharing: by making information available about HI activities• Participation - Listening to the people HI aim to assist, incorporating their views and analysis in program decisions is a key in accountability. It allows beneficiaries to share their feedback on HI activities. Attention is put on encouraging beneficiaries feedback during the implementation of the activities, at the discharge phase and at monitoring stage, through field based feedback mechanisms and discharge satisfaction surveys.• Monitoring and evaluation - HI undertakes regular post intervention monitoring of beneficiaries currently receiving its assistance in order to gather feedback on the quantity and quality of services received, as well as on what beneficiaries found to be lacking or not useful. This feedback is reviewed and considered by HI's operations team and technical advisors, in order to inform project decision-making, as well as HI's long-term strategy.• Requests-Complaints management - having a requests and complaints mechanism for the beneficiaries to report directly any issue to HI. In addition to coordination with the IDP call center. All complaints, responses and follow up are documented. HI remains committed to take into account beneficiary perspectives at all stages of the project cycle, from needs assessments, to collaboratively determining treatment plans, and to decision-making over the course of the project. HI conducts regular needs assessments utilising inclusive methodologies to complete surveys, FGD and analysis of the existing services, meeting with DoH, DoE, community leaders, and interviews with potential beneficiaries, in order to understand needs self-identified by the communities of concern. Findings from these surveys and consultations inform the design of proposals. This ensures that the different needs of men, women and children as well as age are taken into accounts when implementing interventions: for all of rehabilitation and PSS interventions, HI has emphasized the involvement of the beneficiary in determining the treatment plan, as well as in setting goals and timelines necessary to reach them. HI's teams are trained to ensure that the goals set for treatment are always beneficiary-oriented and tailored to both their circumstances and expectations. In a survey carried out in August 2018, (97%) of respondents said they were able to express their expectations, concerns and feedback throughout the treatment and they feel that they have been listened to by HI staff. In addition, (79%) of respondents said their level of well-being improved. Among this population of respondents, (74%) are more comfortable to perform in their daily activities (walking, moving, working, etc.).
