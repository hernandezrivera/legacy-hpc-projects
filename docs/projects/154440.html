Project Summary

The objective of this engagement is to support 1.75 million displaced persons to voluntarily, safely and sustainably return home through the effective coordination of the MA AoR and enhancing the local police capacity to provide a life-saving response to explosive hazards which will contribute to an improved protection environment.
This engagement is in direct support of:
- HRP Strategic Objective 2 to “Improve response timeliness through preparedness: to prevent further degradation of humanitarian needs”.
- Protection Cluster Objective 2 to “Support and advocate with authorities, humanitarian and recovery actors to address protection issues in accordance with protection norms and applicable legal standards, thereby contributing to a favorable protection environment.”

Needs

The conflict with ISIL has resulted in complex, dense, and extensive explosive hazard (EH) contamination. Particularly alarming is the variety of EH many of which were used by ISIL as defensive weapons prior to their withdrawal. EH contamination affects Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs) and returnees alike, as it impedes safe return of IDPs to their areas of origin, and their capacity to resume their lives upon return. Notably, 33,652 IDP households cited EH as a reason for not returning to their areas of origin, signaling a need to ensure they have accurate information and support to return home. Iraq’s extensive EH contamination is detrimental to the safety of civilians, particularly men and boys whose social roles and behaviour – e.g. attempting to clear rubble and repair damaged homes, or engagement with outdoor activities – expose them to grave danger. Consequently, men and boys are particularly vulnerable to explosive incidents and will require EH risk education. From January to September 2018, 3,618 security and explosive incidents, causing 2,056 fatalities and 708 injuries, were reported. The latest data from Iraq indicates that – out of casualties whose sex and age were reported – 90% were men and boys (54% men and 36% boys). Additionally, 81% of casualties were civilians, 12% were military, and 7% were deminers. The majority of the incidents are concentrated in Baghdad, Mosul, Kirkuk, Falluja and Muqdadiya districts.The concentration of EH in liberated areas is having a significant impact on the 1.75 million people who will to be served by the 2019 HRP. As displaced Iraqis return home they may encounter EH in their homes and in public spaces. Reports of booby-trapped homes and public spaces have raised concerns among the government, humanitarian actors and at-risk populations. When civilians encounter a suspected hazard, one of the options available to them is to call the local police who can confirm if the item is dangerous, mark it and report it to the relevant Government disposal team, most often within the Ministry of Defense. The World Bank reports that at least 130,000 houses have been registered as damaged or destroyed in liberated areas. The presence of damaged or destroyed houses is a proxy indicator for EH contamination and therefore the local police will most likely be called upon to provide protection assistance to at-risk populations. As the local police are often the first to respond to reports of suspected hazards, the presence of trained local police is critical to mitigate the risk of death and casualties among those returning home.During the fight to liberate areas under ISIL control, a considerable number of local police personnel trained in bomb disposal were severely injured or killed. For example, 28 Anbar polices were killed while neutralizing IEDs. Given the attrition rate of these specialized personnel within the local police, there is a void in Government capability to respond to EH reported by Iraqis. UNMAS Iraq seeks to train local police to provide life-saving first response capacity to contribute to an improved protection environment. The services provided by local police will directly benefit displaced persons, returnees and host communities.UNMAS, as the lead of the Global Mine Action Area of Responsibility (MAAOR), under the Protection Cluster, has the lead role, as a focal point between humanitarian mine action partners, UN Cluster System and Government of Iraq (GoI), to ensure coordination of humanitarian mine action activities in an effective and efficient manner. At the strategic level, works directly with mine action authorities to develop and/or improve effective and sustainable systems and processes, work with the GoI to understand and respond to GoI priorities, as well as coordinate humanitarian efforts through the MAAoR As part of this response, it will be critical to incorporate gender considerations into the project implementation and the anticipated outcomes of the project. Naturally, women may be more likely to speak to a female police officer. As such, UNMAS Iraq will strive to incorporate female police officers in the training so that they may provide a first response capacity in their respective communities. In Iraq, female EH casualties make up a markedly lower percentage of victims as compared with males. However, women bear the burden of many accidents associated with EH as they take up supporting the family and caring for disabled children and/or husbands. Furthermore, a study commissioned by Oxfam to explore Gender and Conflict Analysis in ISIS Affected Communities of Iraq (2017) outlined the importance of returning to normal life post-ISIL and recommended the creation and rehabilitation of safe public spaces, where women used to be able to go but were then prevented from under ISIL occupation. Local police have an essential role in supporting the creation of safe space for women and girls particularly by removing EHs.

Activities

Activity 1: Delivery of Mine Action training to public service personnel to manage, regulate, coordinate, and respond to explosive hazard contamination in retaken areas. UNMAS Iraq will train at least 150 police in first responder training and 20 in explosive ordnance disposal (EOD) and improvised explosive device defeat (IEDD). The trainees will be police officers affected to Anbar, Kirkuk and Ninewa Governorates primarily due to the density of explosive hazard contamination. The support that UNMAS Iraq seeks to provide would incorporate specific methodologies to ensure that the needs of all those returning to affected areas, specifically the voices of women and girls, are considered in planning and prioritization. The police have a key role to play in liaising with the community as they are the first ones to be called by civilians if a suspected EH is found. UNMAS Iraq will conduct at least one training to female police officers in duty across the country to ensure that women and girls are reached and participating effectively in affected communities, thus improving a needs-based access to EH removal services. The First Responder module (5 days) is designed to help local police develop a better understanding of the variety of EH they might encounter and how to manage the appropriate response (120 staff)The EOD course attended by 30 staff (7 weeks) designed to gradually develop the student’s capabilities from basic explosive theory, range safety, task management, detection, recognition and execution of render safe procedures within the framework of International Mine Action Standards (IMAS). A limited number of the graduates who have successfully completed the EOD course will attend the final module, IEDD (8 weeks), will develop the students' understanding of the IED and the various types of device being used by adversary groups. Protection Risk Analysis

No protection risks have been identified to beneficiaries and humanitarians through the implementation of the project activities. However, the following two protection risks have been identified for the participants: 1. Supporting females to assume responsibility for non-traditional roles (female police officers): training them in first responder training can raise their profile in the community and make them vulnerable to resistance or threats. 2. Supporting police officers to help people safely return home by identifying and removing dangerous explosive hazards left behind by ISIL can make them a target to militia/ISIL.

Mitigation Measures

1. Ensure there are no photos taken of the faces of the officers that are trained; keep low profile during training and in media. 2. Ensure there are no photos taken of the faces of the officers that are trained; keep low profile during training and in media.

How is your project accountable to affected population

Approximately 1.8 million IDPs have yet to return home and 4.1 million have returned. There have been reports of people returning home and opening the door only to have their house blow up. People have returned home to find explosive hazards in their houses, in their yards and in the public spaces in their communities. There are two hotlines are set up by the Government of Iraq to enable affected populations to report suspected explosive hazards and make a request for an assessment: the Civil Defense – 115; and the Directorate of Mine Action – 182. However, these hotlines are not sufficient to meet the number of requests flooding in as people return home. There is a significant need for local authorities to have the capacity to identify, mark and report dangerous items and then liaise with the Iraqi Security Forces to come and retrieve the dangerous item. Therefore, the affected populations have been inherently involved in the design of this intervention as it is directly addressing an identified need in at-risk communities. The communities have the opportunity to provide feedback either through the hotlines or through their local police stations. Furthermore, to enhance the relationship with the community and support an engaged environment, the local police officers will be trained on ‘community liaison; specific methodologies on how to better engage with their communities. Community liaison in mine action refers to the processes, techniques and information exchange that encourage mine action actors to develop a better understanding of affected communities and their existing assets, needs, and priorities. The police have a key role to play in liaising with the community as they are often the first ones to be called if a suspected explosive hazard is found. Notably female police officers will be trained to enhance the liaison activities to women and girls, taking into consideration their gender specific needs and enabling accountability to this gender group as well. Regular consultation with the affected communities is encouraged, and necessary, to ensure that people have accurate information and an opportunity to report.
