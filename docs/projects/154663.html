Project Summary

The IRC will implement a holistic education response in Sinjar, Ninewa as well as Anbar and Salah al-Din. The IRC will aim to increase access to quality formal and non-formal learning opportunities for children in areas of displacement and areas of return which allow for transition into recognized educational pathways. This will be accomplished through support to formal schools via teacher training, ongoing coaching and mentoring, support to Parent Teacher Associations, tutoring programs for underperforming students, summer academic and recreational activities, and material support and rehabilitation to school buildings.
In areas where children are unable to return to school and there remain high numbers of out of school children, the IRC will implement Safe Healing Learning Spaces (SHLS), a secure, caring and predictable place where children affected by conflict and crisis settings can learn, develop, and be protected. An SHLS focuses on creating a safe and nurturing environment to help children cope and build upon their social-emotional skills (SES) while also focusing on key foundational skills, such as Arabic language and math. As children are able to return to school, SHLS will complement the formal system and continue to provide additional academic and social emotional support to underperforming children.
The IRC will also target children ages three to six years old with preschool healing classroom programming. Preschool Healing Classrooms provide children with critical pre-literacy, pre-numeracy, and SES in a structured, high quality learning environment in a protected, child-friendly space.

Needs

As Iraq shifts from emergency to early recovery, education must remain a top priority. According to assessments conducted in Salah al-Din governorate, roughly 25% of primary school-aged children are out of school. Top barriers to education include damage and destruction to school facilities and therefore availability of educational opportunities and distance to schools, as well as expenses associated with schooling such as tuition feed, supplies, and transportation. In Anbar governorate, according to assessments by the IRC all formal schools in Qaim and Anah districts were closed when the towns were under the Islamic State of Iraq and the Levant (ISIS) control; as a result all children of who stayed in these locations have been out of school for up to three years. Before the crisis, there were 121 functional primary and secondary schools in Al Qaim. The Province Council Director of Al Qaim reported that there are currently 31 (eight secondary and 23 primary) schools serving 12,000 children in Al Qaim district. None of these schools are currently receiving support from local NGOs or INGOs. The Provincial Council Director estimates that across the district approximately 40-50% of children are out of school today. The IRC’s household assessment found that 32% of households with children reported that at least one child was out of school. However, the IRC’s sample was relatively small (60 households) and constrained to three communities that were relatively safe to access. It is probable that enrolment rates are lower in less secure areas.KIIs conducted by the IRC with headteachers in Qaim center and Ubaydi village revealed that about half of the teaching corps in Qaim is composed of uncertified volunteer teachers and that certified teachers have not received a salary in over two years. School staff further report that schools have no electricity, heaters, or drinking water. There are very few books and stationary, and many schools have broken windows and other infrastructure damage. Interviews and KIIs in Rayhana found that there were three schools open before the crisis, but none are currently open. One school was destroyed in an air strike, and another was severely damaged from an explosion. The third school is structurally sound and has recently been cleaned by local community members; over 200 children have registered and classes are expected to begin in January, there is currently no electricity or heat, insufficient toilets, as well as a shortage of teachers and teaching and learning materials, including books.Focus Group Discussions with male and female community members in Al Qaim, Ubaydi, and Rayhana identified concerns around land mines, lack of gender segregated toilets, lack of electricity, and distance to school as major barriers to attending school. Additional barriers for girls include early marriage, and having to stay home to care for younger siblings. These reports were borne out of house hold assessments conducted by the IRC, which found that 53% of households reported that children are out of school because of a lack of functional schools or a lack of spaces in functional schools and 34% of households reported that children are out of school because schools are too far or the route to school is dangerous. Parents in all communities also reported that children who are currently enrolled are having difficulty concentrating because they have been out of school for three years. Teachers also expressed a concern that secondary school aged students may not be able to access secondary school. Teachers also report that children who are above primary school age may not be allowed back into primary schools. This could lead to a large number of out of school secondary school aged children, which is troubling given the complete lack of non-formal and vocational education opportunities. In Sinjar, before the ISIS occupation Sinjar City had a population of approximately 80,000 individuals. As of March 2018, the population of Sinjar City was approximately 22,000 while the population in Sinuni subdistrict was approximately 13,000 individuals. When ISIS occupied the city, many Yazidi families fled to the mountains or Kurdistan, while others were killed or captured. Children that fled to camps and host communities in Kurdistan may have enrolled in formal schools but children who fled to the mountains or were captured by ISIS have been out of school for up to three years. In November 2015, Peshmerga forces retook Sinjar from ISIS and the city was subsequently retaken by Iraqi Security Forces, following the Kurdish referendum. In Sinjar, overcrowding, as well as a lack of teachers, school buildings and teaching and learning materials as barriers to access. A mission report from UNICEF, UNHCR, UNOCHA, Health and Logistics Cluster mission report in December 2017 found only one functional school and 3,000 children out of school.

Activities

Safe Healing and Learning Spaces (SHLS) provide a secure, caring, and predictable learning environment where children living in conflict settings can learn and thrive. SHLSs help children to build social-emotional skills (SES) as well as foundational academic skills, such as Arabic and Math. The IRC will support the re-integration of these children into the newly opened formal school, and will adapt the SHLS programming to address any remaining needs. The IRC has female and male community mobilizers and facilitators to encourage both boys and girls to participate in these healing spaces. IRC staff also emphasize the importance of sending both boys and girls to school. This intervention will be support by the IRC’s Child Protection team who will leverage SHLSs to provide recreational activities for children and positive parenting classes for caregivers. If needs for SHLS programming persist beyond the end of the grant, the IRC will identify and support local partners to continue operating the space.The IRC’s Preschool Healing Classrooms offer children a safe, predicable place to learn and cope with the consequences of conflict. The program is tailored for preschools to provide content focused on the psycho-social needs of young children to learn the skills needed to enter school. Children ages three to six years old learn basic literacy and numeracy skills, how to express their needs, play cooperatively, follow rules and manage their feelings. These skills are developed through consistent, nurturing interactions with adult caregivers who provide children with a sense of comfort, security and confidence. The IRC’s teacher professional development program relies on both face-to-face training, as well as ongoing mentoring and coaching throughout the year through teacher learning circles and classroom observations. The IRC’s Child Protection team will also conduct trainings on child protection, safe identification and referral with primary school teachers. Following these trainings, IRC caseworkers will be available to respond to children requiring case management if there are no school counselors available. Where school counselors are available, the IRC will provide case management training and mentoring to ensure children referred to the school counselor receive appropriate care and support. The success and sustainability of both school rehabilitation and the establishment of a safe, secure, and engaging learning environment depends on school governance and the active participation of the school community, especially parents. PTAs provide a structured mechanism for parental engagement in children’s learning and help create a sense of ownership of children’s educational environment within the local community. PTAs receive training on their roles and responsibilities, mechanisms for engaging the community in children’s’ education, and the development of school improvement plans (SIP). The IRC strives for a gender balance in PTAs, by ensuring at least 30% female parents involvement and female and male teachers are included (depending on the gender ratio at the school). The skills and knowledge developed by PTA members will continue to benefit schools beyond the end of the grant. The IRC will work with PTAs and the school community to identify tutors and underperforming children who are enrolled in primary school. Children will receive tutoring sessions twice a week consisting of reading, mathematics and SEL to help them develop foundational skills to catch up to their grade level. The IRC strives for gender balance in tutoring activities, by ensuring that at least 50% of participants are girls. Community mobilizers and facilitators ensure that individuals with disabilities are informed of and able to access tutoring activities. The IRC will continue providing literacy, numeracy, and SEL classes during the summer recess for under-performing primary school students in order to improve fundamental skills between academic years. The IRC will also engage primary and secondary students in recreational summer activities such as art, dance, and organized sports. Summer activities will be led by teachers with the assistance of PTA and Parent Group members who are trained to teach reading and creative and expressive education techniques. The IRC strives for gender balance in summer activities, by ensuring that at least 50% of participants are girls. Community mobilizers and facilitators ensure that individuals with disabilities are informed of and able to access summer activities.Rehabilitation projects can help to reduce overcrowding and risks associated with decaying or damaged structures such as collapsing roofs and/or floors, broken windows, and uneven ground. The IRC proposes to rehabilitate or expand schools in order to address gaps resulting from the limited presence of educational actors in target areas. Learning materials will also be provided to schools based on needs identified by the school.

Protection Risk Analysis

IRC projects assume cooperation with local authorities and ministries, as well as community acceptance, and the schools welcoming the presence and support of the IRC. Particular risks could include: Risk of military action which could displace civilians and lead to the closure of schools or the IRC SHLS. Increased inter and intra communal conflicts may lead to increased tensions in targeted communities, especially in those areas where the population is composed by different ethnic, tribal and religious groups. The IRC aims to deliver high quality services to affected population, through highly qualified staff who are able to work in difficult environments. Considering the level of needs and the specific context of targeted locations, it may be difficult to identify and hire qualified staff directly from the areas of intervention.

Mitigation Measures

To mitigate the above risks, the IRC has certain contingency measures in place. In case of increased violence impacting humanitarian access, the IRC will monitor the situation on the ground including any shifts of alliances among all military actors (including the ones involved in the military campaigns and new possible ones). They will analyze incidents, develop strong links and local contacts in the area of intervention, and work to build community acceptance, enhancing and improving its mitigation measures. The IRC will maintain up-to-date information on all military actors on the ground and regularly update conflict and stakeholders’ analysis. A trigger monitoring process is in place to identify events that may lead to increased violence (both at national and local level) therefore affecting the capacity to deliver humanitarian assistance. The IRC will constantly aim at maintaining humanitarian access, negotiating with different actors and local contacts, within the framework of the humanitarian principles. In the worst case of lack of access to Sinjar, different mitigations measure will be put in place such as hiring facilitators and mobilizers from the target communities. If the community becomes inaccessible to IRC staff from the base offices, the IRC will provide remote support to the facilitators and mobilizers, set up a remote management system, and/or temporarily relocate teams to address previously identified needs in other areas. The IRC also closely monitors inter and intra communal conflicts while also completing conflict and stakeholders mapping which is carefully carried out and constantly updated. The IRC also ensures constant training and on-the-job mentoring to new hired staff to ensure the highest quality of service.

How is your project accountable to affected population

The IRC ensures that beneficiaries are involved at each stage of the project cycle from assessment and design to implementation and evaluation. Needs assessments have been or will be conducted in proposed areas of intervention with school leaders and education personnel as well as key informant interviews and household surveys. The IRC leverages both male and female enumerators for assessments and, when necessary, separates participants by gender to ensure they are comfortable sharing their thoughts and concerns. During needs assessments the IRC specifically investigates the unique challenges that boys, girls and children with disabilities face when accessing learning opportunities and incorporates these findings into program design. The IRC holds General Assembly Meetings (GAMs) at the start of the project, parents, teachers, and community members attended to understand who the IRC is and the project to be implemented. During the GAM, attendees elect parents and teachers to form the PTA for the duration of the academic school year. PTAs then work with the school community to develop school improvement plans (SIPs) which direct funds to school improvements, as well as informed school rehabilitation. Beneficiaries are further involved as teachers identify underperforming students for tutoring and summer programs, and members of local communities are hired as facilitators for tutoring and summer activities. Children themselves also suggest activities in which they would like to participate for summer recreation activities. During evaluation of the project, beneficiaries are also involved as the IRC has a number of satisfaction surveys to gauge the satisfaction of teachers, parents, and children. The IRC has found that PTAs and TLCs are very beneficial mechanisms to engage meaningfully with beneficiaries and understand the realities and challenges faced by these groups, and to solicit feedback to ensure programming is responsive to needs of affected populations. For example, TLC participants decide together on a monthly basis the topics they would like to cover in their coaching sessions based on their challenges and experiences in the classroom. In addition, based on discussions with PTA members regarding challenges in the school community, the IRC created new training modules for PTA members, on topics including student tardiness and violence against children. IRC teams use beneficiary feedback forms to formalize feedback and better incorporate it into the IRC’s learning. The IRC has also adapted a child-friendly version of this tool to properly solicit feedback from child beneficiaries for purposes of course correction and improvement of programming.
