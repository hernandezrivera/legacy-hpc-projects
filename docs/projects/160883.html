Project Summary

Since the beginning of the conflict in December 2013, the International Organization for Migration (IOM) has been providing lifesaving humanitarian assistance to Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs), returnees and host communities in conflict affected locations across South Sudan. The proposed project will build on IOM's significant experience in the provision of health and mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) services, and will continue to support rapid response mechanisms, which provide lifesaving health services to conflict-affected populations in hard-to-access areas. In 2020, IOM will continue to provide and scale up the provision of integrated primary health care (PHC), and MHPSS services through eight static Primary Health Care Units (PHCU), five mobile clinics, fourteen immunization focused static/mobile clinics and through eight rapid response team (RRT) deployments. The different modalities of response and conflict sensitive approaches enable IOM to ensure that services are accessed by both IDPs and conflict affected host communities, prioritizing the greatest needs.
Through static and rapid response teams, IOM will continue to provide essential preventative and curative PHC and MHPSS services in Bentiu, Malakal and Wau Protection of Civilian (PoC) sites, and other locations across South Sudan, targeting a total of 182,531 beneficiaries, of which 80,000 will be reached through mobile rapid response teams, and 102,531 will be reached through IOM’s static PHCU facilities. Health services will be provided in line with the Health Cluster’s essential health care package for PHCUs and Mobile Teams.

1 - Needs

Six years after the crisis, access to primary health care (PHC) services continues to be unavailable for a large majority of the population in South Sudan. Unpublished 2020 health cluster draft HRP indicates a total of 3.3 million people in need of humanitarian health care assistance, out of which 2,049,990 will be targeted. This figure includes 1,246,833 members of the host community and 803,156 IDP returnees, spontaneous returnees and returnees living in IDP like situations. The protracted conflict and unstable political situation, coupled with economic deterioration and inflation has created a disabling environment for a well-functioning health system. The 2019 nationwide Health Service Availability and Readiness Assessment survey (SARA) revealed that over 600 health facilities (HF) out of more than 1,800 across the country are inaccessible, while over 200 are non-functional. Procurement and transport of medicines is another challenge that leads to stock outs of medicines and medical supplies. The prevailing health system challenges are exacerbated by lack of water quality control, infection prevention and control measures, healthcare waste management, expansive malnutrition and a looming threat of viral hemorrhagic diseases, including Ebola, necessitating a need for holistic integration of health responses to ensure the necessary health securities to mitigate disease spread. Access to PHC services continues to be unavailable for a large majority of the population with one primary health centre serving an average of 50,000 people, and only about one in five childbirths involving a skilled health-care worker. Women are less likely to access health facilities due to customary practices concerning decision-making, lack of qualified staff able to understand their unique concerns, distance to facilities, competing responsibilities in the home, lack of female staff in medical facilities and reliance on traditional healing practices. Overcrowding in protection of civilians (PoC) sites pose a high risk of disease transmission especially for children, who are more vulnerable due to their developmental stage, poor nutrition, weak immune systems and inability to identify health risks. Vulnerable and marginalized groups in hard-to-reach areas are even less likely to realize their right to health, due to poor national health systems, distance to health facilities, neglect and a shortage of qualified health workers. Rapid response team (RRT) missions and mobile clinics often constitute the only access to health-care services for these populations. Long-term exposure to conflict and displacement continues to affect the population at large. Loss of and separation from family members, loss of property, lack of social support due to a disrupted social fabric, high rates of unemployment, and overcrowded living conditions in the PoCs represent risk factors for the development of mental health and psychosocial problems. The high level of learned helplessness among IDPs, as well as the lack of socio-economic opportunities, paired with traditional practices such as early marriage, puts especially adolescents and young adults at risk of mental health problems. In addition, women-headed households, and survivors of SGBV, are particularly vulnerable. IOM’s lifesaving emergency primary health care and MHPSS response through static, mobile and rapid response teams are vital to saving lives and alleviating suffering of the most vulnerable IDP, returnee and conflict affected host communities living in PoC sites and hard to reach areas outside of PoC. IOM will scale up its support from 8 static and 5 mobile facilities in 2019 to supporting a total of 27 static/mobile health facilities in 2020 in Rubkona, Wau and Malakal counties, covering both POC and outside of POC communities. IOM will also scale up its MHPSS services from 15 Recreational and Counselling Centres in 2019 to 17 in 2020 in Wau, Bentiu, and Malakal.

2 - Activities

IOM’s approach in providing health and MHPSS services for crisis affected population employs three response modalities, assuring to reach all populations in need according to the different vulnerabilities and site locations. The IOM approach includes groups in PoC sites, IDPs outside of PoC sites, conflict affected host communities, people living in hard to reach areas, and population affected by diseases outbreaks. The three modalities are:• Integrated Primary Health Care services through static health facilities.• Mobile clinic combined by out-reach community engagement interventions and• Rapid response health teams The clinical package of services that will be provided in the three modalities of response include:• Outpatient consultations for common diseases• Immunization for children;• Nutrition screening • Sexual and reproductive health services that include Basic emergency obstetric and neonatal care services (BEmONC); antenatal and postnatal care, PMTCT, family planning services• Clinical management of rape (CMR); • HIV and tuberculosis (TB) testing and treatment;• Integrated mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) through focused non-specialized services; • Non-communicable disease (NCD) management; • Early warning, alert and response to disease outbreaks;• Emergency reactive mass vaccination campaigns.• Water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) in health facilities; • Health promotion and development of key messages in collaboration with MHPSS teams; and referral to secondary level of care. • Training and capacity building for health personnel, from both the Ministry of Health (MoH) and national non-governmental organizations (NNGOs)• Capacity building for MHPSS mobile teams, for social worker trainees of the Ministry of Gender, Child and Social Welfare (MoGCSW) who use an integrated MHPSS approach• MHPSS Mainstreaming for health workers, protection actors, national and international non-government organizations• Coordination of the MHPSS Technical Working Groups at national and state level (Wau, Malakal and Bentiu)

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

Yes, community consultation and community dialogue sessions, community based surveys, and complaint, feedback and response mechanisms are some of the tools IOM uses to include perspective of the affected people in project design and implementation.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

Yes, IOM’s rapid response health team directly works with the County health department to develop the micro plan for intervention and steps to conclude the RRT response. In addition, the scaling up of the routine immunization services is a government owned project where IOM works in close collaboration and coordination with the Ministry of health. In Wau, Western Bahr el Ghazal, the MHPSS mobile teams work in close collaboration witch social worker trainees from the State Ministry of Gender, Child and Social Welfare (MoGCSW). The latter are trained in using an integrated MHPSS approach and help to strengthen the referral system between clinical, protection, and other community actors.

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

Yes, IOM follows the MOH basic package of health and nutrition guidelines in all of its projects. This includes adherence to the WHO and national list of essential medicine.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

In general, the situation, especially outside the PoC, is hard to predict as sudden political changes or inter-communal clashes may result in new displacement or security threats. However, as of recent, no incidents have been reported from the areas where the clinics and centres are and the current situation is stable. The clinics and recreational and counselling centres in the PoCs always allow safe access to medical and mental health care to the IDP population.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

Yes, IOM does capacity building of INGO and MOH staff in and outside of PoC activities. IOM clearly states the milestones and expectations in health facilities where IOM supports the County Health department. These clearly set expectations form part of the hand over strategy. The recreational and counselling centres, particularly in the host community, will be handed over to the community and the church at the end of IOM's project. For example, the stand-alone centre in Jebel Keir, Western Bahr el Ghazal, is built on land that is owned by the church and is therefore already owned and used by the community. Further, the local community is involved from the beginning of the project in designing the center and the schedule and nature of activities held inside and outside of the center. Communities also utilize the centre for community level activities such as community meetings, traditional activities and church activities on weekends.

8.18 - Please indicate how the risks selected above will be mitigated.

Health services will be provided in various modalities to meet population needs and access including static and mobile clinics, as well as rapid response team mechanisms, which provide lifesaving health services to conflict-affected populations in hard-to-access areas. This project will therefore serve to lessen the need for communities to leave their habitual place of residence for the purpose of receiving assistance. This will also serve to lessen the exposure of women and girls to sexual violence in relation to access to humanitarian assistance, and more particularly as a result of distance to and location of humanitarian assistance and services. IOM consults local communities to identify a relatively safe sites that also considers the different vulnerabilities of people with disability, elderly, girls, boys, men and women before establishing a point of care site. The area based/ integrated one stop service/shop strategic approach of IOM also encourages beneficiary to come to one spot in groups, mitigating violence that they could face if they were coming in smaller numbers. IOM strives to provide integrated health, MHPSS and WASH services in any area of intervention. Adequate staffing to provide services with minimum wait time helps patients and care takers return home before dark. Priority in service provision will be given to elderly, people with disability, pregnant and other vulnerable groups. IOM also reports any protection related incidents to the protection cluster and OCHA focal person for access so that protection is always kept high in the agenda.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

IOM client feedback reporting mechanism is put in place comprising a variety of confidential ways of reporting including verbal, telephone and written complaint mechanisms. The community dialogue sessions and the house-to-house health promotion session also include collection of feedback from the most vulnerable and hard to reach people like disabled, elderly, and bed ridden individuals. For those who are illiterate, verbal methods such as direct interview and participatory observation to collect feedback will be utilized. In addition, IOM has trained all its program managers on mandatory disability streamlining. Quantification of the prevalence of disabled people in the PoC and basic needs assessment surveys were conducted in collaboration with Humanity & Inclusion. The collaboration will continue to ensure that streamlining is institutionalized.
