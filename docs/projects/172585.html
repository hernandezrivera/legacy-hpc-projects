Project Summary

OCHA will in 2021 continue to support the Humanitarian Coordinator (HC) and the
Humanitarian Country Team (HCT) in coordinating the humanitarian response to meet the
(protection) needs of the most vulnerable people affected by the conflict / crisis/ impact of COVID-19 in Libya. Through Inter-Sector and Area-Based coordination, OCHA will provide mechanisms and
support to ensure that the interventions of humanitarian organisations are well coordinated and based on robust and timely evidence. OCHA will further facilitate and advocate for humanitarian access, the respect of humanitarian principles and ensure that the protection of people in need is effective.

1 - Needs

Insecurity and fighting triggered new displacement in the first six months of 2020, despite the call made by the Secretary-General for a global ceasefire in the face of COVID-19 in March. Displacement continued in the first six months due to escalation in armed conflict in western Libya. Significant changes to lines of control took place in June, with GNA-affiliated forces re-taking Tripoli, which has resulted in people returning to their homes. The recent improvements in security in the West since June 2020 accounted for the first reversal in the rising trend in displacement in more than 18 months. As a result, between June and August there was a reduction in the number of IDPs across Libya from 426,000 people to 392,000 people, with the majority returning to neighbourhoods in southern Tripoli. A significant constraint to people returning to their homes is the presence of booby-traps, IEDs, landmines and ERWs. Newly contaminated areas, such as Tripoli’s southern neighbourhoods, along with legacy contamination from years of fighting in areas such as Benghazi and Sirt, have prevented the return of many families and present a significant risk to people’s safety and wellbeing. The indiscriminate use of heavy weaponry in civilian areas has resulted in severe damage to housing and civilian infrastructure across the country. High displacement, along with significant damage to civilian infrastructure, particularly in urban areas, increased the demand for safe shelter spaces, adding pressure on their availability and affordability. As a result, rental prices have risen to levels that many poor and low-income families are unable to afford, putting them at increased risk of eviction. This risk is more significant for displaced families, migrants and refugees, as many do not have formal rental contracts. The ability of many people to afford rent, as well as to cover other basic needs, has also been eroded by the impact of COVID-19. Border closures, disrupted trade, movement restrictions and curfews have seen an increase in prices of essential food and goods and a reduced availability in markets. COVID-19 and associated measures have negatively affected all aspects of food security. Food insecurity has also increased among migrants and refugees, due to the impact of COVID-19 on livelihoods. In addition to increased food prices, delayed salaries and loss of employment have forced many households to adopt negative coping mechanisms in order to maintain food consumption. COVID-19 related movement restrictions and curfews have seen many people’s access to livelihoods negatively affected. The impact on livelihoods has been clear on both Libyans and non-libyans. Migrants and refugees, who are most likely to be employed through daily labour or temporary work, and women, who are mostly engaged in the informal sector, have been particularly affected due to movement restrictions or temporary business closures. At the same time there have been increasing calls for, and steps taken, to reenergize the political process. Moves made by parties within the country, and welcomed by the UN and Member States, demonstrate that there are opportunities to move forward towards a comprehensive political settlement. The end to the oil blockade agreed in October would allow GDP to rebound and a political resolution would enable the implementing of critical policies and reforms to strengthen institutions, stabilize the economic framework, and diversify the economy to generate jobs. This would enable the rebuilding of vital infrastructure, restoring public services, and reforming economic institutions. The three aspects- security, political and economic are all mutually reinforcing. Should the negotiations falter, the most likely situation is that humanitarian needs over the next 12 months would increase, rising to 1.8 million people by mid-2021. The most significant increase in needs is expected to be among displaced and non-displaced Libyans, as well as migrants.

2 - Activities

Libya is currently facing a complex juncture, where the prospects of peace or conflict escalation remain the same. OCHA is currently in process of conducting a follow up Peer-to-Peer mission to support the Humanitarian Coordinator and Humanitarian Country Team strengthen the delivery of collective humanitarian assistance and protection in Libya operation. However, with the static changes in the operational context, OCHA’s coordination support, hasto adjust to these ‘new normal’ conditions for the foreseeable future. Through theleadership provided by OCHA, common approaches will be developed under the auspicesof HCT in order to underpin effectiveness in humanitarian response. Activities in 2021 willinclude: • Support to the Humanitarian Programme Cycle (HPC), including. on a strengthening on the monitoring and evaluation part;• Humanitarian Access in line with the humanitarian principles that reinforces recent progress in establishing access mechanisms with the LNA and avoids individual ‘quick fix’ approaches that may jeopardize these mechanisms;• A prioritized approach to fundraising for humanitarian operations, in line with the prioritization set out in the HRP (or any alternative humanitarian planning framework). Also, efforts on how to better support in particular national NGO partners in responding to needs in ‘hard to reach’ areas will continue to remain a priority; • “Speaking with one voice” and “telling the story” on critical humanitarian issues remains important for the HCT to amplify the Advocacy efforts on behalf of the most vulnerable people affected by the conflict / crisis. Better data collection and analysis is imperative in this respect. • Effective and timely Preparedness / Contingency Planning is imperative in the volatile and unpredictable context of Libya.

6 - AAP/ Accountability mechanisms
6.1 - How is your project accountable to affected populations?

Common feedback mechanism

6.2 - Other accountability mechanisms

Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) is a commitment by humanitarian actors to be accountable to the people we seek to assist. In 2021, this includes the strengthening of Common Feedback Mechanism (CFM), to improve transparency with affected communities and enable them to easily access information related to humanitarian assistance. This two-way communication ensures that humanitarian agencies’ decisions are informed by affected communities’ views while enabling them to assess and comment on organizations’ performance, including on sensitive matters such as sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA). In this regard, the PSEA Network in Libya will support a coordinated approach to delivering on commitments to minimize the risk of SEA by humanitarian workers and ensure an effective mechanism for reporting that respect the principles of informed consent and confidentiality, and provides appropriate assistance where required.
