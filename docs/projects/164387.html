Project Summary

Deteriorated living condition due to the prolonged crisis has contributed to the potential epidemic of water-born disease outbreaks, such as diarrheal diseases caused by multiple viral, bacterial, and parasitic organisms. Adequate supplies of clean water are essential to maintain health and reduce the risk of epidemics caused using unsafe water especially in overcrowded setting such as IDP camps. The country has experienced recurring epidemics of emerging and re-emerging diseases the last few years across the country, including HAV and typhoid. In particular, IDPs residing in deteriorated environments are facing heightened risks of epidemic-prone diseases. As one example, in Al-Hol camp in Al-Hasakeh governorate, more than 11 000 cases of acute diarrhea, with 16 associated deaths, were reported among IDPs between 01 April and 30 June 2019. To prevent and mitigate the further spread of epidemic prone diseases, here and elsewhere, WHO will support case management by the provision of lifesaving medicines for communicable diseases.
WHO continues to assess the quality of drinking water in Aleppo (rural areas), Rural Damascus, Al-Hasakeh and Ar-Raqqa including IDP camps. During the monitoring, the different water sources such as water network, ground wells, reservoirs and jerry cans were tested by ATP measurement, chlorine residual, Ph, and culture test to identify pathogens. In 2019, 1501 tests in Rural Damascus, 217 tests in Aleppo, and 1191 tests were conducted in Al-Hasakeh and Ar-Raqqa. WHO also supports the public laboratories in water quality analysis and laboratory equipment and supplies, consumables were delivered to Ministry of Local Administration and Environment and Ministry of Health. Based on the results of the water quality assessment, water sterilization of contaminated water sources by chlorine tablets were conducted. More than two million chlorine tablets (NaDCC) were procured and distributed to Lattakia, Al-Hasakeh, Hama, Rural Damascus, and Aleppo including the IDPs camps and the collective shelters.
Despite WHO’s continued efforts, there is a still gap in access to safe water among the most vulnerable populations, causing preventable communicable disease cases. Also there is a significant gap in safety medical waste management in health facilities due to the deterioration of heatlh facilities and lack of skills and knowledge of infecton control and prevention. Medical waste management is the key to prevent any nosocomial infection among health care workers as well as the patient.
The proposal of WHO is to expand the activities of water quality monitoring as well as safe waste management in the areas of severity scale above 3.

4 - Needs

IDPs are among the most vulnerable in Syria. WASH assessment identified 10.7 million people are in need of support in water. In the northwest, the widescale displacements to IDP sites has contributed to a deterioration of already overused WASH facilities and increased the need for sustainable solutions. Access to WASH services has been identified as a top three priority by displaced people in all camps in northwest Syria. The water quality monitoring and response system is needed for securing safe drinking water in IDP camps in Aleppo, Deir-ez-Zor, Ar-Raqqa and Al-Hasakeh Governorates for ensuring that water tankers do not cause contamination. As regular water supply is being restored in areas of new returnees, water reservoirs are no longer being used. Thus, stagnant water remains in reservoirs and algae starts growing, creating health hazards. Such reservoirs must be flushed with heavy doses of chlorine or removed. Also, providing drinking water to reservoirs where network water is being provided wastes scarce financial resources. There is the high risk among returnees to have infection through the water due to the use of unsafe and uncontrolled water sources. The water network is often not available Returnees may start using uncontrolled drinking sources (i.e. shallow groundwater wells) due to a lack of regular network water supplies. The water quality monitoring programme would help locate and characterize water quality in existing groundwater wells as treatable or untreatable. This would allow UN agencies and INGOs to decide whether they should disinfect existing groundwater wells, provide drinking stations, or designate the groundwater wells as health hazards and hence install reservoirs. This would create more efficient water trucking programme and allow UN agencies to better coordinate the restoration of drinking water networks. To reduce morbidity-related water contamination and improve responses to disease alerts monitoring of water quality is essential.
