Project Summary

In line with the UNICEF Strategic Plan and the Health Cluster Strategy For The
2022 Humanitarian Program, the project is aimed at achieving equitable health outcomes
for the poorest, most-in-need and hardest-to-reach children. This will be achieved through
addressing humanitarian health needs while building the resilience of families and
communities against future health emergencies and linking with development
interventions. UNICEF will respond to emerging health emergencies in accordance to the
Core Commitments for Children in Humanitarian Action. The key modes of interventions
in this project are service provision, national capacity building, community mobilization
and advocacy.

1 - Needs

About 30% of the population of Sudan (12% urban, 39% rural) live more than 5 kilometresfrom a primary health facility, with a wide disparity of coverage by State. Only 24% ofhealth facilities provide a full package of primary health care services. An estimated 2million children under-􀂦ve are still affected each year by diarrhoea. The proportion ofchildren receiving appropriate treatment for diarrhoea increased from 15% in 2010 to59.3% in 2014 while an estimated 90% of children were given ORS or anotherrecommended homemade 􀂧uid47. Wealthier households were more likely to seektreatment. Despite efforts to reduce malaria in Sudan, its prevalence remained stagnant at5.9% in 2016, with 4.1% in urban areas and 6.1% in rural areas . Children under 15 years ofage are the most affected as well as those in the poorest quintile (9.9%) as compared torichest quintiles (1.3%). Con􀂧ict-affected states have malaria prevalence far above thenational average (up to 21.8% in one of the Darfur states). The prevention of malariausing insecticide treated nets remains low. Only 37% of people sleep under LLINs despite66% of houses having LLINs. An estimated 1.2 million children remain under the threat ofacute respiratory infections every year. Vaccination programs have been unable to reachall children in Sudan. The proportion of children 12-23 months who are fully immunizeddecreased from 49% in 2010 to 43% in 2014. Sudan experienced a measles outbreak in allstates between 2011 and 2015, which affected over 12,000 children. Populationmovements, con􀂧ict, inaccessibility of some areas, and inadequate funding coupled withineffective vaccines management all contributed to the increase in unvaccinated children.There has been one promising upward trend however. Children vaccinated withpentavalent increased from 58% in 2010 to 64% in 2014. Sudan has a high rate ofmaternal mortality, related to a lack of access to skilled birth attendants and antenatalcare. Maternal mortality was estimated at 311 per 100,000 live births in 2015 (thenationally accepted estimate is 216 per 100,000 live births from 2010 ). Less than half ofwomen in rural areas regularly visit antenatal care services. In con􀂧ict affected states,only a low proportion of women attend four antenatal care clinics. The estimated HIVprevalence rate in Sudan is 0.2 per cent among adults aged 15-49, with an estimated4,400 new infections annually. Prevention of mother-to-child transmission of HIV ishampered by women’s limited antenatal care attendance and the high level of stigmaassociated with the virus. In 2016, only 83,605 pregnant women were tested for HIV, ofwhich 88 were diagnosed HIV-positive. Only 56 of these women were enrolled in antiretroviraltherapy (ART) centres for care and treatment (MOH). The trend in continuum ofcare between 2006 and 2014 indicates gaps between interventions. “Silo programming”continues to lead to huge lost opportunities for integration of lifesaving child andmaternal health programmes, in a context of limited funding for the health sector. Sincethe 􀂦rst case of COVID was detected in Sudan in March 2020, Sudan has recorded 15,649cases of COVID with an attack rate of 35.3%. In addition to the direct impacts of COVID-19on the health of children and their families, interventions aimed at reducing thetransmission of COVID, such as restrictions to movement and closure of some servicedelivery points have resulted in interruptions of the routine health services and supplychains. In 2020 thousands of children missed out on vaccinations due to the impact ofCOVID-19. Subsequently, vaccine-derived poliovirus type 2 (cVDPV2) re-emerged amongSudanese children with a total of 44 paralyzed children in 13 states up to November 2020.Reductions in Maternal and Neonatal health, IMCI and nutrition service provision capacitywere also reported.

2 - Activities/Outputs

The following are the key activities to be implemented through this project: 1.Procurement and distribute the essential heath supplies including IPC supplies, PPEs,vaccines and the associated supplies and equipment, ORS, Zinc, IMCI kits, MW kits,LLITNs, IEHKs, AWD kits and other critical supplies; 2. Build the capacity of the front-linehealth care providers (women and men) at facility and community levels to deliver qualitylife-saving preventive, promotive and curative health services. this includes capacitybuilding on EPI, case management of childhood illnesses, PMTCT, maternal and newborncare service provision, case management of communicable diseases, COVID-19prevention and management and on IPC; 3. Support referral of severe/complicated casesto the secondary level of care. 4. Provide operational cost for health facilities, mobileclinics, outreach and mobile immunization teams to ensure that services points areopen/functional; 5. Support preparedness and timely response to health emergenciesamong the vulnerable populations including COVID-19 preparedness and response; 6.implementation of community-based and outreach/mobile health services to thevulnerable populations including the IMCI community component, ICCM, and outreachvaccination; 6. Printing and distribution of health related protocols, guidelines, trainingmodules, recording forms and registry books; 8. Strengthen monitoring and evaluation 9.Documentation of best practices.

5.2 - Impact on environment/natural resources

Climate change is one of the causes of the increased vulnerability of the population ofSudan to natural disasters including 􀂧oods, droughts and their consequences such aseruption of con􀂧icts related to competition for resources causing displacements,increase in vector-borne diseases outbreak and others. This project aims at supportingto strengthen the capacity of the health system in Sudan to make it more resilient toshocks as well as to be able to detect early signs of shocks before they happen tominimize their risks. Those localities with vulnerable communities related to theimpact of climate change are targeted through this project. The project’s support at thecommunity level will particularly contribute to community’s resilience to and ownershipof their own health as part of the mitigation measures.

6.2 - Explain how your project aims to ensure protection mainstreaming during its design and implementation

Bene􀂦ciary's safety and protection is one of UNICEF's key priorities in its allprogramming. It has zero tolerance for violence, including sexual exploitation, abuseand harassment. For the last couple of years, UNICEF has been training andsensitizing (both online and class room trainings) its staffs and implementing partnersto raise awareness on Prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA). UNICEFhas put an internal mechanism for detecting, mitigating and reporting any suchviolence not only those may be happening within its staffs bit also with itsbene􀂦ciaries. Because of its focus on this, UNICEF’s highest level (representative) isheld accountable at organizational level to put in place this mechanism. In addition,UNICEF has started screening its implementing partners for their sensitivity to thematter including assessing the mechanisms they have put in place to detect, mitigate,manage and report any violence, be it sexual or otherwise, against bene􀂦ciaries. Thefollowing principles underpin and inform UNICEF’s strategy: -The children, women, menand communities that UNICEF serves have the right to be treated with dignity andrespect and to receive assistance without threat of exploitation and abuse; -UNICEFhas zero tolerance for SEA and SH. As such, it has a responsibility to create andmaintain an environment that prevents sexual exploitation and abuse and aresponsibility to provide timely, con􀂦dential, and effective investigation, assistanceand support to survivors; -All UNICEF personnel have the right to work in anenvironment free from sexual harassment; -All UNICEF personnel are expected to actwith tolerance, sensitivity and respect for diversity. They have the obligation to ensurethat they do not engage in, condone or tolerate behavior that would constitute sexualharassment. The following are its strategic focus on preventing SEA and SH - AnOrganizational Culture of zero tolerance: Create and nurture an organizational culturebased on Accountability where there is a zero tolerance for SEA and SH, where rightsare recognized, promoted and protected and where violations are actively prevented. -Reporting mechanisms that are safe and trusted: Empower and support individuals,communities, UNICEF personnel and partners to feel safe to report violations and tofeel safe that reports will be handled in a manner that respects due process and otherhuman rights. - Swift and Credible Investigation and Sanctions: Ensure a fair processfor swift and credible investigations and sanctions for violations by UNICEF personnel,and actively promote swift and fair investigations and sanctions by (as appropriate)Government partners, implementing partners, commercial vendors, and other UNSystem organizations. - Survivor-Centered Response: Provide survivor-centeredassistance and support that is timely, predictable, sustainable and adequatelyresourced. - Engaging Partners in the 􀂦ght against SEA and SH.
