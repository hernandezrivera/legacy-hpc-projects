Project Summary

To protect livelihoods of vulnerable rural and urban populations and improve food
security through the provision of livelihood support during the main planting and dry
season (crop and vegetable seeds, tools, fishing kits, livestock vaccines and drugs),
support food security information, analysis and coordination and strengthen absorptive capacity and build resilience at household and community level.

1 - Needs

The protracted crisis, which began as a political conflict in December 2013, and quickly took a strong ethnic dimension, continues to drive humanitarian needs in the Republic of South Sudan, with levels of severe food insecurity reaching alarming levels. The unabated deterioration of food insecurity in the country is unprecedented, and in 2017, a famine was declared but quickly contained due to the urgent and sustained multi-sectoral response by the humanitarian community. According to the most recent Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) analysis, for the period December 2020 to March 2021, a projected 5.82 million people (48.3 percent of the total population), will face ‘Crisis’ (IPC Phase 3) or worse levels of severely acute food insecurity. If nothing is done to curb the severity and spread of the food crisis, the total number of food insecure is expected to deteriorate to an unprecedented 7.24 million at the height of the lean season (April to July 2021). The world’s youngest nation has been plagued by multiple hazards in 2020 including sub-national conflict and insecurity, catastrophic flooding (for the second year in a row), intercommunal violence and associated cattle raiding, large-scale displacement, a macroeconomic crisis, high food prices, proliferation of plant and livestock pests and diseases including the invasion of desert locust and the COVID-19 pandemic. All of which exacerbate chronic vulnerabilities underpinned by cumulative years of asset depletion and loss of livelihood, eroding farming, fishing and agro-pastoral households’ coping capacities to feed themselves and their families. The most recent flooding has left over 1 million people affected with the majority of those affected along the Nile, Lol, Sobat and Pibor rivers, and displaced almost 400 000 people (including with their livestock) to higher ground to escape flooding.The economy of South Sudan is marked by high and volatile inflation and widespread market failure. As the country’s only sizeable export, crude petroleum accounts for 98 percent of the country’s Gross Domestic Product (GDP). The national budget has been impacted by low production, a global drop in oil demand and collapse in prices. In the last year alone, the prices for seeds, tools and farm labour have doubled. The economic situation has destabilized markets, food systems, impacted on household access to food and income, and affected humanitarian operations.South Sudan is characterized by fragile and evolving institutional set ups. Lack of resources with limited or no Government institutional capacity and poor and limited infrastructure renders most of the country inaccessible during the rainy season and lack means to implement agriculture development programs with no or limited resources and absorption capacity committed to running them. This has in turn reduced trader appetite to participate in the local market economies, leading to eroded market functionality which further has made the procurement, prepositioning and distribution of humanitarian assistance complex and costly. More than one third of South Sudan’s population no longer lives at their home of origin. By September 2020, an estimated 1.6 million people were internally displaced while another 2.3 million people had fled to neighbouring countries since the War broke out in 2013. Most of these South Sudanese are further expected to slowly return back home in which the signed peace agreement has somehow stabilized the country except ongoing intermittent intercommunal fights. Displacement from farming areas (most notably Greater Equatoria – the green basket of the country) has severely compromised food availability across the country. This situation is exacerbated by economic stressors, weather extremes, water-borne diseases, malaria, livestock disease outbreaks and pests, such as Fall Armyworm and Desert Locusts.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

Cash interventions are informed by different sources of information on market functionality. The Joint Market Monitoring Initiative (JMMI) created by the Cash Working Group in south Sudan conducts markets assessments across the country on prices and market indicators to guide cash and voucher programming. The FAO’s Crop and Livestock Market Information System (CLiMIS) provides real time analyzed data and information on agricultural and livestock products to aid understanding of market performance and planning of relevant interventions. FAO also carries out rapid market assessments in intervention locations prior to guide implementation. Additionally, given the nature of FAO cash interventions, (with the objective of supporting increased/improved production/productivity), these market assessments are complemented with broader assessments including the CFSAM and SSSA which do have a specifically relationship to the agricultural markets, ensuring a holistic process of analyzing market functionality.

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

Cash and voucher is preferred because they offer multiple benefits to the local economy and the recipients of the assistance.Cash or voucher are a dignified form of assistance (where the conditions are favourable). They place the decision making power in the hands of the recipients to identify and meet their own priority needs. The recipients of the assistance have choice over type and quality, therefore their decisions are valued and influence the agro dealers/input providers to endeavor to deliver quality at competitive prices, in a bid to attract more buyers. The beneficiary has control, which is lost with in-kind support. Moreover, resources are directly injected into the local communities, enabling them to access goods/services from the local markets, therefore creating demand, which in turn stimulates supply. These boost the small and local traders and markets, improving their functionality therefore contributing to boosting the local economy.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

FAO is formally signed-up and committed to the IASC Commitments on Accountability to Affected People (AAP). These aim to produce quality humanitarian and development programs by maximizing the dignied and active involvement of communities in all aspects of programs that affect their lives mostly on how power Imbalance can be reduced. FAOSS has a functional feedback mechanism including suggestion boxes, feedback desks, toll free hotline number, AAP focal points that are trained and employed by FAO in each county. The various options for feedback and complaints are expected to facilitate access to the system to ensure the most vulnerable feel safe to speak up. FAO’s project designs and implementation make use of community committees that gather individuals with disabilities, who are not literate, women and youth representatives, among others, to participate and engage in the project. FAO is working with UN PSEA Task force on community feedback mechanism (CBCM). The NGO forum (national and International) is part of the PSEA task force on PSEA. They benefit from the CBCM set by the taskforce.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

FAO is committed to providing a high quality, accountable and dignified response, which takes account of the needs and capacities of children, women, men and vulnerable groups. Together with the international aid and humanitarian community, FAO has adopted the seven (7) Commitments on Accountability to Affected Populations, which means that FAO meaningfully engages affected people at every key stage of their work (assessment, design, implementation and monitoring). These commitments are:* Supporting staff to acquire the understanding, knowledge and competencies that will enable them to meet FAO’s commitments.* Communities receiving assistance should receive information about the services and support available in local languages. Means of communication are appropriate and tailored to local circumstances including communicating the targeting criteria and the available feedback mechanism Ensure technical information on inputs during the seed fair and distribution is provided to beneficiaries using Input leaflets in local languages. * Ensure the Channels are able to capture project based information and offer response to close the feedback loop* Ensure there is a feedback desk in every distribution point recorded clearly on the template.
