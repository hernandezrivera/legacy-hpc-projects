Project Summary

The project aims to enhance access to essential healthcare, addressing key causes of mortality and morbidity among Rohingya refugees and host communities in alignment with the Health Sector Strategic Plan 2024-2025. IOM will provide services across 35 health facilities (11 PHCs, 19 health posts, 3 hospitals, and 2 infectious disease treatment centers), adhering to the Minimum Package of Essential Health Services. These will include emergency and outpatient care, routine immunization, communicable and non-communicable disease management, palliative care, laboratory services, and sexual and reproductive health. Project will also focus on reducing the high prevalence of hepatitis B and C by enhancing early detection & expanding access to treatment. Facilities will be adequately staffed and supplied with essential medicines and equipment to ensure high-quality care.
The project will maintain a robust emergency referral system comprising a medical referral unit, 14 ambulances, and financial support for referrals in life-saving cases. IOM will support 3 secondary hospitals (2 health complexes and the Friendship maternity center) and upgrade services at primary health centers.
To manage potential diseases outbreak, IOM will operate two infectious disease centers. Integrated disease surveillance and case management services—including EWARS, sentinel testing, early diagnosis, isolation, and treatment. IOM will also lead the mobile medical team technical working group (MMT TWG), coordinating and building the capacity of 33 mobile medical teams to respond to emergencies swiftly and effectively.
To help reduce the high maternal and perinatal mortality, comprehensive sexual and reproductive health services including emergency obstetric and newborn care services (EmONC) will be strengthened at supported health facilities. IOM will further strengthen management of STIs including HIV/AIDs, family planning, adolescent sexual reproductive health services, and emergency referral of complicated obstetric cases along established EmONC referral pathways. Care for survivors of gender based violence including rape and intimate partner violence, will be strengthened across supported health facilities, women and girl safe spaces and MHPSS centers, using ‘one-stop-shop’ approach, while maintaining safe and confidential referral pathways for GBV survivors.
A total of 330 community health workers and volunteers (CHW/Vs), including 80 SRH referral assistants (formerly traditional birth attendants), will be engaged. CHW/Vs will act as frontline workers, conducting household visits, facilitating community engagement sessions, and collaborating with leaders for health promotion, preventive behaviors, and community surveillance. SRH referral assistants will identify, refer, and offer non-clinical support to mothers in labor across EmONC-supported facilities, helping to reduce home deliveries.
Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) services will be integrated into healthcare services, providing assessments, counselling, and support for moderate to severe mental health conditions, with referrals to specialized and non-specialized services. At the community level, MHPSS interventions will include support groups, socio-relational activities, creative arts, ritual and celebration activities, sports and play, psycho-education, non-formal learning, and self-awareness sessions, including activities at the Rohingya Cultural Memory Centre (RCMC) for the promotion of psychosocial wellbeing among the Rohingya Community through preserving their cultural heritage and identity. Additionally, IOM is supporting coordination and capacity-building for communities and stakeholders/partners and is co-chairing MHPSS working group. The MHPSS interventions align with the IASC MHPSS guidelines in emergency settings, IOM manual on community based MHPSS in emergencies and displacement, MHPSS minimum services package (MSP) among other guidelines.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

An estimated 1,003,394 refugees are residing in 33 extremely congested camps in Cox’s Bazar and on Bhasan Char Island (UNHCR, 2024). Based on recent data, 59% of individuals in the camps reported needing healthcare in the past 3 months, with 10% unable to obtain it. 76% of households reported barriers to accessing healthcare, with the most common issues being long waiting times and overcrowded services (45%), unavailability of necessary medicines or treatment, and incorrect medication. Additionally, 53% of households requiring healthcare had to pay for services, highlighting financial constraints as a significant access barrier. These challenges underscore the urgent need for accessible, efficient, and adequately resourced healthcare within the camps (J-MSNA, 2023; PHNA, 2024). In 2023, a monthly average of 123,414 outpatient consultations were conducted in 38 IOM-supported health facilities, which is almost similar as the year 2022. This is thought to be not only due to the increase in frequent disease upsurges/outbreaks, but also as a result of increased utilization of the health facilities by community. In 2024, around 45% patients were children and 21% were elderly; and more than 64.96% of the patients were female. Therefore, there remains the need for strengthening the health service with particular emphasis on quality improvement, community engagement and gender and protection mainstreaming.Women and girls of reproductive age continue to face siginifcant barriers to accessing essential sexual reproductive health and gender prevention and response services (UNHCR, 2024; UNFPA, 2024). Home deliveries rate remains alarmingly high at 51%, significantly contributing to the high maternal and perinatal mortality rates (UNFPA, 2024; MPMSR, 2023). A siginifcantly high proportion (29%) of pregnant women, reported to have missed antenatal care services in recent studies (PHNA, 2024). Despite recent studies indicating an improved uptake (55%), of modern contraceptive methods, and reduction (9.3%) of unmet need for family planning, the uptake of long-acting contraceptives still remains low at 7% (UNFPA, 2024). GBV remains a concern, especially for vulnerable populations including SOGIESC, exacerbated by increased insecurity in the camps and prevailing political uncertainities.Crowded living conditions, lack of proper WASH facilities and practices, and heavy monsoon in the refugee camps increase the susceptibility to infectious diseases. Routine immunization coverage remains a challenge, indicating a need for strengthened outreach and vaccination services in the camps. Since 2017, several outbreaks of infectious diseases, such as dengue, COVID-19, measles, cholera, and scabies were reported. Between October 2021 and September 2024, IOM health facilities managed 31,371 dengue cases. Rohingya refugees and adjacent host communities are also vulnerable to the impacts of natural hazards and disasters, including cyclones, heavy monsoons, and fire incidents. All these threats and public health emergencies warrant the need for a resilient healthcare system and strengthened emergency preparedness and response. During the regular community and facility based MHPSS activities, beneficiaries reported that stressors such as difficult living conditions in the camp and insufficient access to basic needs including the reduction of food rations to refugee households, and lack of livelihood opportunities, are contributing to mental health difficulties. All these stressors have a strong negative impact on the daily functioning and psychological well-being of not only the Rohingya refugees, but also the host communities. In addition, according to an ACAPS report published on May 2023, around 60% of the violence and security incidence reported in the Rohingya camps in Bangladesh since 2017 happened between 2022 and April 2023 . This is marked by increasing trends of crimes such as murder, kidnapping, and illicit drug trade.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

The project will incorporate protection principles and promote meaningful access, safety and dignity in health response with the following measures: a) ensure gender, age and disability inclusive healthcare service package; b) adaptation of health facilities infrastructure according to gender, age and disability friendly latrine, ramps and railings, waiting space and privacy; c) avoid causing harm implementing standard guidelines; d) facilitate training on Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) and Code of Conduct for staff, consultants, implementing partners, vendors. e) maintain inclusive and safe complaint, feedback and response mechanism; f) Provision of CMR and psychosocial support for GBV survivors; g) linkage with protection sector partners for protection concerns; h) deploy gender-balanced staffing; i) community dialogue meetings will be conducted with various segments of the community; j) collect, analyze and report gender, age and disability disaggregated data

2.3 - Age & Disability

Data collected will be disaggregated by age and routinely analyzed to assess the coverage, outcomes, and impact of the health response on different age groups. IOM will ensure that children can safely access health facilities, with or without a caregiver, and that facilities have child-friendly waiting areas. Linkages with camp child protection focal points will support engagement with unaccompanied children. The project will ensure health facilities are accessible for the elderly and persons with disabilities by providing transportation support, wide entrances, adapted infrastructure, and improved signage. Physical barriers inhibiting mobility for the elderly and those using assistive devices will be removed from all facilities.Special emphasis will be placed on adolescents, with Adolescent Sexual and Reproductive Health (ASRH) services integrated across supported facilities.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

IOM will engage a network of community health workers, volunteers, and focal points—primarily recruited from within the beneficiary communities—who will serve as primary communication channels between healthcare facilities and beneficiaries, including men, women, girls, boys, gender-diverse groups, older adults, and persons with disabilities. Monthly Community Health Facility Committee (CHFC) meetings will be held at each facility’s catchment area, allowing for beneficiary involvement, improving responsiveness to local needs, and increasing awareness of available services. IOM’s Community Feedback Mechanism (CFM), managed by the Communication with Communities (CwC) sub-unit, actively gathers beneficiaries’ views and shares this feedback with the health program team. This enables the health program to receive, process, respond to, and learn from beneficiary feedback regarding services. Additionally, IOM will conduct facility-based patient satisfaction surveys to better understand patient needs and enhance service delivery.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

IOM has a zero-tolerance policy for sexual exploitation and abuse and will strictly adhere to PSEA core principles. All staff, volunteers, vendors, and third parties will receive training on PSEA. Preventive and administrative measures, including sensitization meetings with staff, stakeholders, and beneficiaries, along with the distribution of IEC materials, will be implemented. A complaints and feedback mechanism will address SEA concerns, with contacts and hotline numbers for reporting readily available. In addition, IOM has a robust online reporting mechanism which is WEAREALLIN.IOM.INT, allowing anyone to report SEA incidents.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Yes, this project will support an environmentally friendly health system by equiping healthcare facilities with solar energy. This will ensure access to quality essential services for women and children, promote healthcare uptake, reduce costs, and protect the climate. Solar power will provide sufficient electricity for entire health facilities, powering medical laboratories, vaccine refrigerators, incubators, lighting, and autoclaving. Additionally, IOM will undertake retrofitting and infrastructure adaptation for health facilities to better withstand climate-related hazards. This will enable healthcare facilities to remain operational at optimal capacity during and after shocks and disease outbreaks, enhancing the resilience of healthcare services for affected communities.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

Emergency preparedness and response measures will be strengthened to build a resilient health system capable of addressing climate hazards like monsoons, disease outbreaks, fires, and cyclones. This project includes prepositioning essential medicines and supplies for rapid response and supports swift deployment of Mobile Medical Teams (MMTs). IOM will lead the MMT Technical Working Group (MMT TWG) and co-chair the Health Sector EPR technical committee, coordinating 33 MMTs. IOM will continue training and strengthening MMTs for public health emergencies, with a focus on mass casualty management, contingency planning, and SOPs to enhance resilience. Integrated case management and laboratory capacities will be expanded at two Integrated Infectious Disease Treatment Centers (IDTCs) and PHCs to support outbreak control and essential health services.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

Yes, this project includes and engages youth by providing tailored health services and actively involving them in community health initiatives. Adolescent Sexual and Reproductive Health (ASRH) services will be integrated across the 35 supported health facilities, ensuring youth-friendly spaces and accessible care. Additionally, through the network of 400 community health workers (CHWs), youth will be engaged in health promotion, resilience-building, and peer education activities. This approach empowers young people to take a proactive role in health and well-being within their communities, addressing their specific needs and enhancing their capacity to support health initiatives for both refugees and host communities.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

Yes, the project promotes self-reliance and resilience for refugees by strengthening healthcare infrastructure, empowering communities to manage health independently. Trained Community Health Workers (CHWs) and volunteers serve as frontline health promoters, building local capacity. Disease surveillance and emergency response integration further support resilience by preparing refugees for health emergencies, ensuring a stable healthcare environment in the camps.

4.2 Do project activities in the host community link to development initiatives, and/or align with development priorities in the Cox’s Bazar district? If yes, briefly describe.If yes, briefly describe.

Yes, the project aligns with Cox’s Bazar’s development priorities by enhancing healthcare access and emergency response capacities, consistent with the 2022 District Development and Growth Priorities. Support for primary and secondary health facilities aims to improve health outcomes for both refugees and host communities, contributing to resilient health infrastructure, addressing critical health issues, and fostering harmony. Additionally, the project aligns with the national agenda for public health sector growth, supporting district-wide health improvements and strengthening coordination across health sectors.
