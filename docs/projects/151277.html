Project Summary

The proposed intervention will improve access to quality education, increase access to critical WASH inputs and enhance child protection mechanisms in schools for 16,200 (8,910 boys and 7,290 girls) displacement-affected children and their surrounding communities in Somalia. Support to schools with large IDP communities in the catchment areas will be a specific focus. Education specific activities will include the provision of education supplies, emergency take-away food rations for students to meet their food and energy needs, emergency teacher incentives to motivate teachers to stay in the schools, and sanitary kits for adolescent girls to encourage their regular attendance at school. Specific WASH activities will include increasing schools’ water storage capacity through the provision of PVC storage tanks, the construction and rehabilitation of latrines, and improving hygiene practices among children through engagement with community hygiene promoters (CHPs). As CHPs will interact closely with students during hygiene promotion sessions and recreational activities, RI will train this cadre on hygiene promotion, psychosocial support (PSS), and child protection and safeguarding. RI will work with the Child Protection Clusters/ Working Groups in each area to ensure up-to-date service mapping is available. Mapping will then be utilized to guide any child protection referrals necessary in the project. RI will conduct a child protection school-level assessment to identify the particular child protection issues faced by girls and boys in schools. Results will be analyzed and action plans developed to strengthen school level child protection systems. Plans will include actions such as the training of child protection focal persons at each school, and awareness raising and training of community education committees (CEC) on child protection. RI, through CECs and other community volunteers, will deliver messages to schools and communities on key protection concerns affecting the community based on the findings of the child protection assessment. These messages will target prevention as well as the response in the event protection concerns arise. RI will also continue to engage with relevant government ministries. Relief International (RI) will provide training to CECs on their roles and responsibilities, food and water management, disaster risk reduction (DRR), civic skills, proposal development, and child protection in the respective schools. To ensure water availability in targeted schools, RI will provide cash grants to CECs. In return, CECs will contribute in-kind or cash to procure safe water and manage its distribution to students. Further, the CECs will be trained on DRR to build their capacity and improve school preparedness against future disasters. Child- and gender-friendly latrines in the targeted schools will be rehabilitated or, where necessary, constructed to minimize disruptions in learning for girls and boys. RI will coordinate a back-to-school campaign with CECs and religious leaders to encourage school enrollment with an emphasis on children affected by displacement. RI, in collaboration with the appropriate ministries, will assess the existing child protection mechanisms at the start of the project, and, as determined from the assessment results, targeted schools will develop appropriate action plans to address areas of concern. To measure the effectiveness of the child protections systems implemented and capture lessons learned, RI will conduct an endline assessment. Through engagement with ministry officials and CECs, RI will leverage the agencies' apparatus and community assets to raise awareness among children on child protection and the established referral mechanisms which will serve to strengthen child protection in schools and the larger communities.

Needs

Drought- and conflict-related displacement has drastically affected children’s access to education services. As a consequence of the knock-on effects of the prolonged drought and pervasive conflict, the education sector experienced the following effects: disruption of school calendar in some regions of the country, scarcity of adequate safe water in schools, scarcity of nutritious food for children and their families, and increased enrollments in displacement destinations resulting in overcrowded and resource-constrained schools. The FSNAU Post Gu 2018 identifies 4.6 million people to be food insecure and in need of humanitarian support. There are currently 2.6 million people who are displaced mainly due to conflict and drought. Livelihood pressures on families result in parents' inability to cover school fees and other related education costs further discouraging attendance in schools on top of limited physical access to education. Somalia is among the countries faced with a serious protection crisis. According to the Report of the Secretary-General on Children and Armed Conflict 2018, cases of violations against children including recruitment, abduction and detention increased in 2017. The violations include various attacks on education including detention of teachers who declined to adopt Al-Shabaab curriculum and forced schools closures.Despite the shifting political landscape in Somalia and strengthening government institutions, limited capacity of government and weak existing governance structures to respond to disasters have a direct link of the Ministry of Education’s (MoE) inability to reach nor prioritize the IDPs, children in hard to reach areas and other vulnerable population groups. From an RI conducted assessment in February 2018, female IDPs are consistently scoring the lowest and facing the greatest number of barriers in obtaining an education. Although boys were more likely to drop out than girls during the drought.

Protection risk analysis & Mitigating measures
Preventing risks of exclusion based on displacement

There is a crisis of displacement in Somalia that has been exacerbated by the prolonged drought, the pervasive conflict and knock-on effects of both. This has led to separations and child protection issues. The project specifically targets displacement-affected communities, including IDP settlements. Based on an awareness that children from displaced families are less likely to be enrolled in school, the ‘back to school’ campaigns will particularly target IDPs, including door-to-door visits to households in IDP camps. In areas where formal school facilities are not available, temporary learning spaces will be constructed/ rehabilitated to cater for the IDP population. In formal schools which have seen a recent influx of displaced children, RI will construct additional classrooms to accommodate the additional population. Teacher incentives, WASH provision and distribution of dry food rations, will act as an incentive to stay, thus helping prevent further displacement, and will meet the needs of those already displaced who are often most in need with a lack of available safety nets. The school child protection assessments and related analysis will consider displacement as a key area of concern: identifying any issues relating to discrimination by teachers or pupils on the basis of displacement status, and exploring the particular child protection challenges faced by girls and boys affected by displacement. RI conducts Post Distribution Monitoring, enabling us to verify that whole school populations have been reached with services such as food provision, ensuring displaced children and teachers are not excluded. Data collected by RI at the school and individual level is disaggregated by displacement status wherever possible.

Preventing risks of exclusion based on gender and age

RI acknowledges that there are differential risks for girls and boys and across the age cohorts. The project is designed to ensure the specific needs of girls are met in recognition of the particular challenges they face. For example, all school latrines will be built according to a girl-friendly design. In addition to the economic and contextual factors faced by girls in general, adolescent girls across Somalia are strongly encouraged to drop-out of school to marry; girls start menstruating and the majority have been subjected to FGM, the combination of which leads to adverse physical and psychological effects, with knock-on impact on their education access and performance. Activities that will particularly help meet the related needs including the provision of sanitary kits and menstrual hygiene information, and training of female teacher mentors to provide psychosocial and mentoring support to the girls. CECs will be trained on the different needs and challenges faced by girls and boys, and appropriate responses. School Development Plans developed by CECs will have specific activities targeting girls, including actions to be taken to keep girls in school and target drop-outs for re-enrollment. In training of CEC members, RI will insist on 50/50 gender spilt amongst participants as much as possible, recognizing the importance of female representation in school community management. Community sensitization campaigns, in which CECs will play a leading role, the value of education for both girls and boys will be emphasized. Child protection assessments in schools will include consideration of the specific issues faced by girls in the school, including areas such as harassment. As mentioned above, Post Distribution Monitoring exercises will be conducted to ensure that no children are being excluded from receiving services on the basis of gender or age. All data collected by RI at the individual level is disaggregated by gender, and further by age where appropriate.

Mitigating the impact of armed conflict and violence

RI recognizes conflict as a key factor affected girls’ and boys’ education in Somalia. Conflict causes migration of entire households and of individual children, which can reduce the ability of families to pay for schooling, and increase obstacles to re-enrollment in their new home. Children who have experienced or fled a conflict situation are likely to face more challenges in accessing and achieving in education: violence and upheaval may have a negative impact on psychosocial wellbeing and often leads to a higher likelihood of dropping out of school. RI’s research has found that transition rates among children in conflict-affected communities are significantly lower than in those less-affected by conflict. Additionally, RI’s evaluations has identified levels of self-esteem among girls in conflict-affected schools to be significantly lower than among girls in less conflict-affected schools. The approach of this project is therefore tailored to meet the particular needs of these children. A key approach is in the training of a female teacher mentor to act as a child protection focal point and provide psycho-social support to the children, who may be suffering from trauma. Critically, the project will provide sign-posting to ensure schools, teachers, and pupils, can access appropriate referral services. Children in areas currently affected by active conflict are one of the hardest to reach groups. RI has a dedicated team of local staff and strong remote management systems in place to ensure services can be delivered in challenging environments. Where communities experience extended school closure due to active conflict, RI will be supporting the provision of temporary learning spaces, which will also be meeting the needs of children who have fled conflict. An aspect of Community Education Committee training will include aspects of conflict management.
