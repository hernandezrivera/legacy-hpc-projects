Project Summary

As a strategy for 2023, Shafak, in line with the education cluster’s objectives, seeks to scale up quality access to formal and non-formal education for a total of 46,879 people: 45,279 school-age children (21,274 boys; 24,005 girls, including 1090 CWDs) and 1,600 education personnel (765 males, 835 males):
a) Formal education activities in 60 schools targeting approximately 35,279 students (16,614 b; 18,665 girls; 5-17 yr. old) through classroom learning modality (with plan B to resort to distance learning modality, based on developments of COVID-19). This intervention will minimize the risk of students dropping out, where monthly incentives to teachers will be provided, as they don’t have any alternative funds in addition to the provision of educational materials for students and teachers and providing schools with necessary operational costs (including heating for winter and school safety equipment) in addition to minor repair of damaged schools and establishment of safe temporary learning spaces through a gender-sensitive and disability-friendly approach. As education is one of the very basic children’s rights, Shafak is planning to provide access to education for children with disabilities by providing transportation services and improving the school environment by providing targeted assistance within the schools to encourage these children to continue their education and integrate them with their peers.
b) Non-formal education opportunities and pathways back to inclusive education at age-appropriate levels are increasingly required to address the complex learning needs of children through classroom learning modality (with plan B to resort to distance learning modality, based on developments of COVID-19), making use of 5 BTL campaigns conducted for that end. Thus, non-formal education will target school girls and boys (aged 8-12 and gender disaggregated) to fill in existing needs, where 24 Non-Formal education centers will be supported, targeting approximately a total of 10,000 children (4730 b; 5270 g; 5-17 yr. old) to provide SLP and catchup for 2416 children (1120 b, 1296 g) in addition to remedial classes for 7584 (3396 b, 4188 g) along with PSS sessions; the PSS activities will be selected from I-Deal curriculum; the curriculum used for non-formal Activities will be curriculum B that combines two academic years in one, SIG for remedial and Self Learning Material books.
Enhancing the quality of formal and non-formal education within a protective environment will be ensured through professional development and capacity-building sessions on child-centered pedagogy and positive classroom management to cope with overcrowded classrooms and address and accommodate host community and displaced children within their classes, in addition to regular monthly conducted PTA sessions.
Stakeholders in the project implementation site, including children, adolescents, and older persons, including PwDs, and their opinions are taken into account while developing the project design and implementation of activities so that they have better access to humanitarian services and information in accordance with their specific situation.

3 - Needs

Syria faces a multitude of challenges that make it one of the world’s most complex humanitarian and protection emergencies. Dilapidated learning environments are unconducive to learning due to overcrowding, lack of school furniture/ supplies, inadequate heating, operational WASH facilities, and electricity shortages have a multi-sectoral impact on critical services by impeding the availability and quality of education (HNO 2023).According to Joint Needs Assessment (JNA) 44% of school-aged children in the northwest of Syria (more than 800,000) are estimated to be out of school. This is fueling harmful practices and behaviors such as child labor, child recruitment, begging and family separation, child marriage, substance abuse, and suicides.Shafak has conducted a recent rapid needs assessment in Jan 2023. The findings are summed up as follows: when respondents were asked about why children drop out of school, the top three reasons were as follows: 95% asserted that children drop out of school due to the need to work to help their families, 60% of respondents asserted that transportation cost is a major challenge for them, while 57% mentioned students with disabilities do not have proper safe access to schools. The main three factors contributing to the well-being of children in the targeted locations are summed up as follows: family and extended family, teachers’ support, and school fun activities.

4 - Implementation of activities

The field teams will consist mainly of education specialists, field supervisors, assistants, field engineers, outreach teams data and entry workers, who will be responsible for reporting directly to the project managers at Shafak HQ, who in turn will provide due support and be responsible for overall leadership of the project. To do so, Shafak will make sure to involve stakeholders, including children, including PwDs and their opinions in the implementation plan and that their voices are heard and taken into account while specific needs assessments are conducted and while implementing activities so that they have better access to humanitarian services and information in accordance with their specific situation. These consultations include FGDs with the participation of different target beneficiaries, including stakeholders such as teachers, school management, local authorities, and students themselves (with age and gender taken into consideration) in order to identify particular needs as highlighted by the various groups. Other crosscutting issues such as GBV protection, child protection from violence, exploitation and abuse, and rights of vulnerable groups will be mainstreamed in the project. Shafak will start by validating the needs in the target schools through field surveys to reassess the needs. Through these surveys, Shafak will identify the exact schools’ needs, children (girls and boys) and teachers (females and males) numbers in the proposed schools.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk1: Occurrence of COVID-19 cases in the targeted communities among children and education personnelMitigating Measure: daily communication and coordination with ED and other Edu and health organizations to know the latest updates about COVID-19. Suspend the schools immediately and prevent any kind of physical attendance at schools and shift into the adopted remote education modality. Risk 2: Occurrence of diarrhea cases in schools among children and education personnel because of unsafe potable water Mitigating Measure: to recommend water needs increase (more frequent hand washing with soap, laundry, and cleaning of surfaces; oral rehydration solution [ORS] preparation, etc.)Risk3: Child safety incidents in school due to bullying and overburdened teachers MM: Training of partners and local teams on Child safety, development of schools’ specific child safety policies; establishment of complaint mechanisms in school; Security/safety training for organization, students and school staff.Risk4: Protection risks of mixing different age groups in the same centre/class MM: Create separate times of attendance for each age group.Risk 5: Some targeted teachers may receive salaries from a third party, or are forced to get part of their incentives deducted MM: Confirmations are obtained from all education directorates and education actors that no teacher is permitted to get paid gain from any other party.


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

Risk: Sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA), including harassment, against women and girls by distributors and male beneficiaries while queuing and before/after distributions Risk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure: - recruit male and female volunteers who are respected in the community and independent of armed groups. No children to be employed (under 18 years old) - make sure staff are already trained on Do No Harm, protection from SEA (PSEA), and child safeguarding; and sign and adhere to code of conduct that respects and uphold the rights of beneficiaries and PSEA - set up communication and complaints mechanisms or desks that are clearly marked; face interviews with beneficiaries, including women and children. - Having clear set pathway with PSEA focal points in place to follow up on any SEA complaints.


9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1. Risk: borders are temporarily closed, or humanitarian access restricted, between Syria and its neighbouring countries because of national regulations, of advances by GoS towards neighbouring countries' borders. Mitigation: - ongoing advocacy with relevant authorities for humanitarian access to vulnerable populations in Syria. Dividing stock of commodities between Syria and neighbouring countries in anticipation of risk; prioritizing local procurements (inside Syria).2. Risk: Existing traditional practices and community structures conflict with child protection principles (e.g. corporal punishment, child rights messaging is perceived as a threat, etc.). Mitigation: - thorough contextual analysis to be undertaken and ensure safe access as part of the project design; - teachers are trained on positive discipline techniques as part of their basic training and sign a code of conduct in the school; - teachers and classrooms are regularly monitored; a child safe-guarding focal point is identified in the school; - Parent-Teacher Committees are consulted on any reports from their homes of children being mistreated.
