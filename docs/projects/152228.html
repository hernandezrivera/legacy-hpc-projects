Project Summary

PROGRAM BACKGROUND AND CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS
The Northeastern states of Nigeria remain a volatile area that is subject to political violence, heightened corruption, conflict, and a growing population of out-of-school children and youth estimated at about 1.8 million, mostly girls and orphans displaced by the spade of armed violence that bedeviled the region as a result of the activities Boko Haram insurgents. An assessment of community early warning early response mechanisms conducted by CCDRN in Gubio, Magumeri, Nganzai and Mobbar LGAs of Borno state in 2017 clearly reveals that high rate of illiteracy amongst youth and adolescent girls as one of the key drivers to violent extremism (VE) in the areas. Consequently, community members and stakeholders identified the provision of safe, quality and protective education opportunities for less privileged children and youth as an effective response measure to prevent them from joining Boko Haram and other violent extremist organizations, thereby sustaining peace in the area through education empowerment. Recent survey (MICS 2016-17) reported Borno as having a high number of girls out of school in Nigeria, with about 71% of school age girls having no access to basic education. A Human rights watch (2016) report titled “They Set the Classrooms on Fire: Attacks on Education in the North East Nigeria” revealed the deliberate and systematic destruction of schools, killing, maiming and abduction of learners and teachers had negative impact that deeply affects the social and economic well-being of the communities. School age children and teachers in Borno state specifically, are traumatized by the horrific experiences resulting from the conflict, a situation that places them on dire need of psycho-social support (PSS) services to cope with the negative effects of the conflict, through emotional stability and restoration of the almost lost hope for access to education in safe and protective learning environments. There is also an urgent need to engage the local communities through the formation of community coalitions (CCs) in supporting education as a longer-term strategy for recovery, resilience and sustainable peace building.
Sensational headlines about Boko Haram depredations have overshadowed the fact that for decades the North East states of Adamawa, Borno and Yobe have suffered chronic outbreaks of conflict and violence, which are deeply rooted in Nigerian history and politics. The states are characterized by political insecurity, high incidence of poverty, and outbreaks of violence by Boko Haram insurgents. Political imbalances and injustices, entrenched social inequalities, corruption, and persistent fear and insecurity has led to disparities in income and access to economic and educational opportunities between males and females, urban and rural residents, high and low economic groups, and northerners and southerners.
The provision of quality schooling is one the most powerful symbols of government credibility and among the most coveted services by households. Failure to provide services and its unequal distribution undermines citizen support to government, as they see their children excluded from future social and economic opportunities, and increases the potential for destabilization. Despite the UBE Act of 2004 (Universal Basic Education Program), which provides for free and compulsory education for nine years for every Nigerian child, the unavailability, poor quality, and unfriendly school environment of formal government schools – coupled with household poverty, cultural and religious constraints, early marriage for girls, weak social protection for vulnerable children and concerns for student safety – have resulted in large percentages of school-age out-of-school children and youth.
The Federal Government of Nigeria (FGON) views education as an important instrument for national development and is committed to expanding access to quality schooling. It recognizes the potential of community-based and accessible religious schools – including ITE and Christian schools – as a means of expanding learning opportunities outside of the formal school system. The National Committee on Implementation of Almajiri Education, set up in October 2010, was tasked with mainstreaming Qur’anic schools into the Universal Basic Education Scheme. So far, it has developed an overarching framework to address the needs of out-of-school children, conducted a series of advocacy meetings with major stakeholders that led to community buy-in, harmonized curricula for the Almajiri Education Program, and adopted alternative models of intervention. These integrated schools are providing basic and post-basic education to out-of-school children and youth, and should be scaled up.
An assessment conducted by CCDRN in April 2018 on the causes and effects of drug abuse amongst youth in Gubio and Mobbar LGAs shows that more than 60% of youths and adolescent girls (10-17 years) in the two major towns engage in intake illicit drugs due to absence or poor state of social and economic facilities such as schools to meaningfully engage them. Most parents send their daughters for hawking at the expense of schooling mainly to restore their livelihoods lost during the insurgency and even with return of relative they cannot afford providing them with materials such as school uniforms and books to enable them return to school.
The current situation in Borno state generally requires an improved and equitable access to basic education program for both boys and girls that will integrate skills acquisition, WASH and peace building studies to produce a generation that can sustain the relative peace returning to the state and ameliorate the current humanitarian and development crisis bedeviling the state and of course the northeast.
Program Approach;
Our technical approach to rapidly expanding equitable access to quality education for out-of-school children and youth takes advantage of the contextual reality that many out-of-school children are actually attending IQTE schools, whose curriculum focuses exclusively on Islamic values and recitation of the Qur’an. We will increase the number of trained LFs, and the quantity of quality learning materials in literacy, numeracy, basic social studies, and basic science. CCDRN will incorporate peace-building skills, psychosocial counseling, and SEL lessons to promote a culture of peace. Working through LGEAs and community structures, we will expand the number of NFLCs in existing IQTE schools, where children and youth will learn the integrated curriculum. We will establish other non-formal centers at AGLCs and YLCs that may be operating out of community centers and/or public/Islamiyya schools to provide an array of wraparound support services for girls and youth who may be enrolled in other NFLCs. Our approach will be to collaborate with CBOs and SBMCs to support and manage these efforts. We will establish Community Coalitions (CCs) that will advocate for continued non-formal education opportunities for children. We will build support among state and local government officials so that government funding is phased in to support these programs, thus making them sustainable. The project will establish appropriate Consultative Groups state, local and community levels to provide political support and technical guidance to the project. All our activities will be designed and implemented with full recognition of the complex environment and some guiding principles. Our approach will:
Rely on existing structures and assets. CCDRN’s will leverage her deep knowledge of the LGAs, relations with key decision-makers, and stakeholders to quickly mobilize support and begin activities. We will review and adopt existing teaching and learning materials to meet current needs and trends in quality instruction delivery.
Be adaptable and flexible. The situation in all three LGAs of Gubio, Magumeri and Mobbar in Borno is fluid and unstable. Awareness of unfolding events is crucial to success, as is recognizing that the situation will be different in each community – specific dynamics and relationships will influence methods of engagement with stakeholders. We will adapt our approaches by continually assessing the security and political situation and adjusting as required. We will coordinate with other actors within EIE sector and beyond.
Embed our work in the community. The best practice for long-term sustainability is to work through existing community structures. Our approach is to work through the SBMCs and CCs, to build local ownership and commitment to an array of local education options.
Work with conflict sensitive awareness; CCDRN appreciates the fact that Education in conflict environments can mitigate or exacerbate tensions. Working with a conflict sensitive lens is crucial. This means paying attention to whom we work with and the signals it sends; developing school-based conflict indicators; ensuring the right LFs are in the right schools; curricula is appropriate to the target population; and that local partners are inclusive. Rolling assessments will help us remain aware of the impact of our work and changes in community perception. Our grants program allows us to adapt quickly to changing needs and circumstances. Do No Harm principles will be infused all our activities
Proposed goal, purpose, and activities;
CCDRN’s conflict-sensitive and community-centered strategy, and technical approach reflect the deep contextual knowledge of Borno state and the critical role education plays in mitigating conflict and crises and fostering resilience, social cohesion, and peaceful coexistence in communities. Our overall goal as derived from the Education in Emergency (EiE) sector strategy is to expand access to quality, protective, and conflict sensitive formal and non-formal education (NFE) opportunities for out-of-school children, ages 13-17 years, in Borno state. This approach builds on robust local traditions of Islamiyya, and Tsangaya education (ITE), options used by nearly all out-of-school children in the north. To accomplish this goal, we will work within three interdependent intermediate results:
Strategic objectives:
Objective 1 - Conflict-affected children and adolescents have access to inclusive quality basic education and vocational skills opportunities within a safe learning environment

Our activities include collaboration with state and local governments, CSOs, community leaders, and parents to conduct assessments, establish benchmarks for establishment of non formal learning centers and identify formal schools and mobilize for enrolment. We shall be targeting conflict affected children aged 3-12 for early child learning program (ECLP) and basic education Primary 1-6 in formal schools respectively. Youth and adolescent girls between ages 13-17 shall be targeted for enrolment into youth learning centers (YLCs) and adolescent girls learning centers (AGLCs) respectively in the Non formal learning centers (NFLCs). We will integrate literacy, numeracy, age-appropriate life skills, social and emotional learning (SEL) as well as hygiene education into existing ITE options to establish the Non-formal Learning Centers (NFLCs). To address infrastructure deficit and mainstream protection into our programming, CCDRN through collaboration with Local education authorities, school based management committees (SBMCs) and other stakeholders conduct needs assessment to identify infrastructure needs in schools. This is to employ a need-based approach to construct and rehabilitate safe and protective classrooms and other education facilities as may be identified through the assessments.
Objective 2 - Conflict-affected children and adolescents receive quality and conflict sensitive educational services to enhance their resilience
Our activities will address the need for more Learning Facilitators (LFs) and more teaching-learning materials to support increased enrollment and quality of instruction. We will increase the pedagogical skills of LFs and school teachers in their numbers on PSS and life skills through training of trainers approach. Our activities include implementing the training program for LFs in the newly created NFLCs, and establishing a coaching system to support LFs. In collaboration with state universal basic education Board (SUBEB), state agency for Mass Education (SAME) and local government education authorities (LGEAs) we will develop standards for quality effective materials in literacy, numeracy, social emotional learning (SEL) and Life skills for the NFLCs and provide government approved teaching and learning materials in core subjects of English, mathematics, Basic science and social studies to learners and teachers in the selected formal schools. Both teachers and learning facilitators will be trained on strategies for delivering quality instructions, adopting a conflict sensitive and child-centered approach. Basing on the findings of our initial needs assessment, we will provide gender-sensitive WASH facilities for learners and teachers in schools and learning centers, in addition to organizing weekly health talks to promote learners’ WASH literacy and practices in schools and the wider community. In this drive, learners especially the adolescent girls and youth will be provided with dignity kits on regular basis to enhance their hygiene practices and in the overall encourage their enrolment, attendance and retention.
Objective 3 - Communities have increased capacity to participate in school development and risk reduction planning and demand equitable access to conflict sensitive quality education for all children and adolescents

Our activities are designed to strengthen community ownership and government support for schools and learning centers. Activities are designed to build knowledge of key stakeholders including establishment, strengthening and training of Community Coalitions (CCs), and building the capacities of existing school based management committees (SBMCs) to plan and execute awareness raising campaigns to strengthen demand for education. Based on our community engagement experience, we expect that communities will quickly assume responsibility for expanding equitable access to quality education for all learners in the NFLCs and schools. Funding and strategic technical guidance from project staff and government will rapidly mobilize communities to meet their educational challenges. Through the SBMCs, CCs as well teachers, we will establish school based peace clubs to enhance learners’ acumen to maintain peace in their schools, NFLCs and communities.
Targets and Anticipated results;
By the end of 2019, we anticipate establishing 100 NFLCs comprising of Girls learning Centers (GLCs) Adolescent Girls Learning Centers (AGLC), and Youth Learning Centers (YLC) as well as supporting conflict affected children 15 formal schools in 3 LGAs that benefit over 15,000 children and youth, including children of IDPs, returnees and host communities.
Theory of Change:
CCDRN’s theory of change will contribute to achieving EiE sector strategic objectives base on the following assumptions:
• If Conflict affected children in ITE and other non-formal learning centers incorporate core subjects (literacy, numeracy, and SEL), and are provided support to improve educational quality in these subjects, this will provide more options for out-of- school children to attend and stay in school, and acquire literacy and math skills to help them access further education and life opportunities.
• If Islamiyya, Qur’anic and Tsangaya and other non-formal learning centers provide life skills, psycho-social support and referral services, students will increase their well-being and strengthen their resiliency and ability to function productively in conflict environments.
• If civil society and communities are sensitized and encouraged to be actively involved in education including School-based Management Committees and other formal management structures, they will advocate for increased resources for formal schools and non-formal learning centers, and will encourage families to enroll, keep, and support their children in school.
• If state and local governments, non-formal center proprietors, and civil society work together to craft standards and align policies, procedures, and practices for non-formal learning centers, support for and quality of non-formal learning opportunities will increase and be sustained.
The critical assumption for delivering the program as proposed is that the conflict will not escalate into civil war which would require significant retooling. If security situation continues to improve, our planned semi-annual rolling assessments and routine M&E data collection will make us aware of the shifts in level and types of conflict, and we will be able to make modest or major program adjustments as needed. Our flexible approach will allow us to adjust the LGEA and NFLC location and design and requirements.
Project management:
CCDRN’s Education experts who have the experience in EiE programming in Borno and Yobe states under USAID funded education access projects will lead the project management team to supervise key technical personnel - who are specialists in Education in Emergency, M&E, Water sanitation and Hygiene (WASH), Gender based Violence (GBV)/Protection, learning materials development, communication and reporting as well as Community Mobilization and partnerships. Other operational staff dedicated to this project include; security and operations manager, Finance and Administration manager and Logistics and procurement officers. The culture of coordination and team work are deeply rooted in our program policies.
Program Principles:
Education as every Child’s right: Our programming will a right-based approach to providing education opportunities for out of school children in Borno state. Selection of beneficiaries shall be conducted fairly without any form of discrimination. Benefiting children would also be trained in life skills to stabilize their emotions and recover from their traumatic experiences. Skills acquisition programs for especially the youth and adolescents will enhance individual household livelihoods that will ensure hawking and begging are never seen as a substitute to attending schools and learning centers. Parents and care givers will also be empowered through cash based incentives to enable release their wards to attend school. Learners and teachers will also be supported with scholastic and instructional materials that will make teaching and learning a fun.
Do-no-harm, Protection and gender appropriateness: Education is central to minimizing harm and supporting psychosocial well-being for conflict affected youth. Educational interventions provide a safe and stable environment for learners and restore a sense of normalcy, dignity and hope by offering structured, appropriate and supportive activities. Many parents and children regard participation in education as a foundation for successful childhood and positive transition to adulthood. We will minimize harm and maximize the transformative potential of education and ensure safe and protective learning environments. The delivery of education services will be fair, transparent, and equitable, cutting across gender, age, language, ethnic groups, and localities, and responding to identified needs.
Critical principles that are embedded in our Do No Harm approach include: maximize participation by communities; evaluate safety and protection needs; monitor safety issues in and around the learning centers; make learning centers ‘zones of peace’; identify key potential external and internal threats such as bullying; violent punishment, gender-based violence, and child recruitment and build awareness and skills to prevent and respond to these threats; rapidly establish safe and child and youth friendly learning environments; integrate relevant, context-driven topics into the learning process; create space for children and youth to express their emotions and experience, and help learners to understand and support one another
Community participation and local engagement;
Through or project on livelihoods, CVE and peace building in Borno, CCDRN effectively mobilized and engaged communities in Magumeri, Gubio and Mobbar LGAs to demand better quality social and economic services form the supply-side actors at various levels. We will apply key lessons learned and build on relationships with communities and other stakeholders for successful program implementation. Our engagement with local stakeholders starts at the design stage – we have solicited input from communities, government stakeholders, and civil society on this project design through key informant interviews and focus group discussions. Described below are ways in which we will engage local stakeholders during program implementations and our understanding of their role in conflict mitigation and peace building.
Situational analysis, dialogue, advocacy, setting up community structures, training and capacity building are key elements of our approach to local engagement. We will develop and coordinate an advocacy strategy with state and local government structures, and will be carried out through the SBMCs, CCs, local chapters of Christian Association of Nigeria (CAN) and Jam’atu Nasril Islam (JNI), and CBOs, targeting traditional and religious leaders, faith-based organizations and local communities and other support groups and stakeholders. The participatory dialogue and advocacy process helps raise awareness, break down barriers, and create an enabling environment to launch NFLCs and integration of core academic subjects while concurrently building trust and ownership among stakeholders. We will work with LGA stakeholders to set up CCs that will bring together representatives from a cross-section of stakeholder groups, including CBOs, faith-based organizations, traditional and religious institutions, and local advocacy organizations to support services delivered through the NFLCs, GLCs AGLCs, and YLCs such as healthcare and other social programs. Both the SBMcs and CCs will play a leadership role in advancing the concept of NFLCs and mitigating conflict in the communities in which they operate. CCDRN will strengthen CCs and SBMCs skills in problem solving and analysis skills, prioritization, action planning, self-management, and M&E processes through a core group of trained facilitators. We will train them on peace building and conflict mitigation.
Our approach to local engagement also includes building capacity of CBOs working in the target LGEAs to provide educational, and other wraparound support services to vulnerable populations. Once CBOs are identified, we will use our organizational capacity assessment (OCA) tool to help identify key needs and develop performance goals related to project activities. We are in building capacities of Community groups in conflict settings with series of effective organizational and group management modules on strategic planning, project management, organizational governance, human resource management, fundraising, financial management, advocacy/communication, and M&E. We will work with local experts to develop a learning/collaboration platform to promote dissemination of technical and programmatic content on education and conflict analysis to better sensitize communities. We will also establish feedback loops across all intervention communities to generate community views and perceptions on our work, with the of making adjustments in our programming where necessary and avoid conflicts.
Monitoring and reporting.
Our M&E approach will be multi layered that will ensure accountability and learning from the complexities of program implementation and adjusting to the dynamics of a conflict environment. Our M&E plan provides for monitoring progress, quick response to needed programmatic changes, and evaluation of outcomes-of-interest. We will create feedback loops through quarterly meetings and dialogues at the LGEA and community levels to discuss progress. With key stakeholders, we will finalize indicators and targets. Data collection will be as follows:
Initial (Baseline) assessment: A comprehensive, multipurpose initial assessment will be carried out start up that will include both community education and conflict analyses. Within the first month of starting, we will conduct a rapid participatory assessment) in the three targeted LGAs to identify NFLC sites as well as schools and design parameters. The assessment will: (i) map and enumerate ITEs and their catchment areas, develop profiles, and determine ITE ability and willingness to host a NFLC; (ii) identify out-of-school children’s learning and support needs; (iii) explore parental demand for and attitudes toward schooling; (iv) assess LGEA capacity to serve as partners and provide oversight; and (v) identify the type and degree of conflict/instability in the area. For each LGEA, we will assemble three teams, who will be responsible for collecting data in 3-4 wards, using quantitative and qualitative methods with structured instruments to facilitate rapid analysis. We will also conduct an initial conflict analysis to identify the type and degree of conflict/instability in the three LGAs, and determine how it affects education, stakeholders, and education service delivery.
Rolling assessments: We will conduct rolling assessments every six months to monitor project performance and “complexity awareness” to inform mid-course design, implementation and management decisions. This will include: (i) effects on beneficiaries, and (iii) changes in the conflict environment. We will use Quality Assurance Sampling methods to keep the sample size small, yield statistically valid results, and provide easily analyzable data. Our instruments will focus on key or sentinel factors of performance and conflict. We will use multiple methods including: school record review, school and classroom observations, student and teacher interviews, and parent and community focus groups
Routine tracking: In quarter 1, we will develop a system of computerized reporting forms for use by project implementers (e.g., NFLCs, CSOs, and CCs) to document ongoing activities, such as LF training, and materials/module development and distribution that can be easily aggregated.
Student, Teacher, School and Community Performance Assessment: In quarter 2 and 4, we will conduct student performance assessments using a pre-post design to determine changes in Children’s learning and SEL outcomes, LF and NFLC performance, and community engagement. We will use instruments adapted to NFLCs and formal schools respectively. We will develop a baseline to measure learning prior to entering the program and at the end of the one-year program
SBMCs Capacity Assessment: To assess increased capacity of SBMCs as a result of the project’s capacity strengthening efforts, we will use an organizational capacity assessment tool administered with SBMC at pre and post capacity strengthening activities.
