Project Summary

Sudan's ongoing food insecurity endangers people's lives, means of subsistence, and stability. A large portion of Sudan's population is at risk of food insecurity as a result of the protracted conflict, environmental degradation, and other disasters such as drought and floods, in 2023, there would be an estimated 11.7 million individuals in need of FSL assistance in Sudan.
This project is designed to improve the food security and livelihood status of 29,650 (14,825 Female, 14,825 male) vulnerable agro/pastoral individuals directly, as part of the project activities, 11,600 individuals (5,800 males, 5,800 females) will receive agricultural assistance, 3,650 individuals (1,825 males, 1,825 females) will receive livestock assistance, and 14,400 (7,200 males, 7,200 females) will receive cash or vouchers to support their livelihoods, as well as their families indirectly. The action is expected to reach up to 177,900 people including children, youth, adolescent girls, people with disabilities, women-headed households, IDPs, and Returnees in South Kordofan, North Kordofan, North Darfur and Kassala States. The project interventions will allow households to grow their own reliable food for family consumptions while earning some money from the sale of the surplus. This will be conducted through improving farmers and pastoralists awareness on good agricultural practices, provision of improved seeds and essential agricultural hand tools, distribution of milking goats to enhance the availability of milk and meat for home consumption and conducting a livestock vaccination campaign to maintain the households' animal assets in the targeted communities and vouchers for goods or services will be provided directly to affected people to meet one or more of their basic humanitarian needs.

1 - Needs

Sudan is experiencing a serious humanitarian crisis and a complex development situation. Since the military coup of 2021, the country is facing an increasing humanitarian caseload due to political fragility, economic deterioration, violence, floods and displacement. These factors have resulted in an increase in poverty levels and unemployment rates among citizens. Droughts across 115 localities in 14 states have affected 5.6 million people. The increasing prices of agricultural inputs led to a decrease in overall harvest size by around 35% from the previous year. According to the Sudanese Humanitarian Aid Commission (HAC), 349,000 people were affected since September 26th (2022) by seasonal rains and floods in 16 out of 18 states. 24,860 homes have been destroyed and 48,250 damaged, further impacting food security.According to the Humanitarian Needs Overview report (HNO 2023) the level of acute food insecurity increased for the third year, to a record 11.7 million people (49% women 51% men). From the affected population 48% are children and 15% are persons with a disability. Women and girls, particularly adolescent girls, face additional challenges having significantly lost control of and access to vital resources such as land, livestock, income and farm inputs, severely affecting their decision-making ability and participation in the household (HNO, 2023). Additionally, women and girls face the double burden of having to carry out household work as well as livelihood activities to support their families. Many have been forced to sell their livelihood assets for income and cultivation is low, thus increasing their vulnerability to disasters and recurrent shocks.North Darfur: Food insecurity in North Darfur increased by 18%, from 38% in 2021 to 56% in 2022. The prevalence of severe food insecurity also increased from 4% to 14%. The localities with the highest prevalence of food insecurity were Malha (90%), Tawila (80%) and Kebkabiya (75%). Mahla has the highest level of severe food insecurity in Sudan at 59%. With a semi-desert climate, North Darfur is prone to droughts and low rainfall, and has a geological system that is unfavourable for groundwater storage. Late rains last year resulted in crop failure and a poor harvest in the 2021-22 season. Sorghum production in the traditional rainfed sector was 90% below the five-year average 6,000 tons compared to 61,000 tons. Kassala: Food insecurity in Kassala remained at 17% in 2022. However, multiple localities have high levels of food insecurity, such as Hamshkoreeb (39%), Rural Kassala (19%) and Aroma (19%), with 7% in Hamshkoreeb severely food insecure. The northern parts of Kassala host over 124,000 refugees and are also considered chronically food insecure and vulnerable to recurrent droughts, floods and tribal conflict. Due to extended dry spells and poor rainfall, the harvest was poor. Sorghum production in the semi-mechanized sector in the 2022 season was 79% below the five-year average, with 45,000 tons compared to 213,000 tons. North Kordofan: Food insecurity in North Kordofan decreased by 9%, from 39% in 2021 to 30% in 2022. The prevalence of severe food insecurity also decreased from 7% to 2%. The localities with the highest level of food insecurity are Al Rahad (43%), Gabrat Al Sheikh (38%) and Om Rwaba (33%). In Sodari and Shikan respectively, 4% are severely food insecure. North Kordofan is a drought-prone state, with generally infertile soils and low crop production.South Kordofan: Food insecurity in South Kordofan increased by 2%, from 28% in 2021 to 30% in 2022.The prevalence of severe food insecurity increased from 3% to 4%. The localities with the highest level of food insecurity are Dallami 52% Habila 45% and Elgoze 44%. Elgoze also has the highest level of severe food insecurity at 12%. South Kordofan has been at the centre of the protracted civil conflict and land disputes, with over 270,000 IDPs and 40,000 South Sudanese refugees.

2 - Activities/Outputs

The project will operate in four different states namely South Kordofan, North Kordofan, North Darfur and Kassala States to achieve the overall objective of supporting self-reliance of affected households through protection and building productive assets and restoring or creating income-generating opportunities to save and sustain lives by the end of 2023. 1. Agricultural Support for 11,600 household (69,600 people at an average of 6 people per household): by providing agricultural extension services that provide technical advice to farmers, and providing them with the inputs they need to produce their crops, such as improved seeds and hand tools. 2. Livestock Support for 3,650 households (21,900 people): by distribution of productive livestock units as livestock are central to the livelihoods of the target communities. Integrated into mixed farming systems, these will increase overall farm productivity and provide households with meat, milk, and revenue. Further, the project will provide treatment and livestock vaccination campaign to protect the animal assets of the households in the selected localities. 3. Livelihood Cash/Voucher Support for 14,400 household (86,400 people): to allow affected populations to recover their livelihoods through the purchase of essential assets, access to services and/or debt repayment, also allow new, more productive and/or resilient livelihoods to be established.

6.2 - Explain how your project aims to ensure protection mainstreaming during its design and implementation

Plan will prioritize safety and dignity to ensure no harm is caused and will seek to identify, prevent and mitigate any unintended negative effects or risks during the intervention to girls, boys, children, young people, and project participants. Plan will target the most vulnerable without discrimination and ensure that all participants are aware of and has access to necessary and relevant services. Plan International implements ‘Do no Harm’ measures to ensure the safety and dignity of children and young people that interact with its staff, associates or volunteers. It has zero tolerance against all forms of abuse and exploitation as articulated in its policy on safeguarding for children and young people. Plan will continue to engage with the local authorities to ensure that activities are context appropriate and will orient all third parties working with and for Plan and keep signed acknowledgements of the safeguarding policy thereafter. Activities will be underpinned by human rights principles and will strictly adhere to the core humanitarian standards and maintain the principles of humanity, neutrality, impartiality and independence. Plan maintains high standards of safeguarding and preventing sexual harassment, exploitation, and abuse PSHEA, especially in emergency situations. Plan’s Safeguarding Policy and procedures are implemented during the design and implementation of project interventions with vigilance. Safeguarding and PSHEA risk analyses and assessments of project interventions will be conducted with mitigation measures/contingency plans identified for each risk. Plan will work to ensure staff, partners, project participants and communities are aware of the safeguarding and PSEA policies, its minimum codes of behaviour, and requirements to uphold high standards of professional behaviour. Project participants, especially children and young people will be respected, and their safety and protection will not be compromised when obtaining and using information on and images of them.Plan will identify groups at risk of unsafe livelihoods practices and prioritise provision of alternatives. Plan will identify the power dynamics (who can access assets and inputs and who can influence decisions) and will monitor and respond to potential exploitative practices or abuses. Information will be communicated through various means to reach the wider community and account for different literacy levels. All layers of the affected population, especially those with intersecting vulnerabilities, will be consulted in identifying and responding to FSL needs to collect accurate information about specific requirements and preferences. Plan will encourage communities to play an active role in identifying solutions and in the decision-making processes to promote a sense of ownership, build self-esteem and improve the sustainability of the response. Collective livelihood projects will be promoted contribute to community protection and resilience.

7.2 - Please outline how the project or your agency will inform and interact with inter-agency approaches to accountability to affected people and feedback mechanisms, including SEA and abuse complaints?

Plan International maintains high standards of Safeguarding and PSHEA (preventing sexual harassment, exploitation, and abuse), especially in emergency situations. Plan’s Safeguarding Policy and procedures are implemented during the design and implementation of project interventions with vigilance. Safeguarding and PSHEA risk analyses and assessments of project interventions will be conducted with mitigation measures/contingency plans identified for each risk. Plan will work to ensure staff, partners, beneficiaries and communities are aware of the safeguarding and PSEA policies, minimum codes of behaviour, and requirements to uphold high standards of professional behaviour. Plan is a member of the AAP/PSEA network and mechanisms and interacts with UNCT/UNOPS call centers and AAP focal points in other organizations that is set up by the humanitarian actors in Sudan. Plan will contribute to enhancing technical standards and AAP and PSHEA mechanisms and approaches based on lessons learned and experience from Plan globally. Plan has robust training and capacity building manuals that can enhance AAP and PSHEA roll out, reporting, and responding. Plan will also ensure to participate in needs/information assessments on AAP and PSHEA as initiated by the AAP network as appropriate. Plan International is committed to mainstreaming principals of accountability throughout its intervention in accordance with the Core Humanitarian Standard on Accountability and Quality Management. Plan International will that target groups and communities have access to information that is relevant to their needs by creating opportunities to persons of concerns and served communities (including girls and boys, adolescents and youth) to participate in programming decisions and to provide feedback on the project interventions using existing and established community feedback and response mechanisms (CFRMs). The CFRM system has multiple entry points, designed with communities to be accessible to all community members regardless of gender, age, mobility, socio-economic status, or any other differentiating factor. Entry points include telephone calls, suggestion boxes as safe entry points to allow target beneficiaries to share their feedback/complaints, feedback forms, community meetings as well as making visits to the Plan office. The feedback/complaints received are collected by the CFRM focal point, classified, and analysed and confidentiality is ensured throughout the process. Complaints and feedback are addressed in line with Plan’s internal procedures and responses are made to those who filled the feedback/complaint. Feedback/complaints concerning other project stakeholders (government staff, partners etc.) working in the same areas will be referred to their senior management as appropriate for the concerned organization to take the necessary actions.
