Project Summary

Children living with disabilities (CWD), particularly those living with severe disabilities (e.g. down’s syndrome, cerebral palsy, monoplegia/quadriplegia), are today the most socially and economically vulnerable group of children in the country. They are more likely to be poor than others and have more difficulties in accessing services. In addition, they are often discriminated against. In a context where poverty is widespread and where there has been significant downscaling in the government social spending, families with children with severe disabilities have even less opportunities to provide for their livelihoods – mainly because of the full-time care requirements, the disruption of social care services and social stigma.
Since 2016, UNICEF is implementing an innovative integrated humanitarian social protection programme to address both economic and social vulnerabilities of CWD and their families. The programme combines regular and un-conditional cash transfers with case management services to facilitate the referral of CWD to available social services and increase their social inclusion. This integrated response is providing a lifeline for CWD in Syria. It enhances the resilience of the most vulnerable families in the face of the ongoing socio-economic crisis. It also strengthens the continuum of humanitarian and early recovery interventions. In 2021, the programme will target 14,000 CWD in 8 Governorates.
While the cash transfer responds to the “demand side” barriers in terms of financial barriers to access services, the case management ensures that certain “supply side” barriers, such as unfavourable attitudes or reluctance of schools to enroll children with severe disabilities are being addressed. In addition to referral to public sector services, the case managers help refer CWD and their families to other types of assistance provided by national and international relief organisations. The services of the case managers also include (i) devising individual action plans for girls and boys enrolled in the programme to address their specific needs and circumstances; and (ii) facilitating the referrals of care givers to livelihood support initiatives, such as vocational and entrepreneurship training or microfinance services. Therefore, the case management is a vital component that seeks to empower families to better respond to the needs of their children, beyond the life of the cash transfer intervention.
Accountability to beneficiaries is ensured through two main channels: (i) UNICEF centralized redress and grievance mechanisms; and (ii) regular Post Distribution Monitoring Surveys (PDMS) conducted throughout the life cycle of the programme to collect feedback from beneficiaries.
The findings of the 2019 PDMS have shown tremendous positive impact of the programme in terms of reducing school drop-out, increasing access to essential specialized services, reducing negative coping mechanisms of families (64% no longer needed to cut essential health care expenses and 30% no longer had to compromise food intake and spending on education), and increasing investment for children (165% and 74% increased investment in education and health care respectively).

4 - Needs

The conflict in Syria has inflicted immense devastation on Syria’s economy, social fabric and children. Families across Syria face severely limited employment opportunities, skyrocketing prices and shortages of basic goods and services, and widespread deterioration of household coping mechanisms. The conflict has been accompanied by significant downscaling in the government social spending. State Budget allocation to MoSAL have decreased in real terms by over 55% between 2020 and 2021 (State Budget 2021). This has led to the complete breakdown of critical national social protection programmes, including the pre-conflict programme for children living with disabilities. The national registration of children with disabilities has been largely disrupted, making it very challenging to provide estimates of children with severe disabilities, particularly those falling under the criteria of the programme: 1- Severe intellectual disabilities2- Profound intellectual disabilities3- Autism4- Down's syndrome 5- Monoplegia6- Quadriplegia7- Cerebral Palsy8- Spinal Cord Diseases9- Lower limbs loss at both sides 10- Upper limb loss at both sides The programme targets a very specific group of highly vulnerable children according to the “Syrian National Classification of Disabilities”, thus limiting the relevance of the geographical severity scale measurement. The identification of children follows a lengthy process of identification and verification using medical certificates to ensure that children identified are indeed eligible for the programme. The average ERL severity scale in the 126 sub-districts where the programme will be implemented is 3(one third are severity scale 2 sub-districts and two thirds are 3-5)

5 - Implementation of activities

For a 12-month period, families of CWD receive a regular quarterly transfer of US$ 120. In addition, each child is followed throughout the period by a dedicated and trained case manager. UNICEF uses the services of 2 financial services providers (FSP) for the distribution of cash. Cash is being handed over the counter to beneficiaries. In 2021, where feasible, beneficiaries will receive an ATM card. A solid monitoring system to ensure transparency and effectiveness of the programme is in place. The FSP follow international standards in terms of financial management and practices including the frequent disclosure, reporting, and the managing and monitoring of solvency risks. The FSPs also follows adequate procedures related to detecting money laundering. The programme is implemented by national NGOs specialized and accredited to work with CWD. IPs are responsible for the identification, registration, and verification of beneficiaries, and for providing case management services. All case managers receive professional training based on a comprehensive package introduced by UNICEF. There is a strong multilayer monitoring mechanism to monitor the effectiveness, efficiency, and impact of the programme: (i) randomized verification by UNICEF of beneficiaries’ eligibility covering 5-10 per cent of the registered list; (ii) Case Managers regular on-site monitoring and home visits; (iii) Post Distribution Monitoring Surveys; (iv) UNICEF verification of payment lists; and (v) FSP verification of beneficiaries based on their national IDs. In addition to the monthly reporting to the sector to ensure better coordinated response, UNICEF also use a data collection system to report on the beneficiaries and geographical locations reached.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk 1: social tensions generated either by lack of clarity on eligibility criteria, or harassment towards those receiving the cash transfer. Mitigation measures: clear communication strategy on eligibility criteria using brochures during the screening, identification and registration phases; strict verification mechanisms, on-site monitoring including post monitoring surveys and beneficiaries’ satisfaction monitoring; complaint and response mechanism through a dedicated service line managed by a UNICEF Accountability Assistant to respond to complaints and share feedback; and every child is assigned with a trained case manager who follow-up on the child situation throughout the period.Risk 2: Abuse or exploitation by the IPs. Mitigation measures i: pre-screening of partners and close monitoring of the implementation through the third-party monitors, including verification of the registration process, on-site monitoring of the distribution and post-distribution monitoring exercises; IPs are required to develop an internal procedures to identify, track and respond to possible cases of abuse, exploitation or gender-based violence.; and UNICEF systematically train its IPs on prevention and Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse, and abuse of authority and conflict of interests. UNICEF’s approach to social protection emphasizes the need to prevent and reduce economic and social vulnerabilities. The linkages between social protection and child protection are embedded in the design of the cash transfer scheme through the case management component, which includes addressing risks of gender-based violence and sexual exploitation and abuse, which are particularly high among the targeted children.

7.1 - Please list any risks related to sexual exploitation and abuse that have been identified during the project design phase and any risk mitigation and prevention measures that have been adopted and built into the project

One of the main protection risks in this programme is the risk of abuse or exploitation by the implementing partner. This risk is mitigated by pre-screening of partners and close monitoring of the implementation through the third-party monitors, including verification of the registration process, on-site monitoring of the distribution as well as post-distribution monitoring exercises. The implementing partners are required to develops the necessary internal procedures to identify, track and respond to possible cases of abuse, exploitation or gender-based violence. Furthermore, UNICEF systematically trains its implementing partners on prevention and Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse.

8 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

The overall assumption is that the socio-economic situation will continue to deteriorate and that the need of children with disabilities will aggravate further.Risk 1: Delays in obtaining official approvals leading to implementation delays. Mitigation measures: (i) development of standardized agreements with the IPs across targeted governorates to ensure speedy review; (ii) establish good communication channels with MoSAL officials to ensure all challenges are shared and addressed in a timely manner.Risk 2: The COVID-19 pandemic can slow down the implementation and affect the delivery of case management services Mitigation measures: (i) closely monitor the situation of COVID-19; (ii) implement alternative ways of delivery of cash (i.e. ATM rather than cash over the counter); and (iii) providing PPE for all case managers/social workers.Risk 3: Cultural factors impede inclusion of girls in the programme.Mitigation measures: (i) strengthen the gender component of the case managers/social worker training manual; (ii) pro-actively identify girls through dedicated case managers; and (iii) identify the specific and broad support needs for the protection of girls.Risk 4: High level of inflation limit the effectiveness of the cash component.Mitigation measures: (i) negotiate with the Central Bank a preferential exchange rate; (ii) adjust transfer values on inflation.
