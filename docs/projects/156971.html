Needs

The war in Syria continues to negatively affect livelihoods opportunities for individuals and communities in Northwest Syria. According to the HNO 2019 sector overview, "eight years into the crisis, the main drivers to food insecurity are prolonged displacements, loss and lack of livelihoods and reduced production capacity which in turn resulted in limited physical and financial access to food, high prices and inflation contributing to reduced purchasing power and continuous depletion of livelihoods assets of the most vulnerable populations.” As per current estimates, 6.5 million people in Syria are food insecure, while a further 2.5 million people are at risk of becoming acutely food insecure. This in total constitutes over half of Syrian population who are in need of urgent live saving and life sustaining food, agriculture and livelihoods assistance. A monthly ration with staple items costs at least 80 percent of an unskilled laborer’s monthly salary and 50-80 percent of a public service employee’s monthly salary thus demonstrating the existence of “working poor” in Syria. IRC assessment data shows many households are unable to meet their basic needs, including food, due to the lack of consistent income and disrupted livelihoods. Recurrent displacements, drought, and disruption to value chains have contributed to severe levels of poverty and unemployment, depleted savings, and a heavy reliance on daily or illegal labor. Internally displaced persons, rural households, women headed households, as well as houses headed by children and the elderly are typically the most susceptible to adopting negative coping strategies. Women and girls are at particularly high risk of gender based violence and early or forced marriage, while young men and boys are at high risk of recruitment and child labor. While markets are largely functioning and prices for staple goods have largely been stable in 2018 in Idleb Governorate, the lack of job opportunities, income, and critical community social services, mean that people are more vulnerable to shocks and stresses and more likely to resort to negative coping mechanisms. In the approximately 19,000 household-level vulnerability assessments conducted by the IRC in 2018, a majority of households did not report earning enough income to meet their monthly basic needs, with an average of 20,000 SYP compared to an average of 174,000 SYP in household debt and with an average of 0.2 members of a household working. Households report barriers to employment and income including a lack of capital to start new ventures, lack of experience in skilled employment, and a lack of qualifications or degrees. Women have particular barriers to employment as reported by this and previous assessments by IRC and other actors; while women are more active in the labor market now compared with before the war due to the need to supplement household income, 90% of women are unemployed (compared with 25% of men) and societal constraints still exist that pose barriers to seeking employment outside the home, including transportation issues. In addition, women generally have lower levels of education and are thus disadvantaged to find well paying work, and rely heavily on agricultural activities for income which are often low paid and seasonal jobs. In addition, in most work women make less money per hour then men. Youth, both male and female, have also missed critical educational opportunities as a result of seven years of war, and are unable to find skilled employment; instead they rely on daily labor and are more likely to turn to dangerous jobs such as working in fuel burners.

Implementation of activities

The IRC will provide multi purpose unconditional cash grants to extremely vulnerable households, including recently displaced IDPs, households headed by women, the elderly, people with disabilities, rural households and vulnerable host community members. Multi purpose cash assistance, while unrestricted by nature, is predominantly spend on household food security. An IRC assessment showed that approximately 70%-80% of a MPCA grant is spent on food. The amount of cash transfer will be coordinated with other organizations and based on the Survival Minimum Expenditure Basket. Assistance will be multi round (up to 3 months) or one time depending on the situation, need and availability of funding. The IRC's beneficiary selection process relies on economic vulnerability scoring but also ensures that particularly vulnerable groups, including people with disabilities, female headed households, and the elderly are automatically qualified for cash assistance. As only distribution cash to women in the household could lead to significant protection risks, cash will be distributed to the head of household, regardless of gender, in order to not increase tensions in the household. As household level support, IRC's MPCA benefits the entire household including youth members. Driven by food insecurity, households can exhibit negative coping strategies, such as pulling youth out of school, youth joining armed groups or taking on exploitative employment in order to meet food security needs for themselves and their households. By providing MPCA to vulnerable food insecure households, the IRC aims to reduce the need for negative coping strategies, including among youth members of a household. Similarly, food insecure women and girls can face specific risks associated with negative coping strategies, which can include exploitation, expectations of sexual favors to reduce debt burdens, or coercion at check points if women/girls do not have sufficient money to cross and pay fees. The IRC's MPCA seeks to address food insecurity and reduce negative coping strategies, including those faced by women and girls. Additionally, the IRC's vulnerability criteria prioritizes female headed households who tend to be more vulnerable since they may not have a breadwinner/income earner in the household. Targeting FHHs further helps to ensure that risks associated with negative coping strategies are reduced for women and girls. Finally, the IRC's MPCA approach is coordinated with the FSL cluster to which the IRC does 4W reporting, and members to ensure where feasible and appropriate, IRC's is contributing to the FSL integration strategy by delivering MPCA that complements in-kind food distributions in the same communities, especially RTERs. MPCA has been found to be an effective second line response in an emergencies as it enables households to meet their fresh food needs that may not be provided in standard emergency kits.

Protection Risk Analysis

The IRC is committed to making the concept of Do No Harm a practical reality in its food security programming by incorporating conflict sensitivity and protection principles within project design, implementation, access, security, monitoring, and engagement with beneficiaries. For agricultural programming in particular, there are a few risks that the IRC needs to be aware of to ensure that proper mitigation strategies are in place. According to Protection Risk Analysis (PRA) guidance, key examples include risks related to heightened visibility for beneficiaries/exposure such as increased tensions with other beneficiaries in the community not selected for assistance; Cash/voucher overly spent on non food items, thus negatively impact food security outcomes; Cash/voucher not being equally shared/distributed within the HH such as increased tensions in a household when financial decision making norms are disrupted (i.e. when a female in the household receives financial assistance, a responsibility usually reserved for the males in a household; and Cash for work opportunities may increase the risk for female to sexual exploitation. The IRC will seek to mitigate these risks by ensuring strong community buy in and support; including community members in the project design and beneficiary selection criteria; setting clear eligibility criteria and a transparent beneficiary selection process; clearly articulated expectations of beneficiaries and expected outcomes for beneficiaries participating; and closely monitoring all activities by an independent team. Additionally the IRC's MPCA is delivered at the household level to the head of household. This mitigates against disruption of financial decision making norms.
