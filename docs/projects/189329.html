Project Summary

South Sudan has one of the world’s most under-developed infrastructures in the world. Out of the estimated 17,000 km of roads, less than two percent are paved. The country’s road density is the lowest in Africa with 15 km of road per 1,000 km of arable land. Building large-scale infrastructure is also challenging due to South Sudan’s increased vulnerability to devastating climate shocks. The average rainy season can last up to nine months. For the last three years, the country has been facing an unprecedented level of flooding which translated into the structural change of the wetlands and increasing its surface.
The 2022 INFORM Index for risk management assesses South Sudan to be the second highest at-risk country globally after Somalia. South Sudan also ranks the highest of all 189 countries in the lack of coping capacity due to weak infrastructure and institutional capacity. It has extremely high socio-economic vulnerability and high numbers of displacements due to extreme weather events. In 2021, flooding affected 39 of the 78 counties (mainly in northern Jonglei and Unity) and, due to a lack of appropriate preventative or flood management infrastructure, has led to new displacements of over 835,000 people.
In August 2021, Bentiu town, which has no flood water management system, began experiencing flooding and water levels peaked in October 2021. Despite the highwater levels, most IDPs from Rubkona, Guit and Koch counties sought refuge from the floods in Bentiu town. Following a series of rapid needs assessments across the country, emergency assistance has been provided to 598,957 people in the flood-affected areas. In addition to food distributions, humanitarian organizations have provided health and nutrition supplies and services; water, sanitation and hygiene services; protection services; and fishing kits for livelihood support. Similarly, a nearly catastrophic situation has also been reported in Fangak and Canal/Pigi counties where communities need to choose between physical security and food/ nutrition/ health security.
Through this project, WFP will develop critical infrastructures along main supply routes, primary dykes and community dykes in marginalized areas in Jonglei, Unity, and Upper Nile states. These areas are the most vulnerable to climate change and home to nearly 40 percent of the South Sudanese population, which has been isolated from basic needs and services (food, markets, hospitals, etc.). Other locations in Lakes or Western Equatoria States are also included in the plan due to the poor conditions of the roads and increasing humanitarian needs in the area.
Another expected result of the project is to reduce isolation and displacement for communities. By repairing key infrastructure, communities will be protected from future shocks and have continuous access to basic necessities. Furthermore, it paves a path for already displaced communities to return to their areas of origin, in time to cultivate and rebuild their livelihoods while contributing to long term disaster risk reduction and climate adaptation.
Improving the transportation network in conjunction with other initiatives aimed at building human capacities (listed within the HRP) and labor force skills and strengthening institutions would provide the basis for a transition to economic growth. Through infrastructure projects, local youth stand better chance to get local employments thus increasing family household incomes. Lastly, in addition to facilitating efficient and cost-effective delivery of food and non-food items to various locations, improved road and river infrastructure will improve rural connectivity, promoting equity and social cohesion and ultimately contributing to peace.

1 - Needs

South Sudan ranks the highest of all 189 countries in the lack of coping capacity due to weak infrastructure and institutional capacity. It has extremely high socio-economic vulnerability and high numbers of displacements due to extreme weather events. For the last three years, the country has been facing an unprecedented levels of flooding. The tributaries that flow into the Sudd/the Nile have overflowed and areas of the country not previously flooded are now underwater and there is an overall expansion in the wetlands surface. In 2021, flooding affected 39 of the 78 counties (mainly in northern Jonglei and Unity) and, due to a lack of appropriate preventative or flood management infrastructure, has led to new displacements of over 835,000 people.In August 2021, Bentiu town, which has no flood water management system, began experiencing flooding and water levels peaked in October 2021. Despite the highwater levels, most IDPs from Rubkona, Guit and Koch counties sought refuge from the floods in Bentiu town. There is a critical need to maintain access to this area to support highly vulnerable population. Similarly, a nearly catastrophic situation has also been reported in Fangak and Canal/Pigi counties where communities need to choose between physical security and food/ nutrition/ health security.The 2022 dry season is expected to be shorter, and some lands are likely to remain water-logged, thus forcing some people into a situation of prolonged displacement until their areas of origin are accessible and lands returned to an arable nature. Repairing damaged infrastructure will allow communities to return to their areas of origin and rebuild their livelihoods. Infrastructure repairs are critically needed to improve humanitarian access which is now severely restricted. Many stakeholders utilize the dry season (November - April) to preposition supplies prior to the heavy rains before access is further limited. This is mostly done using road and river transportation modalities however, the unprecedented floods have cut off access to many locations forcing humanitarian actors to resort to costly air operations to deliver supplies.

2 - Project activities

The project will contribute to the resilience of communities by rehabilitating critical roads, repairing dyke breach points, and building dykes along main supply routes and clearing of river routes. This is not only key to ensure access to Greater Upper Nile (Unity, Upper Nile, and Jonglei states) and other locations across the country and pre-position both food and non-food items ahead of the rainy season, but also serves to prevent future flooding, contributing to longer-term disaster risk reduction and climate adaptation. In total, it is expected that around 534 kilometers of roads and riverways will be repaired. Some of the main project activities will include, among others: • Repair remaining dyke breach points along Baidit-Jalle-Mabior (77 km)• Build the dyke along the Main Supply Route from Bentiu town to Unity oil field (18 km) and build a primary dyke in new and old Fangak (35 km)• Drain flood water in anticipation of the next flooding and, where needed, install culverts • Rehabilitate critical roads for the continuous provision of humanitarian assistance across the country including the Bor-Ayod-West Akobo-Nyirol and Wunrok-Abyeimnhom-Mayom-Bentiu roads which are critical for access to Unity State. The Bor-Gadiang-Duk, Bor-Twic East, Ayod-Old Fangak roads are key in order to access Duk, Twic East and Fangak. To deliver to Unity State and use the Western corridor, roads such as Bentiu-Leer-Adok and Leer-Dabual–Mayendit, Mayom-Mankein-Ajak Kuac, Bentiu-Nhialdiu, and Rumbek-Wau need to be repaired. Additional locations can include Banketa-Mathiang-Maiwut-Pagak, Kaujok-Marial Lou, Juba-Mundri-Mvolo-Rumbek, Mingkaman-Yirol-Rumbek.• Open up river routes including Manga-Bentiu, Malakal to Pultruck-Nyirol-Lankien-Padiang-Nyambor as well as Malakal to Nasser-Khorwuakow-Ngueny and Malakal to Akobo-Pibor. As part of its vision, WFP seeks to address entrenched inequity and isolation, with the goal of “leaving no-one behind” and fostering unified, interconnected and peaceful communities. To do so, WFP will bring together its portfolio of programmatic tools and operational enablers to address food and nutrition insecurity in ways that create the conditions for jobs, livelihoods, markets and trade growth and eventual economic development in the marginalized areas of the country. Investing in infrastructure networks, along with education and peacebuilding dialogue, and continuing to open up local and cross-border road and river networks will connect communities to each other, to markets and trade. To measure the gains and impact of infrastructure projects on communities, WFP will carry out a local economy-wide impact evaluation (LEWIE). WFP is currently in the planning phase of the study with the data collection scheduled for early 2023.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

The project focuses on repairing damaged roads and flood management infrastructures such as dykes. The possible risks are contextual (mainly linked to the security and weather conditions) as well as operational (engagement with contractors and local authorities). WFP will consult local communities on the planned repairs and ensure the proposed interventions are in line with their needs and priorities. Aside from engineering works, WFP will conduct social cohesion dialogues to ensure the project will disincentivize violence and incentivize peace while ensuring community ownership.
