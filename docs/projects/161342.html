Project Summary

CRS supports the protection of vulnerable populations in South Sudan by contributing to the protective environment and enhancing communities’ peaceful co-existence through peacebuilding and social cohesion activities. CRS is also integrating protection into its food assistance programming through awareness raising activities on protection and engaging communities on risk identification and mitigation. CRS is working with vulnerable children and their families to access critical health and social services. CRS is also leveraging its WASH programs as entry points for GBV risk mitigation and meeting the differential needs of girls and women through the provision of dignity kits and critical information relating to menstrual hygiene management. In 2020, CRS will build on the past ten years of programming on early warning and response to engage with community structures and courts to support increased access to justice for vulnerable populations, engagement with traditional courts and community leaders on gender responsive justice. CRS also plans to pilot the integration of Fuel-Efficient Stove production into a livelihoods project.

1 - Needs

The protection activities planned by CRS will address multiple and layered needs, including the need for safe and dignified access to services, access to justice, peaceful co-existence within and between communities, and the realization of people’s right to be free from all forms of violence. The issue of sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV) is pervasive across South Sudan. It is estimated that 65% of women and girls in South Sudan have experienced physical and/or sexual violence in their lifetime , yet the full scope of the issue and the needs are not fully known due to stigma, impunity, and other barriers to reporting. Risks and experiences of gender-based violence threaten the physical and mental health, safety, and wellbeing, especially of women and girls. GBV impedes women and girls’ full participation in society, with negative and potentially lasting effects. In 2020 CRS will work in Jonglei, Lakes, and Juba, three areas affected by GBV and protection risks. Greater Jonglei State has seen decades of conflict, both as a flash-point in the national level conflict between the government of South Sudan (GOSS) and the Opposition (IO), and inter-communal conflict over resources as a result of migration and the decades-old animosities between tribes. The legacy of conflict between GOSS and IO has coincided with and caused increasingly militarized and violent inter-ethnic cattle raids, intra-communal, inter-age-set extortion and revenge killings that deeply affect civilian populations. The civil war has increased impoverishment, and fueled raids and criminality. The prolonged civil war has greatly expanded the availability of weapons and normalized the use of lethal force in disputes between individuals and communities. Lakes State has a long history of inter-communal conflict, with cattle raiding and revenge killing, common. Between 2015 and 2018, there were 255 violent incidents, which caused nearly a thousand deaths, 528 serious injuries, and loss of 14,793 head of cattle in seven of the eight counties in Lakes State. Traditional norms about gender and the role of youth underpin much of the conflict with violence impacting women, men, girls, and boys differently in the communities. The various groups face unique risks linked to traditional notions of gender that influence decision-making power, relations within the household and the community, power dynamics, access to and control over productive resources, and access to justice. Durable solutions in South Sudan are dependent in part on individuals’ ability to harness their self-protective capacities, as well as communities’ ability to co-exist peacefully. In the more urban setting of Juba, some of the needs facing children and their families include food insecurity, barriers to education, lack of access to services, and unsafe/insecure household environments. During the baseline assessment for the Coordinating Comprehensive Care for Children (4Children) project , which drew on data gathered from 331 caregivers and 547 children, the findings showed that families lacked resources for food, with 94% of households indicating that they have gone to sleep hungry in the past 4 weeks. Regarding education, 70% of the children that had missed school did so because there was no money for school fees, materials, or transport and 15% of the children sampled were not in school. The data also revealed that 18% of the total children worked outside of the home, most commonly hawking goods and selling food at the markets. Of the boys and working outside the home, 62% percent worked three or more hours per day, and of the girls working outside the home, 53% worked three or more hours (outside of domestic labor), thus highlighting child protection concerns. Regarding the home environment, almost a third of children (32%) disagreed or strongly disagreed that their caregiver provides a safe environment for them.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

The data for current projects is not currently disaggregated by disability, but CRS will administer the Washington Group Short Set of Questions to identify persons with disabilities when conducting assessments and registrations for future projects. In Juba, CRS has established a relationship with one Disabled Persons Organization, which CRS plans to leverage for consultations on project design and implementation. In 2020, CRS will build on lessons learned in its work in other sectors, including carrying out inclusive and adapted activities in WASH, food security, and agriculture to meet the unique needs of person’s with disabilities. CRS will work collaboratively with community leaders, community-based committees and other community members on the identification of persons with specific needs, including disabilities, to ensure that they are able to benefit from assistance, as well as build on their capacities to participate and contribute to and provide feedback on the project. In line with CRS’ MEAL policies and procedures, all projects are required to have feedback and complaints mechanisms. These mechanisms include community and beneficiary meetings to discuss feedback and complaints, in-person interactions with CRS staff, community-level committees, post-distribution monitoring and exit interviews, and help desks. The breadth of options for feedback - including several at community and household level - help to ensure that even the most vulnerable who may not be able to travel to community meetings have a chance to share feedback. CRS conducts assessments where beneficiaries are engaged in interviews, surveys, and focus group discussions. During distributions, CRS conducts exit interviews and post distribution monitoring with a diverse sample of beneficiaries that explore their views on their ability to access project assistance. CRS hires diverse male and female staff to ensure that all beneficiaries can give feedback to someone with whom they feel comfortable.
