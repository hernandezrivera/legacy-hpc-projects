1 - Needs

Libyan, migrant and refugee school-aged children (6-17 years) face challenges in accessing education due to the ongoing conflict, displacement, or legal status in the country. Prior to COVID-19, 15% of school-aged children do not regularly attend school across Libya, and currently the majority of children remain outside of school. The conflict damaged a large number of education buildings, and initial assessments by TdH IT have shown that 57% of schools surveyed have been damaged by conflict, past and present. In 2018, before the resurgence of the conflict in 2019, 489 schools had been affected: 40 had been fully damaged, 423 partially damaged, and 26 were accommodating IDPs. The lack of safe education infrastructure, the overcapacity of some schools, the lack of gender-sensitive WASH facilities (83% of schools surveyed) and proper access for children with disabilities (57% of schools surveyed), are all detrimental elements to the education of school-aged children. In 2020, the negative impact of the conflict on academic performance was felt in Misurata after Sirte fell to LNA forces and in Tripoli where escalations IDF and drone strikes in Souq Aljoumaa and Tripoli led to schools be closed before the pandemic resulted in full closures. Academic performance of children in Libya is further hampered by the poor pedagogical skills of teachers and other educational personnel, paired with the lack of instructional materials (43% of schools surveyed) in the context of formal education . According to the MSNA 2018, 16% of households interviewed cited poor quality of education as a reason for not attending and/or dropping out of school. As of 2020, minimal change has occurred to enhance/improve this pedagogical capacity, and the pandemic revealed limited engagement of students by teachers in a remote learning modality. Finally, a continued major obstacle to the education of school-aged children in Libya is the widespread lack of access to psychosocial support services: in a context of conflict and displacement, and now pandemic, such as Libya, the ability of children to learn requires the complement of sustained recreational activities and psychosocial support. The impacts of direct exposure to the conflict have been severe, including psychological distress and trauma. Many children, due to the conflict, are suffering from psychosocial distress or trauma, which affects their ability to learn . Vulnerable populations identified by TdH IT face compounding, pre-existing needs as well as new, emerging challenges during this period of crisis and will require education, protection, and MHPSS services. COVID-19 has only compounded these traumas by introducing an unprecedented fear and uncertainty for children, removing a primary avenue for their social development, and exposing them to another danger in a conflict zone.

5 - Mitigation measures
5.1 - For each risk mentioned under 4 above, please describe the ways you plan to mitigate, prevent and/or address these.

4.1Information and understanding on how complaints handling systems and feedback mechanisms function is the primary barrier as of 2020. Focused efforts will be made to educate stakeholders and beneficiaries on the function and process of these systems to encourage utilization. 4.2 Relevant accountability activities and communications as well as necessary visibility efforts will be implemented to prevent/mitigate misperceptions of inequality in service provision. Community engagement and participation and inclusive programming throughout the project cycle is another critical step that TDH utilizes to mitigate this protection risk. 4.3TDH utilizes an inclusive and participatory process with stakeholders and beneficiaries and makes efforts to ensure a balance in demographic reach as resources allow. 4.4Guidance and protocols from TDH, the cluster system, and local authorities will be used to ensure that increased exposure due to the necessity of programming does not result in harm to beneficiaries nor staff. 4.6TDH will be engaged in CM activities based on assessed/referred need and therefore will be privileged to possess sensitive information. Agency safeguarding measures and data protection policies will be adhered to at all levels internally, and agreed upon confidentiality and data-sharing procedures determined by sectors and sub-sectors will be followed. 4.8TDH will work with local authorities, stakeholders and partners to support durable solutions for person in forced displacement. 4.9TDH will maintain impartiality as required by using agency standards for assessing and determining need, and escalate with local authorities, OCHA, donors and the Libyan INGO Forum when necessary to eliminate external interference. 4.14 - A "do no harm" approach will guide our emphasis on programming acceptance, which will be achieved by working with community leaders, school staff, and parents and help to mitigate any adverse responses to activities that endanger the target population. Child safeguarding measures, including agencies procedures for responding to protection issues, will be put in place and align with sector and sub-sector standards. Membership on the Case Management Task Force will guide interventions requiring case management. Training for staff and education personnel participating in the activities will include education on these protection issues and how to support mitigation, prevention and intervention.
