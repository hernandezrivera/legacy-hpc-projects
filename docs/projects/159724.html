Project Summary

The proposed intervention builds off of existing RI activities and targets 45,659 vulnerable communities (29,318 IDPs and 16,341 host communities) within North Darfur State (localities: El Fasher, El Kuma, El Malha, El Taweisha, Kebkabiya, Mellit, and Saraf Omra) and Blue Nile State (localities: Bau and Geissan) to reduce morbidity and mortality rates through direct service provision of emergency services and providing capacity building trainings across sectors and provision of sustainable infrastructure to build resilience. RI proposes integrated programming covering Health, Nutrition, WASH, FSL, and Education.
The interventions will support all three Strategic Objectives. SO1 Emergency Life-saving Assistance will be supported through free services within PHCCs/BHUs and an Emergency/Rapid Response Mechanism under Health, and within SCs, OTPs, and TSFPs under Nutrition; associated capacity building of staff and infrastructure improvements will be made to implement services. SO2 Resilience Building will be supported through access to basic water and sanitation services with accompanying hygiene promotion under WASH emphasizing community management and sustainability; FSL activities integrate nutrition-sensitive kitchen gardening, livestock production training and CAHWs support, and multi-purpose cash assistance emphasizing supporting a HH's ability to build foundational skills and meet immediate HH needs; and increasing access to quality Education through classroom building, training materials dissemination, and training with an emphasis on girls' education to build the communities' human capital and long-term capacity. SO3 is addressed through protection mainstreaming across interventions.

1 - Needs

Within both North Darfur and Blue Nile needs remain acute as it does across Sudan due to the macro-economic crisis and the fragility of the new transitional government. Lines of people waiting for bread and fuel remain long across the country, and access to basic health, nutrition, and WASH services remains low with people having limited levels of household assets to meet immediate needs (e.g. purchasing power for food, education, medical expenses, etc.). According to a World Bank and Ministry of Agriculture 2016 report, Enabling the Business of Agriculture, the agriculture and livestock sector accounts for approximately 30-35 percent of Sudan’s GDP and is a source of livelihood for about 65% of the population. Eighty percent of Sudan’s poor live in rural areas, with rural poverty more than twice that of urban. Average food and livestock prices are steadily increasing (+53% for sorghum and + 116% for goats compared to June 2018) (WFP Monthly Market Update, Jun 2019). The price of sorghum in North Darfur increased from SDG 5.09/kg to SDG 19.8/kg from January 2017 to June 2019, and the price of a goat from SDG 535 to SDG 2,782 in the same period. Livestock diseases, including PPR, Sheep Pox, and HS are gravely affecting the livelihood of the purely pastoral and agro-pastoral communities in North Darfur (COOPI Livestock Vaccination Report, 2019). Localities targeted are classified as Stressed (IPC Phase 2) or Crisis (IPC Phase 3). Sudan has one of the largest numbers of out-of-school children in the MENA region with one reason being inadequate school WASH (HRP 2019). Displaced children add pressure to existing education facilities that are limited in space and resources. Decreased livelihood options impede a household’s ability to enroll their children in school, and poor education spaces decrease the functionality of the learning environment.In North Darfur, new and protracted displacement due to conflict, chronic poverty, under-development, and recent economic crisis impede access to basic services and disrupt livelihoods, exposing vulnerable communities to high levels of food insecurity and malnutrition. The WHO Q1 2017 HeRAMS shows a health facility functionality rate of 67%, which suffers from the SMoH’s ability to fully cover the costs of staffing, facility management and maintenance, and supplies. Average monthly consultations at RI/OFDA-supported health facilities have almost tripled from mid-2018 to early 2019 (11,146 to 32,936). Diarrhea, malaria and acute respiratory infection are the most common morbidities seen at RI-supported health facilities, which provide over 16,000 consultations per month. Outbreaks are common, and SMoHSD staff face significant capacity limitations to respond appropriately. The December 2018 S3M conducted by the Federal Ministry of Health in Zamzam IDP camp showed a GAM rate of 14.7% and SAM 3.6%. North Darfur has the third-lowest proportion of households (7.9%) using both improved water sources and improved sanitation facilities, and open defection remains prevalent.In Blue Nile, areas are only recently opening up to humanitarian access and are still highly affected by the conflict-related disruption in services and access to basic care and household resources. The 2019 HRP has ranked Blue Nile as one of the six states in Sudan with the most severe needs requiring prioritization amongst humanitarian actors. Only 37% of health facilities in Blue Nile were classified as fully functional. Health facilities suffer from shortages of qualified staff, equipment, furniture, and medicines, as well as poor infrastructure. The SMoHSD reported 46.7% of children under five (CU5) were stunted and 11.1% wasted in 2018. The poor health and nutritional status of the target population are aggravated by a lack of access to quality and reliable WASH services, as well as poor hygiene practices. Access to safe drinking water is extremely limited, with over 80% of the water sources unsafe for drinking.

2 - Activities / Outputs

The proposed activities are in line with the Cluster priorities and will be integrated through an area-based approach where the health/nutrition facility catchment population that is crisis-affected and vulnerable also receives the WASH, FSL, and Education services. Protection and environmental mitigation will be mainstreamed across all sectors. Health: a) Provide a basic package of services through 50 PHCCs/BHUs to cover 45,659 consultations (assume 20% locality PiN targeting with a 65% coverage rate) for communicable diseases, EPI, and maternal and reproductive health; b) respond to conflict, natural emergencies, and disease outbreaks through facility staff and community leadership capacity building on early warning systems, and mobilization of an Emergency/Rapid Response Mechanism; and c) strengthen the health system through training of 500 SMoH seconded staff on provision of the basic package of care and health facility management in line with the SMoH/WHO guidelines, and facility rehabilitation; and c) response to outbreaks .Nutrition: a) Use the CMAM approach to continue providing timely and equitable lifesaving assistance to 2,919 CU5 with MAM, 1,321 CU5 with SAM without medical complications, and 198 CU5 with SAM with medical complications and to 2,237 PLWs with MAM through 3 SCs, and 49 OTPs/TSFPs integrated with a health facility; and b) Provide IYCF counselling at the nutrition centers through 245 mother-to-mother support groups and 784 quarterly NIPP cycle groups to capture secondary caregivers, fathers, and other community leadership that can positively influence social norms around nutrition behaviors (4 NIPP groups per OTP/TSFP per quarter). WASH: a) basic water services through rehabilitation and new construction (where existing infrastructure in non-existing) of 5 water yards and 5 mini water yards serving a total of 26,085 and O&M capacity building of 100 Water User Committees’ members; b) limited sanitation services using CLTS and CHAST approaches for 1,405 household and 5 schools supporting 5,250 teachers and students; c) hygiene services through HH visits, campaigns, and media coverage with increased efforts in preparation of and during the rainy season for 14,049 HHs; d) Minimum WASH for Nutrition package for SAM-affected populations with messaging at health/nutrition facilities, household messaging through integrated community volunteers, and hygiene kits distributed to SAM-discharged caregivers; and e) Minimum WASH for Health package for outbreaks affected and ‘at-risks’ households through targeted response interventions supported by Health sector early warning and response activities.FSL: a) Support 490 female caregivers of SAM CU5 with kitchen garden training using the TerraFirma method of triple dig keyhole gardens that are climate-smart and adaptable to arid climates; b) Provide capacity building training on small ruminant livestock production using the One Health approach and small business skills to 500 HHs and 250 CAHWs (10 HHs and 5 CAHWs per health facility catchment); each CAHW will also be provided with an equipment and drugs kit; and c) multi-purpose cash assistance to 500 vulnerable HHs to help meet basic needs and pre-position for the lean season. Education: a) Construction/rehabilitation of semi‐permanent classrooms in 5 schools to increase access to education; b) Provide teaching, learning and recreational materials for 3,500 students and teachers; c) Train teachers and PTA members on facility O&M and budget management, and teacher training on ; and c) Conduct awareness sessions/campaign for students (focus on schools’ boys and girls leadership clubs) and communities on the importance of education (emphasizing girls’ education) and school-aged appropriate messaging on integrated health, nutrition, and WASH.

5.1 - Impact on environment/natural resources

6.1 - Explain how your project aims to ensure protection mainstreaming during its design and implementation

RI promotes and adheres to the Minimum Inter-Agency Standard for Protection Mainstreaming for Health, Nutrition, WASH, FSL, and Education. This tool is part of RI sector standards and provides specific lists and higher-level protection standards for each sector. Promoting equitable access to services will ensure that the project reaches the most vulnerable members of the targeted communities. All indicators are gender, age, and disability disaggregated (as applicable), and it is expected that over 50% of beneficiaries will be women. RI’s participatory approach also mainstreams protection within RI activities, ensuring that the most vulnerable households are selected for participation in activities; that the needs of men, women, children, the elderly, and people with disabilities are considered; and that activities occur at a time, location, and in a manner that ensures safety and dignity. Special attention will be paid to mothers and PLW through reproductive health services, malnutrition treatment for PLW, and IYCF counseling for mothers. Through the new approach of integrating hygiene messaging into existing health and nutrition programming, RI will look for opportunities for gender mainstreaming, such as incorporating menstrual hygiene management as awareness session topics. Community influencers, such as men, grandmothers, and traditional healers will also be included in IYCF and other nutrition awareness sessions. RI will seek to prioritize people with disabilities and the elderly in the participant selection and provide assistance to reach services as needed. RI will also ensure that all clinics are accessible to the disabled and elderly. For eligible health and nutrition participants with disabilities and/or caretakers with a disability, CHWs and the CNVs will recommend and ensure the provision of home visits if the disability impedes travel to RI facilities. RI adheres to a Do No Harm (DNH) approach. All staff will be oriented and held accountable to RI’s Code of Conduct on professionalism, impartiality, and confidentiality.

7.2 - Please outline how the project or your agency will inform and interact with inter-agency approaches to accountability to affected people and feedback mechanisms, including SEA and abuse complaints?

Under RI’s global Accountability to Communities Framework and Incidents Management Policy, accountability is a key pillar of RI’s programming. RI is committed to mainstreaming principles of accountability throughout our program in accordance with the Humanitarian Accountability Partnership (HAP) standards and the Core Humanitarian Standards (CHS) and includes minimum standards for beneficiary participation, information sharing, complaints, and feedback mechanisms and staff competencies. RI will promote accountability by ensuring that the target communities have access to information about RI and to the planned activities, and by creating opportunities for those communities to participate in programming decisions and to provide us with feedback on the project interventions.RI’s Feedback and Accountability Mechanisms include three forms: sex-disaggregated focus group discussions on a monthly basis led by a MEAL officer; feedback boxes situated at each project site opened bi-monthly by a committee comprised of an RI MEAL Officer, a VHC member, and a clinic staff member; and a telephone hotline managed by an Accountability Officer for highly sensitive feedback.
