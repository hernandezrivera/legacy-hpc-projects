Project Summary

The humanitarian crisis in Iraq is entering a new phase. Combat operations against the Islamic State of Iraq and Syria (ISIS) have ended and hundreds of thousands of displaced people are returning to their homes and communities. In 2019, Save the Children (SC) will continue a multi-sectoral response to households with food insecurity and socio-economic vulnerability, targeting conflict-affected families across Kirkuk governorate.
The project will target the most vulnerable families in Hawija sub-district who demonstrate the highest vulnerability scores as per the Cash Working Group’s (CWG) socio-economic vulnerability criteria. SC will prioritize 200 families who will receive three cash transfers ($400 per transfer) over the course of three months. Each transfer size is large enough to cover the basic needs of the family of six for a month as per the CWG guidelines. Households who do not qualify for the three-month multi-purpose cash transfers will be referred to other organisations to ensure that all vulnerable households are able to access critical humanitarian and national services. Households who fall on the livelihood vulnerability assessment criteria, who are food insecure based on the food coping strategies and food consumption share criteria, will be included in emergency livelihood activities. SC will prioritize 400 families to be included in cash for work, 100 families will receive cash for food, and 200 families will receive agricultural inputs (seeds, fodder and fertilizers) through vouchers. The CWG vulnerability assessment will also identify households which fall on the governmental non-contributory social protection criteria. SC will also advocate for the inclusion of those households in the social protection program at different forums.

Needs

Communities fleeing or living under ISIS were exposed to multiple sources of emotional distress, such as the loss of family members, being witness to killings, explosions, physical injury, abduction, sexual slavery, forced recruitment, rape, torture and other abuses. While combat operations have since ended in 2017, returns to Sinjar, Hawija, and Baiji remain particularly slow due to the high levels of destruction and slow rehabilitation of infrastructure and essential services (DTM September 2018; REACH Assessment September 2018). According to assessment findings across all targeted locations, main concerns for not returning to areas of the origin are the lack of job opportunities, inability to afford basic services such as health and education, and lack of support needed to rehabilitate livelihood opportunities (SC WASH and Livelihood Assessment Hawija; REACH MCNI September 2018). Currently there are 10% returnee families who are food insecure, in the area of return, showing the 4% poor food consumption. Most of the returnee families in Hawija and Baiji have reverted to negative coping strategies such as spending savings, borrowing money, begging, child labour, resorting to forced marriage as a means to gain cash or decrease household spending, and reducing meal size to afford basic services (DTM September 2018; REACH Assessment September 2018). The top food coping strategy adopted by the families is “shifting to lower quality or cheaper food” overall 55% of the non-displaced families are vulnerable to food insecurity. Across villages in Hawija, namely Al-Madhoria, Abo Al-Gees, Al-Ghazia, Al-Musana’a, Al-Agedat, Al-Karaghol and Al-Juboria, 72% families rely on the selling of assets to meet their basic needs, of those more than which 50% share goes to the food (SC WASH/Cash Survey, October 2018). Of those surveyed, 57% reported having to reduce the amount or portion of meals in the last week, and 53% of total respondents reported that adults are eating less so their children will be able to have greater amount of food portions. Poor coping strategies, coupled with fractured traditional family and community support structures, are leading to intensified violence and the increased risk of social exclusion and poverty, and violence for at-risk and vulnerable groups. Overall, 37% people’s houses in Sinjar are damaged and 21% people lacks livelihood sources. In Baiji, 45% of the locations report that markets operate normally and 40% report scarcity of goods. Due to the absence of returnee packages and the limited access to basic services, SC will provide multi-purpose cash assistance to the most vulnerable families to support their access to basic services such as shelter, food, health and education (Global Policy Institute Aug 2017 and Oxfam Hawija Report Aug 2018). Cash transfers are often directed at women, due to the belief that they make better funding decisions than men (Devereux et al, 2007), give them the potential to achieve other development goals including the empowerment of women and girls. In fact, SC will make conditional cash transfers with certain conditions such as sending girls to school, attending health services or spending the cash on food.

Activities

Save the Children will implement the following multi-purpose cash assistance and emergency livelihood activities across targeted governorates: - Conduct market assessments to determine the level of cash transfers to enable vulnerable families to meet their immediate needs - Select the cash transfer modality and procure a cash transfer financial services provider- Identify the vulnerable households through CWG Socio-Economic vulnerability assessment criteria to receive support from MPCA, Food Security Cash Transfer, Cash for work and Agricultural inputs through vouchers. A joint tool of will be used to identify socially and economically depressed and food insecure families. The tools will be composed of MPCA questions which measure the socio-economic vulnerability, the food security assessment tool, CARI, which measures the food insecurity the household by collecting information on the current food consumption, and food security coping strategies. Based on the MPCA and CARI tools, households showing the only food insecurity will be provided with the cash for food, cash for work, and agricultural inputs. Households showing only socio-economic vulnerability will be provided with the MCPA. - Identify the households in need of the referral to critical humanitarian/national services and eligible for the MoDM social protection safety net program- Identify communal assets, roads and markets, to be rehabilitated by the CFW workers- Monitoring of community assets rehabilitation works- Market and traders’ assessment to identify the traders for the provision of agricultural inputs through vouchers- Vouchers distribution to agricultural input beneficiaries - Distribute Multipurpose and food security monthly cash transfers to Female Headed Households, Child Headed Households and Households with PWD and other vulnerabilities - Conduct post distribution monitoring on the impact of the cash transfers

Protection Risk Analysis

Resources diverted to non-vulnerable beneficiaries: Resources may be diverted to terrorist groups or proscribed groups during distribution that may hurt beneficiaries. Additionally, movement of communities would increase the likelihood that some beneficiaries are connected or affected by ISIS. Security forces may also influence and pressure SC to support certain people or relatives. This would damage the reputation of SC, potential loss of funding, and suspension of programs. Inclusion: Communities may not allow the meaningful participation of women and girls, People with Disabilities, and other marginalized groups in proposed activities. Cash may be received by only men and women and children’s needs are not prioritized, resulting in an imbalance within the household. Distribution surveys and sites may also take place far from disabled households, elderly households, and female-headed households, resulting in project delays and SC not being able to achieve the intended beneficiaries. Beneficiary data protection: If beneficiary data is stored on the Cloud or a web server it is at risk of being attacked or stolen. Additionally, bank transfers and mobile money transfers may be shared by the external companies resulting in the breach of beneficiary data security and privacy.

Mitigation Measures

Resources diverted to non-vulnerable beneficiaries: To mitigate risks, SC will follow the humanitarian principle that humanitarian organizations are neutral and impartial in supporting vulnerable communities. Community mobilization will be prioritized with the different community leaders to support their understanding of the objectives of the cash. SC will co-ordinate closely with local authorities, community leaders, to ensure vulnerable communities such as female single headed households, children with disabilities, and adolescent girls are targeted. Internally through SC’s risk management plan, assessment tools, vetting procedures, and due diligence procedures, vulnerability selection criteria, and cash transfer procedures will be followed to ensure activities are targeting the intended communities. Inclusion: To mitigate risks, SC prioritizes community-awareness sessions focused on gender equality with parents and caregivers, school teachers, community leaders, and girls and boys. SC staff are trained on gender sensitive programming and gender sensitive indicators are closely monitored and reported. SC prioritizes disabled households, elderly households, child-headed households, and female-headed households through close coordination with the local community, community leaders, INGOs and government officials. Beneficiary data protection: To mitigate risks, SC performs regular system security assessments and maintains data storage and backup policies. SC maintains beneficiary data confidentiality agreements, protocols, and clauses in all supplier contracts to ensure beneficiary data is protected.

How is your project accountable to affected population

SC’s accountability system provides opportunity for the communities to receive information about the project and to give direct feedback or make complaints about the delivery of program interventions. SC regularly provide accessible information to affected populations on program activities based on preferred communication methods and information needs. We share this information in languages, formats and media that can be easily understood by children and communities we work with. SC’s information sharing strategy covers the following:• Information sharing about our organization, our core values, the staff behaviours and the way we work• Information about our approach, project activities/interventions and services that will be provided.• Information about selection criteria• Information about the feedback mechanism itself explaining the clear process on how it works, for what purpose; and how the community/children can provide their feedback/complaints using what tools etc Through the life of each project, additional sessions will be organized to update the communities on project progress, challenges and changes in activities. A multi-pronged feedback and complaints mechanism is introduced in all project locations and shared widely using posters, business cards and community gathering meetings. The following mechanisms are in place for all sectors: • Telephone toll-free hotline: A telephone hotline is established and the number has been widely disseminated to beneficiaries through different communication means in the targeted areas. WhatsApp messaging and Viber call have also been set up as alternative channel for receiving direct feedback• Suggestion boxes: Suggestion/complaint are established in the relevant locations accessible to both male and female members of the community where possible, to allow people/children to provide their complaint/feedback directly.• Direct visit and interaction: During the regular monitoring visits, SC mixed gender MEAL team members organize FGDs, community meetings and one to one interaction with the beneficiates, boys and girls and communities in gender safe spaces and at times appropriate to women and girls. Complaints received through SC systems and are logged, investigated, addressed, and outcomes shared, aiming at immediate resolution and improvements. Complaints of serious nature (if any) are investigated and responded following a standard organizational protocol. Communities are regularly provided with feedback as to how their input was used. In order to make sure that the complaint and feedback mechanisms are accessible to all people, group consultations (including girls and boys, women and men, elderly and children and adults with disabilities) are conducted through the project life, to understand if the established community feedback mechanism can be easily accessed, useful and which information sharing and feedback channels the local community prefer and trust most. The ensure the safety of SC feedback mechanism, the places where community will be conducted and suggestion boxes will be placed, will be carefully assessed by SC with regards to its safety and accessibility. The consultation will be conducted during the day and close to the place where people live. SC will ensure that all complaints and feedback will be treated confidentially and the focal person will be trained in information protection procedures. SC will inform beneficiaries of the UNOPS IDP call centre and also coordinate with the call centre to update on activities and geographic operations to ensure accountability to beneficiaries and donors.
