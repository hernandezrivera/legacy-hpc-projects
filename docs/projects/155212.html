Needs

There are 906,511 Rohingya refugees now in Cox’s Bazar. This massive increase has also had an enormous impact on host communities’ food security, economic vulnerability, labour opportunities and environment in south of Cox’s Bazar. As highlighted by the Rohingya Emergency Vulnerability Assessment (FSS, WFP, Nov-Dec17), 38% of host communities are vulnerable to food insecurity, (12.5% highly vulnerable). More than 80% refugees are vulnerable to food insecurity and dependent on food distribution by humanitarian actors for their nutrition minimum requirement. Access to diversified and nutritious food is limited. The environment has suffered dramatic impacts, including deforestation, due to construction and firewood collection; depleting forests, exacerbating the risk of environmental degradation and landslides. The labour market captured by Rohingyas is 64% (Self-Reliance Assessment, WFP, UNHCR, Jan ‘18). Many Host Communities within Teknaf have been reliant on casual and daily labour/work which has been highly impacted since the influx of the Rohingya population who accept reduced daily wages for the same level of work thus being chosen by employers rather than members from the host community. The area south of Cox Bazaar was vulnerable even before the influx and these changing dynamics have resulted in increased tensions and heightened concerns from the Host Community about the effect that the Rohingya population are having on their lives. Limited government services within this region coupled with the increase in population has also resulted in mass environmental concerns such as a significant increase in solid waste in and around host communities and camps, both of which have a degradation effect on the environment and on the populations overall health. This coupled with the increasing changing climatic events all of which drastically affect both the camp and Host Community populations means that all support and activities that take place now should have a certain element of DRR and environmental management/restoration.Lack of strong institutional setups and therefore government vulnerability is present in both national and local level government agencies. This directly affects the allocation of resources, inclusion of the population within programming, ability for national level decisions to reach local level structures and the extent to which preparedness to recurrent shocks is strengthened at community level. Equally, the need for water for agriculture is becoming more critical. Salinity will continue to be a rising issue due to land and riverbank erosion meaning the sea is encroaching into low laying land. Large scale shrimp production and aquaculture results in saline water being discharged back into previously non saline environments, yet with the market being so profitable there is unlikely to be a reduction of government sanctions placed upon the activity. There is an overall predicted median increase of 26% in soil salinity by 2050, with the potential to reach as high as 55% in some coastal areas. Without effective coping mechanisms the overall loss in yield production will be more than farmers can absorb. Overall, the needs of the population with regards to water services (waste water management, potable drinking water and agricultural water) are extremely high both in rural and urban areas. Again this demonstrates the need for SI to have an integrated approach to programming ensuring WaSH and FSL activities are both integrated as well as cross cutting with DRR and protection for a full response to these issues.

Activities or outputs

Recognising the need for creative solutions to the ongoing challenges of land and water, SI aims to introduce a number of activities in 2019 that were used on a smaller scale throughout 2018. The activities involve aspects of permaculture design, thus attempting to alleviate some of the restrictions found in space and water availability for both camp and host community levels. All activities will be aimed at increasing self-reliance and either being able to diversify food intake through self-production or being able to supplement food diversification through income generated through project activities. SI will aim at targeted both short term needs such as cash for work schemes and longer term needs such as IGAs. SI plans to work with refugees in camps to deliver sack gardening – internally known as “Bagriculture”. Bagriculture seeks to address the space issues in the camps whilst still allowing people to increase their self reliance. We would be seeking to target 252 households with this activity in camp 27. The criteria for selection includes whether the HH is a female headed household to ensure focus is given to women. 12 demonstration gardens will be established in the following unions, Balachoura, Teknaf Sabrang, Teknaf Sadar, Hnilla and Whykong. These demonstration gardens will be tended by farmers who can then demonstrate the key techniques to others. Once the gardens have been established, 450 households will be reached with home gardening training and in kind donations to support them. Again one of the key criteria is whether the household is a female headed household ensuring preference as well as household income. Home gardening will allow the beneficiaries to diversify their diet through self-production of vegetables and give them the possibility to make small savings from the reduced purchase of vegetables induced by self-production.395 households will be reached with IGAs, to be determined according to the village. IGAs most commonly include, stove creation, drying fish and livestock. All 395 will also be encouraged to develop business plans and given supportive training to do so. Accountability is a key aspect of SI’s work, and it is ensured through commitment to the CRM (complaint response mechanism) internal to SI. The hotline number is widely disseminated to all beneficiaries.
