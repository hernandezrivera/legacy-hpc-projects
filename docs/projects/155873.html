Project Summary

The protracted crisis in Syria, now in its eighth year, has forced 2.1 million, over one-third of children, out-of-school and has put a further 1.3 million children at risk of dropping out. Over 5.8 million school-aged children (including more than 100,000 Palestine refugee children) and about 245,000 education personnel are in need of education assistance inside Syria, 61 percent of whom are in acute and immediate need. The provision of education services inside Syria is limited. Hostilities have resulted in over one in three schools being damaged, destroyed, no longer accessible or occupied for shelter and other purposes. Schools are reported as the most common building type used for IDP sites in Syria, accounting for an estimated 32 percent of collective centers. Due to a shortage of functional classrooms, classes are overcrowded and over one million children required to attend double-shift schools. An extreme shortage and under payment of qualified teachers also limits the quality and provision of education. Children who are out of school face increased protection risks including child marriage and the worst forms of child labor

Needs

As the crisis enters its seventh year, there are now 5.8 million children and youth from preschool to secondary-age (including more than 100,000 Palestine refugee children) and over 300,000 education personnel in need of education assistance inside Syria. While new EMIS132 data for the school year 2016/17 is currently under review, an estimated 1.75 million children, or almost one third of school-age children (aged 5-17 years) from the 2015/16 school-year are out of school and a further 1.35 million are at risk of dropping out. Around 180,000 education personnel, including teachers, left the education system, which has negatively affected the quality of education for all children. Hostilities have seen over one in three schools damaged, destroyed, no longer accessible or occupied for shelter and other purposes. Schools, accounting for an estimated 33 percent of collective centers, were reported as the most common building type used for IDP sites in Syria.133 Increased internal displacement has put the current infrastructure under enormous pressure and highlighted the need for school rehabilitation Using developed need assessment, IHR MEAL team assessed the needs of households in three districts which are Al Ma'ra, As-Suqaylabiyah, Azaz which follow to Idleb, Hama, and Aleppo governorates respectively. During seven days of assessment, 14 interviews were conducted with KIIs from deferent categories of community. Meanwhile, six focus group discussion focusing group discussions (FGDs) were held with samples of parents. Based on analyzing of collected data the following issues has been reﬂected: 1- The most important needs in Hama and Idleb were schools rehabilitation and salaries for teachers, followed by other needs such as stationary for children, educational tools for teachers, winterization material and fuel. 2- In Aleppo, the educational requirement such as stationery, educational tools, and fuel were more required. 3. It was clear that more than 30 % of teachers were non- specialist. So, there is a need to build their capacity through educational training. 4- In Idleb and Aleppo, 28% of targeted schools have not received any kind of educational support. 5 - The targeted communities’ traditions is welcome with female participation in educational activities.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1. Assumptions include the willingness of the education committee of the local council to partner with IHR in this project and enable access. Such projects are usually welcomed by local governance structure, particularly in the case when strong coordination is present-as will be with this program. The presence of an NGO providing services is one that strengthens the governing structure present in the area, as it supports them in providing services that otherwise are not there due to the lack of government in the proposed areas of intervention. 2. The number one risk is security, as schools have been one of the main targets in the conflict. Although the situation has slightly improved due to the cessation of hostilities, it is unknown how long it will last. The security situation is not one that IHR can play a major role in mitigating, besides perhaps providing additional safety and security training for teachers to use with their students in the case that security concerns arise. Other mitigation will be the pausing of activities in the case of major conflict escalation including rehabilitation and trainings (as was previously done in past IHR education programs). In the case that schooling is stopped, IHR, in cooperation with the parent-teacher committee will look into alternative mechanisms for education delivery, such as smaller sessions, home based learning etc. Finally, IHR has chosen a location that is relatively safer, at this moment in time, and has historically been a location where IDPs flee to, not where people are fleeing from. 3. Another assumption includes the presence of capable local contractors and subsequent access to materials and resources necessary for the rehabilitation activities. When IHR previously conducted similar activities, several contractors had placed bid for the rehabilitation activities, and nothing was found to be missing from within the local markets. In order to mitigate other risks, such as supplier fraud, IHR takes certain steps in order to lessen such risks. For example, prior to contracting a certain supplier several references are requested, portfolios of previous work, recommendations from the local councils etc are requested as a part of the bidding process. One risk includes the disinterest of parents to take part in the parent teacher committees. As the context in Syria is one of war, there are often priorities that families list, and most often they focus on food and health. Secondary is usually education and PSS support. In order to mitigate this risk, the IHR team will have to conduct strong advocacy and outreach during the project set up phase of the project when meeting with stakeholders. Furthermore, the rules and mandate of the committee will be formed by the community itself (with IHR field team guidance), so as to encourage ownership and interest in the topic at hand.
