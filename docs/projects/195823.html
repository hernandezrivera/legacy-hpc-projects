Project Summary

Recently the effects of climate change have become more visible in Syria, especially in the last two rainy seasons 2020/2021 and 2021-2022, where there was a significant delay in the onset of rainfall for at least two months across all Syrian governorates, along with an earlier cessation of rainfall. In addition to the poor temporal distribution of rainfall, which is compained with temperature anomalies from their long-term averages during the season, especially during critical periods of crop growth, resulted in drought-like conditions and significant water scarcity in Syria, further worsening food insecurity prevalence.
The water scarcity, damaged irrigation infrastructure and the low rainfall level has forced most farmers, to rely on wells to cover part of their irrigation needs. This will have negative impact on groundwater decline and depletion, leading to high pumping costs. The low production of wheat and other crops during the 2021 & 2022 harvest seasons has resulted in a significant gap in supply and demand, mainly for wheat and other crops such as legumes. Expectations indicate a most probability the agro-climatic conditions of the last two seasons will be highly frequent, especially the distribution and amounts of rain during the upcoming agricultural seasons, especially in northern and southern Syria.
The limited natural resources in Syria, and the exacerbation of the impact of climatic changes, especially drought, led to a decline in the production and yield of crops and livestock, an increase in the vulnerability and food insecurity of farming households. The effects of climate change call for urgent actions, measures and approaches that will help farmers sustainably increase their agricultural productivity and incomes, adapt and build resilience to climate change and greenhouse gas emissions, where possible.
In this context, the proposed project targeting 128,000 households (equivalent to 768,000 people) is based on two main outputs:
Output 1: Awareness raising of farmers’ on the risks of climate change and adoption of climate-smart agriculture (CSA) approaches enhanced. Output 2: TOT for agricultural extension technicians to conduct farmer field schools (FFS) and train them on climate-smart agriculture approaches.
This project will help farmers rationalize the use of natural resources in a sustainable manner, as well as adopting climate-smart agriculture (CSA) approaches that will contribute to improving local food production at the household and national levels as well, and alleviating losses due to extreme climatic condition.

3 - Needs

Agriculture remains a key economic sector in Syria and a major source of livelihoods for most crisis-affected families. Crop production being sensitive to weather shocks and climate volatility is being affected negatively by climate change and there is urgent need to scale-up and enhance adaptive actions. Many key strategic crops in Syria contribute significantly to the countries food basket and nutritional requirements. For example, Syria is recently a wheat deficit country, meaning that the country does not produce enough to meet its needs. This has resulted in wheat being imported into the Syrian Arab Republic mainly through its seaports and possibly through land border ports. The wheat deficit and and other affected crops production losses is due to various challenges affecting the respective production segment of the various crop value chains. These include the high cost of inputs and unavailability of quality seeds, fertilizers, herbicides, pesticides, fuel and key among these is the erratic rainfall and the increased water scarcity for both rainfed and irrigated crops and climate change.Climate change remains a contributing factor to the worsening food insecurity. The overall estimated Sector PIN for 2022 – of food insecure and those at risk of food insecurity – is estimated at 13.9 million, with 12 million facing acute food insecurity and 1.9 million people at risk of sliding into food insecurity.
