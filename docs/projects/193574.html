Project Summary

Aligned with HRP 2023–SO2 “vulnerable HHs in oPt affected by shocks and stresses due to conflict and natural disasters supported with MPCA met their immediate essential needs, accessed lifesaving services, and decreased the incidence of negative coping strategies,” Catholic Relief Services (CRS) and local partners, Life and Hope Association(LHA) and Palestinian Organization for Development (POD), propose a 12-month multi-purpose cash assistance (MPCA) project to support 900 vulnerable households (HHs)(5,400 individuals), across all five Gaza governorates to meet their multiple basic needs. The project anticipates reaching 600 HHs (3,600 beneficiaries) with MPCA for six months to enhance safety nets and protect current assets using the Minimum Expenditure Basket (MEB) value. Given the perennial nature of conflict in Gaza, CRS anticipates targeting an additional 300 HHs (1,800 beneficiaries) to receive MPCA assistance for two months to address critical and essential needs in the case of an emergency. These 300 HHs would receive the Survival Minimal Expenditure (SMEB) value.
The economic and humanitarian crisis in Gaza continues to deteriorate with increased rates of unemployment and poverty. According to MSNA (2022), 81% of HHs reported difficulties in meeting basic needs including food, transportation, utility payments, and medical care. Thus, many families are forced to adopt negative coping strategies, which may provide immediate relief but undermines long-term recovery. The MSNA, and CRS’ own assessments and program data revealed that the protracted crisis takes an especially pronounced toll on vulnerable groups—e.g., female-headed HH (FHH), people with disabilities, and the elderly—who are already marginalized.
To ensure the project reaches acutely vulnerable and conflict affected HHs, CRS will follow the Gaza Cash Working Group’s (CWG) recommendation to apply different socioeconomic criteria, whilst prioritizing marginalized groups (e.g., FHH, people with disabilities, the elderly). The project will utilize CRS’ current cash distribution systems to deliver rapid and direct assistance to HHs considering the modality preferences for men and women. CRS will coordinate closely with the Ministry of Social Development (MoSD) in Ramallah and with two local partners to reach highly vulnerable HHs across all five Gaza governorates. CRS will validate HH needs through phone calls, field visits, and UVAT, when feasible. CRS will also work closely with the CWG, Food Security Sector (FSS), World Food Programme (WFP), United Nations Relief Works Agency (UNRWA), and other actors to avoid duplication. Data will be disaggregated based on sex, age, and persons with disability. CRS will use this data to improve the project design through various learnings over the project lifecycle.
Working in oPt since 1961, CRS has extended experience implementing MPCA projects with systems, policies, and networks already in place. Since 2016, CRS has provided cash and voucher assistance to more than 57,000 vulnerable HHs in Gaza. Based on CRS’ CVA learning assessment, the MPCA modality was found to be “fully appropriate” by men and women in meeting household needs. This type of assistance upholds people’s dignity, in that they enabled choice, economized their time, were found to “do no harm,” and could be delivered in a safe, accessible, accountable, and participatory manner. CRS will implement the proposed project alongside two local implementing organizations, LHA and POD. CRS adopts the Community Engagement and Accountability approach building on CRS’ longstanding engagement with local implementing partners through intentional capacity strengthening, which supports the ongoing sustainability of interventions. The local partners will work alongside CRS in all activities including assisting in beneficiary selection, orienting beneficiaries, conducting monitoring visits, and reporting community feedback to CRS.

1 - Needs

Gaza has endured social and economic impacts of a land and naval blockade for 15 years. The recurring hostilities have worsened economic conditions, contributing to de-development. In the past two years, Gaza witnessed multiple escalations with the most devastating ones in May 2021 and August 2022. In August 2022, nearly 1,500 housing units were damaged or destroyed, leaving around 600 individuals internally displaced. This is compounded by the previous shock of the May 2021 escalation, where 62,000 housing units were damaged or destroyed, resulting in the displacement of 12,000 individuals. Following the August 2022 escalation, CRS’ rapid needs assessment assessed the economic impact of the escalation on different sectors. Results revealed economic effects across different sectors, but the most affected sectors were agriculture, fishing, industry, and other sectors reliant on daily job workers.Prior to the recent conflicts, Gaza was on the verge of a full-scale emergency and economic collapse due to the impact of the blockade, years of intermittent armed conflict, and the COVID-19 pandemic. The 2023 Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO) identifies over half of the population in Gaza as food insecure and in need of assistance. The unemployment rate for the second quarter of 2022 was 44%, with unemployment among males in Gaza reaching 39% and a staggering 64% among females. The poverty rate in Gaza has increased to 59% in 2022, a marked increase from 53% in 2017. CRS’ assessments of HHs from the PA and MoSD lists, showed a great disparity between HH income (ILS 472) and consumption needs (ILS 1,600).High unemployment and poverty rates have limited families’ ability to purchase food and basic living supplies, eroded savings, and forced families into debt. As a result, Palestinians are increasingly less able to cope with sudden shocks, man-made or in connection to natural events, such as spikes of violence, heatwaves, or winter storms. MSNA (July 2022) found that food expenditure compromised 56% of HH income in Gaza, with 57% of HHs reporting their typical monthly income had decreased compared to the previous year. Further, 81% of HHs reported facing financial difficulties in meeting their basic needs. 51% of HHs reported that they rely on NGO/charity assistance as a primary income source. A high percentage of HHs employ negative coping mechanisms such as buying food on credit, taking on debt, reducing expenditure on non-food items, and selling assets. CRS’ recent post-distribution monitoring (PDM) data for mixed-wallet assistance evidences a continued high reliance on debt among targeted families with 21% percent of the total unrestricted cash assistance reportedly going to repay debts - an increase from 16% six months prior. Of the households who reported spending on debt, 74% repaid debt incurred to purchase food on credit. According to the same MSNA findings, 38% of HHs have reduced the number of meals eaten in a day, 44% limited portion sizes at mealtimes, while 37% borrowed food from relatives or friends. Together these negative coping mechanisms increase the susceptibility of already vulnerable families to additional shocks. An overwhelming majority of MSNA respondents (92%) also expressed the need for additional humanitarian aid or assistance in the future; this need was especially pronounced among female-headed HHs (FHH), of whom 99% expressed a need for support. Women in the oPt are particularly disadvantaged with few opportunities available for women to generate income. Protection risks facing women and girls are also substantial. According to the MSNA 2022, 11.4% of households reported that women and girls avoid or feel unsafe in at least one location in Gaza strip - 50.4% reported fear of verbal harassments while 23% reported feeling unsafe going to markets. UN Women and WFP report that depleted HH resilience is also observed due to high female unemployment, women’s limited access to resources, and the high concentration of women in economic activities. According to a recent study by the Shelter and NFIs Cluster in oPt, there is limited knowledge within vulnerable groups—such as widows, FHHs, and women with disabilities—regarding the services offered by different humanitarian actors and the terms and criteria for receiving assistance. This often results in unequal access to these services, and ultimately to humanitarian responses that fail to consider age, gender, and location. Further, HHs with people with disabilities are one of the most vulnerable groups in Gaza. According to the MSNA (July 2022), findings revealed that 62% of HHs with a member with a disability reported an unemployed member of their family. Additionally, these families face additional barriers to employment; 11% cite that available jobs are either low-skilled, socially degrading, dangerous, or low-paying. Moreover, 91.5% of these HHs report that they are in need of future assistance to meet their basic needs. Finally, while markets continue to function, Gaza has been severely impacted by global supply chain disruptions coupled with the rising cost of food and other commodities. Through a rapid price assessment in August 2022, CRS documented recent price increases for basic food commodities, including an increase in wheat flour prices between April 2022 and August 2022 with an average increase of 15.5% in August. This demonstrates the ongoing impact of the war in Ukraine on global markets, with acute impacts in Gaza and the West Bank where Ukrainian products account for an estimated32% of wheat imports and 20% of corn and sunflower oil imports. The impact on already vulnerable families in Gaza has been profound. The high unemployment and poverty rates, coupled with soaring price inflation have further eroded the purchasing power of these families, driving them even more deeply into poverty and inhibiting any meaningful recovery from compounding stressors and shocks.

2 - Activities

In line with the CWG MPCA guidance and identified needs, the project will provide cash assistance to 900 vulnerable HHs (5,400 individuals) across all five Gaza governorates through two activity streams. CRS will first select 600 HHs (3,600 individuals) to receive CRS’ market-based approach to deliver unrestricted and unconditional cash for six months to support families’ needs basic needs and recovery from past shocks. CRS will follow the transfer value of the MEB approved by the Gaza CWG. CRS anticipates providing 1,081 ILS (roughly 305 USD) per HH per month. Receiving assistance for six months will enable the HHs to meet their basic needs and will enable CRS to monitor and analyze the impacts. Further, this will allow CRS to use our referral system to connect beneficiaries to livelihood opportunities or additional support both in other CRS programming and non-CRS programming. To ensure that CRS can quickly respond during or immediately after a potential conflict, CRS will provide an additional 300 HHs (1,800 individuals) with emergency assistance for two months following the S/MEB of 754 ILS (roughly 215 USD). If there is not an acute emergency, CRS will look to support additional HHs through the MEB. CRS will work with two partners—LHA and POD—to deliver these activities. Participants Selection and Verification: CRS, LHA, and POD will identify and select HHs through a multi-pronged approach. First, CRS and partners will coordinate with MoSD to obtain updated lists of vulnerable HHs that meet project-defined criteria, updating and verifying their demographic and vulnerability data, and conducting phone and in-person verification of a sample of HHs. Second, CRS and partners will identify and refer extremely vulnerable HHs who are not registered with MoSD. This combined approach will ensure the most vulnerable HHs are selected. In case of a rapid onset emergency, CRS will select 300 HHs for two months of assistance. For the selection of these HHs, CRS will follow the CWG recommendation to prioritize the most vulnerable HHs by applying vulnerability and socioeconomic criteria: families with low income, people with disabilities, medical needs, elderly, FHHs, and families with physical damages due to the crisis. To avoid duplication, CRS will share the list of selected families with FSS in Gaza to crosscheck with other humanitarian actors to ensure selected participants are not receiving similar assistance from others. Vendor Preparation: CRS has active agreements with 45 vendors across the five Gaza governorates. CRS will continue working with these vendors based on their demonstrated capacity to date to deliver the unrestricted cash for targeted HHs. CRS has validated vendors’ safety and security standards and their ability to comply with policies and contractual conditions. CRS and partners will provide refresher orientations to vendors on project activities, program rules and regulations, technical aspects of the transactions, responsibilities, and the cash-out process. These orientations will highlight protection against sexual exploitation and abuse principles. Transfer of Assistance to Participants: Each month, HHs will receive cash assistance following procedures that include receiving a unique 7-digit cardless code generated by the Palpay electronic system and shared with the HHs via SMS. They will use the cardless code to withdraw money through the point-of-sales available at any of CRS’ participating vendors by showing their IDs and the code. To ensure cash is delivered to the participants themselves, a text message will be sent to each participant automatically after each transaction, informing him/her of his/her balance details. In addition, CRS staff monitor the transactions on the electronic system to identify any abnormalities.Participant Orientation: CRS and partners through gender-balanced teams will provide orientations to all selected HHs introducing the project, how to use the card-less code, vendor names and addresses, and how to use the FCRM. CRS includes appropriate messaging to participants during orientation about their rights, including safe and dignified access to assistance. CRS conducts orientation prior to activities with vendors and partners, to complement the training provided on safeguarding, their rights, and responsibilities, including prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse. To ensure gender mainstreaming and female engagement and in order promote their role in family decision-making, the project will invite the heads of HHs and their spouses to receive the orientation sessions. NEXUS and Referral: To achieve longer-term impact for HH recovery, CRS will look for linkages to other programming by providing referrals for additional services through CRS or other actors. These include Small Capital Grants, Cash for Work, and other livelihood opportunities. In addition, CRS will refer HHs to other non-CRS programs including safety net/social protection schemes and development programming implemented by other NGOs. CRS will aim to refer 15% of the MPCA HHs and will prioritize giving this referral opportunity to female HH members and person with disabilities to increase their chances to join the labor market in Gaza. Baseline and Post-Distribution Monitoring (PDM): CRS will monitor and analyze the needs of targeted HHs baseline assessments to analyze priorities and expense patterns before using the assistance. CRS will also use the PDM tool to study the quality of the received assistance and participant satisfaction. The PDM will collect information to verify the amount received, vendor accessibility, redemption experience, satisfaction, priorities for the use of assistance, etc. The PDM will explore decision-making dynamics within the HH regarding spending and monitor the accessibility of the assistance across all populations, including women, people with disabilities, the elderly, etc. Data will inform project learning and adaptive management.

5.2 - Please briefly explain why the specific modality/ies for this response were chosen.

CRS will provide MPCA to targeted HHs to promote dignity of choice and respond to individual HH needs. The MPCA approach was chosen to support families and allow for greater flexibility in how they use the assistance to meet the diversity of their basic needs, including transportation, utility payments, and medical care. This MPCA approach aligns with the CWG priorities and with CRS learnings. Data from CRS’ MPCA programs shows that 88% of HHs reported being able to meet their basic needs 30 days after receiving assistance. This demonstrates that the MPCA modality enables HHs to decide how to spend the assistance compared to a restricted e-voucher or mixed-wallet approach. The previous PDM results for a CRS MPCA project showed 99% of participants were satisfied with the MPCA modality (98% and 99% of females and males respectively). Further, an MPCA modality can empower women and persons with disabilities to be more involved in decision-making and have greater access to assistance. CRS will include sensitization sessions with husbands and wives, encouraging them to jointly plan and decide how to spend the cash to meet the needs of all family members and will orient them about their rights including protection from different kinds of sexual exploitation and abuse. 89% of CRS-supported HHs reported that MPCA assistance was spent based on joint decisions including both husbands and wives. For persons with disabilities, vendors are selected and evaluated based on the accessibility of their shops. Of CRS’ 45 current vendors, 44 are accessible for persons with disabilities. CRS also offers a home delivery option improving access to assistance for those with mobility issues (e.g., the elderly, people with disabilities) or responsibilities in the home (e.g., FHH).

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

For CRS, the FCRM provides opportunities to learn from the people we assist and to identify problems early. Through CRS’ dedicated FCRM channels, feedback is compiled to identify trends across projects and activities. All non-sensitive feedback will be routed by a CRS staff member or affiliate from the feedback, complaint, and response mechanism (FCRM) channel through which it was received into the feedback management system. The project FCRM focal point will check all feedback cases on a weekly basis to verify that registered cases are correctly coded and categorized with sufficient descriptions, to uphold accountability. All non-sensitive cases will be followed up by assigned project staff, who may resolve the case and respond directly to the person of concern on their own. The Program Manager or CRS Country representative may support on more complex cases. Sensitive feedback related to PSEA or fraud will be immediately sent to the CRS Country Representative (CR) via a confidential email address and excluded from the database and reports due to the confidential nature of such cases. The Country Representative will review and manage such cases in accordance with CRS policies and procedures, including the Fraud Allegation Management Procedure and the Safeguarding Allegation Management Procedure. The CR is responsible to respond within 72 hours. Quarterly project reviews and FCRM effectiveness checks enable CRS and its local partners to aggregate and analyze community feedback. This analysis and interpretation inform “learning-to-action” discussions and adaptive management to build on success and address areas for continued improvement. This allows CRS to make improvements to our programs through proactive adaptive management approaches, alongside identifying and mitigating risks. The FCRM data management system allows all feedback cases to be stored in one place for documentation, rapid analysis, and response.

9.1d - Explain how you have ensured that mechanism are accessible to all population groups?

CRS adopts a client-oriented, active approach within its accountability. CRS’ feedback, complaint, and response mechanism (FCRM) is a robust system that ensures two-way communication with its participants via the toll-free hotline, WhatsApp channel, FCRM-dedicated email address, face-to-face feedback during field visits, and participants visiting the CRS office or the partner organization offices. The FCRM engages all CRS’ stakeholders, especially members of the target communities, ensuring they can communicate directly with CRS and that their feedback is addressed in a timely manner. The FCRM channels are shared with participants in the project’s booklets and accountability cards. Posters will also be provided at vendor shops to maintain people’s trust. CRS ensures these mechanisms are accessible, providing safe complaint channels for all groups. The diversity of the FCRM channels ensures all groups, including women and people with disabilities, have multiple options to reach the project in ways that are both culturally sensitive and consider different communication preferences. People with a hearing impairment can contact CRS by WhatsApp or email. Women, if they feel more comfortable, can ask for a female staff member. Moreover, during the orientation sessions, participants will be instructed on the FCRM as well as the process of handling this feedback, including receiving, processing, and responding to sensitive and non-sensitive ones. CRS guarantees a response to each feedback within a two-week period, although the feedback is often resolved within 24-48 hours. As a part of its safeguarding policy and commitment to prevent sexual exploitation and abuse, CRS trained all its partners and FCRM staff on its standard operating procedures including how to escalate sensitive cases directly to senior management. CRS has agreed to sign onto the UN inter-agency PSEA standard operating procedures to ensure appropriate reporting and follow-up of any suspected PSEA case.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

CRS takes a proactive approach to mainstream protection and promote Do No Harm, ensuring that project activities (1) prioritize safety and dignity and avoid causing harm;(2) provide equal and meaningful access for all target groups; (3) include mechanisms for participant accountability; (4) implement preventative measures to avoid market disruptions; (5) emphasize participation and empowerment. CRS’ Do No Harm approach begins with knowledge and prevention. CRS adopts the Community Engagement and Accountability approach to understand community needs and build trust. To enable this approach, CRS selected two local partners, LHA and POD, who are embedded in and will help the project reach the most vulnerable communities. Including local actors helps promote contextualized knowledge of the access environment, ensures the cultural sensitivity of activities, and enables the project to anticipate challenges and identify timely solutions. CRS has long-term partnerships with both selected organizations dating back to 2017. They have strong familiarity with project operational areas, and CRS’ MPCA programming. CRS also provides technical support and targeted capacity building activities to partners including safeguarding and PSEA training to promote DNH approaches and improve their program quality and internal controls.CRS staff sign a Code of Conduct and CRS ensures partners have appropriate safeguarding measures (including policies) in place, including on Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA). CRS and partner responsibilities are included in staff onboarding, project start up, and through regular refreshers. CRS also ensures that project participants are fully informed of their rights. Beneficiaries’ rights and responsibilities are explained during participant orientation—this includes setting expectations for the roles and responsibilities of CRS and partner staff. During sessions, participants are also informed about CRS’ FCRM, where participants and community members alike can request information on the program, report an issue with assistance, or fi le a complaint. Please see additional information on the FCRM in the previous section of this proposal. Ensuring participants are fully informed about their rights, responsibilities, expectations for the project, and reporting mechanisms helps to mitigate risks of harm. Do No Harm also requires understanding the unique needs, barriers, and risks vulnerable groups face, alongside adapting the project activities to ensure safe, equal, meaningful access for all populations without discrimination. Foremost, MPCA not only promotes dignity by allowing participants to spend their cash in the way they prefer, but it also enables HHs to meet the diverse, multiple needs within the family, including for women, people with disabilities, and the elderly. To promote this access to support, during orientation and throughout implementation CRS emphasizes that all HH members have a share in the assistance and encourages shared decision-making, particularly between husband and wife of dual-headed HHs. CRS also designs activities in response to the needs marginalized populations face. For example, CRS has included the option of homedelivery. This helps the participants who cannot go to supermarkets due to mobility issues (e.g., the elderly, people with disabilities), concerns about existing risks they may face, or responsibilities in the home (e.g.,FHH).In addition, MPCA will enable participants to spend the cash at different markets such as open markets and small shops giving them the ability to buy at lower prices. This practice helps avoid market disruptions by distributing spending throughout the market. CRS will continue monitoring the impact of the MPCA on the local markets using different tools including CRS’ expense tracker and PDM to track the participants’ patterns of spending and local market prices.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

As CRS seeks to provide equal and impartial assistance to the most marginalized communities based on the level of their vulnerability and needs. This project will ensure that participants have safe access to assistance based on need, without discrimination. Duplication of assistance will be mitigated by thorough coordination with main the actors including MoSD, FSS, etc. In addition, CRS coordinates with its local partners to reach additional vulnerable individuals who are not registered in MoSD for different reasons but who are still eligible to receive the MPCA assistance. Based on MSNA (July 2022) findings, 18% of HHs reported that a member of their HH had experienced difficulties in accessing one or more services including markets. Thus, CRS’s MPCA is designed to deliver services which are easy to access. CRS has considered the geographical distribution of the vendor shops to ensure there is a location easily accessible to all participants across Gaza; CRS has signed active agreements with 45 vendor shops covering all the geographic areas of the Gaza Strip. The variety and distribution of these vendors enables participants to freely choose the nearest or the most convenient shop to redeem their cash assistance. Additionally, RS has ensured that 44 of these 45 vendor locations are accessible to persons with disabilities. Further, CRS has considered the unique needs of various vulnerable groups, including women, the elderly, and those with disabilities. For example, a homedelivery option is available for elderly and disabled beneficiaries to expand access to services. CRS will provide participants and vendors with a project booklet and SMS that include details on using the PIN code and point of sale machines to access the unrestricted cash. The equality of access to assistance is demonstrated in the FCRM system as well, where all complaints, feedback, and concerns are managed confidentially. In additionto providing safe access to report any concerns with no discrimination. CRS’ targeting strategy has been developed to optimize equal and impartial access to aid, relying on a multi-pronged approach designed to address potential barriers to access. This includes use of multiple sources and approaches to identifying vulnerable households including MoSD referrals, CRS partner referrals, and CRS program referrals – which minimizes risk of inclusion or exclusion. Furthermore, CRS reviews programmatic data on a monthly basis to ensure targeting approaches and vulnerability criteria are relevant and most responsive to needs. In the last year, CRS provided cash-based assistance to highly vulnerable groups, with 19.1% of the overall targeted HHs being FHHs, 25.1% have PWDs in the HH, 7.2% were elderly HoHs, while 15.2% were living in a rented home, and 44% were living in makeshifts rooms or a single room inside another home.
