Project Summary

Strengthening and sustaining delivery of standardized secondary and specialized health care services addressing obstetric care, infection prevention and control activities (IPC), Tuberculosis, Hemodialysis, Neonatal Resuscitation services, Comprehensive Emergency Obstetric Newborn Care (CEmONC), trauma disability and rehabilitation, Thalassemia and emergency preparedness and response planning for mass causality management and chemical events and referral services across the country. The main objectives are to Improving sustainable and quality life-saving secondary health care for men, women, boys and girls requiring life-saving surgical interventions and secondary health care, in addition to women of reproductive age requiring emergency obstetric care; and strengthening the capacity of secondary care at hospitals’ level to cope with the emerging demands is important. WHO will continue to provide technical support through TWGs to maintain services mapping, conduct a regular needs assessment, and monitor and evaluate the implementation of secondary and specialized healthcare services in Syria through the provision of continued education and training, endorsement of standardized clinical guidelines, treatment protocol, and standard operation procedure of different subdivision of secondary healthcare services, chemical incidents in NWS. Furthermore, the project aims to strengthen infection prevention and control activities through delivery of Basic IPC training, provision of technical support, field monitoring, and measure the quality of healthcare in terms of inputs, the material, resources needed to provide IPC standard measures, and outcomes of the activities based on IPC assessment framework (IPCAF) for health facility. In addition, as last resort funding, the WHO will focus on supporting critical gaps of secondary and tertiary health care, based on a need’s assessment and in coordination with Health Cluster to ensure the vulnerable population has access to emergency care services.

3 - Needs

Based on HeRAMS data in 2021, only 48% of public hospitals are fully functioning . With this percentage of hospitals fully functional, the COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated weaknesses in the health system and essential health services were disrupted as agencies and health workers alike rushed to respond to COVID-19.Due to cumulative effects of concurrent crises, cost of health services; lack of medicines, supplies or health staff; and fear of exposure to COVID-19 have replaced distance, over-crowding and long wait times as the main self-reported barriers to health access with persons with disabilities disproportionately affected. Disrupted medication supply chains, were previously identified as a key gap in the health response and a contributor child mortality (according to HNO 2021: 536,753 older people are likely to require health services, as well as those with early-onset non-communicable disease which is estimated to account for 45% of all mortality in Syria. The national stock for medicines in the MOH warehouses in Damascus stands at only 20% of the needs for 2022.There is need to ensure access to quality, life-saving and life-sustaining health services including ICU medications, oxygen supplies, ICU beds, ventilators, and consumables. It is imperative to rehabilitate non-functioning public hospitals, repair extensive damage to crucial infrastructure, support ongoing medical training for human resource for health, sustain availability of EML.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

Hostilities in Syria continue to take a heavy toll on the lives and livelihoods of civilians and the availability, access and sustainability of essential health services and medical supplies was and continues to be always in risk due to the security situation, internal displacement and donors’ support. According to the HNO 2021, 12.4 million people in need of humanitarian health services this year, the already-disrupted health system in Syria. 1- Shortages of qualified staff and health workers.2- Lack of emergency and secondary health care services for PIN among targeted population, due to non-functionality of hospitals, which in turn contribute to an increased burden of diseases (morbidity & mortality rates).3- Attacks on health facilities and health care providers; 28 attacks on hospitals, in the period between January and December 2020. Mitigation measures:1. Strengthening the capacity building of existing health workers and promoting partnerships.2. Advocacy for rehabilitation of hospitals and priority units.

9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

RISKS:-Shortages of qualified staff and staff turnover, staff lack of knowledge in key practices like IPC-Lack of adequate patient data upon hospital admission and discharge-Attacks on health facilities and health care providers,-Lack of emergency and secondary health care services for PIN among targeted population, due to non-functionality of hospitals, which in turn contribute to an increased burden of diseases (morbidity & mortality rates).-Lack of received funds and delays in approvals can trigger critical shortages of life saving medicines and specific medicines MITIGATING MEASURES:-Support MoH to implement new human resources strategy in affected areas;-Strengthening the capacity building of existing health workers through capacity building, including ToTs, and promoting partnerships - Support the minor rehabilitation & equipment for partially damaged priority units at hospitals to enhance health coverage-expand the capacity of the functional hospitals with requirements.-WHO regularly conducts Prioritization Exercise in collaboration with health cluster partners aiming to identify needs, target population, accessibility, availability of services, monthly case load and overlapping with facilities.-Advocate for hospital information system at national level,-Maintain supply chain of critical medicines and supplies based on the prioritized EML.
