Project Summary

The Anglophone Crisis continues to have devastating effects on the people of the North West and South Regions of Cameroon. Girls and boys are the primary victims of the current crisis. This translates into serious violations of their fundamental rights and constant insecurity, causing both visible and invisible trauma in the short and long-term, separation from their families and limited access to basic social services such as education, for the past four years. Being direct or indirect witnesses of violence linked to the conflict, their wellbeing, both physical and psychological, is fundamentally at stake. Through this project, UNICEF and its partners will work to protect children affected by armed conflict from violence, abuse, and exploitation through a series of interventions. These will include mental health and psychosocial support targeting 326,800 children and caregivers; case management for an estimated 42,000 unaccompanied and separated children and other vulnerable children; birth registration for an estimated 2,000 conflict-affected children; sensitization campaigns with the aim to reach out to 157,260 persons, with specific attention to parents to pass on messages on child protection in emergencies, trauma early warning signs for children affected by armed conflict, effects of family separation, GBV in emergencies and other types of violence children are at risk of at the community level so that parents and community members can play an active role in child protection in emergencies.

1 - Besoins / Needs

The crisis in the North West and South West regions has caused the displacement of an estimated 400,000 children within those two regions. Another ‭estimated 110,000 children have been displaced to West and Littoral Regions (MSNA, September 2022). Adolescent boys and girls, children with specific needs and unaccompanied and separated children (UASC) remain the primary categories at risk among all affected groups of IDPs, returnees and affected host communities, especially in rural areas and hard-to-reach areas. Lack of access to basic social services including CP services remains a key risk factor, especially for children in rural areas and most remote localities such as the ones along the Nigerian border where access and insecurity prevent protection actors to implement any program. Physical violence and killing of children are reported. Most importantly, trauma and the psychological impact of the violence is directly affecting their emotional and cognitive development and remains one of the highest priority needs in 2023. Specific data pertaining to children living with disability is not available, but they remain one of the most vulnerable categories at risk due to the disruption of basic social services. To better understand the extent and causes of family separation, in 2023, CP AoR actors will be undertaking a needs assessment, focusing on this group of children and then craft interventions that will address the root causes as well as the protection needs of those already unaccompanied or separated. Lack/loss of birth certificates as a result of displacement and destruction of homes is another key limiting factor for children to attend education facilities when security situation makes it possible and also constitutes an obstacle to benefitting from the protection services they are entitled to as minors. Adolescent boys are more likely to be victims of arbitrary arrests, forced recruitment and extrajudicial executions and are at greater risk of injury or death directly related to the crisis. As a result, some families limit their sons' movements to eliminate the risk of their children being killed or forcibly recruited. The phenomenon of recruitment and use of children by NSAGs is difficult to measure but rather than forced recruitment, the reported growing interest of young boys (and girls to a lesser extent) in joining NSAGs as they continue to be deprived of learning opportunities, is worrisome. Children leaving NSAGs receive no support for their reintegration, are stigmatized and risk being arbitrarily killed. Children are exposed to SGBV as a direct consequence of the violence but also as a negative coping mechanism from families forcing their children (especially girls) into child marriage. The pre-existing discriminatory gender norms and practices disadvantaging girls; combined with the weakening of family and community support networks, leave girls and teenage mothers in a vulnerable situation. Girls living with disability are five times more exposed to sexual violence in the ongoing crisis. Due to lack of documentation, fear of forced recruitment, harassment and arrest of boys and young men, parents often send girls to collect humanitarian assistance or to fulfill their traditional roles by collecting basic items for the household, exposing them to further risk of sexual violence as they are on the move. Absence of functioning health facilities in rural areas, lack of sexual and reproductive health programmes and challenges in sharing efficiently information on existing services and referral pathways are aggravating factors. In urban and peri-urban areas, both in NWSW and outside where IDP children and families from NWSW are present, support will be provided to 2,700 families identified as at-risk to protect children from child labour and exploit as CP AoR lead, will continue to consolidate the coordination, build capacities of members and ensure alignment and complementarity of interventions.
