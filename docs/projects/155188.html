Project Summary

This project will provide GBV prevention and quality services for GBV survivors in a refugee camp and neighbouring host community, addressing existing gaps in assistance and contributing to strengthening social cohesion, focusing on establishing safe spaces for women and girls, peer support and awareness-raising activities to enhance capacity, social empowerment and safety; case management, individual protection assistance and referral; psychosocial support and participatory prevention with a total reach of 4,013 beneficiaries.

Needs

The Rohingya are a conservative community, with social and cultural norms that create tensions around women’s empowerment. Women generally experience barriers to freedom of movement and access to and control over resources, with girls’ access and mobility restricted once they reach puberty. Various reports have shown that crowded settlements, a lack of appropriate WASH facilities and increased vulnerability are putting women and girls at risk of GBV, including sexual harassment, assault and sexual violence, with numerous incidents of GBV reported weekly. A lack of lighting is affecting refugees’, but especially women's and girls' mobility at night and is a particular concern in relation to GBV risks for women and girls who chose to wash at night not to be seen. Women’s mobility is also restricted by the observance of purdah, which limits their ability to access aid or GBV services, a problem compounded the limited information to which women have access. Adolescent girls are highly vulnerable to GBV threats and have very restricted mobility outside the home, so their access to services and information is even more limited. Cases of child marriage and forced marriage have been documented, involving girls as young as 15 and attributable to poverty and displacement. Forced prostitution and trafficking are also risks faced by women and girls in the camps, and such cases are likely to be under-reported. Polygamy has also been reported to have increased within the Rohingya community as a result of displacement. The lack of space for community structures also limits the ability of humanitarian actors to provide protection services, including community centres, child-friendly spaces (CFS) and safe spaces for women and girls. The JRP reported that 62% of refugees are unable to communicate with aid providers; this figure is likely to be higher for women, given the traditional expectation that they should stay at home and perform care work. Female-headed households or households with no male relatives are those least likely to receive information or support. In addition, the fact that almost all majhis are men means that the voices of women and girls are often not heard. There is an evident need for female leadership, especially in the Rohingya community; in the host community, there are some female leaders who can be engaged. There is also a need for more female staff to provide services for women and girls. Women in host communities have reported increased limitations on their freedom of movement and have expressed fear of the new arrivals, due to overcrowding and the lack of privacy. The risk of GBV is high in the host community and is likely to increase in times of economic stress. Child marriage is common in both the Rohingya and Host communities and can often be used by poorer households as a coping strategy in times of crisis. Intimate partner violence (IPV) is also common in both communities, with reports of increased IPV experienced by Rohingya women since displacement, a common and well documented occurrence that happens during and post displacement. IPV is perceived as an issue to be dealt with internally by the family, with no external interference. Female-headed households are likely to be far more vulnerable. The IOM’s Needs and Population Monitoring (NPM) report estimates that 12% of households in the Rohingya community are likely to be female-headed and that 17.35% of the Rohingya mothers are single mothers. Research for the ACAPS Host Community Review found that, as of December 2017, 45% of female-headed households in the host community were vulnerable or very vulnerable, compared with 35% of male-headed households. The Rohingya Emergency Vulnerability Assessment (REVA) assessment from December 2017 concludes that food insecurity for women in the host community is almost as bad as for the Rohingya community, with only one in three women having access to a diversified diet.

Activities or outputs

In 2018, DRC has been implementing GBV programming in the host communities (HC) of Palongkhali (2 wards). Based on GBVSS data (August 2018), there are gaps in GBV services in camp 13 (with only 25-50% coverage) adjacent to the Palongkhali host community, which makes DRC well-suited to extend its GBV programming to this camp. COMMUNITY CENTRE: Establish entry point in one camp and maintain one entry point (in a neighbouring host community. DRC-supported Community Centres (CCs) will provide a safe space for women and adolescent girls and creation of Woman/Girl groups which will benefit from awareness sessions on GBV topics, peer support and recreational activities to enhance social empowerment and safety. CCs will also function as emergency shelters during monsoon/cyclone seasons. DRC will explore a possibility of linking up women’s groups in the two communities through joint activities/events to identify avenues for strengthening social cohesion with active engagement of women and girls. DRC’s longer term vision is to develop the capacity of these communities to eventually take over the operation of community centres. CASE MANAGEMENT (CM): DRC will continue to implement case management (CM) services in targeted host communities and will start CM services in the two targeted camps to support vulnerable individuals in a holistic manner to prevent and respond to GBV through a tailored approach, and with consistent and sustained follow-up. REFERRALS: DRC will facilitate referrals as part of the CM procedures following extensive discussion with the individual of the risks and benefits of accessing each potential service. Case workers will accompany survivors to medical referrals when requested and may do so for other referrals. DRC is a member of the GBVSS, MHPSS, SRH TF, PWG and CPSS and ensures coordinated approaches among the sub-sectors. At camp level DRC Protection, CP and GBV teams will attend sub-sector coordination meetings and will be in contact with camp focal points and other service providers (including DSS and MoWCA, as well as Health, WASH, SMS and Shelter actors) for effective use of established referral pathways. In the host community, the DRC GBV team will be engaging closely with local and international organisations as well as MoWCA to develop effective referral pathways. PSYCHOSOCIAL SUPPORT: DRC will incorporate focused, semi-specialized psychosocial support by lay counsellors (case workers) into case management services, in accordance with the Interagency CM Guidelines. To ensure the quality of this service all case workers will receive ongoing CM supervision from managers throughout the project.INDIVIDUAL PROTECTION ASSISTANCE (IPA): In cases where material support is needed in order to address GBV concerns, DRC will support clients with IPAs to meet specific needs. IPAs aim to mitigate barriers to increased safety by facilitating access to services or reducing specific vulnerabilities to violence.PARTICIPATORY PREVENTION: CM and non-structured PSS sessions will inform the design of activities to facilitate women and girls direct engagement in developing a strategy for engaging men and boys in reducing GBV in their communities. SUPERVISION: One Team Leader will be recruited to ensure quality PSS support and case supervision for staff exposed to secondary trauma while working on CM for GBV survivors, and will be trained by a consultant to support with the establishment of this service. Women and girls will be consulted at every stage of the project to ensure their needs/risks frame the project (setup of the CCs, activities , etc.). Regular feedback from women and girls will be received via GBV staff, through CCs, FGDs, feedback boxes. Women/girls’ involvement in the project will help to mitigate negative and unintended effects, adapting the project where possible. GBV staff will be trained on global GBV standards and curriculums, and GBV core concepts trainings will also be provided to the all staff
