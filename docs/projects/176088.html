Project Summary

NRC seeks to improve the overall protective environment in South Sudan by not only responding to people’s most acute material needs during a crisis, but also by promoting their safety and dignity and to prevent, reduce or mitigate the protection risks they face. It is also to address the underlying causes of displacement and to contribute to creating conditions conducive to durable solutions for displaced populations. To this end, NRC proposes a graduated approach to support the protection of displaced and conflict-affected populations, including static, mobile, and emergency responses.
The project will offer the following services:
• Protection context assessments in all locations and, where possible, community-based protection monitoring, in which community focal points will be trained to report on protection needs and risks. Protection incidents will be documented and analysed to understand trends and evolution in the protection context, and results disseminated to communities and relevant stakeholders.
• Provision of individual protection assistance (IPA), to persons with urgent protection needs.
• Creation of community-driven protection (CDP) mechanisms in hard to reach, underserved areas through mobile, emergency response teams. NRC teams will work with communities to establish a series of local committees, including marginalized groups, through careful selection of representatives to ensure effectiveness and inclusiveness. CDP teams will mobilise community volunteers and solidarity networks- including GBV task force teams- to implement community protection initiatives and actions plans.
• Facilitate improved access to legal identity documentation, including nationality certificates for displacement affected persons who cannot access basic rights and services, especially school-aged children who have challenges applying for state exams. NRC will provide both awareness raising sessions to at-risk populations and individualised assistance in accessing national identity documentation.
• Foster improved ability to exercise HLP rights for individuals and groups affected by displacement with priority given to the most vulnerable, including female and child-headed households, the elderly, individuals with physical and mental disabilities, and those who are extremely poor. NRC will target conflict-affected areas and areas of potential return. NRC will provide awareness raising sessions to communities, as well as offer tailored individual counselling to vulnerable persons requiring further support, up to and including representation in court.
• Enable peaceful coexistence and support inclusive resolutions of local conflict through training and mentoring in collaborative dispute resolution (CDR) mechanisms. CDR trainings will target chiefs, youth groups, older people, opinion leaders, customary authorities, local government authorities, and include members of host, returnee, and IDP communities. NRC will ensure that women are well-represented. Trainings will be complimented with monitoring and mentoring, and support to see inclusive solutions realized.
NRC is in the process of building up this suite of all protection interventions, to ultimately offer all protection services in all areas of existing static implementation. This is in Equatoria(Juba and Kajo Keji, NBEG( Aweil Centre, Aweil West, Aweil East), Warrap(Twic), Unity( Mayom, Leer and Koch) and in Jonglei(Bor South, Duk, Akobo and Twic East). For Child Protection and GBV, NRC will prioritize IPC 3, 4 & 5 locations including Akobo, Duk, Jur River and Mayom. NRC will concentrate on HLP responses in areas of potential return including Equatoria (Juba and Kajo Keji), Jonglei (Bor South and Akobo) Northern and Western Bahr el Ghazal(Wau and Jur River) and Unity State(Mayom and Leer). Community based protection responses will be expanded in Unity and Jonglei. In areas subject to recurrent support from mobile teams; a narrower version of the project will be offered.

1 - Needs

Despite the formation of the Unity Government in South Sudan in February 2020, the humanitarian crisis continues to worsen. South Sudan’s transition has experienced concerning delays in implementing the Revitalised Agreement and finalising security arrangements. The transitional government institutions including a national legislative assembly have stalled. Due to the impact of devastating floods, conflict, and worsening macroeconomic conditions, most households are still facing large food consumption gaps or using extreme livelihoods coping strategies to mitigate them. This is exacerbated by the invasion of locusts that have proliferated East Africa and the Coronavirus pandemic which has made it harder for humanitarian aid to reach those in need. Education, food insecurity, health outcomes are likely to suffer because access to services, food and healthcare has been weakened. The loss of assets due to conflict or floods combined with the economic crises (and price inflation for staples) pushes households to resort to negative coping mechanisms. Such negative coping mechanism include GBV and child protection issues, e.g. forced/early marriage, child labour, denial of access to resources incl. education, increases in intimate partner violence. The social and economic breakdown of communities, their displacement can also result in heightened intra- and inter-community low-level tensions, restrictions to freedom of movement and likely secondary displacement.Protection of Civilian sites(PoCs) which have been a safe haven for the displaced will now face more challenges with their re-designation to IDP settlements fast-tracking the conversation on solutions despite the fact that there has been no concrete progress in realising any accountability, national healing, or reconciliation in South Sudan. Ethnic segregation continues to be concerning specifically related to the pressuring of IDPs to return to particular locations, actions which could ethnically reengineer the country with worrisome potential protection outcomes. The struggle for access to and control of land has shaped the nation’s history and will affect the realisation of durable solutions. Land acts as an identifier of community, belonging and place, as well as a source of income, subsistence and survival. With such strong cultural and historical significance, land is inherently contested and politicized, and access to housing, land and property is unequal, well beyond economic constraints. Gender and ethnicity are significant determining factors in who can exercise their rights to housing, land and property (HLP). When it comes to women’s rights to land, the gap between law and practice is significant; despite legal provisions recognizing the equal rights of women, widespread knowledge and application of these laws remain limited. Across assessments done by NRC in 2020, respondents reported that food insecurity, lack of WASH services, and absence of livelihoods opportunities were due to protection risks, which exposes all sections of the population. Men and boys are at risk of forced recruitment, restrictions on freedom of movement, ill-treatment and killings. Girls and women of every age are at heighted risk of GBV which is widespread. Women and girls in South Sudan experience GBV at a higher rate due to the significant cultural power imbalance between men and women, which is intensified during conflict and displacement. Male perceptions of women and girls as prizes in the context of conflict and violence, and poor female representation within traditional community leadership structures and customary law practices, bear significant implications for addressing these issues which can often result in conflict and violence. The trauma and psychosocial support needs resulting from violence and abuse are critically underserved: while mental health data is limited, one study found that 40% of respondents demonstrated symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder.

2 - Project activities

NRC will implement a community-based protection monitoring project. NRC will synthesize actionable information products relevant to the protection and assistance of displacement and conflict-affected populations and inform further decision-making, prioritisation, advocacy, and programme design for both NRC and the Protection Cluster. The project will train community focal points to collect data and report on protection incidents. Protection monitoring teams will verify incident reports and trends with supplementary key informant and household interviews, as well as focus groups discussions. Utilisation of focal points will result in a greater immediacy of information gathering, as volunteer enumerators will be able to collect and report data in near real time. NRC will offer individual protection assistance (IPA) to persons with urgent protection needs. IPA focuses on addressing a central, immediate protection risk through one-off support with limited follow-up. IPA interventions will engage a client-centred approach aimed at empowering the client – and where appropriate the caregiver – by giving them increased awareness of choices they have in dealing with protection risks, and assisting them to make informed decisions about how to address the risks they face. IPA will usually include some element of material assistance, including facilitate refer, procurement of NFI, or unconditional cash transfer. NRC will offer individual protection assistance (IPA) to survivors of GBV or persons facing urgent GBV risks.NRC will continue prioritizing linking protection responses to education responses especially mainstreaming child protection, provision of PSS in schools targeting mostly girls, providing crucial child protection training to community members and caregivers.Through awareness raising and protection monitoring activities, NRC teams will work to identify community members who are active on issues of protection and resilience to serve on gender and age disaggregated community-based protection committees. While individual committees will be gender-specific, in order to foster more open communication between participants on sensitive issues, NRC will ensure that the committees overall are gender-balanced and non-proportionally representative of larger community make-up, ensuring that otherwise marginalized groups are included. Committees will be empowered to identify and analyse protection risks in their own communities, addressing negative coping and promoting positive community-wide coping mechanisms through engagement with community leaders, awareness-raising sessions and sensitization campaigns. On HLP,CDR, LCD and ELP, NRC will utilize findings in assessments and studies to provide the needed assistance using the following approaches: (1) Information dissemination through group sessions, community sensitization and outreach and mass campaigns when appropriate; (2) Counselling and legal assistance including administrative law procedures for legal identity and business registration, and collective cases aimed at increasing access to land and security of tenure through dialogue between the local government authorities and traditional leaders and other representatives of the disputed parties; (3) Capacity building activities which will involve provision of technical and material support in addition to legal identity, HLP and CDR trainings targeting customary and formal land institutions, with the aim to increase beneficiaries’ access to standardized justice by increasing the knowledge and awareness of those involved in resolving land disputes in both the customary and the formal justice system. HLP mainstreaming trainings will target secondary targets as mentioned above; (4) Research, Advocacy and Policy Initiatives that would build on the expertise the programmes have developed on various ranges of legal issues related to land, legal identity and employment laws and procedures.

3 - Project indicators

NRC will consolidate information on the indicators at intervals throughout the project cycle. This will allow for sufficient time for progress to be demonstrated and adjustments made if needed. The Project will follow the NRC M&E plan to track indicators in the logical framework. The data in the M&E plan will be collected using the 5W tool by the Cluster Members to track age, gender, geographical local and nature of displacement. Results from the monitoring will be shared with the cluster members for learning, adoption of best practices, advocacy and resource mobilization. NRC will collect feedback on NRC contribution to cluster coordination and to the overall humanitarian coordination effort, lessons learnt and best practices. Monitoring and evaluation of the direct implementation activities of the project will be undertaken in line with NRC’s guidelines that emphasize participatory approaches, relevance, accountability and timeliness of information and data. The focus will be on results monitoring which will measure project's progress towards and achievement at output and outcome levels. NRC will develop monitoring, reporting, and evaluation frameworks with gender and age sensitivities. The M&E system will consist of following key tools:a) An M&E Work plan/matrix outlining plans for data collection, analysis, and use and reporting. The plan will be a living document that will be reviewed periodically based on emerging needs. In this, NRC will use KOBO mobile data collection tools to conduct survey including multi-sector rapid assessments, PDM and baseline/end line surveys for measuring outcome indicators.b) An Indicator Definition and Analysis will provide a detailed description of each indicator, sources of data, the measurement parameters and beneficiary disaggregation by sex and age.c) An Indicator Tracking Tool will track the progress against a target on monthly basis and measure if a result is achieved based on the prescribed indicators in the log frame. The tool will be shared during quarterly reviews of the project.d) A Detailed Activity Implementation Plan will outline key activities and tasks to be undertaken to achieve the planned results. This tool will track activity implementation in order to ensure planned activities are implemented on schedule and where appropriate adjustments are made.e) A simple beneficiary compliant/feedback response mechanism will be implemented to enhance accountability and transparency in the delivery of the project activities. This will also consider child-friendly feedback methods.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

The Project design has considered the principle of ‘Do no harm’ to consumers and markets in areas of intervention by making use of market systems when and where they are functional. In South Sudan, practice has shown that the market functionality varies considerably over time and ranges according to seasons and movement of populations. Various studies and assessments on the complexity of market functionality will be taken into consideration to address the potential risks that different interventions may have on the market actors and people’s access to markets, and the risks that local markets might pose to the response impact and effectiveness.

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

Cash and Voucher Assistance is an efficient and effective mode of humanitarian assistance especially in upholding humanitarian dignity through upholding individual agency of the beneficiaries. It also strengthens local economies by stimulating local markets.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

NRC takes prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse very seriously. All NRC staff engaged in the project will be trained in PSEA; further, staff will be trained in PFA and identification of potential survivors of SEA, and receive training and mentoring in how to respond appropriately and with empathy. All communications with affected populations will stress their right to receive services without coercion or exploitation. NRC will work with communities to develop confidential, appropriate complaints and feedback mechanisms, and respond to any complaints promptly and transparently, to the extent that we are still able to assume both the survivor and perpetrator of their safety. NRC will strive to recruit gender-balanced teams and take every effort to ensure that vulnerable women are able to work directly with competent woman staff.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

The Project will incorporate feedback and complaints mechanisms that will be understandable by older people, women, girls, people with disabilities addressing the feelings of these vulnerable individuals on what they feel are the safest and most appropriate ways for them to provide feedback and make complaints. This includes the use of simple language and easy to use feedback forms and provide support to these populations in providing feedback, including using alternative authorized persons (like caregivers, family members who are literate etc.) to give their feedback. Older persons, women, girls, people with disabilities will be sensitized on the importance of complaints and feedback mechanisms to address their specific needs and on what they can expect from humanitarian organisations. NRC will act on feedback and complaints from Older persons, women, girls, people with disabilities etc. in a way that respects their safety, dignity and rights and adapt organisational policies if they are not inclusive of these groups. NRC project staff will continually receive training to make them aware that Older persons, women, girls, people with disabilities etc have the right to submit feedback and complaints on an equal basis with everyone else and to this end, they will be trained on supporting these individuals to provide feedback either directly or indirectly through community focal persons. Feedback and complaints from older persons, women, girls, people with disabilities etc. will be shared with national and international partners involved in humanitarian action, respecting the confidentiality and privacy of individuals who have submitted this feedback.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Accountability lies at the core of this project. Prior to activity implementation, the protection team will ensure that beneficiaries and stakeholders are involved in all stages of project development and informed of the feedback mechanisms available to them. Beneficiaries will be able to make confidential suggestions for improving the project, lodge complaints where they disagree with implementation. NRC will ensure that the project will systematically engage with all beneficiaries and relevant stakeholders, including the most marginalised, to deliver information about assistance to be provided in such a way as to maximise meaningful participation and consultation. Information shared will include the scope of the proposed interventions, entitlements of those involved, clarify any targeting of vulnerable groups, and information essential to accessing companion services, as well as other relevant information. NRC will consult with communities about the optimal way to share information, and provide information in forms accessible to those with limited literacy and in local languages, to the extent that it is possible. Taking precautions to make the project as transparent and inclusive as possible will both service to make the program more inclusive and contribute to a sense of local ownership, as well as mitigate possible negative consequences of targeting or prioritising vulnerable persons and minority groups. NRC has utilised a variety of approaches to provide beneficiaries with information, including holding community awareness raising sessions, both general and targeted at specific groups according to gender, age, and other considerations. IEC materials used and messages shared will be tailored to meet the needs of these unique groups. NRC has also training community mobilisers to go door-to-door in targeted communities and share information, as well as sharing information over the radio. These and other approaches would be used in the current project, following consultation with communities about the best way to reach affected groups, and depending on the duration of the intervention (i.e. static versus mobile).Messages are both context-specific and tailored for gender and age-groups. In particular, NRC revisits all protection messages after each protection needs assessment, to ensure that messages are addressing identified needs and targeted to the most vulnerable groups based on the contextual needs. Messages are particularly tailored to reach children, people with disabilities, and older persons. NRC strives to include local context in its messages, such as alerting beneficiaries to existing referral pathways for complementary services. NRC also considers messages from a Do No Harm perspective, moderating messages as appropriate.The project will offer several complaints and feedback mechanisms, depending on the needs of the community. In initial assessments, NRC will consult with targeted persons about the best approach to establish CFMs and strive to be responsible and flexible in our implementation.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The project will use the beneficiaries' feedback for adaptive learning, improvement and decision making. In line with NRC’s global commitment to ensure that all aspects of humanitarian intervention – including monitoring and evaluation – are informed by and reflect the voices of the communities with which we work, NRC will ensure that monitoring and evaluation activities are inclusive and accessible. Whether utilising IPA case closure and evaluation forms, PDM tools, or more general community evaluation FGDs and KIIs, NRC teams will seek to engage a range of community members from both sexes and across a range of diversity characteristics. In assessment stages and throughout implementation, NRC teams will discuss with communities the ideal mechanisms for monitoring and evaluation to ensure inclusive approaches. In hard to reach areas, or those with emergency responses that might mitigate the kinds of monitoring and evaluation approaches possible in more static locations, NRC will take particular care to discuss with communities safe, realistic options for monitoring and evaluation and means of improving accountability to affected populations.NRC believes that all projects must be informed by and reflect the voices of the communities in which we work. To that end, NRC has conducted several gender-sensitive protection needs assessments in 2020 in areas that will be targeted by the project in both static and mobile locations. Areas included host communities, informal settlements of displaced persons and occurred in five different states. Assessment teams conducted focus group discussions and key informant interviews. Purposive and convenience sampling was used, depending on the location, timing of the assessment, and capacity of the team. Persons that were targeted for inclusion included conflict-and disaster-affected IDPs, host community members, recent refugee returnees, people with disabilities, older persons, and female-headed households. Across these assessments, NRC teams spoke with adult men and women and children above 15 years old to gather information to tailor assistance and response to best suit the community needs.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

South Sudan is above all in a protection crisis and NRC is aware that the complex protection context in South Sudan has the potential to both negatively impact or be negatively impacted by the intervention. As such, the project has been designed in accordance with Protection and Do No Harm principles, and will follow a gendered conflict sensitive approach. All staff associated with the project will participate in a Do No Harm training with particular emphasis on gender-related protection concerns and conflict sensitivity. Gender-informed conflict sensitivity assessments will be conducted regularly for all activities in all locations, with activities adjusted accordingly. Some of the key protection and conflict risks associated with the proposed activity design include housing land and property (HLP) disputes and conflicts linked to wider land access and use issues. Returns from displacement sites and neighboring countries will likely lead to HLP disputes, as the land and properties of many returnees has been damaged or occupied, with women and girls experiencing heightened risks due to social and cultural norms. As part of the mitigation measures, NRC is proposing activities that aim to remove barriers to HLP that impact the ability of vulnerable groups and raise awareness on land rights. As designed, the proposed project has several activities explicitly intended to mitigate risks of main activities; social cohesion activities, such as inter-community dialogues and formation of youth peace committees. Likewise, collaborative dispute resolution trainings for local leaders will work to counter conflict cycles and maximization of mutual benefit. Put simply, various activities will not be implemented discretely, but in such a way that they are more than the sum of their parts. Due to the prevalence of GBV and disempowerment of women at the household level throughout South Sudan, as well as the well-documented risks associated with livelihoods activities targeted at women in a range of settings, NRC is particularly aware of the potential for exacerbating domestic and intimate partner violence. Teams will be trained in gender-sensitive implementation and how to identify and approach potential survivors of GBV. In all project locations, NRC will conduct qualitative service maps of services available to survivors and conduct a workshop of local actors to facilitate a functioning referral pathway. Dedicated teams will further work with communities to prevent and mitigate GBV: first, training local leadership on prevention of GBV and especially the prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse; and second, conducting targeted community dialogues with men, women, girls, and boys to challenge gender norms and mitigate harmful practices. Both community-level activities will be undertaken with the intention to promote equal, safe participation of men and women of all ages in the project, as well as to further prevent GBV and support the creation of greater gender parity and empowerment. Risks of SEA are also present within any provision of humanitarian services. Project staff will therefore be trained on PSEA including refresher trainings for the number of staff members who have already participated in PSEA awareness and training. Awareness raising on SEA and accountability of humanitarian organizations will also be conducted in targeted communities to mitigate this. A safe Feedback Complaints and Reporting Mechanism will be established within target communities. Beyond this, NRC will conduct regular activity and location-specific protection risk assessments considering the specific risks associated with each activity and coordinating mitigation efforts. It is also acknowledged that the current COVID-19 pandemic environment poses a risk to individuals physical and emotional safety and access to project activities. COVID-19 prevention measures will be adhered to within project activities.
