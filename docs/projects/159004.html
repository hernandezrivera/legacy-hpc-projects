Project Summary

It is estimated that 80 per cent of 282,360 students (145,867 boys and 136,493 girls) registered at UNRWA schools in the 2019/20 school year in Gaza require assistance in overcoming a variety of challenges, including poverty, additional learning needs, access restrictions and electricity shortages at home. Recurrent hostilities and the blockade continue to have a deleterious impact on the learning environment for Palestine refugee children in Gaza, who are accommodated in 276 schools, 70 per cent of which operate on a double-shift basis. In response, the Agency-wide Education in Emergencies (EiE) approach provides inclusive, equitable, quality and safe education to Palestine refugee children and youth in times of emergencies. In addition to implementing the EiE approach across all UNRWA schools in Gaza, UNRWA will: (i) recruit and train 600 support teachers to provide learning support to more than 70,000 students in grades 3 and 4 who are struggling to meet minimum academic standards in Arabic and mathematics, out of them, more than 17,000 students will also receive remedial materials to assist their learning; (ii) ensure that 80 percent of students have access to basic school and learning materials through the distribution of basic stationary; and (iii) ensure that all students have an access to mental health and psychosocial support at school.

1 - Needs

In the school year 2019/20, 282,360 students (145,867 male and 136,493 female) attend 276 UNRWA schools across Gaza. This represents an increase of more than 3,400 students compared to last year. To accommodate such growth, 70% of UNRWA schools are operating on double shifts basis. The increasing number of students further stretches the capacity of UNRWA schools, leaving little time for support or extracurricular activities although these are vital to foster a child’s psycho-social well-being. According to the country report on out-of-school children (OOSC , 2018), barriers to accessing and completing education include: low academic achievement and lack of interest in education among children which is a main reason for dropping out; well-being of the child and the family e.g. external shocks such as health crisis or death in the family; the economic barriers arising from the cost of education e.g uniforms, textbooks, school stationary, transportation; exclusionary administrative policies and practices. In-light of these, UNRWA estimates that 80% students are in need of humanitarian education assistance. Such students are from poor families, students with special needs (estimated 10,000), low achievers (approximately 75,000), new entrants or ﬁrst graders (more than 32,000), repeaters (approximately 1,300 boys and 600 girls), drop outs (approximately 700 boys and 130 girls), over age students, students living in Access Restricted Areas who are all in need of support and empowerment. The unprecedented deterioration of the economic situation compiled with the energy crisis seriously impact the educating environment of children. This situation does not facilitate at-risk-of-failure students, who are already in need for extra support in several topics, including Arabic and Mathematics. Moreover, there is a critical need in terms of providing all low-achieving students, repeaters and at-risk-of-failure students with learning materials not only in Arabic and Mathematics but also in the other curriculum's core subjects. The blockade, as well as the dire socio-economic situation, has had serious repercussions on the psychosocial well-being of Palestine refugees. As a result of the current crisis in Gaza, the WHO estimated that approximately 210,000, or over one in 10 people (10% of the population) will have severe mental health problems and about 54% of Palestinian boys and 47% of Palestinian girls aged six to 12 years reportedly have emotional and/or behavioral disorders, and the overall disease burden for mental illness is estimated to account for about 3% of disability-adjusted life years. UNRWA conducted a study in May 2017 in Gaza assessing the psychosocial well-being of 2,262 adult refugees attending 15 health centres and 3,142 refugee students attending 45 UNRWA elementary and preparatory schools. The study indicated a high level of psychosocial stress among both students and adults, with almost one half of adults (48.9%) experiencing poor well-being (63% of these warranting further screening for depression) and almost 30% of children experiencing serious diﬃculties. Both children and parents reported a prevalence of emotional problems (students spoke of a continuous sense of fear and anxiety, as well as learning diﬃculties and lack of future career opportunities; adults had diﬃculties identifying their strengths and coping resources, focusing instead only on problems and hardships). Therefore, psycho-social support to student is an integral component of building resilience for students during emergencies.

2 - Activities

In response to the described needs to enhance the learning environment for refugee children, UNRWA aims to ensure access to protective, inclusive and child friendly quality education for all boys and girls attending UNRWA schools. UNRWA focuses on three key interventions to ensure that: students who are struggling to meet minimum academic standards are supported by support teachers with remedial education materials, students have an access to basic school stationary and appropriate learning materials needed, and all students have an access to mental health and psycho-social support at schools. The first intervention is to support students from grades 3 and 4 who failed or at risk of failing in Arabic and Mathematics, struggling to meet minimum academic standards. They will be supported by support teachers throughout the year. The teaching materials will include the formative assessment tools that guide the teachers on the mechanism of the student’s academic improvement, focusing on the basic concepts and skills that the students need in order to catch up with the curricula in addition to increasing the drills of evaluation activities and exercises related to individual differences between students. Support teachers will check students’ work, monitor and follow up low achieving students’ performance, communicate with parents and school management, encourage and motivate students, prepare regular quizzes to follow students’ progress, follow up students’ homework and assignments and liaise with regular teachers on catch up plans and student levels. Regular-class teachers and school principals will monitor and evaluate the performance of the support teachers to make sure that they support students in the best way. Support materials will be used by regular teachers, support teachers and parents and will be prepared by UNRWA’s Professional Development and Curricula Unit coordinators in cooperation with the education specialists at UNRWA Education Programme. In line with cluster logical frame work and plan, this intervention will target more than 70,000 students assisted by 600 support teachers, and more than 17,000 of them will also receive remedial education materials for further support. The second intervention is to ensure that children are prepared to go back to school by providing about 225,000 (80% of all) students with basic stationary at the beginning of each semester in order to participate in class and complete study at home. Basic stationary includes a range of subject notebooks, pens, pencils and erasers thereby ensuring the students’ learning requirements according to minimum humanitarian standards. The third intervention is the provision of the psycho-social activities and interventions at 276 UNRWA schools, which will include school guidance program complemented by parent education sessions to promote a holistic and integrated approach to child and family well-being. Individual and group counselling remain important interventions for children experiencing more serious psycho-social diﬃculties.

9 - Protection mainstreaming
9.1 - Do No Harm

The agency is taking the principles of “Do No Harm” seriously within its operations to ensure safety and dignity of all served refugees including women, girls and disabled people. In general, basic education in UNRWA schools are provided in a manner that keeps all service receivers away from any potential to face any hazards or threats, physically and emotionally. The UNRWA schools are equipped with proper equipment and provide spaces for boys and girls including students with disabilities to study comfortably without being harmed or exposed to any danger. UNRWA has a dedicated Protection and Neutrality Unit and human resources working on Protection mainstreaming, Gender mainstreaming, as well as several GBV services and referral system across its programs. Within this scope, UNRWA regularly assesses the level of compliance with Protection standards of the services provided to refugees through a Protection Audit. UNRWA schools are therefore regularly monitored against the four main protection mainstreaming pillars, as defined by the Global Protection Cluster and echoed by UNRWA, the assessed principles are: (i) Prioritize safety, dignity and do not harm; (ii) Meaningful access to services; (iii) Accountability; and (iv) Participation and empowerment of refugees.

9.2 - Equal and impartial access to aid

UNRWA welcomes all Palestine refugee children from grade 1 to 9 in its schools across the Gaza Strip. The location of schools takes into consideration of safety concerns, especially for girls, including the accessibility, potential risks to reach the area for different groups of population. Further, schools are clearly marked and visible, respecting UNRWA neutrality policy. Educational staff, who are regularly targeted by capacity building activities including on protection mainstreaming and gender, are able to refer specific protection cases to MHPSS counselors for follow up. UNRWA estimates that 80% students are in need of humanitarian education assistance. This project is targeting all students in need (80% out of total registered students) for the provision of stationary in order to maintain the students' minimum learning necessities. It will also support low-achieving students to meet minimum academic standards to prevent them from dropping out, while school counselors will provide comprehensive MHPSS support at schools.
