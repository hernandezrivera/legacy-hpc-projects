Project Summary

During 2021, BRAC will provide overall shelter provisions in eleven Rohingya camps and four host community unions. Community led shelter programme will be established with leadership of women groups at block level in camps and women leaders in host areas, who will be facilitated to conduct community-based PRA. Based on a set of criteria, most vulnerable households, prioritising women, elderly and disabled, will be identified. 11% HHs in camps and 6.81% HHs in host community have persons with disability. These vulnerable groups will be represented and engaged in the PRA. Through the women leaders, individual and collective beneficiary feedback mechanism in-built into the project, feedback and complaints will be received and redressed.
1000 two-storey steel-framed, stilt-structure shelters will be constructed for2000 HHs in nine camps, equipped as required with assistive devices for the disabled and elderly if needed. 1000 single storied new shelters for host community HHs will be constructed. Shelter reinforcement through vouchers or in-kind assistance on need basis will be provided for an estimated 900 HHs in nine camps and 15,000 solar lamps for eleven camps and for 500 HC HHs along with 15,000 solar lamps across four unions. Beneficiary will be selected by house to house visit considering people in extreme vulnerability, need in solar lamp whether it is damaged or not, prioritization of women, adolescents, elderly and women headed households. Split of the assistance per camp will be coordinated with other actors implementing similar activities to avoid duplication and avoid tension between beneficiaries.
BRAC’s M&E unit will conduct PDM of shelter materials, other NFI items and the shelter quality. This with the community feedback mechanism, will guide project to cater appropriately to both communities. A structured and rigorous M&E plan will guide assessment of project achievement as per target, on-going assessment, data collection and analysis.
BRAC will maintain coordination with different sectors to ensure context specific shelter management in camp and host communities and evidenced-based application of non-food items. As per BRAC’s strict safe-guarding policy, all staff (21) will be oriented on PSEA/safeguarding and gender mainstreaming, thus ensuring appropriate environment for both beneficiaries and staff, and interventions tailored as per needs of the vulnerable groups.

1 - Identified Needs

In 2017, almost a million Rohingyas fled to Bangladesh in a very short period of time. Currently, over 860,000 Rohingya reside in makeshift and emergency shelters across 34 camps, most of which meet less than the basic humanitarian standards, overcrowded, and will not withstand seasonal and adverse weather conditions. The quality of the current shelters and poor terrain as well as the lack of privacy within these shelters has a serious impact on the physical and psychological well-being of the refugees, especially for women, children, older persons and person with specific needs (PwSN). Also, according to the government decision to support the host community who have been impacted by the Rohingya influx, BRAC will ensure that the host community receives at least 25% of the support.Shelter improvements and upgrading in terms of durable and long-lasting materials provision, including household level site improvements intervention like construction of ramp and hand-railing are urgently required prior to the need of elderly, PWD. But it is well known to all that space is limited in the very crowded camps. Shelters on hills are at risk of landslides and in flood prone areas. BRAC Shelter Sector performed a need assessment on Shelter and Site Improvement interventions this year, 2020. BRAC’s assessment along with that of the Joint Multi Sector Needs Assessment (Oct 2020) has found that during the COVID-19 lockdown that in the camps there was lack of sufficient shelter assistance to make repairs was a common concern; some shelters have been completely destroyed (collapsed/flooded) –in particular, shelter located on hill-side and in low-lying areas reported to be at risk; Rohingya HHs lack money to repair shelter themselves and additionally were not able to maintain social distancing because of lack of space. It was not possible to venture to collect shelter materials and the quality of materials currently in the shelters are deteriorating. 28% households have not been able to make improvements to their shelter despite reporting issues and another 28% was able to purchase materials to make improvements. In the host community, the main concern was about not being able to make repairs due to loss of income and an increase in price of shelter materials. 39% HHs reported that they had no money to pay for materials, 9% reported they could not pay for labour, 8% could not access materials and 5% did not receive any support. Despite the efforts of the humanitarian response to increase women’s participation in community based discussions, so that their specific needs are heard and addressed, there is a community resistance to this, especially in the camps. Women and girls still lack access to information about provisions and services, complaints and feedback mechanisms, making them less confident to voice their needs, complain and seek redressal. BRAC has found that the needs of the HHs in the target areas vary considerably according to shelter condition and geographical location of HHs. The government has approved two-storey shelters in the camps since the camps are very crowded. Within limited space, more HHs can be accommodated and each HH may have a little more space. Given the space limitation and durability, the 2-storeyed steel-frame structure of the transitional shelter and improved shelter types is more suitable to meet their needs. Rohingya households require essential needs and replenishment of items, such as solar lamps which have been distributed in the previous year and is amongst the commonly requested NFI items. Currently 58% Rohingya HHs report not having enough light.
