Project Summary

The project intends to provide a holistic, comprehensive and intersectoral response to the problems of the Education System in
EES and Lakes State, starting from the assumption that schools represent platforms through which also Protection, Nutrition, Hygiene, Health and Psychosocial needs of vulnerable children can be addressed. Moreover, AVSI response wants to support the entire
education system, starting from nursery and primary school, up to secondary and university program, and including the alternative programs offered by the Accelerating Learning Program (ALP), such as the establishment of Adult Literacy centres. As the main
sponsor of St. Mary College, the only institution in the country offering a 3-year diploma program devoted exclusively to the training of primary school teachers, AVSI has contributed to mitigate the downfall in the public and private investment for the training of
new teachers and dealt with the challenges of providing in-service teacher training to primary schools. Finally, after 14 years of experience in running a comprehensive and personalized scholarship program for primary and secondary students in South Sudan,
AVSI has refined an approach that has proved to reduced dropout, improve cognitive results, and promote female education. Therefore, learners and teachers from all the different steps of education system will be supported in an integrated way, by providing
scholastic materials, training and sensitization sessions on subjects such as education, child protection, SGBV, health, psychological well-being including early-entrance-into school awareness campaigns. In order to avoid the absenteeism of teachers in schools,
the project intends to provide an integrated and comprehensive support to the teachers through the provision of food incentives, teacher mentoring in the schools, support to county education departments and payams, re-service trainings for female teachers in
order to increase the enrolment of female teachers (only 20%). Furthermore, considered that many of the teachers have not completed S4 it will be important to complete their certificate, while they continue the teaching. Structural interventions will be done in
schools to improve the quality and safety of the learning environments, i.e. Temporarily Learning Spaces, renovation of classrooms, construction of latrines and school kitchens, erection or rehabilitation of fences, construction of boarding schools or dormitories,
drilling or maintenance of boreholes to ensure that all the schools are having water point, for safe re-opening after Covid-19 pandemic. Schools will be used as an entry point to promote good hygiene and health practices through hygiene sessions, HIV/AIDS
prevention and reproductive health sessions including Covid-19 prevention campaigns. The students and teachers will also be supported in improving their food security status, by receiving training and agricultural inputs for school gardens for their daily
consumption. On the basis of a holistic concept of education, the project aims to involve also the other relevant actors in this process, first of all the parents and the communities at large. For these reason, parents, community members, PTAs & SMCs will be involved in
campaigns and meetings to promote the importance of education and discuss about sensitive topics such as education for girls, violence and SGBV, child protection, among other. Since COVID-19 pandemic still represents a menace to the already fragile education system, the project will have a cross cutting component in the whole intervention to ensure all COVID-19 mitigation measures are in lace. The objectives of the project are: To improve the quality of education and to ensure access to safe learning spaces for vulnerable children, adolescents, and youth; To provide access for children, adolescents and youth to integrated social protection services; To promote resilience and capacity building of teachers, parents, and community at large.

1 - Needs

There are 3.6 million people estimated to be in need of education services in 2022 in the Republic of South Sudan based on Feb 2022 HNO . Of the estimated population, 97% are children, 50% are female and 15% are with disabilities. The interplay of underlying forces such as COVID 19, broader socio-economic challenges, destruction of some schools in prone-flooded parts of the country and insecurity in volatile areas overwhelmed the education system and impacted on school functionality. Indeed, the prolonged closure of schools (March 2021 – April 2021) for all grades except the candidate classes (Grade 8 & S4) as a result of COVID 19 exacerbated existing education challenges and inequities. Recurrent communal violence through 2021 in Lakes, Jonglei, the Greater Pibor Administrative Area, Upper Nile, Central Equatoria, Warrap, Unity and Western Equatoria led to school closures. The displacement of teachers and children and revenged killings limited children's access to schools.Learners in the 2021 Education Need Assessment (ENA) cited insecurity in and around schools as a major reason for learner absenteeism and drop-out in 2021. There was a significant learning loss due to these factors. The assessment findings cited the delays in the payment of teachers’ salaries and incentives across South Sudan as the main reason for the high teachers’ attrition and absenteeism rates and poor quality of teaching in schools. AVSI’s 2020 multi-sector survey findings reveal the same trend in Eastern Equatoria and Lakes for high attrition and absenteeism rates due to delays in payments of teachers’ salaries. The survey4 revealed that 43% of its population in Magwi County pulled out their children from respective schools for lack of teachers or truancy on the part of the teachers. Direct and indirect costs (SDF , volunteer teacher payments, uniform, examination fees) were cited as barriers to regular attendance and drop out in Eastern Equatoria and Lakes states. In Lakes state, the most stressing factors for not attending school by both children below 10 years and up to 15 years is lack of money to pay for scholastic materials & parent contribution fees. Other barriers to children/adolescents accessing education in Eastern Equatoria and Lakes state are inadequate learning spaces, untrained teachers, hunger, distance to school and lack of school supplies. 60% of ENA assessed schools do not provide food for learners; thus, the reason why hunger is a key barrier to education and one of the determining factor for regular attendance, performance and retention. Early and forced marriages and teenage pregnancies are widely reported in the ENA as reasons for drop-out. In both AVSI’s multi-sector surveys in Lakes and Eastern Equatoria, lower classes P.1- P4 girls’ enrolment is promising but it keeps on reducing in the upper classes (P.5-P.8). Over-age girls who enter into the education system late are vulnerable to early and forced marriages and pregnancies before completing their primary level education simply because of some harmful cultural perceptions inherent in the communities. During the prolonged closure of schools, a sizeable number of girls fell victims of forced marriages or teenage pregnancies in Eastern Equatoria and Lakes. Children with Disabilities (CwDs) also face numerous challenges in accessing quality education because of lack of mobility or assistive devices. Over half of schools in Eastern Equatoria do not have latrines accessible for CwDs. In addition, teachers are not trained to foster or deliver inclusive education and school communities are not sensitized on the need to create enabling environments for CwDs.Half of the schools in AVSI’s multi sector survey are reported to have no access to safe clean water and lack menstrual health management to improve female retention and progression. Over 70% of schools in South Sudan reported having a washroom for girls and half said they had never received dignity kits for female learn

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

AVSI has in place a tested system of yearly multi-sector surveys that through the employment of more than 400 questions (including question on GBV, hazards, protection, disabilities, education, gender inclusion, etc.), a large number of interviews (more than 400 HHs) capable of providing detailed reference at the subcounty (payam) level, an analysis centred on specific groups (single mothers, families with members with disabilities, poor farmers, etc) and the participatory discussion of such findings with the staff andrepresentatives of the communities ensures not only that projects are designed on the base of the specific needs of the different groups of the population, but also that such knowledge is interiorized by AVSI staff and becomes the base of an informed conversation and a common understanding with local communities, ensuring feedback from the most vulnerable within thecommunity. In the last 12 months AVSI has produced 4 of such surveys which, along with other research aimed at monitoring the results of AVSI’s projects, are accessible on the “Assessment” section of the Humanitarian Response’s website. The questionnaire used for the Multi-Sector Survey can be accessed at this webpage https://ee.humanitarianresponse.info/x/#Yztl. Beside these surveys, AVSI has a tested system of detailed post distribution Monitoring which is used to gather precise feedbacks over the targeting, timing, transparency and overall implementation of activities oriented at the community level. During each field visit, AVSI personnel is required to fill apposite forms that include a section for open-ended feedbacks and complaints. Finally, the numerous permanent bases that AVSI has across the targeted communities are equipped with mail boxes, suggestion boxes; focal point protection help desks for unanimous and isolated reporting of abuses or complaints.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Concerning protection, AVSI Foundation will work collaboratively with other actors (UN agencies, NGOS, FBOs and CBOs) involved in child protection issues to safeguard the delivery of protection services in the schools and communities. It will work directly with the project schools’ teachers and the PTAs to promote zero-tolerance for sexual exploitation and abuse in the schools including all forms of discrimination against girls and CWDs. AVSI will be accountable to the beneficiaries by enabling them to participate in the project at the planning, implementation, and evaluation phases. Finally, appropriate mechanisms through which affected populations can measure the adequacy of interventions, and address concerns and complaints will be set up. All AVSI South Sudan staff members have been trained on child protection, disability and gender mainstreaming, has own policies in place on PSEAH, Child Protection, Child Safeguarding, Code of Conducts that are explained and countersigned by each staff and key supplier and have capacity to share the importance of this policies with any stakeholder they interact with. On regularly basis, AVSI conducts community engagement meetings such as awareness campaigns, innovative youth events that allows open participation/discussion over the quality of progress of the activity conducted. AVSI embrace a last-mile approach through field-based personal and daily visits to the field and conducts impact analysis and post monitoring reviews, which are analysed distinguishing relevant categories of people with the objective of providing detailed information about the targeting of the intervention and beneficiaries barriers to access services. Subsequent projects are designed to address such barrier and put in place a system of monitoring that puts special emphasis on monitoring the targeting of those groups of beneficiaries found to face particular barriers.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

AVSI Foundation will place protection and gender issues at the centre of the intervention by engaging directly with the IDPs, returnees and host communities’ children and CWDs so that their needs, priorities, rights are addressed within the scope of the project. AVSI Foundation will always create conducive and interactive environments that encourage them to express their needs, rights and priorities. Apart from the active involvement of the rights holders in the project, AVSI Foundation will ensure that the duty bearers (Government, PTAs/SMCS, institutions and non-state actors) play an effective role to promote the rights of the CWDs and girls. Much emphasis will be placed on these rights during the PTAs/SMCs trainings and the awareness-raising campaigns. The project is committed to gender equality (G2a marker). Activities will be developed with the active participation of women teachers and parents in all stages of the implementation process, to ensure that girls’ priority needs are taken into consideration and that WaSH infrastructures meet the right gender standards (IASC, 2015, Guidelines for Integrating Gender-Based Violence Interventions in Humanitarian Action: Reducing risk, promoting resilience and aiding recovery). Key gender- Mainstreamed activities: - Training will be conducted by both males and females facilitators; - Provision of dignity kits to allow regular attendance to school; - Training classes will be mixed and the participants will work together; - Training will cover menstrual hygiene and girls education awareness campaigns; - Hygiene clubs members will be both males and females; - Boarding schools for girls will be supported in this project - GBV awareness campaigns - Latrines will be sex-segregated - safety audits will be conducted to address major protection concerns, particularly regarding GBV. Moreover, prevention of GBV (Sexual Exploitation) is pursued by displaying SEA code of conducts at service access points (schools, health facilitates, distribution points and community). Community members, staff and stakeholder’s capacity will be strengthened through specific PSEA trainings.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

The capillary and continuous presence that AVSI has in the communities targeted by the intervention (AVSI has more than 80% of its staff, including senior positions outside Juba), and the extensive use of staff recruited locally among people recognized by the communities, ensures that such fruitful cooperation with project beneficiaries continues throughout the implementation of the project. All project’s beneficiaries (female and male IDPs and returnees children, CWDs and host communities’ children) will be considered as capable and active resources rather than as passive recipients who do not have a say in the project. To include the perspectives of the very pupils and teachers, moreover, AVSI conducts in the schools that supports surveys that provide a reading of people and students wellbeing, expectations, and opinion about the schools. For this goal AVSI has prepared specific tools adapted to the context and of proven efficacity. By running this survey, AVSI found that in the schools of Magwi, where more pronounced had been the displacement, nearly 40% of students reported to have lived in refugee camps over the course of the previous two years. In the first year of this proposed project, AVSI intends to conduct an equivalent Pupil-Teacher Survey in all the schools served by the project, to share and validate the results with the head teachers and to promote a reflection of its finding in the context of the peer-to-peer activities of knowledge sharing among teachers. Thanks to a very localized approach with AVSI staff regularly present and usually coming from the same community of project locations, accountability is pursued also directly through its staff that can act as bridge between top management and beneficiaries. Furthermore, considering the purposely field-oriented management team (officers and also project managers are field based), beneficiaries and affected population at large as constant possibility to interact and file complain to staff at different level. Furthermore, AVSI periodically shares finding of monitoring and evaluation activities with SMoE, PTAs and communities at large, ensuring that project decisions are justified, shared and finally owned by the affected population. Yearly, AVSI conducts surveys which results are discussed through open meetings with the community, the county and payam officials and traditional leaders (Boma and sub Boma Chiefs). During these events, AVSI explains to the community its plans for the incoming years as a means to an informed discussion among community members and receive solid suggestions. AVSI extension officers, AAP committees before selecting a person/group/community as beneficiaries, establish a case specific dialog where AVSIs expectations are compared with the one from the beneficiaries in order to evaluate possible challenges/ intervention failure chance. Beside the surveys, AVSI’s beneficiaries have access to information during participatory meetings which provided platform to address issues linked to power-relations whereby some strata of the population culturally marginalized can find the opportunity to express themselves, include the view of beneficiaries in programming and allow intervention in case of issues reported thus increase transparency and accountability through awareness of beneficiaries on protection-related issues as well as project-design.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

Firstly, as is often reported from communities, humanitarian assistance is at timeschanneled to more organized communities, leaving other communities (even morevulnerable populations) less assisted. To minimize this, AVSI always attempts tocover large as well as small communities in both central and more peripherallocations. For the second risk, rather than lack of awareness of referral services, thereis more a problem of lack of access to psychological support to traumatized childrenand victims of GBV. AVSI focuses on prevention and mitigation by providing safespaces to children, promoting female education and gender inclusion in general.Lastly, beneficiaries may not access or use complaint and feedback mechanisms. 12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

As anticipated above, AVSI will ensure that the project will use the pivotal role of schools in their communities to improve general basic services in the served communities. All targeted schools will have clear referral pathways for protection services, with contact of individuals or organizations who would help in case of referral. Wherever some services are not available, basic and advancetrainings are included in the project to provide at least minimum response, particularly in the most remote areas, where few services are available and only few partners are operating. AVSI’s Foundation shall ensure that the assistance during the project does not harm the safety, dignity and integrity of women, men, girls and boys receiving it, and is provided in a way that respect their rights. Likewise, it will be making sure that trainings will target at least an equal number of female and male, that the monitoring will include sex and age disaggregated data, that the project personnel is trained on gender/protection topics including GBV identification, handling, and referral. Equal participation will be ensured through the involvement of women and men, equally in decision making right up from induction meetings, and routine focus groups discussions. For instance, Inclusive selection process with set % for specific population components typically victims of inequalities (min 60% of females in project implementation, inclusion of elderly and people with special needs, and returnees). Finally, AVSI operates through numerous permanent bases distributed across the targeted communities and serves an adequate number of service sites equally distributed though out the counties to avoid crowding of beneficiaries per sites, reducing distance to access services, and thus diminish the gap for remote, inaccessible communities. Finally, AVSI will have a gender & protection specialist, a gender officer and a protection officer dedicated to the analysis, supervision and adjustment of activities from the gender perspective.
