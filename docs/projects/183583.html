Project Summary

This project aims to mitigate and respond to the critical mental health crisis faced by young people in oPt, which results in increasing high-risk coping mechanisms. The focus of this project is on young people (10-29 years), who are often left behind, as interventions often target children or older adults. In emergencies, young people are particularly vulnerable to the risk of mental health and psychosocial consequences and destructive behaviors. Young people in oPt are exposed to high levels of traumatic experiences and violence through e.g. the Great March of Return, the recent recurrent escalations in Gaza, and the forced evictions of Palestinian families in Sheikh Jarrah and Silwan in addition to harassment (GBV), movement restrictions, house demolitions, imprisonment etc. The outbreak of the coronavirus pandemic has also added to the complexity and increased critical needs among young people of mental healthcare services to be addressed (HNO 2021).
The continuous exposure to traumatic and violent experiences, and the impact of COVID-19, leads to progression in high levels of mental, behavioral and emotional issues among young people that urgently need to be addressed. There is a humanitarian responsibility, to both protect and address young peoples’ needs and to engage them in humanitarian preparedness, response and recovery, where they can play a vital role. MHPSS is underdeveloped at a national level and in most instances, life-saving MHPSS services and information are out of touch and sparse, especially those offering adolescent and youth-sensitive services for the unique needs of young people, which have increased based on the disruption of health services delivery during COVID-19 and the crisis especially in Gaza. ‘’We have been through four wars, I don't know how to feel anymore’’, a 16 year old girl from South of Gaza described (UNFPA, Juzoor Rapid Assessment on Adolescent health, September 2021). It is especially vital during the ongoing pandemic and the crisis that adolescent and youth-sensitive services and information are developed, and provided through different physical and virtual platforms. It is of importance to consider the existing mistrust in confidentiality and stigma that are hindering young people from seeking assistance. Capacities of communities need to be built to ensure critical access to MHPSS through tackling cultural and societal barriers leading to stigmatization and misconceptions of mental health.
UNFPA plans to respond to the growing need of MHPSS among young people during COVID-19, covering communities in Gaza, Area C, H2 and EJ. UNFPA and through its interventions will be complementing WHOs previous work with MoH with a focus on providing services through b NGO health clinics and health providers.
The interventions will cover the following key areas:
- Provision of physical and virtual adolescent and youth-sensitive mental health and psychosocial support services through university and non-governmental clinical sites, including both fixed and mobile settings, to increase availability and accessibility of such services to young people.
- Training and mentoring for health and social providers (NGO and university clinics) to increase capacity building in managing and providing adolescent and youth-sensitive MHPSS services through early detection of symptoms, assessment and treatment as well as referral pathways and triage of mental health cases.
- Delivery of critical community awareness and educational sessions (physically and virtually) on mental health, stigma, negative/positive coping mechanisms and misconceptions around depression, suicide, violence, SRHR, GBV and similar; delivery of tailored community-based training on adolescent and youth mental health.
- Outreach young people on the importance of the prevention and protection against sexual exploitation and abuse, its impact and consequences.

1 - Needs

The on-going protests and clashes between Palestinians and the settlers as a result of the Israeli supreme court decision’s to forcibly evacuate 28 Palestinian families from the Sheikh Jarrah in addition to the recent hostilities in Gaza and the overall deteriorating situation in Jerusalem have done more damage to the already fragile socioeconomic and political conditions. Throughout the various conflicts that have taken place in Gaza/Palestinian territories over the past years, the images carried by media and seen all over the world managed to capture the destruction of physical assets; buildings, houses, and infrastructure however, the psychological and mental toll on the people especially children and youth are often overlooked. Palestinian Youth often find themselves forced to live under constant fear of violence, family loss and a feeling of insecurity; such experiences can negatively shape their attitudes for a lifetime and in many cases have lasting psychological consequences. Thus, the protracted humanitarian crisis in oPt is characterized by high levels of traumatic experiences, leading to a widespread crisis in mental health, which is underreported, under-treated, and underfunded. As stated in previous research on the impact of the 50-day conflict in Gaza in 2014 on children and youth, one year after the escalation; seven out of ten children were still suffering from severe emotional distress, nightmares, anxiety and high stress levels. Similar results were documented after the 2018 escalation (Save the Children, 2015, 2019).The feeling of safety and security are the building blocks of mental health and wellbeing where in the absence of psychosocial security; needs are often left unmet. In the Occupied Palestinian Territory, young women and men aged 10-29 constitute roughly one third of the population and this number is expected to grow (Oxfam, 2021). In spite of the important role that this group will play in shaping the future of the Palestinian society, Palestinian youth face numerous obstacles that limit their potential. Among these obstacles are the high unemployment rate, poor living conditions and the constant fear of violence stemming from the Israeli occupation and the on-going vicious cycles of violence and escalation in Gaza Strip, West Bank and East Jerusalem. Moreover, Palestinian youth face various pressures caused by a society structure in Palestine that is often characterized by patriarchy, gender discrimination and unequal distribution of political and social power. These limitations are likely to negatively impact Palestinian youth mental health and overall well-being. Young people (10-29 years) are among the most vulnerable and to a greater extent affected during the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic which has added to the complexity and the urgency of addressing the increase in mental healthcare services (HNO 2021; PCBS, 2020; SAWA monthly reports emergency hotline). The continuous exposure to traumatic and violent experiences, and the impact of COVID-19, leads to progression in high levels of mental, behavioral and emotional issues among young people (Youth in Palestine, UNFPA). Based on the latest data, 38% of young people in the Gaza Strip have considered suicide at least once (ReliefWeb, 2020). Gender and age-differences, form of vulnerabilities and place of residency influence the level of exposure and risk to traumatic experiences affecting the mental health, accessibility to services and information. The multidimensional impacts of COVID-19 on young people ranging from health, safety protection, education, and economic stability etc. are especially amplified in humanitarian contexts (COMPACT; IANYD Statement on COVID-19 and Youth). Routine health services and information are disrupted, especially those delivered through schools and universities. The recurrent lockdown measures may reinforce and extend these faced challenges which result in negative coping mechanisms, such as substance abuse, self-harm and other harmful behaviors (COMPACT). Therefore, the provision of critical adolescent- and youth sensitive MHPSS through services and information becomes crucial. The way young people are supported when it comes to preparedness and response is critical and has a lasting effect on the recovery process of individuals and communities alike. It is crucial to build resilience through targeted life-saving humanitarian responses. MHPSS, including psychosocial first aid and community-based support, must be integrated into the humanitarian response to meet the urgent need of providing equal and impartial age and gender appropriate services and information. Health services are often not deemed by young people as “friendly”, limiting the number of youth who seek services as well as the number of cases dealt with. Providing In-service training and mentoring for health providers will enable them to integrate adolescent and youth-sensitive services (mhGAP and other standardized guidelines) and guidelines for COVID-19 and PSEA that will ensure advanced, safe, non-judgmental and disability sensitive individualized case management services are being provided. The integration of MHPSS services for young people into fixed and mobile clinics will strengthen and expand delivery of community awareness sessions and community-based psychosocial support services and empowerment sessions by trained youth educators (already started to be integrated into mobile clinics) on MHPSS, COVID-19, SRHR, GBV, stigma and misconceptions. In this context, the use of coping mechanisms is highly needed to increase the accessibility and acceptability of services and promote a protective and self-helping environment. It is worth mentioning that UNFPA- a member at the education cluster- is working closely with other organizations on MHPSS including Save the Children and WHO to ensure complementarity.

2 - Activities

Activity 1. Delivery of critical MHPSS for young people in Gaza, Area C, and EJ. MHPSS for young people will be provided by integrating adolescent and youth-sensitive MHPSS into existing YFHS centers within universities (Birzeit, Al-Quds, Al-Ahliyeh, Al-Azhar and Palestine Universities) and PMRS fixed and mobile health clinics, and home visits. The service delivery will be adapted to COVID-19 restrictions and be flexible towards offering young people with physical or virtual (e.g. phone, email and virtual platforms) assistance. For example, MHPSS will be incorporated into the offered services in existing youth-friendly centres (closed due to COVID-19) present within universities in Gaza and West Bank. By focusing on strengthening and offering young people with services and information through alternative ways (phone, email and virtual platforms) and make use of existing referral systems. Adapting and offering alternative ways of receiving services, will reach a wider set of young people by striving to overcome barriers in form of restrictions, diagnosed with COVID-19, no access to fixed PHCs due to remote location, vulnerabilities (ensuring services are available and accessible for PWDs) and existence of cultural norms hindering seeking life-saving care e.g. within post-operative outreach programs for those with new disabilities as a result of the GMR. Activity 2. Conduct a TOT training for health providers in a number of universities’ existing youth friendly health centers (Birzeit, Al-Quds, Al-Ahliyeh, Al-Azhar, Palestine Universties) and NGOs in the health sector, providing adolescent and youth-sensitive MH services and information. The training sessions will target NGO health providers, complementing WHO’s previous work with MOH in the field of PHC-service delivery. The training will focus on the provision of adolescent and youth-friendly services and information, incorporation of COVID-19 response guidelines, provision of individualized MHPSS for young people with a special focus on PwD using mhGAP guidelines and manual. The training sessions will equip health providers with the needed skills to further disseminate the information to other community workers.Activity 3. Conduct 6 trainings for geographically spread community workers and mobilizers on community-based support services and MHPSS (Y-Peer Educational Network and PMRS volunteers). Trained community workers to provide services and tailored sessions through e.g. community workers joining mobile clinics’ teams and conducting virtual interventions. The training will incorporate guidelines for COVID-19 response and mhGAP guidelines will include, PFA, PSS, mitigation of coping mechanisms and stress management, usage of mhGAP algorithm to increase engagement in detection, monitoring and assessing early signs. Also, community workers will be trained on how to identify and assess the needs and communicate with PWDs. Furthermore, they will be exposed to local perspectives on stigma and misconceptions and messages and principles. Delivery of community awareness sessions will address young peoples’ mental health, SRHR, GBV, incorporating specific needs of PWDs, specific MH gender and age-related needs, coping mechanisms, detection of early signs, stigma and misconceptions of vulnerable groups and MH etc. By offering virtual sessions, a wider set of young people will be reached, erasing the factor of location. Activity 4. Train young people (from Y-PEER, Youth councils and other youth networks in Palestine) on PSEA and sexual harassment. The training will focus on the impact of sexual exploitation and abuse and sexual harassment on the victim and the consequences of such abuses. Trained youth will then be able to provide sessions to other adolescents and youth in and out of schools and universities and workplaces. and enforce the anti-harassment policy and complaint procedure especially in universities and reinforce the already existing.

An email to be established for usage when needed help e.g. informative purposes, cases of referral and complaints. Gained feedback/complaints will be addressed and used in the MEAL processes. It will generate valuable insightful information about the project’s course, to assure and improve meeting the urgent needs through the planned humanitarian actions. As stated previously, feedback from all target groups will feed into evidence-based management decisions. Management decisions and changes depend on the nature of received feedback e.g. change in targeted communities, more and/or change in training and support for health professionals and community workers, need for scaled-up or reconstructed sessions and initiatives, reallocating funds etc.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

Due to the ongoing pandemic, it is crucial that the project integrates COVID-19 protection and prevention measures, such as risk mitigation measures and adapts interventions. UNFPA will act upon the gained understanding with the priority to consider and uphold the rights and to not cause any further negative impact and harm. Safety and security will be guaranteed by upholding dignity and protection in the provision of services and information in friendly spaces and focusing on and strengthening alternative methods during lockdown such as mobile clinics and virtual platforms. NGO health providers will be trained and mentored to deliver non-judgmental, disability-sensitive and high-quality adolescent and youth-friendly services ensuring confidentiality, privacy and respecting the wishes about the care of those seeking help, including meeting the specific needs of PWDs. Safeguards are to be put in place in ensuring confidentiality to not be compromised when mobility is restricted. At the same time, the specialized personnel need to be supported in delivering services and upholding their right and dignity through being treated equitably and fairly. It is especially important to train on COVID-19 response guidelines both to protect the patient and the service provider. In relation to health facilities (both fixed and mobile), safety and protection will be reached by identifying and securing surrounding areas minimizing threats and vulnerabilities, especially following and implementing guidelines on COVID-19. Infrastructure adaptations will be made when needed e.g. ramps, railings, braille signs to NGO health facilities in order to make services more available and accessible for everyone. Also, if needed, providing separate waiting areas for males and females, specific service hours and the availability of both female and male staff is essential. At least one qualified NGO health professional will be working in the clinics (mobile or fixed) and on virtual platforms. Including taking relevant measures for locations where community-based support sessions will be conducted. The priority is to conduct virtual sessions, however, where applicable to conduct physical following procedures. Sessions need to take into consideration different needs, in particular PWDs (accessibility to and within facilities, sign language translator, accessible and available information to all). For young people, it is important to provide a physically, emotionally and socially safe and supportive environment both physically and virtually. Inequities and divides among them and their communities must be considered, and ensure that, when participating in sessions or receiving care (on MHPSS and/or PSEA), they do not get backlash from community or family. Meaning, culture and customs must be respected throughout the whole cycle in order to ensure that people are not put at risk. Further, negative effects on the local economy and livelihoods have been addressed by the project not building a parallel system for the provision of health services, instead, strengthening local capabilities, by utilizing existing fixed resources. The responses through this project will, therefore, be carefully coordinated to aim for complementary structures e.g. utilizing existing referral systems. Further, it considers the impact on the environment that can affect safety, rights, and dignity of people, aiming for a low level of carbon footprint and to handle materials and equipment in an environmentally friendly manner based on available options throughout all activities and the whole implementation.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

As mentioned under ‘Do no harm’, due to the ongoing pandemic and recent escalations in the Gaza Strip and East Jerusalem, it is very crucial to integrate COVID-19 protection and prevention measures, such as risk mitigation measures. Which will require adapting interventions and to especially promote meaningful and equal access of vulnerable groups. It will take into consideration needs based on gender, age, vulnerabilities and disabilities since the need for MHPSS and accessibility to services are different. Primarily, ensuring that selected communities get needed MHPSS services and will gain a better understanding and knowledge about MH and psychosocial wellbeing/issues, coping mechanisms, available MHPSS, vulnerable groups etc. This project is designed to provide equal and impartial accessibility without barriers and discrimination through all planned activities. Impartiality is upheld through striving for a large outreach and accessibility in coordination with other operating actors and continuously conducting needs assessments. It is of importance to highlight and promote young people’s rights to health care and how/where to obtain it by comprehensive awareness and informational campaigns e.g. utilizing existing referral systems. The project is based on the needs of young people, but taking into consideration possible differences in urgent needs, faced risks and faced traumatic experiences, which are gender or age-specific, vulnerabilities (including specific needs of PWDs) and connected to the location of residency. It will primarily target identified vulnerable young people at risk by providing crucial MHPSS, bearing in mind the arrangement of peoples’ access and availability to services including adapting services and information (developing a specific alternative communication strategy in order to ensure reaching everyone, especially those at risk of exclusion). That will be achieved by offering different alternatives in order to be able to widen the reach and meet the needs, through existing health clinics (fixed and mobile) and home visits when applicable and enhance services and interventions through other channels such as phone and virtual platforms. This will allow barriers to be tackled that may restrict freedom of movement and/or people from seeking help, for instance, making sure all disabilities are considered (people with reduced mobility and non-mobility-related disabilities), and those who cannot access services due to, for instance, restrictions related to COVID-19, no access to internet or technological device, socio-cultural norms and restrictions by tackling stigma and misconceptions at all levels. Mobile clinics and virtual platforms will especially have the role to enable services, information and community-based support interventions to reach people in more vulnerable communities. Further, community action is in focus by delivering tailored sessions and initiatives based on different outreach strategies by community workers (peer educators and volunteers) within communities targeting different population groups.
