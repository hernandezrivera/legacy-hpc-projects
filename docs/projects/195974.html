Project Summary

By addressing immediate acute and chronic health needs of refugees and host communities through multi-sectoral mobile units as well as center-based services, HI is continuing more than 13 years of broad support for refugees and FDMNs. The proposed intervention will be delivered through twin modality:
1. Multisectoral mobile units comprised of qualified nurses, physiotherapists, MHPSS officers, protection officers and social workers will be deployed to identify and provide tailored and individual assistance to those most vulnerable, especially persons with disabilities and their caregivers that might not be able to access services due to mobility or social barriers. In this setting, mobile teams deliver services focused on educating beneficiaries and their caregivers on safe, effective therapeutic exercises and reasonable accommodations.
2. Static points: Multidisciplinary teams support beneficiaries with rehabilitation, MHPSS, basic healthcare through nurses and protection. Training is provided to persons and caregivers on basic home-based rehabilitation and proper use, maintenance and repair of mobility devices. Group & individual sessions on MHPSS and assessment for protection beneficiaries take place.
-In home-based and center-based services, Nurses will support beneficiaries with: wound care, bedsores care, ante and post-natal care. A key focus will be to provide maternal care for women who cannot access health facilities (i.e. women and girls with disabilities), including the prevention and detection of pregnancy- related risks and complications. Nurses refer to the nearest Health Facility and follow up to ensure they receive needed specialized services.
- Support to government hospitals and health facilities both in the host and refugee communities to provide rehabilitation services. HI coordinates at Age & Disability Working Group (ADWG) as co-founder to provide services to unserved camps if disability specific services are required. HI also have modality of outreach rehabilitation team to serve the areas where HI does not serve on regular basis.
- PSEA focals are assigned at each site offices who ensure PSEA principles are mainstreamed through all activities through extensive training of all staff and volunteers using PSEA Network materials and recommendations. Various forms of feedback are regularly collected, including suggestion boxes, hotlines for refugee communities and community discussions. To ensure referral for needed services and survivor advocacy, a dedicated Focal Point is assigned in the Cox's Bazar base to receive complaints.
HI is currently implementing five projects that address the above mentioned health services. HI supports 5 health facilities including Cox's Bazar Sadar hospital, Ukhiya Upazila health complex, Teknaf Upazila health complex, KTP MSF-OCA-331 and IOM Leda-200 with physiotherapists providing rehabilitation services. These facilities serve refugees as well as beneficiaries from the host community.
Beneficiaries' complaints and feedback are welcome as part of the project activities. At present, different complaints and feedback collection modalities exist in both host communities and camps, which are led by the HI MEAL team.
Face-to-face interviews, group discussions, a hotline, and feedback boxes are the methods HI uses to provide prompt solutions to complaints.
Support women, men, boys and girls in the camps with basic nursing and SRHR services (ANC, PNC, FP awareness), with a focus on vulnerable populations (persons with disabilities, pregnant women, older people, chronically ill people and those who are unable to access the facilities).
The total budget targeted for HI in 2023 is $2,464,732. Of that, $1,864,731 has already been secured from different donors ($1,326,973 for refugees and $1,137,759 for host communities) with the remaining $6,000,00 likely to be secured, but not confirmed, but HI is appealing for this amount.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

According to REACH age & disability assessment study (May 2021), the prevalence of persons with disabilities was 12% among refugees and ranged from 6% to 19% based on the camp and the presence of mobility barriers. In shelters and around camps, 52% and76% of persons with disabilities reported struggling with mobility issues. In addition, a survey based on the WHO rATA tool during REACH's Age and Disability Inclusion Needs Assessment (ADINA) reported that 51% of people had unmet needs in terms of assistive products. There are 89-98% of individuals who have functional limitations in different domains that still have unmet needs. As of GoB-UNHCR Factsheet August 2022, a total of 943529 individuals was recorded, among which 52% are children, 44% are adults and 4% are older persons while women and men are 48% & 52% of the refugee population. In Bangladesh, Rohingya refugees have no legal status, denying them the rights associated with refugee status and income generation needed to fulfil their basic needs. Consequently, Rohingya refugees remain the most vulnerable group in Bangladesh, relying heavily on humanitarian assistance. As Rohingya women face specific protection concerns and barriers to accessing services, they are particularly susceptible to human rights violations and negative coping mechanisms, including early marriage, survival sex, and child labor. This is partly due to social cultural norms that prevent women from participating fully in public life. Limited or no access to quality sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services during emergency and crisis conditions puts women and adolescent girls at higher risk of morbidity and mortality that requires utmost importance in terms of service design, delivery and implementation. Furthermore, it is well documented that Rohingya refugees are suffering from severe mental health problems, such as depression and psychosocial impairment caused by persecutions, violence, and forced displacements in Myanmar, aggravated by their impoverished and inadequate living conditions and unemployment. Further, older persons and persons with disabilities are often at increased risk of violence, abuse and neglect because of attitudinal (stigma and discrimination), communication and environmental barriers. As a result, negative perceptions of persons with disabilities and their perceived limited ability to report violence and defend themselves put them at risk for further protection risks and violence. Additionally, persons with disabilities and older people face significant barriers (e.g. lack of accessibility) to access mainstream services, including specialized services. A lack of available specialized services (i.e. Rehabilitation services to prevent and reduce functional limitations, limited access to assistive devices (REACH study, 2021 says 56% reported wanting to receive access to assistive devices, but have not been supported), medication, chronic disease treatment, etc.) thus puts them at greater risk of exclusion. In addition, the influx of humanitarian support has concentrated essential services like health and protection services in camp areas the camp fencing creates additional barriers for services to reach the host community having to walk an hour or more to receive health services.. In an HI internal assessment 56% of host community members surveyed said that assistance they received was not frequent enough, only 8% were visited by a community health worker in the last 14 days and 43% said not enough relief was provided. Resentment and frustration at the lack of support for the extremely poor area coupled with property disputes and fears of COVID 19 spread have created a conflicted environment. In regards to the above-mentioned needs, HI has begun to expand services into host communities (Nhila union of Teknaf) and in the camp 7, 15, 16, 24, 25 and 26 to provide dynamic and multi-sector services at a household and center basis to the most vulnerable households there.

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

HI provides direct specialized services, including physical/functional rehabilitation, mental health and psychosocial support to vulnerable people and persons with disabilities within the Cox’s Bazar district. Multi-disciplinary qualified rehabilitation professionals provide centre-based and home-based services. HI ensures a holistic intervention through synergies with other HI projects, external actors and development actions. Additionally, HI is an active member of the Health, Protection, Education, and Logistic Sectors, as well as the MHPSS working group and chairs the ADWG to ensure regular interactions between all sectors. With its active participation, HI ensures effective coordination and synergies with other interventions and actors in the area, avoiding duplication of activities in the camps and host areas, and promoting inclusive humanitarian actions at the sectoral and ISCG levels. HI also coordinates with Bangladeshi development actors to ensure the rights of persons with disabilities and their physical rehabilitation by creating sustainable changes. As part of its sustainability strategy, HI strives to build capacity in local communities and local partners to foster community resilience. HI has effective coordination with government authorities regarding integrated approaches, specialized services, and capacity building for individuals and community members, especially persons with disabilities.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection

The program incorporates protection principles throughout all the phases including design and inception phases. In doing so, the program systematically takes into consideration protection risks and potential violations. Starting from the design phase, HI has carried out protection needs assessment through community consultations, analysis of data from ongoing projects, and analysis of specific feedback received through accountability mechanisms. Analysis has given special attention to age, gender and disability in order to analyze protection risks specific to different community members. Consultation and dialogue with communities, including women and girls with and without disabilities have been ongoing and will continue throughout continued implementation to promote active participation of affected populations.

3.2 - Gender

The organization has Gender, Disability and Age (DGA) Institutional Policy. HI’s “Age, Gender, and Disability Policy”, whose guiding principles are participation, equality and non-discrimination, accessibility and protection, inform all aspects of project design, implementation, monitoring, and evaluation. The policy supports the development and implementation of transformative gender-sensitive projects, where age and disability are fully considered. HI has in-built human resources and technical tools dedicated to identifying the specific needs and capacities of people from different groups. HI strongly follow the DGA Policy Guidelines and DGA Marker in every project.

3.3 - Age including child safeguarding

"HI’s Code of Conduct, Safeguarding Framework, Child Protection Policy, and Protection of Beneficiaries from Sexual Exploitation, Abuse and Harassment Policy create enforceable accountability mechanisms that apply to all staff, partners, volunteers, and contracted service providers. Meaningful participation is one of the pillars of HI’s Disability, Gender and Age Policy. Meaningful participation of boys, girls, women and men of different age groups, including people with different abilities, in the design, implementation, monitoring and evaluation of actions or policies affecting them is essential for ensuring a “do no harm” approach, relevance of the actions, and equity of outcomes. Engaging locally recruited volunteers who are capable of translating and contextualizing key messages in the implementation of the program facilitates meaningful participation of beneficiaries.

3.4 - Diversity

Handicap International-Humanity and Inclusion (HI) advocate inclusion and participation for everyone, upholding diversity, fairness and individual choices. HI is an organization that recognizes the people with different backgrounds, beliefs, attitudes, and experiences bring new ideas and perceptions to the group. The organization’s all the projects workforce comprised of multiple races, ages, genders, ethnicities, and orientations which ensure the welcoming environment, resulting in services provision being open for all diversified people. HI believe that diversity and inclusion benefit not only the bottom line for justice services but also society as a whole and each of us as individuals. HI vision is outraged by the injustice faced by people with disabilities and vulnerable populations, we aspire to a world of solidarity and inclusion, enriched by our differences, where everyone can live in dignity.

3.5 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

Prior to service delivery, HI will conduct individual assessments, protecting each beneficiaries’ rights to confidentiality, in consultation with beneficiaries and caregivers. Informed consent will be obtained before service delivery or referral, and beneficiaries may refuse HI services at any time. CFRMs enable teams to respond effectively to program participants' complaints and feedback in a timely manner by establishing two-way communication channels. The two-way communication mechanism is designed specifically to gather and respond to feedback from program participants and other community stakeholders. This mechanism includes accessible complaint boxes in static points, a hotline, an email address, and contact of Country PSEA focal points with phone number. Information related to the CFRM is shared during individual sessions and community awareness sessions and posters are displayed in static points and distributed at household level including local language.

3.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Recognizing the lack of consideration of persons with disabilities in existing disaster risk reduction (DRR) service planning and implementation, the program will deliver specific activities to improve DRR stakeholders’ understanding of barriers limiting access to DRR information as well as facilitators for ensuring persons with disabilities are included in all disaster response programs. Technical support will be provided to Protection Emergency Response Units (PERU) and Emergency Preparedness and Response (EPR) working group members. Accessibility works will include removal of environmental barriers currently present in evacuation routes and safety shelters. Awareness sessions will be delivered to persons with disabilities and their family members to ensure they are empowered with knowledge of local emergency response plans, evacuation routes, and safety shelters.
