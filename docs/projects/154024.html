Project Summary

This project is to provide cash assistance for conflict-affected returnee, remainee and IDP households. Cash enables households to appropriately and effectively meet their emergency needs, including rent, food, utilities and others.

Needs

The humanitarian response in Iraq in 2018 had to deal more and more with the effects of protracted displacement and the on-going if not worsened effects of food insecurity, a lack of livelihoods opportunities and adequate shelters and ongoing security concerns. Both Ninewa and Kirkuk saw significant destruction of livelihoods and infrastructure. With assets being looted, savings depleted and debt incurred HHs have no resources to become independent and remain reliant on humanitarian aid. All of these factors play into this protracted crisis which prevents people from returning to their pre-crisis places of origin and feeds into the high number of 1.9 million people who are still food insecure (out of the estimate of 6.5 million people in Iraq who still rely on humanitarian aid in 2018). TF has conducted 2191 HH assessments from 8 villages in Hawiga district (April to November 2018) which indicate that 26.0% of HH are remainees. 67.1% are returnees. 6.4% are displaced. There are 17.7% female headed HHs and 34.2% of head of HHs reported not being able to work due to a disability or chronic illness. With the previous agricultural livelihoods having been destroyed, most HHs are reliant on temporary work. From the HH assessments conducted 26.3% of HH have had no adults working with more than 70% of HHs with no adults having regular employment. 8.1% of HHs reported no income in the month preceding assessments whilst of the 91.9% who did have an income, the mean average was 277,000 IQD per month. This is inadequate to meet HH needs as it is only less than 60% of the survival minimum expenditure basket. Debt is a huge issue with 92.4% of HHs reportedly having debt of an average of 5,303,158 IQD. There also continues to be large numbers of displaced people who are unable to return to their places of origin due to continued insecurity, the presence of ERW or the destruction of buildings and infrastructure. DTM data (October 2018) indicates an estimated 15,000 HHs in Kirkuk city with many of them highly vulnerable and in need of assistance. High numbers of returnees have been witnessed in parts of Kirkuk governorate, mainly Hawiga and other surrounding districts as part of the wave of secondary displacement following the referendum in October 2017. In order to meet their basic needs as they look for options of restarting their lives after a period of displacement, MPCA is the preferred form of quick assistance for these returnees. Ninewa and Mosul city in particular have been in the centre of heavy military operations and fighting to liberate the area from ISIS control in 2017, which has led to a high level of destruction and displacement within the governorate. Due to the complex political and socio-economic situation has the reconstruction of infrastructure, shelter and governmental safety nets in aid of the vulnerable population been delayed. MPCA assistance has proven to be still crucial and often the preferred method of addressing the basic needs of the conflict- affected population due to the multi-sectoral needs of the affected populations. This has also decreases the likelihood of resorting to adopt negative coping mechanisms as described in the Humanitarian Needs overview in the HRP. The percentage of people who qualify for MPCA assistance in 2018 has been higher (47% of all assessed HHs) than in the previous year (in 2017, only 24% of assessed HHs were considered eligible for MPCA assistance) as a consequence of this situation. In Ninewa, TF has conducted 1,162 HH assessments in 4 villages in Wana Sub District and 1,144 in Al Yarmook neighbourhood in West Mosul (May to September 2018). In Wana, 18% assessed were female headed HHs of which 68% are widows; 12% of HHs reported that the head of the HH has a disability; of those , that 26% were female headed HHs. 66% report living in unfinished buildings; with average reported debt of over IQD 4 million per family; and earnings of less than IQD 100,000 per month, which means that they fall under the country wide poverty marker of 92,000 IQD. In the Al Yarmook assessed groups, 52% were remainee, 43% returnee and 5% IDPs. 19% are female headed HHs of that group 84% are widows. Reported average income in the last 30 days on average was IQD 210,000, with only 10% of family members eligible or able to find regular work; the average family is IQD3 million in debt. In both locations in Ninewa and Kirkuk, people overwhelmingly requested cash as the preferred modality of assistance; and the top three needs reported are shelter, food and cash, as quoted in the Intentions Survey II, 2017/18. Reliable and permanent work is challenging to find, and unemployment is high. In the Ninewa area 73% of callers to the IDP call center are from non camp areas; and of that the top reported request is for cash.

Activities

1). FGD and Market assessment for target area. This serves to ascertain market functionality a prerequisite for cash based assistance.2). Set-up of Community Committees. With the potential for cash to be divisive within a community it is important that there is a good system of communication to allow for agreed selection and feedback. TF therefore sets up community committees and of trusted representatives of the community with the involvement of beneficiary accountability (BA).These committees aim to represent a diverse representation of the community, including both gender groups, older and younger member of the community as well as members with disabled family members or from a minority group. If this is not given, multiple committees can be formed in the same community to allow a wider inclusion of diversity. 3). Wider internal team coordination with other sectors in the TF and also through the cluster referral system to ensure priority needs for other sectors are covered with vulnerability assessments 4). Conducting HH assessments. TF conduct blanket HH vulnerability assessments in targeted locations , thus giving all HHs the opportunity to be selected . These assessments are ideally done on house to house, but where security situation does not permit, a public location (such as a school) is identified. If the security situation deteriorates and there is reduced access to the field, phone call assessments are also a possibility. The assessments are conducted using an electronic form agreed nationally by the Cash Working Group..5). Selection of beneficiaries. TF plans to use the new CWG scoring criteria which consider different weighting of a number of socio economic factors and base vulnerability on the feed consumption score of the individuals assessed. Where possible and safe for the Cash team then go through the list of potential beneficiaries with the Community Committee(s) and receives their feedback as a way of verifying the assessment data and to the exclusion and inclusion errors. This step has been critical in ensuring that the list is accepted as fair. With the re-introduction of one, two or three months payments as a guideline og CWG, depending on the level of vulnerability, HHs will be eligible for a one off cash assistance (of 480,000 IQD) or the more vulnerable qualifying for a multi month assistance (of a second 480,000 IQD or two more disbursements of 480,000 IQDs following the first payment).6). Distribution of cash. TF currently uses Mobile Money in Kirkuk (and has a current Framework Agreement with Zain). TF organises registration events which are attended by both TF and the Mobile Money agent where the process and feedback mechanisms are explained and the sim cards distributed. After validation, TF initiates the disbursement through a Purchase Order, SMSes are sent to beneficiaries who pick up the cash at local Point of Sale vendors.In Ninewa, TF is currently using the Hawala system for the distribution of direct cash payments whilst the coverage of mobile money providers is further explored. Once procurement processes have been completed, the Hawala agent joins the team at the distribution site; after identity validation, the disbursement continues in partnership with the Hawala.7). Post distribution monitoring. TF conducts spot checks two weeks after the disbursement of cash and further monitoring one month after, both to pick up on any issues and to collect information about the spending and impact on beneficiaries. Cooperation with other sectorsThrough recent assessments in Ninewa and Kirkuk, an overall high vulnerability of the area has been established. The Cash team within the wider TF team has been working in close cooperation with the Livelihoods and Protection team to ensure that those teams can follow up with the community regarding their wider needs. This general process is also planned for this activity, to ensure that MPCA addresses the populations basic needs but more long term solutions are also found. With the recent assessments conducted in Mosul city, the vulnerability assessment was extended by a short questionnaire designed by the livelihoods team to ensure that the data collected can also be used to design a specific livelihoods project, addressing the specific need of the community as soon as possible after the Cash team has completed their intervention.

Protection Risk Analysis

TF is aware of protection concerns relating to HHs that are perceived to be affiliated to ISIS. There have been complaints raised to community and governmental authorities that assistance has been going to these HHs; there has then been pressure on TF to share beneficiary lists. In line with OCHA and Cash Working Group, TF’s stance is not to share lists as this is breaching the mandate that NGOs have for independence. All civilians are impartially entitled to receive humanitarian assistance. There are concerns that this will continue to be an issue, and that community leaders will be under pressure to exclude HHs. Cash is probably one of the most needed and therefore sought after humanitarian interventions. There is therefore significant a significant risk of creating conflict in the community between those selected and not selected. This is particularly likely if there is a lack of transparency, or a perceived unfairness (and therefore anger) in the selection criteria. There are a few cases of female headed HHs who would struggle to attend an assessment or distribution due to the perceived cultural appropriateness of being present in a public place. Previously-displaced persons may be more vulnerable in terms of needs, but those that did not flee may face increased vulnerable situation as a result of perceived connection with the suspected perpetrators. These issues can influence project activities when selecting beneficiaries for projects. In addition, vulnerable groups may not have social integration and acceptance in the community to apply for the project at all, e.g. IDPs in host communities. As previously mentioned, the project target locations are very diverse with different ethnic and religious groups, balance among these communities in proportion and size may have shifted significantly due to displacement or/and impact of the conflict. Negative drivers of the conflict, favouritism and stigmatization are expected issues that can as well interfere with the project selection of beneficiaries. Gender and age considerations: Female-headed HHs are being recognized as highly vulnerable and between 10-25% of the HHs in Ninewa Plains are assessed to be female headed (REACH, MCNA, 2018). There are cases of female headed HHs who would struggle to attend an assessment or distribution due to the perceived cultural appropriateness of being present in a public place. Access for the elderly particularly those who have no support from family can be challenging, and often the elderly risk being missed during the assessment and distribution process. Risk of fraud/extortion/looting: Distribution of cash or cash equivalents always includes risks of financial corruption such as fraud, bribery, extortion and theft. Another risk related to project activities is markets becoming inaccessible and out of stock of essential items, fraudulent activities are being carried out by Hawala or Zain agents, community or project staff or even attract attacks on distribution sites or increase insecurity in and around distributions.

Mitigation Measures

TF will continue to use a blanket assessment approach, refuse to share lists with authorities and advocate for the inclusion of civilian HHs who are perceived to be affiliated with IS. TF uses a community based approach to reduce the risk of conflict. This includes blanket assessment of all HHs (HH) in the location and the application of an objective scoring system, aligned with recommendation from CWG. However, it has previously been found that this does not necessarily match community perceptions of need - with issues arising such as inaccurate weighting of applied criteria, individualised cases and untruthful completion. TF therefore works through the lists with the community committee who are able to highlight inclusions/exclusions for TF to further check, when this is appropriate to the security context in the area. TF is also part of the national Cash Working Group’s technical working group to look at vulnerability and scoring mechanisms. For female-headed HH, TF will seek alternative options to ensure they can access assistance, such as a HH visit by female assessors. Protection Mainstreaming: Following the key criteria 1. Prioritize safety & dignity, and avoid causing harm; 2. Meaningful access; 3. Accountability; 4. Confidentiality and 5. Compliance with humanitarian principles TF ensures that all staff and casuals complete e-learning modules covering safeguarding (children and adults), fraud awareness, whistleblowing, corruption and bribery; which give staff clear guidelines on how to conduct themselves. TF develops community acceptance through regular meetings with community leaders, establishing community committees, and listening to the community concerns through key informants. Solutions including access for women and the elderly are discussed, and solutions for the safe delivery of cash modalities resolved. In line with best practice and the recommendations of the CWG, TF mainstreams protection throughout its project. Protection needs are considered in the selection of beneficiaries. Vulnerability criteria include: individuals missing documents (which can affect their ability to receive government assistance and return to their area of origin); child headed HHs; widowed or divorced women; and families demonstrating one or more negative coping mechanisms. TF has internal data protection guidelines and trains staff on them. TF is committed to ensuring robust data sharing agreements are in place with actors when coordination requires data sharing. Gender and age participation: Proportionate participation by men and women will be guaranteed during staff recruitment, FDGs, beneficiary selection and creation of community female feedback groups to advise how to increase female participation and ensure gender sensitive approach trainings. Community committees will include representation from both men and women. TF trains staff on gender-sensitive service delivery. TF has experience in ensuring gender sensitivity. Given that it often tends to be the men who attend distributions, TF insists that at least one of the two people attending a voucher distribution needs to be a woman, so they are included in the selection of items for their HH. TF has mixed gender teams at assessment, implementation, monitoring and BA level (including a BA desk), ensuring that issues can be discussed with men and women separately so each gender is able to speak freely. Assessments have shown that men and women have often had differing priorities and preferences of modality of distribution. During distributions TF works with community leadership, community committees, Mokhtar's & camp management to ensure individuals with specialised needs (particularly relevant for elderly, or those with disabilities) are identified and steps taken to ensure that they have equal access to assistance and distribution sites.

How is your project accountable to affected population

TF works with Community Committees to determine fair and transparent beneficiary selection. Feedback is actively solicited through help desks at sim cards or cash distributions, regular communication with community members, information flyers and a toll-free hotline run by an independent MEAL team. Understanding the local context: In the initial assessment and analysis of community’s needs, FDGs are carried out with women, youth and different ethnic/religious groups to get a better understanding of their vulnerabilities and abilities. Key Informant Interviews are used to better understand the impact of the crisis on their activities and their ability to meet basic needs for their families and to identify bottlenecks for successful project activities. Complaints handling and Beneficiary accountability:: Feedback from communities, beneficiaries and other stakeholders will be received by one of the several ways designed by TF namely through the help desk that will be manned by TF staff, suggestion and complaint boxes that are available during holding of activities and community meetings by TF as well as through the hotline number. All feedback received is logged and action taken when this is the appropriate response.. Programme teams and MEAL staff coordinate to address all complaints and suggestions that may be received pertaining to the MPCA assistance, as well as other activities. Any complaints from our targeted areas received from the UN IDP call centre are also followed up and feedback is given. TF has a dedicated beneficiary accountability staff to receive complaints and feedback as well as to manage the complaints database. Monitoring Evaluation Accountability and Learning (MEAL): As part of the project cycle the teams will be supported by a system of monitoring, evaluation, accountability and learning through the MEAL team who operates independently to the project team. The MEAL team will take responsibility to manage beneficiary database to cross-check transparency and accountability of vulnerability assessments and registration process. The MEAL team will facilitate a midterm lessons learned workshop will be conducted with both Partner’s project to reflect and learn from the first project cycle. Relevant findings, learnings and/or success cases of will be shared with communities. Additionally, an external evaluation will be conducted for the project to assess the impact of the project. Exit strategy:Developed with the beneficiaries and service providers (e.g. veterinarians) for ongoing support if required post distribution/project period. Community meeting for closure of project targets and feedback; hand over to those community networks for ongoing monitoring and support. Referral information provided to beneficiaries about accessing service providers as needed on issues they may face in the future. Ongoing access to the TF BA hotline
