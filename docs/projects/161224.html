Project Summary

South Sudan continues to face extremely high prevalence of Gender-Based Violence (GBV), driven by a rigidly patriarchal system and historic marginalization of women. It is imperative that humanitarian interventions are sensitive to this reality, given that they may both cause and exacerbate the incidence of GBV cases. As such, Action Against Hunger proposes to: a) continue its efforts to mainstream GBV and gender considerations in its programs, and b) support the humanitarian community in South Sudan to remain accountable for GBV and gender-sensitivity. We will additionally expand our work on GBV mainstreaming in nutrition to WaSH programs, capitalize on achievements made in 2019 and support the WaSH Cluster and other interested clusters. In 2020, the organization will conduct Gender Analyses in all of our areas of intervention to evaluate programmatic changes initiated by their previous iterations (e.g. Men Group Discussions) and continue to monitor gender dynamics and protection risks on the ground. Other actors will also benefit from this analysis (and they can be done upon request from specific partners). In collaboration with relevant clusters, we will continue to conduct semi-annual Safety Audits primarily for nutrition / health / WaSH services, to assess protection concerns and enable improvements. Through sensitization of men and boys, and engagement of men caregivers on a dialogue on protection issues in Men Group Discussions, the project will tackle gender stereotypes and some of the root causes of gender inequality. These discussions will also facilitate an enabling environment for women’s access to basic services. In 2020, Action Against Hunger will continue to train all staff at community, site, and base levels on recognizing forms of GBV and strengthening awareness on existing referral mechanisms, in addition to Psychological First Aid. This is especially important as our staff are actively solicited for counselling on GBV cases that are reported at nutrition sites. The project will directly capacity-build 753 individuals and directly benefit 16,000 persons (75% women). Working with other partners through respective clusters, and leveraging our participation in the GBV sub-cluster, we will amplify this impact. Due to the project’s focus on mitigating risks for the beneficiaries of Action Against Hunger’s programs in South Sudan, 223,616 individuals will benefit indirectly from project results, by receiving more appropriate and accountable humanitarian services.

1 - Needs

South Sudan is a rigidly patriarchal society where women have limited access to education, productive assets and are most often denied decision-making power. They also experience extremely high rates of Gender-Based Violence (GBV) in all its forms. Although other types of conflict abated at the signing of the 2018 revitalized peace agreement, violence against women and girls has persisted and even escalated as a tool for power-grabbing and humiliation (Refugees International, 2019). Action Against Hunger 2018 Safety Audit indicates that most prevalent forms of GBV in the Gogrial Region include forced early marriage (25.2%), domestic violence (21%), and rape/attempted rape. Most affected age groups of women were those who were middle aged (28%), followed by young adults (27%). This is similar to the findings of Ellsberg & Contreras (2017) in a study conducted in Juba where they found that violence against women and girls (VAWG) is endemic - with 65% of women and girls reporting having experienced physical and sexual abuse. Another study conducted in the states of Greater Upper Nile (Jonglei, Unity and Upper Nile states) found that 27% of respondents had experienced physical abuse with 7% having suffered forced sex (CARE International, 2014). Also notable was the fact that women cited all these forms of GBV, while most of the male respondents did not seem to identify these activities as GBV-related. Through subsequent Focus Group Discussions, it emerged that in the South Sudanese context, Intimate Partner Violence (IPV) involving male partners as perpetrators is widely accepted. Respondents reported fears faced by the receiver of retaliation and stigma attached to having experienced GBV, which explain why many cases go unreported and unaddressed. GBV can be detrimental to the nutrition status of women as well as her children, given that she is denied decision-making and purchasing power: this causes deterioration of physical health and mental wellbeing. Having little control over one’s sexuality and pressure on women to bear additional children increases the stress on household food and nutrition security and limits the ability to breastfeed. Our study revealed that those who experience SGBV are often ostracized, excluded from valuable income-generating opportunities. Moreover, men’s lack of knowledge and engagement in nutrition programs often leads to their restricting women’s movement to services in favor of attending to household needs, a reality that inhibits the access of women to critical basic services. Further, the stigma around GBV, especially sexual exploitation and abuse, has precluded many from seeking treatment, exacerbating the physical health and nutrition effects of the attack. Humanitarian agencies in South Sudan largely act as providers of health, nutrition, WaSH, education and other services, in support of state duty bearers. However, not all partners are equally equipped to ensure a gender-responsive and do-no-harm approach: hence the need for mutual support and standardization. The response from the Nutrition Cluster partners to our Safety Audit tool mainstreaming in 2019 was overwhelmingly positive and corroborates the need of a sustained effort in the coming year, across clusters.

2 - Activities

Gender and GBV Risk Analysis:We will conduct Gender Analyses in each of Action Against Hunger’s areas of interventions to assess gender and GBV dynamics that affect or undermine current humanitarian intervention strategies. Results will be analyzed against our 2018 exercise to reveal contextual changes and the effectiveness of our gender-targeted activities that took place in 2019. This will inform changes in approach for our organization as well as for cluster partners. We can also provide this service to other partners. Safety Audit: In 2019, Action Against Hunger, in partnership with the Nutrition Cluster, validated and rolled out the Nutrition Safety Audit, meant to assess protection risks in and around nutrition facilities and suggest measures for risk prevention and mitigation. W will continue to implement the Safety Audit in each of our facilities (as part of the HRP indicators) and formulate associated monitoring tools to analyze Safety Audit results and improvements made over time. We will continue working with other cluster partners to foster systematic safety audits. Findings will be shared with the relevant clusters (primarily nutrition, WaSH and health) and partners to promote protection-sensitive programming. Focus on WaSH Safety Audit: In 2020, we will develop and rollout a similar tool for assessing protection risks in and around WaSH facilities at household and institution levels. In partnership with the WaSH Cluster’s AAP Technical Working Group, WaSH Safety Audit tools will be shared with all national WaSH partners of South Sudan for promotion of protection-sensitive WaSH programming. Despite its WaSH focus, the safety audit will benefit partners across sectors. Promoting Awareness on GBV Referral Pathways:Action Against Hunger will train all site and community level staff in our areas on identifying available referral mechanisms for cases of GBV, as well as raising awareness among program participants on such pathways. As part of this effort, we will work with protection partners (IRC, ARC, CARE, WVI, MSF, NRC) to gather information on available services and harmonize referral tools and systems. Men Group Discussions:Recently, the organization piloted several different methods for increasing the knowledge of men and boys on health care practices and engagement of men and boys in nutrition programs. We will formalize this engagement through Men Group Discussions and implement them alongside Mother-to-Mother Support Groups in all program areas. As part of the rollout, we will refine a context-appropriate curriculum along with training guidance and monitoring tools to ensure an effective behavior change intervention. We aim to conduct a learning exercise in the end of 2020 to analyze the impact of this activity, as well as generate ideas on effective GBV-related messages and GBV-mainstreaming strategies for sharing with the greater humanitarian community in South Sudan. Staff Capacity Building and Gender Focal Points:In line with its internal Gender Minimum Standard certification in 2019 for the South Sudan mission, Action Against Hunger will continue to train its entire staff on gender and GBV concepts to establish a universal commitment to the values of gender equality and GBV awareness raising. Knowledge on gender and GBV dynamics will ensure that all programs are implemented in a gender-sensitive manner. Psychological First Aid (PFA):Finally, we will ensure that all facility and community level staff are capable of delivering high-quality Psychological First Aid. This is especially important as many cases of GBV are reported at the site level, and staff are often unequipped to appropriately interact with survivors. PFA will ensure that at each project site, staff are available to support survivors and safely facilitate referral to available services. This is particularly important for the emergency response in areas where incidence may be high and very few or no options exist.

6.6 - Will beneficiaries' rights be legally recognised?

Entitlements will be legally recognized as far as humanitarian assistance is recognized under the laws of South Sudan.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

According to latest IOM DTM analysis, roughly 30% of populations in our target regions are displaced persons. As such, we invite specific participation of displaced persons in FCM discussions as well as Men Group Discussions to capture their perspective on nutrition, health and GBV services.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

We will involve notably the Ministry of Health and Ministry of Gender Child and Social Welfare in knowledge sharing and design refinement around project activities. Ministry of Health officials have already been heavily involved in the formulation and validation of the Nutrition Safety Audit in 2019. Moreover, we aim to raise awareness on the GBV referral pathways within target communities, including by engagement with local leadership to promote the support of duty-bearers for GBV services and acknowledgement of the impact of gender inequalities and GBV on health, nutrition and wellbeing outcomes of the population.

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

The project is in line with the second HRP objective of the Protection Cluster, which is to “Prevent and mitigate protection risks through enhanced preparedness and resilience.” Action Against Hunger aims to strengthen GBV referral pathways and identification skills in an effort to promote preparedness for responding to GBV/protection cases.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

Despite continued emergency needs, South Sudan experienced some improvement in physical security in 2019. Nevertheless, pockets of violence still occur. For the purposes of this project, the level of physical security is conducive to intended outcomes.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

All project activities are linked to raising awareness on GBV and gender-related issues, especially as they relate to nutrition, health and wellbeing outcomes. Increased knowledge and awareness will ideally motivate long-term women’s empowerment and reduction in the current levels of GBV and deprivation. The staff and partners we train are current and future community, health and nutrition workers and agents of change; thus, project achievements will be carried forward after project end. Action Against Hunger knows the complexities of GBV in South Sudan and how long it takes to change behaviors and practices. That is why we have identified potential sources of support and keep advocacy for further resources towards this project.

8.18 - Please indicate how the risks selected above will be mitigated.

To prevent instigation of SEA and other forms of GBV by leading gender-targeted interventions, Action Against Hunger will aim to sensitize all gender and age groups on project activities before their implementation. Moreover, project activities are designed to safely challenge harmful gender dynamics in a context-appropriate manner. Results of gender analyses will directly inform project design and implementation strategy. All staff implementing GBV referral pathway awareness, Men Group Discussion facilitation, Psychological First Aid, and any other sensitive discussion with target community members will be instructed on our mandatory policy for maintaining the identity of project participants strictly confidential unless participants have agreed to otherwise. Data related to GBV and other sensitive topics will be anonymized in organizational databases to preserve the privacy of survivors. Moreover, survivors or reporters will not be solicited for further participation/discussion unless explicitly agreed to by all parties. Any GBV cases concerning staff or beneficiaries which proceed to investigation will only involve the Country Program Ethics Committee, consisting of the Country Director, Head of Logistics and Head of Operations (Security). All information will be confidential with access restricted to those personnel. We train key stakeholders on the use of the feedback mechanism at each project location. Additionally, the organization will print complaint procedures in the local language and in simple English. The project team will regularly review the use of the feedback mechanism and information gathered from the review process will be used to make improvements. A diverse set of FCM modalities ensures that differing gender, age and ability groups have access.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

Safety Audits will serve as an additional Feedback and Complaints Mechanism (FCM) modality in our programs. All focus group discussions occurring as part of Safety Audits will ensure provision of an enumerator who is the same sex as the participants. As part of the MEAL system surrounding Men Group Discussions, participants will be specifically asked to share their concerns with the program and suggest improvements. In each of our project sites, we avail five FCM modalities as “minimum standards”: 1. Monthly staff meetings with first-line implementers, 2. Focus Group Discussions with direct beneficiaries, 3. Individual interview form, 4. Comment Box at Field Office Gate, 5. Hotline Number. The identities of all feedback and complaints mechanism (FCM) complainants are kept strictly confidential by program management staff when they are provided. We avail a comment box and anonymous hotline for those who wish to keep their identity strictly confidential. We have standard, context appropriate FCM IEC materials (posters, stickers, flyers), which are displayed at all project sites and on all project structures (e.g. equipment used by beneficiaries). IEC materials include images and text in both English and the local dialect to ensure comprehension by a maximum number of individuals. During all community meetings, trainings, and sensitization sessions, a standard script is used by staff to inform beneficiaries on available FCM modalities. Depending on the urgency, feedback will be delivered to the first line program implementers on a monthly basis during the monthly meetings. Specific responses can also be discussed bi-monthly at the beginning of focus group discussions at the site level to inform beneficiaries of what changes are being made given previous feedback, or if any concerns are being addressed at the national level. Urgent issues are addressed immediately by the Field Coordinator and PM through appropriate channels. Our FCM is compatible with CBCM.
