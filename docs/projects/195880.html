Project Summary

IMA World Health, a member of the Corus International family, will provide essential primary health care services to 25,635 men, women, boys, and girls in one mobile health facility serving returnees in Morobo, such as in Panyume or Lojulo payams, the Kinyiba PHCU in Kajo-Keji, and the Bori Primary Health Care Center (PHCC) also in Kajo-Keji. Kajo-Keji and Morobo counties contain the second and fourth-highest numbers of people in need of health cluster assistance according to the 2021 Humanitarian Response Plan Health Cluster People in Need (PiN) target sheet, motivating IMA’s selection of these locations for humanitarian intervention within the broader context where the health situation has further worsened throughout 2020 due to intensified conflict, a second year of severe flooding, and the outbreak of COVID-19. IMA’s operates ongoing emergency health projects reaching seven payam-level health facilities and in both Kajo-Keji and Morobo counties which reach over 61,162 people with primary healthcare, reproductive, neonatal, and maternal health, community health, epidemic control, pharmaceutical support in coordination with established pipelines, and SGBV response. The existing presence provides a strong platform from which the proposed project can build in an efficient and cost-effective manner.
The proposed 6-month project aligns with the HNO’s projected health needs improving access to and utilization of essential lifesaving basic health services to reduce excess morbidity and mortality. Disease outbreaks will be investigated within 48 hours’ notice to contribute to the Early Warning Alert System (EWARS) and Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) mechanisms. IMA will adapt healthcare provision in light of the evolving COVID-19 pandemic in the target locations, ensuring appropriate infection prevention and control as well as community health responses. Referral mechanisms from the PHCC, PHCU, and mobile facility will be strengthened and utilized to ensure continuity of secondary care, SGBV, MHPSS, and integrated response for severe acute malnutrition.
IMA World Health, a member of the Corus International family, has worked in South Sudan since 2008, providing primary health care, MNCH, protection, and Visceral Leishmaniasis Control. IMA is the lead for the global $200 million USAID Momentum Integrated Health Resilience project, already operational in South Sudan. IMA is creating, testing, and informing the global conversation around health resilience in fragile settings through this project. Under $57 million in World Bank and South Sudan MOH funding, IMA from 2013-2018 strengthened Upper Nile & Jonglei’s health system, reaching 3.1 million people- over 25% of the country’s population.

1 - Needs

On 3 October, 732 new IDPs (137 households) have been registered in the Morobo county in one week. The displaced fled violence by an armed group (suspected to SPLM-IO) in their villages and are now sheltering in the Tanganyika Episcopal Church and Morobo Catholic Church in Morobo town. More people are expected to arrive as attacks on civilians continue in their villages. Urgent needs include food, shelter, medical and non-food items (NFIs). Undeserved IPDs, returnees, and host community members in Kajo Keji, Lainya and Morobo counties are severely affected by ongoing chronic health problems due to limited access to health services, alongside related needs for food, clean water, sanitation and hygiene. These locations have attracted increasing numbers of returnees and IDPs over the past year due to poor conditions of the refugee camps in neighboring countries and relative peace (RTGNU). Kajo-Keji county contains the fifth-highest numbers of people in need of health cluster assistance according to the 2023 HRP Health Cluster People in Need (PiN). According to 2023 HRP estimates, there are 329,688 people in need (PiN) of urgent humanitarian assistance across the 3 targeted counties; (117114 Kajo-keji, 71085 Lainya and 87488 Morobo respectively). The Health Cluster is targeting 50% (164,844) of the PiN across the 3 counties, which includes 32,199 returnees, 32,003 Internally displaced persons (IDPs) and 100,641 vulnerable host population. The IPC report of 2023, the 3 counties are in IPC 3 (Acute Food security). The health cluster targets 164,844, 50%, of the people in need (PiN) In the 3 counties, which includes 32,199 returnees, 32,003 Internally displaced persons (IDPs) and 100,641 vulnerable host population. Ayod and Akobo counties of Jonglei State are in classified as IPC 4, IMA will support 2 Health centers to reach 29511 IDPs and host population. IMA will directly target 26745 Women and girls of reproductive age (18-60 years), (5542 Returnees, 4919 IDPs,16282 Host pop) and 11670 of Children (boys and girls) under the age of 5 years (1940 returnees, 2944 IDPs, 6787 Host Pop) which represent 60% of the Health Cluster PiN targets in each of the sub-sects respectively who will be provided with lifesaving medical, prevention and curative consultations, maternal and child and reproductive Health assistance via support to 9 Static and 1 mobile health facility for a 12-month period, starting from Jan 1 -Dec 31 2023. Overall, IMA will directly and indirectly reach 96,000 (58%) of the 2023 Health cluster targeted PiN, which includes 48697 in Kajo-Keji, 21799 in Lainya and 25504 in Morobo county respectively. With the ongoing Ebola public health emergency in Uganda and high number of EVD suspected cases reported along the border by South Sudan EVD surveillance report by WHO/MoH, the health needs in these locations remain severe. IMA’s joint assessment in 2020 showed that only 20 out of 46 health facilities in the two counties are functional. The remaining 26 are either non-operational or severely damaged. Respondents reported that accessibility of services, especially among returnees from Uganda and DRC, remains a barrier since the functioning facilities are often located long walks away from homes. The protracted conflict in the two counties and across Central Equatoria State also displaced health workers and prevented the restoration of destroyed health facilities. The main hospital in Kajo Keji has not been functional since it was damaged in 2017, so referrals for serious health issues remain difficult and costly. Malaria, acute respiratory infections, diarrheal diseases, and typhoid remain top diseases. In mid 2022, the health pooled fund cut support to 4 HFs in Lainya, and in Oct 2022 BHA/USAID has cut funding to IMA supporting the 10 Health facilities and IMA is currently using internal funding and UNFPA which will run until December 2022 with no extra funding to continue lifesaving health services for the people in need.

2 - Project activities

Activities are aligned with the Health Cluster’s 2023 Response Plan and will be tailored to the needs of affected populations, IMA will 1) Provide outpatient consultations and treatment of high prevalence communicable diseases (Malaria, ARI, Diarrhea) though established WHO and MOH guidelines, in 10 supported Health clinics, while Incorporating Zinc (zinc-sulfate WHO interagency kit) as standard treatment for childhood diarrhea 2) Train Nurses, Midwives and community Health workers to improve their knowledge and skills, including MoH treatment protocol and guidelines 3) Provide surveillance of diseases and provide IDSR report on timely basis. All the projects will be registered under the DHIS2 and the EWARS system to facilitate weekly reporting to Health Cluster. 4. Community awareness and Infection prevention and control of COVID-19 and EVD 5) Support supplementary EPI activities through health education, risk communication and community engagement as an extension of mobile facility care with the CHD. 4) Facilitate health education and awareness by trained community health workers using MOH approved IEC/BCC material 5) COVID-19 and EVD awareness raising among staff, communities, other agencies and instituting prevention and control preparedness measures at all health facilities Objective 2 Improve access and utilization of integrated life-saving quality health care services to crisis-affected and vulnerable populations through static, mobile, community-based health service delivery systems 1) Minor rehabilitation based on infrastructure needs assessments. Rehabilitation could include delivery rooms, outpatient department (OPD), pharmacy, waiting rooms, waste pits, doors, locks, windows, fencing, etc. 2) Conduct outpatient health consultations for children, adults, and elderly populations. Target facilities are staffed by SSMOH workers & supervised by IMA. Consultations will focus on medical evaluations, diagnosis & treatment of common illnesses including malaria, typhoid, upper respiratory infection. The project will work with the CHDs in Kajo-Keji and Morobo to register health care workers in the health facility locations and within the counties among the IDP community 3) Supportive supervision & quality assurance: IMA will provide a senior health manager with clinical and management background to oversee medical teams in Kajo-Keji and Morobo. A Medical Supervisor will provide daily management of health services and supervision of health staff 4) Provide focused ANC services including vaccination (tetanus toxoid), provision of iron/folic tablets, and Intermittent Preventive Treatment (IPT), distribution of Safe Motherhood Kits™ (RH kit #2A) free of charge if available from UNFPA. 5) Ensure 24hr skilled deliveries services through midwives at health facilities and supply of Misoprostol according to MOH and UNFPA protocol to mitigate high rates of PPH in an environment where 90% of women deliver at home. Strict control of Misoprostol will be observed at use at the community. 6. facilitate referral systems for specialized obstetric care including C-sections to referral hospitals 7. Conduct on-site SGBV screening and CMR care so SGBV victims 7. Emergency / outbreak investigation and response through mobile clinics.

3 - Project indicators

IMA adopts the HRP Health Cluster Broad Activities and associated indicators, in addition to internal measures of quality around patient satisfaction and community input into project design. Project design involves consultation with community members, including displaced, returnees, IDPs, local leaders, men, women, adolescent girls, adolescent boys, and elderly men and women to understand their access and barriers to healthcare. Consultations and feedback sessions continue weekly throughout the project with community health workers reaching out to households and regular meetings with community stakeholders taking place at payam level. IMA will implement an extensive framework of tools for the monitoring and evaluation of the activities conducted. All the indicators are designed in order to closely monitor the project objectives and adopt the Health Cluster's definitions and disaggregation. KII and FGD will be conducted in the targeted areas at different stages of the project in order to understand the barriers to accessing care and patient feedback on quality of care. All data will be disaggregated by sex, age, and disability status to enable IMA to ensure care is equitable. Roles and responsibilities for monitoring and evaluation. IMA staff will supervise data collection and verification at the facility level, and will liaise with the Health Cluster and relevant partners to obtain requisite data collection and analysis tools. They will conduct daily data collection activities, train staff, conduct primary verification, and data quality assurance checks. Capacity building of facility staff and CHDs to ensure accurate and security data collection will take place including training on DHIS2. IMA’s headquarters Senior Program Manager and technical experts (as needed) will provide support to the country team. IMA will remain in close touch with the Health Cluster and relevant partners if any questions or concerns on tools, data collection, analysis, or reporting arise. The Director of Programs will ensure that project teams address any target shortfalls to improve the quality of service delivery. The IMA Country Director holds ultimate responsibility for project success and as a medical doctor will also provide senior-level technical input to the project. The project will contribute directly to the following HRP Health Cluster Indicators: 1.1 Malaria Incidence Rate 1.2 Percentage of children aged six months to 15 years who have received measles vaccination, on completion of a measles vaccination campaign 1.3 Percentage of alerts, rumors, and outbreaks investigated within 48 hours of notification 2.1 Number and Percentage of deliveries conducted by skilled birth attendants 2.10 Percentage of functioning health facilities provided with supportive supervision. 2.2 Number and % of consultations per person per year (Note that while the target is 2 consultation/person/year, IMA's proposed 12-month project will target 1 consultation/person during the project period to contribute to the overall target.) 2.5 Number of community health workers per 1000 people 2.6 Coverage of PENTA3 in <one-year-olds by administrative unit 2.8 Percentage of health facilities assessed using Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) scorecard 2.9 Percentage of facilities with community feedback mechanisms 3.1 Number of SGBV survivors receiving CMR services IMA will also monitor the internal indicator of patient satisfaction to understand the overall satisfaction of beneficiaries with the project, and track the frequency and content of community feedback on the project.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

As a signatory to the UN Global Compact, IMA maintains a strong organizational commitment to putting into practice human rights and labor principles to prevent bad conduct, and sexual exploitation and abuse. Beneficiaries will be informed of measures the project has in place for prevention of sexual abuse and exploitation that implementing staff must abide by. Beneficiaries will also be given the appropriate contact information, including a hotline number for IMA’s national and globally managed system and any donor-specific tools, to use for reporting cases of abuses and misconduct. As discussed in section 7.4.1, IMA will use the same outreach tools used to inform community members about project services to increase community awareness of the policies protecting them from sexual abuse and exploitation. Beneficiaries will be informed about IMA and donor policies to protect them against sexual abuse and exploitation in the facilities and via community health workers who reach out into local communities about the principles the project will uphold and the codes of conduct and behavior all staff and health workers must abide by. Patients and community members will be provided with contact information, including for the IMA World Health and any relevant donor hotlines or reporting mechanisms. Staff all sign onto a code of conduct and a child safeguarding policy to which they are held. Staff, including field staff and extension agents, will be required to pledge to IMA’s code of Ethics, including the Safeguarding and Child Protection policies prior their engagement. Severe sanctions will be imposed for code violations, to include termination of employment.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Information about the health facility re-opening and package of services available will be broadly shared with community members in close coordination with the CHD, local authorities, youth groups, traditional leaders- including female leaders, religious leaders, etc. The health workers recruited from the local communities will also act as ambassadors into their communities for health care. Displacement-affected health facility staff who relocated or returned will be targeted for re-employment to ensure that displacement-affected communities feel comfortable in the facility. Community health workers will conduct door-to-door visits and meetings to build awareness of health services and build health-seeking behavior among potential clients of the PHCCs and PHCUs. IMA ensures communities, CHDs and local authorities are involved in all phases of the project cycle. IMA will enhance community feedback mechanisms at each facility to meet community needs and will activate community health committees (50% female) to ensure community participation and support information sharing on services. IMA will work with CHDs to organize weekly and monthly health coordination and feedback meetings with CHDs, county or payam community health committees, and community leadership. IMA ensures vulnerable groups are proportionally represented in such discussions. Project staff, facility staff, community members, and local leaders will also be trained on use of the IMA country-wide and global Code of Ethics and Business Conduct, which includes the complaints and feedback mechanism. The mechanism which is overseen jointly between headquarters and the country coordination office in Juba enables beneficiaries, community members, staff, and other stakeholders can confidentially report any problems or complaints, including issues around sexual abuse and exploitation. The facility-level complaints and feedback mechanism will be designed based on IMA’s experience in nearby health facilities with input from the community health committees, CHDs, and patients- including marginalized groups such as the elderly or people with disabilities. Community health workers, the community health committees, religious leaders, and others with outreach abilities into communities will be informed about the facility-level complaints and feedback mechanism and requested to inform the broader population around the facility. The same representatives will work with IMA staff to inform community members of IMA's national and global feedback and complaints mechanism, and how to access it. Written and verbal outreach materials will be used.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

The health project will mainstream gender, age, and disability at every stage, from contingency planning through each step of the response. Based on the IASC gender markers and internal project design requirements, IMA will adapt its existing baseline study from a complementary, ongoing, health project conducted in Morobo, Lainyaand Kajo-Kejji which integrated gender, protection, social inclusion, and conflict analysis to identify the needs and preferences of the local population including women, girls, men, boys, IDPs, and persons with disabilities. Given low levels of literacy in the target areas, IMA adapts project interventions to ensure communication can take place with low literacy community members. IMA will continue to enhance community feedback mechanisms to meet community needs and will activate community health committees (50% female) to ensure community participation and support information sharing on services. IMA will work with CHDs to organize weekly and monthly health coordination and feedback meetings with CHDs, Morobo and Kajo-Keji community health committees, and community leadership. IMA ensures vulnerable groups are proportionally represented in such discussions by ensuring meetings include representation from all groups during each meeting. Each representative will be asked to provide their opinions in each meeting. If beneficiaries do not feel comfortable sharing, they are given the option to speak directly to the team leader handling the discussion. These interpersonal meetings enable people with low or no literacy to express their suggestions and complaints. During community surveillance and screening, IMA will ensure both women and men are involved in data collection, reporting and exit interviews. The project will establish mechanisms so that women are represented at all levels of group leadership structures.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Ensuring that project participants and communities around the healthcare facilities have access to safe and effective formal and informal feedback mechanisms is a key component of protection mainstreaming. With IMA present in the proposed project areas, the organization has been able to work with the beneficiaries and communities over time, receive feedback, and learn about their preferences and needs, and adapt activities to increase demand for and access to health services. The design of the project has been informed by consultation with stakeholders, including women and displaced people, in the target areas, and needs assessments which included and consulted with women and girls were used for project design. While the mechanisms used to ensure feedback from vulnerable groups are outlined in section 7.4.4 below, IMA will use this feedback on a monthly basis to improve the quality of care provided to patients at the health facilities and to community members receiving community health messaging. Project monitoring will include beneficiary satisfaction with the health services provided. IMA will activate community health committees (50% female) to ensure community participation and support information sharing on services. IMA will work with CHDs to organize weekly and monthly health coordination and feedback meetings with CHDs, county or payam community health committees, and community leadership. These meetings provide direct input from beneficiaries to adapt project assistance. IMA will also leverage its presence in seven other local health facilities to understand the types of feedback beneficiaries commonly provide across a broader geographic area and consider these recommendations for the proposed 10 Health clinics. IMA ensures vulnerable groups are proportionally represented in such discussions. IMA maintains records of feedback disaggregated by group.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

While the ongoing protection crisis in South Sudan results in a large number of risks facing the target populations in Morobo , Lainya and Kajo-Keji, IMA World Health has identified four risks which are most relevant to the Health cluster activities proposed by the project. First, women, girls, and boys face sexual violence including sexual abuse and exploitation in the Morobo, Lainya and Kajo-Keji areas, including around four selected project locations. The legacy of recent armed conflict in Central Equatoria included sexual violence, and incidences of conflict and sexual assault in the area continue. Exposure to violence could occur in the area around a health facility, though the risks in the four selected health facility locations are not higher than in the broader communities surrounding these four facilities. To address the risk, IMA coordinates with the SGBV referral pathway and its community feedback mechanisms to ensure that any risks related to the distance or location of a health facility is noted by facility staff, beneficiaries, community leaders, community health workers, IMA staff, local county health department (CHD) staff. Meanwhile IMA provides clinical management of rape within the health facilities and coordinates support to survivors within the referral pathway- for example, assistance to transfer a beneficiary in need of critical care or refer them to NFI, MHPSS, or other services. Second, IMA sees a risk that populations in Morobo and Kajo Keji may not have access to or awareness of protection assistance or referral services. With a highly mobile population in both locations- frequently moving across the border in Uganda and DRC through informal border points- populations often are not regularly in contact with a reliable service provider who can identify their needs and provide an appropriate referral. IMA has observed that health services are a key priority for populations and that health facilities are often the first point a survivor of SGBV, sexual abuse, or exploitation may arrive for assistance. IMA thus recognizes the importance of a functioning referral pathway into and out of the health facilities. The proposed mobile health facility will also enable IMA to move along with mobile populations, providing them with access to service and thus to the broader referral pathway IMA contributes to. Third, IMA recognizes that health data is highly sensitive and must be kept confidential. To mitigate the risk of sensitive health data on beneficiaries being disclosed, IMA takes the following measures: IMA in its support to health facilities ensures proper utilization of health information management systems, specifically DHIS2, by health facility staff. Facility staff receive training on the proper collection, use, storage, and protection of patient health data. Only doctors and nurses will process patient personal data and data processing is not done on a large scale. OPD data on children and mothers will be entered in the MOH facility registers and admission cards. To define a level of authority on who can access sensitive and personal information, only the medical doctor, qualified nurses, midwives and data registrars will have access of the sensitive and personal information. All used and active MoH health facility registers will be kept in a lock-and-key in a steel cabinet drawer in the health facility, and keys will be kept by the Nurse Facility In-Charge and the Medical Doctor. Data related to survivors is particularly sensitive and will receive focus in collecting, storing and sharing key information on SGBV incidents, with all paper data under lock and key and an electronic information password protection and information transferred by encrypted and password-protected files. Fourth, the Central Equatoria area is at high risk from ERWs. IMA will strengthen coordination and referral pathways for mine risk education and removal services with relevant mine action groups.
