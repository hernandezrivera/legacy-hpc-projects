Project Summary

The school feeding program in Sudan seeks to enhance enrollment and retention, regularize attendance and address short term hunger through the provision of a daily meals and fortified snacks in schools and through take home rations leading to overall better learning outcomes for extremely vulnerable and food insecure children, while working closely with other development partners.

1 - Needs

There are 2.9 million conflict and disaster-affected primary and secondary school-aged children (686,928 internally displaced; 244,830 refugees; 187,853 returnees; and 1,803,781 non-displaced vulnerable children), who require humanitarian assistance in Sudan. Out of the 9.6 million pre- and primary school-aged children (5-13 years), 3.6 million are out of school (49.9% girls and 51.1% boys). While the proportion of girls among out of school children reduced from 52% in 2014 to 49.9% in 2017, the proportion of boys increased from 48% to 51.1% during same period. Majority of the out-of-schoolchildren, nearly 2.2million, are of ages 6-11 years (Dimension 2 in the 5DE framework); followed by 704,315 children at pre-primary school age (D1) and 687,083 at lower secondary age12 -13 years (D3). Of the 3 million children, out of school in the basic education category (ages 5-13), the number of out-of-schoolchildren of primary school going age is 1.96 million. In Blue Nile, Kassala and Red Sea States 46.8 percent, 45.1 percent and 29.4 percent, respectively, of the children of primary school going age are out of school. Gender disparity is highly varied across states. In Red Sea State, almost 25 percent of primary school going age girls are out of school whereas in West Darfur it is 53 percent. Over the past decade, the rate of enrollment has increased from 62% to 68.6%, only highlighting the critical need for education sector partners to work collaboratively and holistically in efforts to achieve SDG4. Low education is identified as a factor in the high prevalence of child marriage in Sudan for girls. Moreover, families cover 63 percent of the cost of education whereas the government contributes 37 percent. Sudan has been affected by economic instability as the monetization of fiscal deficits, loose monetary policy, devaluation of the Sudanese pound, the reduction of fuel subsidies and the elimination of wheat subsidies have contributed to rapid inflation. In addition, the capacity of government institutions to tackle the emerging challenges of economic instability and climate variability is limited. The displaced children are adding pressure to the already poor existing education facilities and because of the limited capacity. Moreover, the vast depreciation of the currency and low purchasing power of households will have immediate impact on education and more children won’t be able to afford schooling costs. By being out school, the risk of children being abused, exposed to violence, explosive and unsafe labour is becoming high. Priority was given to localities with severity ranking 4 and 5, only few localities with severity 3 were considered by type of vulnerability, age and gender from IDPs (new and protracted), returnees and vulnerable residents/host community. According to the Government of Sudan, about 36 percent of the population lives below the global poverty line, with 25 percent being classified as living in extreme poverty. Based on the latest education statistics book for 2016-2017 the level of completion for boys is 40.2%, for girls is 44.6% and for both is 42.3% reflecting a critical problem which school feeding, among other education’s partners interventions, would try to address its root causes, of which short term hunger and drop out are two of them. The targeting is based on Food Security indicators and the unit of measurement is the locality i.e. if the locality is said to be vulnerable, all students would be included in the school feeding programme.

2 - Activities/Outputs

1. Continue to undertake assessments to identify priority areas and programmatic impact in cooperation with partners; 2. Daily provision of hot meals and micronutrient powder as a fortificant, where possible, to vulnerable and food insecure school-aged children through partners; 3. Plan and implement the distribution of fortified nutritious snacks to vulnerable and food insecure school-aged children through partners; 4. Capacity strengthening support for improved implementation by government and NGO partners; 5. Enhance coordination and participation in inter-agency groups and government forums to enhance integrated programming.
