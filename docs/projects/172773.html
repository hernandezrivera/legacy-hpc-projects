Project Summary

As part of continued efforts to improve access to life-saving health care and protection services among IDP and conflict-affected host communities and to build on its program currently supported by BHA, International Medical Corps (IMC) will continue to provide a comprehensive package of primary health care and protection services through the deployment of six Mobile Medical Units (MMUs) operating in Benghazi, Misrata, Sabha, and Tripoli areas. Aligned with the 2020 Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP) for Libya and the Global HRP for COVID-19, this intervention is designed (1) to improve access of vulnerable people to preventive and curative health services, (2) to increase the capacity of the Libyan health system to cope with the COVID-19 pandemic, and (3) to provide specialized protection and GBV services to people impacted by the protracted armed conflict in Libya.
IMC will continue to provide a continuous supply of essential medications and on-site primary health care services, in addition to bolstering the capacity of isolation units in selected hospitals to cope with the increase in severe COVID-19 cases. IMC will also integrate MHPSS into primary health care and protection activities, which will build local capacity and enable IMC to provide essential mental health services in line with World Health Organization (WHO) and Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) guidelines.
IMC will continue to implement GBV prevention and response services, which include the provision of specialized GBV case management services based on a survivor-centered approach. Under its current BHA award, IMC established a Women and Girls’ Safe Space (WGSS), where counseling and safe referrals to the appropriate existing services are provided to survivors, and empowerment activities are carried out for increasing the resilience of women and girls. To ensure complementary services under this project, health staff will also be trained to identify and safely refer GBV cases to GBV caseworkers. IMC will also maintain a field-level referral system with other non-governmental organizations (NGO) actors providing relevant services like emergency cash assistance, child protection, and shelter.

1 - Needs

The review of the SARA data found that most PHC facilities were operational in the municipalities reviewed, but the level of service delivered differed markedly between municipalities. Generally, municipalities performed better with respect to the availability and readiness of emergency and trauma care and in child health. They performed less well concerning the provision of services for communicable diseases, sexually transmitted infections and mental health. Concerningly, no municipality reviewed offered services for mental health or sexually transmitted infections. Given the increasing burden of non-communicable diseases in Libya, low service readiness scores for cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and chronic respiratory disease is also a cause for concern. In addition, noting the ongoing conflict and the increased chance of disease outbreak, the low scores on communicable diseases warrants further attention. The specific results of how each municipality performed against the indicators can be found in Annex1. Insights gained from KIIs and FGDs indicate that primary health care services are performing poorly in the municipality reviewed. The situation appears graver than what the SARA report indicates. The findings from the KIIs and FGDs depict a primary health care service, which in large part, has ceased to function. In the majority of facilities, there is a significant lack of health staff, equipment, or essential medicines. In several municipalities, including Janzur, officials reported ‘ghost’ employees, who are on the payroll of a PHC facility but do not in fact, perform any professional duties there. In some municipalities with robust private sectors, doctors employed at PHC facilities also work in the private sector, as is the case in Misurata and often undertake truncated hours at the PHC facilities as a result. In some conflict-affected areas, medical equipment has been stolen from PHC facilities. Janzour reported theft and vandalism at PHC facilities and noted that some PHC facilities were located “outside the secure zones.” In some areas, supplies had not been provided for years or in insufficient quantity. As a result, the public appears to have lost confidence in the primary healthcare service and are seeking treatment from the private sector and hospitals.There was an overwhelming consensus across all municipalities that PHC facilities lack essential medicine and equipment. Stockouts were frequent, and budgets irregularly replenished. In some areas, chronic disease medications were available for some months and not others or where they were available. There was no doctor or pharmacist to prescribe them. Also, a lot of equipment was old and required maintenance and medications had expired. In most areas, patients were required to pre-purchase (at their own expense) essential equipment and medicine for treatment.In addition to the lack of medications and medical equipment, there is a marked absence of specialized doctors in the PHC sector. Officials and community members in nearly every municipality reported that doctors with expertise in ENT, oncology, gynecology, obstetrics, pediatrics and orthopedics were sorely lacking. In extreme cases, like Brak Alshati, some PHC facilities have operated for years without the presence of specialized physicians. In Al Buwanis (Sabha) the situation was the grimmest with reports of no doctors at all except IMC’s doctor who provides services at Tamanhent PHC facility. Moreover, most officials noted an operational referral system, particularly between PHCs and hospitals and specialist services. Referrals are communicated either verbally or with a written prescription, but it was unclear what patient reporting was subsequently provided back to the referring practitioner. Finally, several vulnerable groups have been facing considerable barriers to access.

2 - Activities

6 Mobile Medical Units will provide essential primary healthcare in areas with high concentrations of IDPs in Tripoli, Benghazi, Misrata, and Sabha. 4 COVID-19 Medical Specialists will work within 4 hospital isolation units to increase human resource capacity to handle the burden of COVID-19 patients. This project also includes the continued operation of a Women and Girls' Safe Space (WGSS) in Janzour, in which women and girls are able to access lifeskills and women's empowerment activities, as well as GBV case management. 4 PSS Counselors and 1 Psychiatrist will also be working alongside our medical and GBV teams to provide MHPSS services in all locations on a rotational schedule.

5 - Mitigation measures
5.1 - For each risk mentioned under 4 above, please describe the ways you plan to mitigate, prevent and/or address these.

IMC plans to mitigate the risk of increased tensions and conflicts in the community due to perceived or actual unequal distribution of assistance through ensuring clear communication to the affected population about how, where, when, and why assistance is provided. IMC also consults with communities before, during, and after activities to more accurately gauge, and then respond to, the needs of each community with a particular focus on vulnerable groups including women, children, and people with disabilities. IMC plans to mitigate the risk of increased tensions and conflicts in the community due to perceived or actual affiliation to one group or another by ensuring that all IMC staff working on this project follow guiding humanitarian principles of impartiality. IMC also communicates with local, regional, and national government bodies to explain our status as an impartial INGO who is not affiliated or deferential to any particular group. IMC will ensure that all staff are trained on proper infection, prevention, control standards and are give the necessary PPE to ensure their safety and the safety of beneficiaries. Any group activities will be led ensuring social distance and proper use of PPE are maintained. As a medical organization, we are particularly well suited to address these concerns. These measures act as mitigation to the risk of increased exposure to COVID-19 transmission.
