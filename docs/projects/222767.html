Project Summary

In line with the goals of the UN Decade on Ecosystem Restoration, FAO is dedicated to revitalizing both ecological and social systems in Cox’s Bazar. This initiative emphasizes collaboration with local communities and authorities, striving to establish shared goals and practices for sustainable resource use and ecosystem management.
The project aims to ensure protecting and rehabilitating the ecosystems in Cox’s Bazar. Cox’s Bazar is highly vulnerable to natural disasters such as cyclones, landslides, flash floods and drought and the influx of Rohingya refugees since 2017 has exacerbated pressures on the environment. The increased population has led to significant strain on forest resources, reducing the region's ability to recover from frequent natural hazards. To address this, the project will focus on restoring overall ecosystem health to enhance the region’s resilience.
FAO will prioritize the rehabilitation of degraded land and streambanks, support roadside and riparian plantations based on high-risk analyses conducted by SCCCM for landslides to minimize soil erosion and implement slope stabilization measures using nature-based solutions. These efforts will be guided by a landscape-based watershed management approach, which will help protect against multiple hazards like landslides, floods and cyclones. As part of this, the project will reforest 200 hectares within the camps by planting a variety of indigenous tree species, including slope stabilizing species and medicinal plants, providing both ecological benefits and accessible sources of traditional remedies for the Rohingya community. Many medicinal plants have strong root systems that bind soil effectively, reducing erosion and promoting slope stability. It also supports ecosystem, biodiversity and offers cultural and economic value to local communities. For example, Neem (Azadirachta indica) known for its medicinal properties and drought-resistant characteristics, useful for stabilizing slopes in degraded areas. The plantation, fencing, care and maintenance activities will also offer financial incentives to Rohingya volunteers, strengthening their livelihoods.
To enhance community preparedness and resilience to natural hazards, FAO will provide training on landslide risks and preparedness, utilizing the landslide early warning system currently being developed in collaboration with the Bangladesh Meteorological Department for anticipatory action. The FAO's Landslide early warning SOP will enhance coordination among stakeholders by aligning with the BMD's landslide early warning system, ensuring timely warning dissemination, anticipatory actions, and volunteer engagement across pre-, during-, and post-disaster phases to strengthen landslide resilience.
Additionally, FAO will support partners in monitoring and implementing ecosystem restoration and biodiversity conservation efforts by creating guidelines for tree species selection, watershed management and landslide risk awareness training. This project integrates both disaster response and ecosystem rehabilitation to build long-term resilience for vulnerable communities in Cox's Bazar.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

Cox’s Bazar, situated along the Bay of Bengal, faces significant vulnerability to natural hazards such as cyclones, landslides and flash floods, which have become more frequent and intense due to the accelerating impacts of climate change. The region’s susceptibility to these disasters is compounded by ongoing environmental degradation, including deforestation, soil erosion, and stream siltation. Human activities, particularly agricultural expansion, illegal logging and hill-cutting for settlement construction, have further exacerbated environmental stress, leading to a substantial reduction in the area’s natural resilience. From 2001 to 2023, Cox’s Bazar lost nearly 10,000 hectares of tree cover—a 23% decrease—which has resulted in the release of 4.87 million tons of CO₂ emissions and a 32% reduction in humid primary forest area.The situation is further strained by the influx of Rohingya refugees, who have placed an immense burden on the already stressed ecosystem. Over 2,500 hectares of protected forest were allocated by the Government of Bangladesh for refugee shelters, but the increased demand for fuelwood from both refugees and host communities has led to the degradation of more than 7,000 hectares of forest. Forest resource extraction now outpaces biomass regeneration by around 10,000 tons annually, further jeopardizing the environment and exacerbating climate vulnerabilities. Though the SAFE+ and SAFE+2 initiatives have led to the replanting of 594 hectares, limited land availability continues to be a significant challenge, necessitating more strategic and coordinated efforts for restoration and sustainable land management.In addition to environmental degradation, shelter and safety within refugee camps remains a critical concern. A 2023 report from the Shelter-CCCM sector revealed that 41% of camp shelters are situated in areas prone to slope failure and fire risks, with Camp 11 having the highest proportion of shelters in unsafe zones. The safety of these shelters is contingent upon factors such as slope stabilization methods, including terracing, bamboo or sandbag retaining walls, proper drainage and slope vegetation. However, despite efforts to stabilize these areas, many camps remain highly vulnerable to landslides, underscoring the need for improved understanding of landslide dynamics and enhanced preparedness.There is an urgent need to restore degraded lands and reforest large areas to mitigate the risks of soil erosion, landslides and flooding. This will also help absorb CO₂ emissions and support the recovery of ecosystems vital for both the refugee and host populations.Women and girls are disproportionately affected by the combined impacts of natural disasters, climate change, and environmental degradation. They are often more vulnerable due to limited access to resources, decision-making roles, and mobility. Women in particular play a central role in household energy needs, including fuelwood collection, which exacerbates the pressure on local ecosystems. Addressing these gender disparities is crucial by promoting women's participation in ecosystem restoration activities, disaster preparedness training and leadership in community resilience efforts. Providing access to alternative energy sources and improved shelter designs will also help reduce the burden on women and girls.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

FAO integrates “Do No Harm” approach in designing, developing, implementing and monitoring programme interventions in Cox’s Bazar to ensure that all activities target and benefit the most vulnerable individuals, enhancing safety and dignity. In addition, FAO follows PSEA guideline, maintain hotline and internal referral pathway to report sexual exploitation and abuse and other forms of sexual violence. A dedicated hotline is also maintained to receive programmatic complaints to promote accountability to the affected population. Ensuring gender, age, and disabilities will also be taken into account acknowledging the fact that climate change and natural disasters will affect different societal groups differently. Throughout the project period the feedback of the different groups will be collected and addressed in the program design if required.

2.3 - Age & Disability

FAO follows GEDSI (Gender equality, disability, and social inclusion) approach in program interventions to ensure that all individuals have equal access to resources, services, and decision-making processes, that their diverse perspectives and needs are considered. The elderly and persons with disability (at least 3%) will be prioritized considering their vulnerable status in the beneficiary selection process. Washington group questions will be used to identify the person with disability at the beneficiary selection stage. The aggregation center structure will be designed/updated to make it accessible for all age groups including for the person with disability. In addition, the feedback from all age group including person with disability will be recorded and will be incorporated in the intervention design if required. Thus, FAO strives to create inclusive environments and promote equal opportunities for all individuals.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

FAO strictly enforces a policy of Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) which ensures that all information relevant to project stakeholders is shared in advance of commencing project implementation organizing stakeholder consultation workshops. At the initial stage of the project, beneficiaries will be selected in an accountable and transparent way through a beneficiary selection survey. All age groups especially women, older persons, PWD, and youth will be prioritized as beneficiaries. Throughout the project implementation period, feedback from the project beneficiaries will be recorded through Post Distribution Monitoring (PDM) survey, random spot checks, and organizing Feedback sessions with the project beneficiaries. To ensure need based and inclusive response the feedback will be incorporated into the project design at any stage of the implementation if required. A dedicated hotline number will be set up so that the beneficiary can directly access with any complaints and feedback to FAO. The targeted beneficiaries will be sensitized with the hotline number and AAP messages when delivering services at the field level. Separate feedback session will be organized with the adolescent boys, girls and person with disabilities to communicate the hotline number and to undertake the feedback on project interventions. If the beneficiary comes up with any complaint through the hotline number, then the responsible staff will immediately go for the field investigation to verify the complaint and report to the senior management. The investigation and reporting will be done by maintaining a high level of confidentiality. Immediate actions will be taken within shortest possible time to address the grievance from the community level.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

FAO has a well-established PSEA mechanism that follows zero tolerance for any form of sexual exploitation and abuse. In FAO Cox’s Bazar sub-office, a hotline number is maintained to receive any PSEA complaint which is 24/7 standby and operated by a PSEA focal point. For wider dissemination of the hotline number and PSEA policy followed by FAO, flyer/poster is designed and attached to the fixed points. In this project, the implementing partners who will be at the frontline will be trained on PSEA principles. Besides the beneficiaries who will be selected for cash for work will be properly communicated with the PSEA hotline number at the selection stage. Furthermore, before signing partnership with any NGO it is carefully checked with support from the PSEA network to determine whether the selected NGO gone through PSEA capacity assessment or not and all the partner staff are properly oriented on PSEA policy immediately after onboarding of the partner. Thus, FAO is mainstreaming PSEA in its intervention.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Yes, the project incorporates climate action. It enhances resilience against climate-induced risks, such as landslides and flash floods, by expanding a landslide early warning system, and stabilizing high-risk areas with reforestation and biological land stabilization. These actions help mitigate erosion, reduce landslide risks, and improve ecosystem health. Additionally, reforestation and watershed restoration contribute to carbon sequestration, supporting broader climate mitigation efforts. By strengthening ecosystems and community preparedness, the project promotes long-term climate resilience for both refugees and host communities.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

Yes, the project includes Emergency Preparedness and Response (EPR) measures designed to enhance resilience and continuity in disaster situations. The project has developed a business continuity plan to ensure essential operations remain functional during and after emergencies. This includes coordination with local authorities and key stakeholders to adapt swiftly to disruptions, maintaining critical services and resource flows for both host communities and Rohingya camps in Cox’s Bazar. The Emergency Response Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) development works can start from 2025 to organize responses to landslide events. The project does not have any contingency funds to cover immediate needs and mobilize resources during landslide-related crises.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

Yes, the project actively includes and engages youth. Youth will be trained in activities such as planting, data collection for early warning systems, and participation in landslide response teams, providing them with valuable skills in environmental stewardship and community safety.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

Yes, the project promotes self-reliance and resilience for refugees by engaging them directly in environmental restoration and disaster preparedness activities that provide both income and skills development. Through cash-for-work programs, refugees participate in tasks such as reforestation, landslide risk reduction, and ecosystem monitoring. By involving refugees in these restoration efforts, the project fosters a sense of ownership, strengthens community bonds, and enhances their capacity to respond to natural hazards.
