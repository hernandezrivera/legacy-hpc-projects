Project Summary

Addressing the gaps in services to one of the most marginalised and vulnerable groups in Gaza, and factoring in differences along age, marital status, geographical location and health, and drawing on a gendered-responsive, holistic, multi-sectoral, GBV-centred, human-rights and needs-tailored approaches, the project will provide services to women and girls with physical and learning disabilities in the Gaza Strip, especially in the northern and southern parts (North: Al Qaraya al Badawiya al Maslakh, Beit Hanun, Beit Lahiya. South: Khan Younis, Mawasi Khan Younis, and Rafah).
With the help of experienced operating local partners in the field, Women’s Health Centre (WHC) – Jabaliya and Culture and Free Thought Association (CFTA), the project will provide GBV case management, psychosocial support, legal support, health and reproductive health services, referral, and cash for work opportunities.

1 - Needs

Recent developments have intensified protection concerns as well as significantly harmed the physical and mental wellbeing of Palestinians in the OPT. The May 2021 escalation of hostilities between Israel and Palestinian armed groups have greatly exacerbated the humanitarian crisis facing Gaza’s two million residents. The recent escalation killed 260 Palestinians (including 40 women and 63 children, 23 of which were girls under the age of 18), wounded almost 2,000 Palestinians (including 398 women and 600 children), displacing 113,000 who sought shelter and protection at UNRWA schools or with hosting families at the height of the escalation (more than half of them are women and girls). A CARE rapid needs assessment undertaken in Gaza after the escalation revealed that 76 per cent of those surveyed reported an increase in security concerns facing women and girls since the conflict began while 88 per cent of women surveyed expressed the need for psychological support. In Gaza, service providers underscored “crucial gaps regarding the provision of specialised health services for more serious mental health cases” stating that “There is a lack of specialised services in the community and a shortage of qualified staff to deal with the trauma of ongoing violence”. People with disabilities, of which many are children, continue to face unique challenges especially for rehabilitative and caregiving services. In Gaza a recent CARE needs assessment revealed that 32 per cent of respondents with disability and/or whose family members are disabled were not able to access health services or medical treatment. In the OPT women with disabilities often lack access to disability-friendly services (such as sexual and reproductive health services as well as justice and security services). Across the oPt, voices of women with disabilities are routinely ignored, even in times of crisis. A survey of women and girls with disabilities in the oPt, undertaken in March and April 2020, discovered that “no official body, whether at the level of a ministry or a department, has contacted any woman or girl with a disability in order to examine their needs, or to check on their conditions in light of the emergency, whether in the West Bank or Gaza Strip”. Impacted by entrenched social norms and lack of resources at both household and state levels, women and girls with disabilities in Palestine “are denied the opportunity to make decisions, participate in society, find employment or enjoy their right to inheritance” (UNFPA, 2019). In the OPT, as in many parts of the world, persons with disabilities experience challenges related to their ability to access coping mechanisms. In particular, women with disabilities experience reduced resilience. CARE estimates that women with disabilities in the OPT are “up to four times more likely to experience intimate partner violence” while often having no option but to rely on their abuser for mobility. This in turn limits their ability to build resilience or access other services. Women with disabilities have minimal opportunities for economic resilience as an estimated four per cent of women with disabilities participate in the workforce. Violence against women and girls with disabilities is more serious and common. In the oPt men are overwhelmingly the main touchpoint for cash and voucher assistance. For example, of households receiving Ministry of Social Development (MoSD) assistance, 78.8 per cent are headed by a male family member while only 21.2 per cent are headed by women (e.g. mother, daughter, or grandmother). Recent CVA in the Gaza Strip have demonstrated that beneficiaries have used cash buy food and pay off debts. Ongoing CVA in the Gaza Strip have highlighted the challenge of ensuring women obtain and control an equitable portion of resources received by a household as these decisions are subject to household dynamics which, as highlighted above, tend to reinforce a system that favors men having the ‘final say’ on family matters. All of this, therefore, demonstrates how CVA may contribute to building women’s access to cash, knowledge, social capital, and interactions with institutions and thus introduce additional opportunities for other benefits including self-esteem, self-confidence, self-perception, autonomy, and mobility. Boys and girls in Gaza live in a protracted protection crisis under occupation, magnified by the May 2021 escalation, a 14-year blockade, and internal divisions between the PA and Hamas. UNICEF has estimated that 250,000 children were in need of mental health and psychosocial support services (MHPSS) due to trauma and fear from “injuries, widespread destruction and the loss of lives as a result of the escalation”. While protection mechanisms are weak in general, Gaza faces more significant challenges. These include the higher disability rate due to the recurrent rounds of conflict and the deteriorating political and economic conditions, which in turn increase the risk of violence for women and girls with disabilities in Gaza. When it comes to this group access to services, significant obstacles and challenges remain in place, primarily in the form of the weak protection system; limited service delivery to survivors of GBV; failure to respond to the special needs of women with disabilities survivors of GBV. There is also an apparent lack of ability on part on women with disabilities who are survivors of violence to access information and services in relation to prevention of violence, protection, recovery and others. It is important to note that in the absence of adequate services and income opportunities people living with disabilities are often dependent on their relatives to satisfy their needs, which creates additional responsibilities for caregivers – who are mostly women.

2 - Activities

Through this project, UN Women will build on its experience in GBV prevention and response, legal aid, structured psychosocial support, and economic and livelihood support. UN Women will highly capitalize on the experience of civil society partners (CFTA and WHC-Jabaliya) in providing multisectoral services to women and girls with disabilities (physical and intellectual and what these two wide categories include) with a particular focus on women and girls living in the Northern area of the Gaza Strip (Al Qaraya al Badawiya al Maslakh, Beit Hanun, Beit Lahiya) and Southern area of the Gaza Strip (Khan Younis, Mawasi Khan Younis, and Rafah). This will include addressing the needs of women and girls with disabilities affected Covid-19 and GVB. The overall objective of the project is to that Women and girls with physical and intellectual disabilities are supported to use available, accessible and gender-responsive multisectoral services. UN Women’s and partners’ interventions will be carefully designed following holistic/multisectoral and survivor-centred approaches that will take into account individual differences among women and girls with disabilities such as area of residence, age, marital status, and economic status with an understanding that such differences (age, location, health, economic and marital status) can all be drivers of vulnerability and need to be addresses with tailored approaches. UN Women will ensure throughout project implementation that multisectoral GBV services are provided according to the GBV case management Standard Operating Procedures (SOP) as approved by the Ministry of Women’s Affairs and that project interventions are well-coordinated among the partners and well-consulted with the GBV sub-cluster, the Protection cluster, the child protection working group, the disability working group, and the Food Security cluster, Health Cluster, cash working group. UN Women will also make use of tools and guidelines that have been developed by humanitarian partners such as GBV case management and referral SoPs, CFW assessments, different tools on services for persons with disabilities, child protection protocols, and the Database and guidance of the Cash working group. 3 - Indicators

Monitoring will be a continuous and consistent process occurring throughout the programme’s life span. Ongoing monitoring will be led by the UN Women in collaboration with implementing partners and other stakeholders including beneficiaries. The monitoring tools and system will be designed to focus on upward accountability (i.e. towards the donor, humanitarian partners) and downward accountability (i.e. towards programme beneficiaries and primary stakeholders). The programme workplan will be used to measure how well activities have been implemented and whether these contribute to the overall achievement of programme objectives. As part of continuous monitoring throughout programme implementation, UN Women and partners will ensure the collection of sex and age disaggregated data (SADD), which will be inclusive to the extent possible of data on women and girls with disabilities. SADD will be used to inform project direction towards being more inclusive and sensitive to addressing gender-based vulnerabilities. UN Women and partners will employ various M&E tools applying their internal oversight mechanisms including the internal monitoring and reporting mechanisms such as reporting to HQ, the regular monitoring activities by its monitoring/project officers, and the partners’ monitoring. UN Women and partners will regularly convene project teams’ meetings, field visits and discussions with relevant clusters to assess progress via set results. The partners will conduct a pre and post impact assessment and this will support the fine-tuning of baselines, also conducting project midterm FGDs to monitor progress. Overall, UN Women and partners will collect information on a quarterly basis. UN Women programme team will ensure full consultation with the humanitarian clusters including the Protection cluster, child protection cluster, the GBV sub-cluster and OCHA and also with project partners to decide on how the activities can be geared to supporting women and girls with disabilities affected by COVID-19. Depending on the situation, a team of women early responders will be composed out of service providers who can do outreach and services that are home based to WGWDs. Cash for work alternative schemes will be discussed with the cash working group. 10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

UN Women partners will support the development of protection measures, codes and internal policies that respect accountability for affected population and followed the SOPs referral pathways and “Do no Harm principles” which will be illustrated in filling out pre assessments for CBOs to assure the protection measures for target groups such as location and privacy. As well as signing MOUs with employers under (CfW interventions) which respect women’s privacy and dignity as GBV survivors. UN Women will ensure that the interventions will focus on addressing women’s immediate needs in a way that decreases their exposure to various risks. Risk factors within Cash For Work (CFW) are varied according to the setting, geographical area, and type of placement, type of vulnerabilities…etc. UN Women, through systemize documentation of the good practices/lessons learned from previous CFW interventions, will work closely with partners to mitigate risks. E.g one if identified risks is that cash for work beneficiaries face spousal tension or domestic violence as a result of receiving cash (external), the while the mitigation is that coupling the cash for work with GBV assistance and also targeting men and family members of women beneficiaries. Addressing risks will also go hand in hand with strengthening women’s capacities and role as actors of change in their communities and their role in informing/guiding humanitarian efforts at large.
