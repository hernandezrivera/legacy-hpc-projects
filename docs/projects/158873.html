Project Summary

This project is in line with and contributes to the Protection Cluster Objective Two, which calls for putting in place protection response mechanisms to prevent and mitigate the effects of the occupation and conflict related violence. This project will support the delivery of quality, integrated child protection and psychosocial support services to the most vulnerable boys and girls aged 4-18 in Gaza, especially those associated with injuries during the ‘Great March of Return’ protests. The crisis has demonstrated the almost absence of available child protection services to address critical needs. The project will ensure that 12 family centres and 30 outreach sites in Gaza, are fully operational, responding to the pressing mental health and psychosocial, as well as child protection needs, of affected children. Service delivery will be targeted to the most affected locations and the most vulnerable children through a coordinated continuum of services in Gaza. Each family centre will be connected to a community-based child protection committee that informs the family centre of community needs and acts as a focal point for identifying vulnerable children for referral. Family centres will operate through established Community Based Organizations (CBOs) and mobile units, and will assess/screen and refer children for further services through:
1) a case management approach for the worst affected;
2) referral of complex child protection cases to child protection services (relevant stakehoders and Civil Society Organizations (CSOs); and
3) referral of children with further psychosocial support needs to more focused and specialized psychological support services.
Services will also strengthen outreach capacities to detect and link up children in need with service providers, whilst providing mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) support through the family centres.

Implementing Partners

UNICEF will select partners among the cluster working group and those who have experience and expertise working in the area of child protection, in particular provision of community level structured mental health and psychosocial support. The organisations should meet the following selection criteria:
1) Active within the child protection working group;
2) Expertise in operating community level child friendly spaces, and direct expertise in participatory case management, life skills programming, psychosocial support services and recreational activities in Gaza.
3) Excellent outreach capacities and geographical proximity to children and families in the most affected areas of the Gaza Strip with specialist trained staff with expertise in case management, strong commitment to the case management approach, strong coordination and relationships with relevant stakeholders and organisations.
4) Are guided by contextualized manuals and materials developed and selected in the best interests of children, taking into consideration the “do no harm approach” during the various stages of the project life cycle in line with the Child Protection Minimum Standards (CPMS) and IASC MHPSS standards.
5) Have referral expertise and networks for cross-sectoral referrals, especially with Education, and Health Cluster partners.
6) Proven monitoring and evaluation capacities to ensure tracking of progress and monitoring of results in line with UN Harmonised Approach to Cash Transfer (HACT) modalities

1 - Needs

It is estimated that 258,071 children in Gaza need structured psychosocial support (PSS) and child protection services (2020 HNO). The great need for case management and PSS services in Gaza is confirmed by 2018 child protection assessment. In addition, a total of 22,578 children suffering severe and moderate mental health disorders are estimated to require case management. In Gaza, once the child is detected with child protection needs, the case manager should reach out the child to assess the needs. Recommended response varies depending on the risk level with cases referred to MoSD or family centers as per Gaza referral pathways. The MoSD was only able to reach 74 children with case management in 2019. Thus, fully functional family centres providing case management services are a necessity. Overwhelmed child protection services are exacerbated by high number of causalities and injuries affecting children in the context of the “Great March of Return” (GMR). From 30 March 2018 to 20 September 2019, the GMR demonstrations have resulted in the injury of 35,503 people including 8,209 children. A collapsing economy, the internal political divide and the ongoing energy crisis have exacerbated the already fragile situation. About 54% of the population in Gaza live under the poverty line, affecting the capacity of families to provide their children with basic education supplies and transportation to/from school. According to the UNICEF country report on out-of-school children in oPt (2018), an estimated 5.5% and 4.3% of 5-year-olds and 10-15-year-old children are respectively out-of-school. Those children are more exposed to violence, abuse and harmful practices with the result of increasing demands for PSS and child protection support on the already overwhelmed system. Analysis of data on injured children shows that 95% of them are boys (63% aged between 14-17). Adolescent boys are more exposed to a range of protection threats including death or serious injuries with long-term effects on their well-being. Maimed or wounded boys especially face major challenges to attend school as they need to rehabilitate from injuries over a long period of time, which in turn puts them at risks of school drop-out and of child labour. Children (aged 6-14) compromising 39% of the total population are the most vulnerable to the escalations and continuous deteriorating security situation. Ministry of Education data also shows that 42% of the drop out children are female, presumably adolescents, at risk of early marriage. UNICEF is proactive in identifying and visiting injured children. The Governorate Protection Focal Points and family centre Case Managers have visited 3,517 injured children to assess their situation and to provide them with psychological first aid in 2019. This has helped refer 812 children to case management and 1,138 to PSS services. Through case management of injured children, access to medical, PSS, rehabilitation, and educational support services is facilitated in line with the Gaza referral pathways. Limited and intermittent funding to the family centres limits their capacity to meet the spikes in needs, and significant resources are needed to expand centres’ outreach and responsive capacities to ensure critical services are in place. Through case management, this project will respond to the protection needs of children and adolescent boys and girls within the scope of the family centres, in particular injuries from demonstrations and Explosive Remnants of Wars as well as child labour, PSS distress, neglect and violence cases. Family centres will provide outreach and follow up with key service providers to ensure access to needed services. Unless critically under-resourced child protection services are funded, children will remain at significant risk of developing serious mental health issues. Training of field staff on MHPSS minimum standards, in line with IASC Guidelines is needed to ensure no harm to benefiting children.

2 - Activities

With the support of UNICEF implementing partners, individual protection needs of both girls and boys (4-18), especially adolescents aged 14-17, will be addressed. This will be done through the project, including, but not limited to, case management of boys affected in the demonstrations and by Explosive Remnants of War (ERW)-related injuries and child labour and female children affected by early marriage. In addition to case management of early marriage cases, psychosocial support, life skills education and awareness raising will also be provided in coordination with the GBV Sub-Cluster. Both men and women will be targeted through the project, making sure that they understand their contribution to their children’s well-being and ensuring their safety and protection. Since the majority of children injured at the borders are adolescent boys, support through family centres will take this into consideration including adaptation of the life skills manuals used. Both male and female case managers will be available to provide support and follow up through in centre, as well as outreach activities. To support Children with disabilities (CWD), implementing partners will target them in coordination and/or through disability organizations they partner with. Currently, four out of the twelve family centers are disability organizations. In 2019 and through the twelve centers, UNICEF supported more than 400 children with disabilities.Project outputs include:Output 1: Holistic child protection services equally provided to boys and girls aged 4-18 through 12 family centers and 5 Child Protection Networks. Activity 1.1 Provide structured child protection and psychosocial programs – multi-week life skills (including sessions on ERW awareness), individual and group counselling using gender and age sensitive resilience building approaches, parent/child interaction programs and expressive arts programs, especially for injured children and children with disabilities. Activity 1.2 Provide case management services for children with protection needs accessing the family centres and identified through screening. Priority will be given to children who have been injured during the Great March of Return demonstrations and by ERWs, children who lost caregivers, and those subject to abuse, neglect, and exploitation, as well as children with disabilities. Output 2: Integrated child protection services supported to strengthen parental capacity to respond to children 's needs. Activity 2.1 Organize awareness raising sessions for parents on gender specific child protection issues and positive parenting (including a session on ERW awareness). Output 3: Capacity for professionals working on mental health and psychosocial support strengthened. Activity 3.1 Conduct trainings on mental health and psychosocial support guidelines for field and emergency workers.

3 - Indicators

In order to track project deliverable, the following indicators will be used; • Indicator 1.1: Number of children (50% boys and 50% girls), including the injured, and children with disabilities aged between 4-18 benefiting from structured child protection interventions including life skills, group and individual counselling and child-parent interaction programs by December 2020 (Target 9,900 including 400 CWD).• Indicator 1.2 Number of children (60% boys and 40% girls), including the injured and children with disabilities, benefiting from case management support by December 2020 (Target 2,250 including 50 CWD). • Indicator 2.1 Number of caregivers (25% males) benefiting from awareness raising on child protection & positive parenting and community initiatives by December 2020 (Target 5,508)• Indicator 2.2 Number of children (50% males) and caregivers (20% males) benefiting from ERW risk education sessions by December 2020 (Target 14,045)• Indicator 3.1 Number of MHPSS and child protection workers (50% females) trained on guidelines on mental health and psychosocial support by December 2020 (Target 115). UNICEF will ensure monitoring and evaluation of the project on the basis of outputs delivered, including the number of referrals to emergency services, child protection case management and psychosocial support services. Monitoring the adherence of service providers to the case management standard operating procedures will also be included, as well as use of resilience building approaches. The project will be monitored using the UNICEF regular monitoring framework. Project implementation will be reviewed as part of the Annual Programme Review jointly conducted by UNICEF and project implementing partners. Regular field visits to project sites will be conducted. Partners will submit quarterly progress reports as a component of the project monitoring mechanism. In addition, UNICEF will provide technical support to partners through regular coordination. Partners will assess benefit to children, caregivers and professionals through pre- and post-questionnaires, as well as focus group discussions. UNICEF is committed to reporting towards the Humanitarian Response Plan periodic reporting and the updating of the OCHA Financial Tracking System.

9 - Protection mainstreaming
9.1 - Do No Harm

To ensure sustainability of child protection work within the community, UNICEF and its implementing partners will work with at least 12 formed Child Protection Community Committees (CPCC), this will ensure community-based capacity to capture child protection issues even if the center ceases to exist. Working with volunteers, both males and females, from the same community will also contribute towards creating child protection capacities that will provide the support in absence of the centers. Additionally, the family centers will use context appropriate manuals, including The Win-Win Deal manual developed by War Child Holland for the Life skills corner, while in house developed group counseling manuals will be utilized for group counseling. Normally children receive 12-16 structured sessions. The Gaza Case Management Standard Operating Procedures will govern the work of the case managers in the family centers.

9.2 - Equal and impartial access to aid

Individual protection needs of both girls and boys (4-18), especially adolescents aged 14-17, will be addressed through the project, including, but not limited to, case management of female children who might be affected by early marriage and boys affected in the demonstrations and by Explosive Remnants of War (ERW)-related injuries and child labour. Both men and women will be targeted through the project, making sure that they understand their contribution to their children’s well-being and ensuring their safety and protection. Since the majority of children injured at the borders are adolescent boys, support through family centres will take this into consideration including adaptation of the life skills manuals used. Both male and female case managers will be available to provide support and follow up through in centre, as well as outreach activities.
