Project Summary

In order to continue to address the burden of the leishmaniasis epidemic across northern Syria, The MENTOR Initiative will continue to partner with stakeholders and communities to ensure that the most vulnerable receive:
1. Prevention of leishmaniasis transmission within their homes. MENTOR strives to provide protection to 992,630 households through an Indoor Residual Spraying (IRS) campaign in northern Syria in Idleb, Aleppo, Al-Hasakeh, Ar-Raqqa and Deir-ez-Zor governorates.
2. Information about leishmaniasis and its transmission. MENTOR will run combined Information, Education and Communication / Behaviour Change Communication (IEC/BCC) to ensure households are empowered to prevent leishmaniasis transmission within their household. This will be done through various media and delivery strategies, to maximize uptake and correct utilization of leishmaniasis prevention and case management services.

4 - Needs

Population displacements combined with overcrowding, poor sanitation, inadequate waste management, impaired nutrition, destruction of infrastructure, and a lack of healthcare provision have provided the perfect drivers for the spread of vector-borne diseases, particularly leishmaniasis. In Syria, MENTOR has recorded dramatic increases in the incidence of CL and VL in both NWS and NES in 2020, and 2021 seems to continue on the same trajectory. In 2019 MENTOR diagnosed and treated 17,935 cases in NWS and 16,418 cases in NES, while in 2020 MENTOR recorded 35,577 and 33,456 cases respectively. At the same time, MENTOR conducted 564,463 treatment consultations to treat and heal all cases of CL. Of the overall cases 66.36% were children and 21.77% were children below five years of age. In the first two months of 2021 MENTOR has already diagnosed 8,679 cases in NWS and 8,400 cases in NES, surpassing even the numbers of 2020. In addition, MENTOR also recorded 54 cases of VL in NWS in 2020, and already 18 cases in the first three months of 2021. This is particularly concerning, as such an increase in a vulnerable population with little immunity against the disease can quickly lead to outbreaks of the potentially fatal disease. Here, prevention is key to reduce transmission. MENTOR bases its needs assessment on a network of MENTOR-supported health facilities and MENTOR mobile clinics across NWS and NES, which collect and send comprehensive data to the MENTOR M&E department every week. All activity locations and programmes are based on the most recent incidence and population movement numbers and after taking into consideration the needs laid out in the 2020 Syria HRP as well as the presence of partner organisations.

5 - Implementation of activities

Indoor Residual Spraying (IRS): The IRS campaign will be strategically conducted prior to or just inside the disease transmission season. Sandflies require a minimum temperature of 16.5°C to prosper, although larva development can take place from 9°C and above. Generally, this means a transmission season lasting from March to October, with peak sandfly numbers in September. As IRS protection lasts 6-9 months, depending on the substrate sprayed, correct timing ensures maximum benefit, keeping vector population densities low and breaking the transmission cycle. The sandfly is 1/3rd the size of the mosquito and silent, but the residents perceive the benefit of IRS because of the reduction of more conspicuous nuisance pests such as cockroaches, bed bugs, flies and mosquitos, which is an added health benefit. MENTOR will use WHO-approved ICON & FICAM as the insecticide for IRS in 2021. Spraying will include animal houses and outbuildings, and a minimum coverage of 85% of households will be achieved following WHO guidelines to ensure community level protection. Community resistance to IRS in a country where suspicion of chemical reagents is at a height can be a problem. MENTOR has worked to overcome this issue through effective awareness campaigns, the employment of local workers, engendering the trust and confidence of the local community, the recruitment of female workers to facilitate access to homes when the male head of household is absent as well as printed IEC/BCC materials accompanied by verbal explanation during the house-to-house visits. We aim to also highlight the general benefits of eliminating a range of household pests and spraying animal houses, beneficial to both the animals and the household.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

Child protection: Likely critical impact. All newly recruited staff sign the Child Protection Policy. MENTOR provides its representatives with clear guidance about ways to minimize risk to children and the standards of behaviour and practice required when they are in contact with children. Strict hiring policies prevent children of being hired for any activities. The monitoring method is Complaint Response Mechanism (CRM) and ongoing monitoring activities to an acceptable risk level.Gender-based Violence (GBV): Likely critical impact. MENTOR analyses and addresses any GBV-related risks, report and refer potential GBV cases to the related stakeholders. Referral pathways are regularly updated to facilitate referrals. Staff are obliged to follow up with the referred case. Acceptable risk level.Risk of theft of supplies: Likely critical impact. MENTOR stores all supplies that are needed for IRS and IEC campaign in warehouses that are guarded around the clock. Critical commodities only arrive shortly before the campaign commence to lessen the likelihood of theft. Risk of reactions to IRS insecticides: Likely moderate impact. MENTOR provides a thorough briefing to beneficiaries prior to the start of an IRS campaign to follow a set of specific rules in order to minimise the occurrence of allergic reactions, such as not to enter the house for two hours after the spraying, to not leave any food items in the house and to cover any furniture, cleaning door handles and light switches etc. after the spraying and some more. These are generally followed well and if any allergic reactions occur, these are usually limited to minor rashes. All beneficiaries are informed where they can find immediate medical help, if needed.

7.1 - Please list any risks related to sexual exploitation and abuse that have been identified during the project design phase and any risk mitigation and prevention measures that have been adopted and built into the project

The possible risks for sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) are:1. SEA as an exchange for treatment.2. SEA as an exchange for promises of hiring to work at the NGO. 3. SEA as an exchange for money. All MENTOR staff are briefed on and have to sign the MENTOR Code of Conduct (CoC) and the Safeguarding, Sexual Harassment and Bullying, Whistleblowing, and Child Protection policies. These policies outline the principles under which MENTOR operates, and incorporates the Inter-Agency Standing Committee principles on PSEA. MENTOR enrols all staff on PSEA training and emphasizes refreshing the PSEA training regularly. Through the Complaint Response Mechanism (CRM), MENTOR makes available channels to facilitate reporting of any misconduct of MENTOR staff via a WhatsApp hotline that is advertised in IEC/BCC materials, complaint boxes, and a feedback tool to collect feedback regularly during monitoring activities. As part of our accountability to beneficiaries, MENTOR notifies all communities in which we work that our staff operate under a strict CoC, the standard of behavior that should be expected from our staff, and the confidential reporting mechanisms available to communities and beneficiaries. MENTOR staff are trained to identify sexual violence and report these immediately through their line management to the Country Director for further action. In case of any PSEA reporting, MENTOR applies a strict follow-up method on a high level to address and solve the issue.

8 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Security: Before IRS implementation, MENTOR conducts a security risk assessment (SRA) to avoid any security risks to beneficiaries or staff. MENTOR regularly updates the SRA in the operational area and take the required actions. If the security situation interferes with MENTOR direct access, MENTOR switches to a different area with similar needs. Coordination and duplication: MENTOR coordinates with the WASH Cluster and WASH partners to ensure that there is no duplication in programming. Necessary approvals for services are not secured: MENTOR maintains high coordination levels with the authorities to obtain/renew the permission of implementing the activities. MENTOR signed various Memorandum of Understandings (MoUs) with local authorities across northern Syria. Accountability to affected populations: MENTOR involves the needs of the beneficiaries at the preparation stage by conducting several assessments in the area that target community members, local authorities. During the implementation phase, MENTOR prioritizes the beneficiaries’ feedback received via CRM (WhatsApp hotline, Facebook page disseminated on complained boxes at HFs/MCs, printed IEC/BCC materials, social media), and the interviews with the community during monitoring activities. At the end of each grant, and via the final evaluation, MENTOR comes up with lessons learned to be implemented in future grants. Fraud: MENTOR staff need to sign all logistics policies and refer to manuals such as the Logistics Manual, HR Manual, Financial Manual and the Field Operational Manual where fraud and corruption are explained in detail. MENTOR regularly checks the financial, HR and logistic files from the field to headquarter level.
