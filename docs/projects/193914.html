Project Summary

The proposed intervention seeks to contribute to the integrated protection and promotion of the rights of women and girls with and without disabilities affected by the protracted humanitarian crisis in the oPt. The response will strengthen the protection and resilience of women and girls who are survivors or at risk of Sexual and Gender-Based Violence (SGBV) in Gaza, the West Bank and East Jerusalem. The project proposes 3 axes:
1) Improve access to and strengthen the quality of life-saving, inclusive SRH and multisectoral SGBV services for women and girls affected by/at risk of SGBV.
2) Promote inclusive, women-led community emergency response mechanisms, with a special focus on the prevention, protection and response to SGBV.
3) Support the economic autonomy and resilience of women survivors of SGBV affected by the multiple impacts of the Occupation, the blockade, COVID-19, and the energy and cost of living crises through the provision of MPCA to the most vulnerable individuals (245 women and their HH receiving MPC, 185 women and their HH receiving CVA, 100 women receiving CFW opportunities).

The intervention will directly benefit 35.823 people (32.694 women, 3.955 girls, 135 men and 32 boys), including 350 women with disabilities. More specifically, looking at Result 3, 245 target persons will benefit from MPC; of which, 175 target persons and their HH will benefit from regular MPCA (with transfer value of ILS 1,081 for 5 months) and 70 target persons and their HH will benefit from emergency MPCA (with transfer value of ILS 754 for 3 months). Furthermore, 185 women will benefit from restricted CVA (food and hygiene vouchers for transfer value of ILS239 on average for 5 months; specific sum to be calculated according to HH size), and 100 women receiving CFW opportunities (with transfer value of ILS 1,200 for 3 months). It will be implemented by Alianza por la Solidaridad, Awda, CFTA, PFPPA, other Protection Cluster partners and in close coordination with the Gaza CWG as well as relevant local governmental agencies in a way that supports localised and women-led humanitarian response.

1 - Needs

The situation in the oPt is considered one of the most long-standing protracted crises, characterized by 55 years of Israeli military occupation, lack of respect for IHL and HRL, internal Palestinian political divisions, and recurrent escalations of hostilities between Israel and Palestinian armed groups. The past year has seen yet a further deterioration of the humanitarian situation in the oPt, due to the latest Israeli military invasions of Gaza in May 2021 and August 2022, Israeli military and settler attacks on Palestinian towns and cities in the WB, including East Jerusalem; and the ongoing consequences of the Covid-19 pandemic on already overburdened health and social protection systems. All of this has negatively affected livelihoods and access to essential services, particularly in Gaza, and has been compounded by a reduction in donor funding. The latest MSNA conducted in July 2022 finds that 1.32 million people in Gaza (63% of its total population) are in need of humanitarian assistance, with urgent needs identified in the sectors of protection, food security and health (MSNA 2022). Meanwhile, it identifies the situation in the WB as one of chronic crisis, driven mostly by economic and protection concerns; with the Israeli-controlled part of Hebron Area H2, marginalized locations in Area C, and specific populations groups within East Jerusalem being the main factors for the identified needs and vulnerabilities (MSNA 2022). This situation has a multilevel and differentiated impact on the affected population, degenerating into a complex set of material and social vulnerabilities. Protection concerns in particular are among the most frequently cited across all sectors, with specific population groups, including women, children, with and without disabilities, facing greater risk of experiencing protection threats. A significant percentage of Palestinian households identify specific safety and security concerns for children with disability (38%) and without disability (25%), and women (16%) (MSNA 2022). The GBV Secondary Data Review published in September 2022 confirms these findings. According to the most widely cited study conducted by the Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics, 29% of Palestinian women (38% in Gaza and 24% in the WB) between the age of 18 and 64 had experienced violence within the 12 months preceding the survey; of which, 57% experienced psychological violence, 18% physical violence, 9% sexual violence, 33% social violence, 41% economic violence (PCBS 2019). Resulting key protection needs are exacerbated by the volatile security conditions. The 2018 report of the UN Special Rapporteur on violence against women reported two main reasons for the high level of GBV in Palestine: the occupation and its consequences, and patriarchal norms and values. This has contributed to a behavioral dynamic of more frustrated men, unable to fulfil their expected roles in the traditionally patriarchal society, often reverting to negative coping mechanisms to sustain their livelihood and provide for their families. The high rate of unemployment and lack of economic opportunities are thus interlinked with many of the protection threats. Women and children are disproportionately affected by this vicious cycle of economic instability. The provision of economic support through cash and voucher assistance through different modalities is a type of intervention widely used among several actors in the sector, however findings from a study recently published by APS, “Cash-Based Initiatives on gender dynamics and households in Gaza” (2021), that humanitarian agencies operating in Gaza do not systematically consider protection risks or mitigation measures to such risks as part of the planning and implementation of cash distribution programs in Gaza. It further observed the generally positive impact of cash, as it helps alleviate stress and tension linked to poverty among families in Gaza. However, it also points to the importance of an appropriate cash modality for female recipients upon individual consideration of each case, taking into consideration the individual family structure and potential protection risks that might result in physical or psychological abuse; as well as the need to include male beneficiaries in awareness and educational activities regarding women’s role in and outside of the household. The additional pressures faced due to the recurring violent aggressions and emergency situations are thus a breeding ground for rising numbers of SGBV for women and girls, who are often unable to receive the support they need. SAWA, an emergency helpline telephone, reported a 135% increase in the number of cases in May 2021 compared to the previous year, highlighting the need for remote service provision for those living in isolated areas (HNO 2022). Citing from the GBV Secondary Data Review published as recently as September 2022, also in East Jerusalem, the surge of SGBV cases has been unaddressed and neglected by both Palestinian and Israeli authorities (UNFPA 2021). Women and girls with disabilities encounter additional barriers that limit their access to specialized protection services, exposing them to a situation of additional risk. In Gaza, 58% of people with disabilities lack information about the SGBV prevention and response mechanisms, including how and where to access relevant support services during emergencies (HNO 2022). Apart from that, people with disabilities seeking help must also contend with stigma and lack of awareness about the importance of mental health and wellbeing in Palestine. These figures have been confirmed by a study conducted by APS in 2021 on disability inclusion of GBV service providers in the Gaza Strip, providing recommendations to address the gaps in service delivery and accessibility, as well as information management and data collection systems, in order to integrate a more cross-cutting approach to disability in GBV programming.

5.2 - Please briefly explain why the specific modality/ies for this response were chosen.

Health service providers act as crucial entry point for the detection and referral of SGBV. APS works with local partners not only providing a wide range of SRH services, but also equipped with specialized staff trained in the applicable GBVSC Referral Pathway and Service Directory. Based on the established case management guidelines, women and girl GBV survivors and those at risk, are referred to specialized actors providing quality multi-sectorial services aligned with the minimum standards for prevention and response to GBV. Additionally, regular capacity-building is carried out, to ensure the highest quality in accordance with international standards.Three CVA modalities are contemplated to cover and adapt to the needs of women survivors or at risk of SGBV, all of which will be fully aligned with the recommendations of the Protection Cluster and CWG in Gaza, specifically, 1. MPCA (175 women), 2. Vouchers for food and hygiene items (185 women) and 3. Cash for work (100 women). While all modalities are rooted in the special focus on families with the greatest vulnerability, an exhaustive selection process based on specific criteria will be carried out jointly with the implementing partner to ensure that the respective modality is best adapted to each recipient’s individual protection needs. This process will be facilitated by the case managers through the completion of a questionnaire including targeted questions on the economic/family situation. The answers will be categorized through a pre-defined scoring system, attributing more points to those persons in a situation of greater vulnerability. In general, as all 3 modalities are entailed under the wider umbrella of CVA for Protection, these criteria do not differ broadly, however there are specific questions to evaluate the most appropriate and suitable delivery method for each woman (financial literacy, education level, no. of children/elderly, disability, debt), as well as risk analysis and own preference.

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

Alianza’s Complaints and Response Mechanism Framework Policy and Procedure sets out how any complaints should be dealt with depending on the nature and object of the complaint and how it is received (written, verbal). Minimum standards for dealing with complaints include: 1. All complaints should be acknowledged within 5 working days. All complainants will receive a copy of our policy; 2. All complainants should receive a response giving the outcome of their complaint within 30 working days of receipt. If more complex, a further acknowledgement should be sent explaining what further investigation is required to resolve the complaint and the likely timeline; 3. All complaints should be recorded on a Complaints Record Form; 4. All complaints will be treated respectfully, whether it is felt the complaint is justified or not. The feedback received through the Complaint, Feedback and Response Mechanism (CFRM) will be recorded and categorized into a database in order to process and analyse trends afterwards. The database has to be analysed and a report produced within an agreed timeframe. The categorization of the feedback and complaints is done in collaboration with the local partners directly implementing the project, who will also be the ones informing the beneficiaries about the Feedback and Complaints Mechanism. The findings will be integrated not only in the running implementation of the project, to employ adaptive measures if needed, but also in the final evaluation of the project to better tailor future interventions. Furthermore, accountability sessions will be held with the beneficiaries to discuss feedback and non-sensitive complaints.

9.1d - Explain how you have ensured that mechanism are accessible to all population groups?

The CFRM is designed to reduce barriers to access by offering different means and access points for the target population. It is built in accordance with the affected communities and aims to reflect existing local or traditional communication and dispute resolution mechanisms, while integrating new feedback channels adapted to respond to social groups with limited access to public spaces. Criteria such as gender, age, lack of literacy or disability are taken into account as well as the different physical and social barriers to guarantee equal access to CFRM are addressed. In order to ensure this, the reference for an effective and sensitive mechanism is rooted in the tool guide “Humanitarian inclusion standards for older people and people with disabilities” (ADCAP, 2018). Both the elderly and people with disabilities, along with their representative organisations, will be consulted to establish the basis for a culturally and vulnerability-appropriate collection of feedback. The language used will be understandable and respectful of beneficiaries with low literacy level and the variety of formats will be adapted according to what is required, such as universal or local sign language interpretation, live captioning, easy-to-read format (simple text combined with images), or Braille (if applicable). Furthermore, it will be ensured that written information is easily readable, for example by using large fonts and colour contrast. Physical barriers are and will be targeted and facilities will be adapted to allow beneficiaries with physical impairments to experience complete freedom of movement and access the points where CFRM will be materially located.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

APS has a strong track record in gender-sensitive capacity development interventions and this response has been designed and planned in a gender-sensitive manner, avoiding further damage and suffering as a result of our actions. APS aims to improve accountability towards target persons through a thorough understanding of the context and community dynamics in which projects take place. In compliance with the “do no harm” principle, APS will implement measures to mitigate pre-identified negative effects, before, during and after implementation of activities. The measures and actions will consider the target beneficiaries and people that may be indirectly affected as part of their family and social networks. An important component of this endeavor is community participation and self-assessment of the needs of the local community through a meaningful consultation with communities at the project identification stage. Methods we use include: - Needs assessment; - Focus group discussions - Key informant interviews; - Desk research; - Surveys; - Expert interviews; - Community consultations/focus groups; - Workshops with staff, partners and other relevant actors. The introduction of a Complaint, Feedback and Response Mechanism as well as secure channels to collect and store confidential data are integrated components of APS’ programming. Furthermore, disaggregated data collection according to the most vulnerable categories helps the APS team adapt the intervention and measure the results according to gender, age and additional vulnerability markers. With regard to COVID-related prevention and do-no-harm, APS always ensures that facilities and arrangements are safe for the target group and staff and adhere to all minimum precautionary safety measures, including physical distancing and hygiene promotion, in accordance with local health regulations. All activities are subjected to potential modification in order to adapt to COVID-19 restrictions or other concurrent situations of emergency at any time. Remote services to survivors will be activated as soon as face-to-face is not advisable, and regular need assessments will be conducted to identify emerging needs.With regards to the CVA for Protection, special attention is paid to the eligibility criteria to ensure that the Do no Harm approach is followed and that recipients are under no circumstances exposed to additional risks or hazards. While unrestricted MPC is the preferred modality for CVA for Protection due to its greater flexibility it attributed to the recipients in deciding how to spend/save the received monthly disbursements, it may be decided to opt for the voucher option in cases where the safety of the women may be at risk. Also, it is worth noting that the modalities are not exclusive; rather, complementarity between the different types of CVA is in fact intended in applicable cases, e.g. where vouchers may be used to cover basic needs while the cash for work option gives a parallel option for increased economic autonomy and positive coping strategies. Furthermore, during the orientation sessions and in all dissemination materials, the inclusion of PSEA is an important component, as it introduces the complaints and reporting mechanism for potential cases of PSEA-related incidents, providing women with the needed information about PSEA as well as the hotline number and other relevant reporting channels, as another mechanism to avoid doing harm or exposing the women to additional risk.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

APS’s relationships with affected persons are based on expectation management to ensure a clear understanding of its mandates and scope, together with the explanation that humanitarian assistance is provided to the most vulnerable as a priority. The principle of impartiality, which implies equal distribution of aid among beneficiaries with similar needs, complements the principle of the equity, so that an assistance has to be given in priority to those who need it most. The organization’s overall goal is to build a response targeting the immediate needs with a life-saving purpose and the improvement of local capacity to avoid aid-dependent coping mechanisms that may degenerate into negative side-effects of the interventions. The empowerment of the local populations is ensured through an heterogenous composition of the local staff and the involvement of the beneficiaries in the management of aid delivery. Gender is mainstreamed in all actions and all activities are planned carefully to ensure meaningful access of the most vulnerable groups. GBV services, awareness, self-care sessions, and many other actions are conducted taking into consideration the unpaid and care responsibilities of women to ensure they can take part in them. Moreover, specific attention is paid to the training of the field staff regarding protection mainstreaming (e.g., "# of humanitarian staff (non-protection specialists) who received training in protection mainstreaming"). Furthermore, special considerations are taken to mitigate any potential reputational or stigmatization risk, that may prevent women to engage in the activities. ApS and its partners are all committed to enhance the participation of people with disabilities, either physical or other type of impairments. To ensure an adequate evaluation of the engagement of beneficiaries with disabilities, specific indicators have been developed during the formulation phase (e.g., “# of women and girls - with and without disability - accessing specialized sexual and reproductive health (SRH) service” and “# of humanitarian partner organisations who received specialized training in disability inclusion”). When preparing for each activity, the assessment will be conducted to ensure that any corrective measure can be taken in a timely manner to promote their participation. In addition, the activity cycle will be designed according to the international accessibility standards, with the objective that all processes; from the information and knowledge dissemination about the rights to the provision of services itself; will guarantee that no one is left behind. Among others, the present intervention specifically women and girl survivors of SGBV with and without disability, a group that traditionally face barriers in accessing aid for different reasons. Direct target persons will be reached through the network of hospitals, clinics and centres run by Awda, CFTA, PFPPA and other partners in the Gaza Strip and the West Bank, including East Jerusalem. Health will be used as the entry point for the identification of SGBV cases among women and girls with and without disability, in accordance with the Standard Operating Procedures (SOP) of the partner organizations, as well as the inter-institutional SOPs for the prevention and response to SGBV and the child protection in Gaza. These hospitals, clinics and centers operate on the basis of the humanitarian principles of humanity, neutrality, impartiality and independence, and are open to all persons, regardless of gender, age, ability, race, religion or political beliefs. Given that several of the clinics and centers in question are based in refugee camps in the Gaza Strip, it is anticipated that a significant percentage of the target population will be refugees.
