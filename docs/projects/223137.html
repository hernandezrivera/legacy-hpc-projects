Project Summary

The School Feeding Programme in Cox’s Bazar, targeting both Rohingya refugee camps and the host communities of Ukhiya and Teknaf, provides fortified biscuits to young learners to support their nutritional and educational needs. In the camps, each enrolled child aged 3 to 18 receives a 50-gram packet of fortified biscuits. Meanwhile, students aged 4 to 14 in government and community primary schools in the host communities receive a 75-gram packet, as learners in the camps benefit from a wider range of food and nutrition programmes.
The primary objectives of this programme are to increase enrolment, improve attendance, and reduce dropout rates. By providing fortified biscuits, short-term hunger is addressed, and children receive 16 essential nutrients that contribute to cognitive development. Additionally, these biscuits serve as a useful incentive for regular class attendance, lessening parents' financial burdens for tiffin. The fortified biscuits meet half of the learners' daily energy needs on each school day.
With collaboration from the education sector within the camps and the local government education office, the programme aims to support 326,000 children by 2025.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

Teknaf and Ukhiya, in the Cox’s Bazar region of Bangladesh, are uniquely vulnerable to natural disasters due to both geographical and environmental factors. These subdistricts are regularly affected by tropical cyclones, storm surges, and heavy rainfall, largely because of their coastal location near the Bay of Bengal. The area’s flat, low-lying landscape, combined with its dense population, further amplifies its exposure to these risks. Climate change has intensified this vulnerability, with rising sea levels and warmer sea temperatures contributing to more frequent and severe weather events, which erode livelihoods, damage infrastructure, and drive the population into deeper poverty. The arrival of Rohingya refugees in 2017 has further intensified these challenges, placing added pressure on resources, intensifying job competition, and limiting economic opportunities.In this context, the need for a School Feeding Programme is evident. Students from both the camps and host communities often attend school on empty stomachs, impairing cognitive function and hindering their learning abilities. To address this, fortified biscuits provided by the school feeding programme supply 16 vital micronutrients and meet 71% of daily nutritional requirements essential for cognitive development. This programme aims to reach 285,000 children aged 3 to 18 in the camps and 41,000 children aged 4 to 14 in the host communities, with daily 50-gram and 75-gram biscuit packets respectively. The vulnerability of Rohingya and host communities identified in the Refugee Influx Emergency Vulnerability Assessment (REVA-7, July 2024) Report further justifies the programme. By 2023, 97% of Rohingya households faced moderate to high vulnerability, with 86% classified as highly vulnerable. In host communities, vulnerability rose to 69%. Additionally, economic constraints are notable, with per capita expenditure in Rohingya households at only BDT 1,252 (US$11.3) monthly, compared to BDT 3,131 (US$28.3) in Bangladeshi households. Food constitutes the largest expenditure, consuming 68% of the Rohingya household budget and 63% of the Bangladeshi one. The impact of recent ration cuts (2023) is stark, leaving 70% of Rohingya households with poor or borderline food consumption and 10% suffering from poor diets. The programme therefore plays a crucial role in providing children with reliable nutritional support, which directly contributes to their cognitive growth, physical health, and school performance.Economic hardship has led to a high dependency on food assistance. Without it, 94% of Rohingya households would fall below the Minimum Expenditure Basket (MEB). Financial constraints force many families to adopt coping strategies; in 2023, 67% of Rohingya households resorted to reduced food quality or borrowing, while 56% incurred debt to meet basic needs. A lack of resources affects access to healthcare, with 52% of Rohingya and 49% of Bangladeshi households reporting barriers, primarily due to costs and limited medical supplies. These conditions severely impact children’s health, school attendance, and overall development, reinforcing the necessity of a school feeding that ensures at least one nutritious meal a day.Social factors also contribute to the programme’s necessity. Gender inequality remains a pervasive issue within the Rohingya refugee community. Conservative cultural norms and limited public engagement restrict women’s and girls’ access to education, healthcare, and economic opportunities. Overcrowded camps increase risks of gender-based violence, and inadequate sanitation facilities compromise women’s safety and dignity. Additionally, female-headed households, especially those with three or more children, face higher vulnerability, limiting their ability to provide adequate nutrition. For these families, the programme provides essential support, as it lessens the caregiving burden on mothers and encourages consistent school attendance among children.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

Protection mainstreaming within the School Feeding Programme in the Rohingya refugee and host community contexts is essential for ensuring the safety, dignity, and inclusivity of beneficiaries. This approach includes integrating protective measures that address the unique vulnerabilities of children, adolescents, and other at-risk groups, particularly as 97% of Rohingya and 69% of host community households face moderate to high vulnerability. To safeguard beneficiaries, the programme prioritises culturally sensitive food distribution practices that minimise risks of crowding in learning facilities with known or unknown personnel, which could lead to protection concerns, especially for adolescent girls and children. By involving female staff and Rohingya volunteers in distribution, the programme creates a safer, more comfortable environment, particularly in the conservative Rohingya context where cultural norms often restrict women’s public engagement.Clear, accessible information about the programme is crucial, enabling all learners to understand distribution schedules, nutritional benefits, and their rights to safe and respectful treatment. Feedback channels (CFM) are provided for reporting safety concerns, allowing beneficiaries to voice issues without fear of reprisal. Continuous monitoring and evaluation, with community participation, assess the programme’s impact on protection and ensure it adapts to emerging needs or risks.In the host community, where economic strain and competition for resources are high, protection mainstreaming also involves reducing dependency and promoting a sense of inclusion through consistent support. Capacity-building initiatives engage the community in advocacy and enable participants to take ownership, reducing vulnerability and dependency while enhancing social cohesion. By embedding protection principles into every stage, this programme meets immediate nutritional needs and fosters an environment of respect and safety.

2.3 - Age & Disability

The School Feeding Programme aims to improve nutrition and education outcomes for children aged 3–18 in camps and 4–14 in the host community. The initiative ensures inclusivity by including children with disabilities in biscuit distribution, supporting equal access to nutritional support. The Washington Group Questions (WGQ) will assess the degree of disabilities to facilitate proper identification and tracking. Beneficiary data, including the number of children served, will be submitted monthly to the education sector through the standard 5W reporting template. While the programme strives for inclusivity, it cannot engage persons with disabilities as distribution volunteers due to the physically demanding nature of the role, which involves carrying heavy loads and frequent travel to deliver biscuits to learning centres. Alternative roles may be explored to promote broader participation. This approach aligns with the programme’s commitment to fostering both nutritional well-being and an inclusive educational environment.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

In the challenging context of the Rohingya refugee crisis, ensuring Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) is crucial for the School Feeding Programme’s success. Central to this effort is active community engagement, achieved through regular consultations and feedback sessions, which involve the Rohingya community in key decisions and align the programme with their needs. Effective communication also plays a significant role, as clear, accessible information about the programme's objectives, distribution schedules, and nutritional content helps keep the community informed, especially given their diverse linguistic and educational backgrounds.A robust feedback mechanism further supports AAP by providing a two-way communication channel where Rohingya families can express concerns and offer suggestions. WFP and its partners manage this system, which ensures that the programme can adjust based on community input. Cultural sensitivity and gender considerations are carefully integrated to foster trust and inclusivity, while monitoring and evaluation—conducted with community participation—enable timely, responsive adjustments to the programme.Additionally, capacity-building initiatives empower the Rohingya community to advocate for their own needs and promote a sense of ownership. This includes engaging Rohingya volunteers in distribution processes, which not only enhances efficiency but strengthens community involvement. By adhering to these principles, the School Feeding Programme seeks to provide not only nutritional support but also dignity, respect, and collaboration, ensuring that humanitarian assistance is responsive to the community’s specific preferences and priorities.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

The School Feeding Programme in the Rohingya refugee and host community contexts integrates strong measures for the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA), ensuring the safety and dignity of all beneficiaries, particularly vulnerable adolescent girls and children. Given the crowded camp conditions and heightened risks faced by female-headed households, strict PSEA protocols are embedded into every aspect of the programme. This includes mandatory training for all staff and volunteers on safeguarding standards, ethical conduct, and recognising signs of exploitation, reinforcing a zero-tolerance policy toward any form of abuse. Both WFP and its partner organisations establish clear guidelines to ensure respectful, safe interactions with the community, helping prevent power imbalances that may lead to exploitative situations.Information on PSEA is widely disseminated through accessible channels, considering diverse literacy and language levels in both the camps and host communities. These efforts ensure that all beneficiaries understand their rights and know how to report any form of abuse. Confidential reporting mechanisms, managed independently by both WFP and cooperating partners, enable beneficiaries to voice concerns safely and without fear of retaliation. Feedback channels are also monitored closely to swiftly address any arising issues, ensuring a safe environment for participants. Regular monitoring and community consultations support these initiatives, helping adapt the programme as new needs emerge. By embedding PSEA policy and principles into the School Feeding Programme, WFP fosters a safe, transparent, and respectful environment for beneficiaries, upholding their dignity and rights throughout the programme.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Incorporating climate change adaptation and mitigation measures into the School Feeding Programme enhances its sustainability and resilience. Efforts include integrating disaster risk reduction (DRR) and disaster risk management (DRM) plans and strategies to ensure uninterrupted food distribution during emergencies. Anticipatory actions (AA), such as pre-positioning supplies and risk-informed planning mitigate potential disruptions caused by climate events. Environmentally conscious practices, like collecting empty packets and returning them to warehouses, enabling upcycling initiatives that repurpose waste into useful products. Additionally, promoting awareness of climate change among students and communities fosters long-term adaptive behaviours. These measures strengthen the programme’s capacity to address climate risks while supporting education and nutrition goals.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

The School Feeding Programme integrates a comprehensive business continuity plan and emergency response standard operating procedures (SOPs) to ensure uninterrupted service during crises. Emergency stocks are pre-positioned in coordination with the General Food Assistance Programme, enabling rapid response to unexpected disruptions. Contingency funds are allocated to address urgent needs, such as scaling up food distribution or covering logistical challenges during emergencies. Close communication and collaboration with government authorities are prioritised to align responses, ensure timely approvals, and integrate school feeding into broader national emergency frameworks. These measures strengthen the programme’s resilience, ensuring that nutritional support continues to reach vulnerable children even in times of crisis.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

The School Feeding Programme in the camps provides fortified biscuits to adolescent youth aged 15 to 18, supporting their nutritional needs and enhancing learning outcomes. This support is specifically available to youth who are actively enrolled in learning centres, as there are no other engagement or assistance mechanisms for this age group outside these educational settings. By targeting only those enrolled, the programme encourages consistent attendance, while ensuring that participating adolescents benefit from essential micronutrients to support their cognitive and physical development.
