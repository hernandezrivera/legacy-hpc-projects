Project Summary

The protracted conflict in eastern Ukraine has a devastating impact on the health and well-being of the affected population showing signs of long-term consequences. The conflict has isolated rural health facilities in Government-Controlled Areas (GCA) from major health centers located in urban areas in Non-Government Controlled Areas (NGCA). Of the approximately 3.3 million people living within the 20 kilometres of the contact line, 38 per cent of households in the 20 km area along the ‘contact line’ and 57 per cent of households in the 5 km area along the ‘contact line’ (GCA) reported having problems accessing healthcare and require life-saving and essential health services. The insecurity, lack of maintenance of outdated health facilities and medical equipment, shortage of medicines and medical supplies, understaffing and curtailed access to referral hospitals and pharmacies undermine the health care system’s ability to cope with both new and pre-existing health care needs
The ongoing process of health care reform in Ukraine impacts the access of the population to essential medical services on both sides of the ‘contact line.’ The decentralization of the healthcare system in GCA and the centralization of primary health care in NGCA have the potential to further exacerbate the difficulties to access health care services.
The lack of access to primary, secondary and tertiary health care services particularly affects vulnerable populations such as women, children, elderly, people with disabilities, those living in isolated settlements. This is further compounded by the lack of qualified medical staff and brain drain. Those who remained are confronted with the significant challenges to access quality professional education and continuous training. Health care workers are also exposed to traumatic events and are being overlooked by the health system and humanitarian organizations.
It is difficult to ensure quality management of the healthcare system, since standardized protocols and operational procedures, based on the globally accepted guidelines are not being widely deployed or even tailored to the local context. Patient safety is also a major source for concern, given that little standardization occurs in the management of diseases, infection prevention and control measures, administration of the healthcare facilities. Essential medicines, renewables, equipment, and core medical technologies are also lacking. In 2019, WHO assessed the emergency medical services in eastern Ukraine, and 85 percent of the patients stated that the ambulance service was free, they did however had to pay up to the equivalent of one-month salary (UAH 3,600) for hospital care.
The underdeveloped health information system prevents evidence-based practices for the improvement of the health system’s performance and epidemiologic surveillance is seriously challenged by the security, limited availability of the trained personnel and supplies, difficulties in timely detection of the cases and their laboratory confirmation. This also affects the early warning and response system for public health threats.
To respond to the humanitarian needs, WHO will continue implementing the following key activities, which are based on the strong cooperation with health authorities:
1- Technical advice to health authorities in using evidence-based tools to promote emergency preparedness and readiness, improve quality of healthcare at all levels and improve access of the vulnerable population
2. Support to primary, secondary and tertiary health care facilities through the distribution of medical supplies and equipment, development of capacity building programs and rehabilitation of health infrastructure.
3. Reinforcing technical capacities of health care workers in mental health and psychosocial support.
4. Improving trauma care and emergency medical services based on the result of WHO EMS Survey (WHO 2019)
5. Supporting Public Health system and preparedness mechanism

1 - Needs

The protracted conflict in eastern Ukraine has a devastating impact on the health and well-being of the affected population showings signs of long-term consequences. Of the approximately 3.3 million people living within the 20 kilometres of the contact line, 38 per cent of households in the 20 km area along the ‘contact line’ and 57 per cent of households in the 5 km area along the ‘contact line’ (GCA) reported having problems accessing healthcare and require life-saving and essential health services. The decentralization of the healthcare system in GCA and the centralization of primary health care in NGCA have the potential to further exacerbate the difficulties to access health care services. Hostilities and persisting security threats are barriers for isolated populations to access essential and specialized health care services at primary, secondary and tertiary levels. This lack of access to health care particularly affects vulnerable populations such as women, children, elderly, people with disabilities, those living in isolated settlements. Access to the quality medical services for war-wounded and other trauma patients is limited in GCA and NGCA affecting the most vulnerable populations including women, children, and elderlies. Lack of evidence-based health data, equipment, essential medicines, trained staff, standardized treatment protocols and insufficient infrastructure for trauma care and emergency medical services are especially critical in eastern Ukraine. In 2019, WHO assessed the emergency medical services in eastern Ukraine, and 85 percent of the patients stated that despite the free-of-charge ambulance service, they had to pay up to the equivalent of a one-month salary (UAH 3,600) for hospital care. The underdeveloped health information system prevents evidence-based practices for the improvement of the health system’s performance and epidemiologic surveillance. This affects the early warning and response system for public health threats. Frequent interruptions of vaccine supply during the last five years have negatively impacted childhood vaccination coverage which increases the incidence and risk of communicable diseases. The lack of timely detection, reporting, and response to outbreaks continues to be a burden. In an ongoing measles outbreak since 2017, over 115 000 people in Ukraine have been affected, and 40 people died because of measles. Vaccination against diphtheria remains suboptimal in Ukraine – only 46% of children eligible to receive diphtheria, tetanus, and pertussis (DTP) received all three recommended doses in 2019. Conflict-affected individuals, families, and communities experience an intensified need in the mental health and psycho-social support services, whereas the public medical infrastructure has become more limited. Physiological signs of distress (70%) and emotional difficulties (58%) are most prevalent within 0-20 km of the ‘line of contact. Quality, evidence-based MHPSS at the community level are largely unavailable, which is in part due to the low level of integration of MH in community-based supports (i.e., PHC) with health care providers being trained in early identification and prevention of MHPSS conditions and a low-functioning referral system. People with moderate to severe MHPSS conditions face difficulties in accessing focused-psychosocial support, specialized MH services and medications. TB and HIV/AIDS has been an issue in Donteska and Luhanska even before the conflict. These two oblasts were among the most affected in Ukraine by HIV and TB, especially multidrug-resistant TB. There is a high dependence on international aid for HIV and TB treatment in both GCA and NGCAs. An estimated 35 000 people are living with HIV in NGCAs, but only 16 000 know their status and are followed up in health settings. Each year more than 1500 new TB cases are registered in Donetsk NGCA, of which more than 30 percent of cases are multidrug-resistant TB.
