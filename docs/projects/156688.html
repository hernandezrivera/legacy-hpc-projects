Project Summary

Medair’s proposed action will provide life-saving primary health care with integrated nutrition at facility and household-levels to 214,840 target beneficiaries (excluding overlap), building on the experience, expertise and relationships from its previous project cycles to reach the most vulnerable and in-need, and with protection mainstreaming and sustainability at the core of its programme. Medair will focus on rehabilitating and reequipping primary healthcare facilities in areas with high needs, prioritizing hard to reach and newly accessible areas, such as south Syria, Deir-Ez-Zor (DEZ) and north rural Homs, and possibly respond to Idlib displacement. Following infrastructure rehabilitation, a 12 month programme of monitoring visits and capacity building training for staff will maximise the quality of service provision. Key health, hygiene and nutrition behaviours will be improved through targeted messaging at household level in the clinic catchment population. MHPSS services, nutrition screening and treatment, healthcare for people living with disabilities and initial support of GBV cases will be integrated into the package of services provided by the PHC facility.

Needs

Health systems have been heavily damaged by the conflict resulting in a severe lack of access to quality primary healthcare. Of the primary healthcare (PHC) facilities functioning in Syria before the conflict; 48% are non-functioning or partially functioning (HeRAMs, 2018). Medair assessed 27 facilities in 2018, of which only 11 were partially functioning with an average of only 26% of key clinic services provided at those. While various actors rehabilitate clinic infrastructure few provide equipment or support for services to resume. Rehabilitation of a facility takes on average four months which further delays access to primary healthcare. Shortages of essential drugs are widespread: none of the clinics assessed by Medair had more than 5 of the 10 essential tracer drugs present. The most common morbidities in children under 5 (CU5) are diarrhoea and acute respiratory infections (Medair Clinic Data, 2018). Vaccine coverage remains low, particularly in Deir-ez-Zor and Raqqa, where estimated DPT3 coverage is 10-20% (Draft HNO 2019). 22,656 cases of measles and rubella were reported during 2018 (EWARN, 2018). ). Polio re-emerged as a health risk during 2017 with 74 cases in Deir-ez-Zor, Homs and Raqqa. Poor water and sanitation access increases the risk of outbreaks of waterborne diseases such as Hepatitis A, Hepatitis E, typhoid and dysentery. Continued population movement and the economic situation of many families create an ongoing risk of acute malnutrition. In Dar’a, a GAM rate of 7.8% in children U5, and 11.5% in PLWs was found (SMART Survey Aug 2017). IYCF practices are poor: exclusive breastfeeding rates were only 25.8% (2016 SMART survey). Access to reproductive healthcare is poor due to lack of functioning health care facilities and specialised staff. Only 44% of women of childbearing age use a modern contraceptive (UNFPA, 2018). ANC and PNC uptake is low. GBV cases are likely to be grossly under reported due to high levels of stigma and mandatory reporting. Community health workers (CHWs) and clinic staff report intimate partner violence is common. Child marriage is widespread. Clinics are poorly equipped to support survivors of GBV due to restrictions on GBV training and treatment. A recent survey showed an average of 14% of population in Idlib, Aleppo and Raqqa reporting a high level of anxiety or depression symptoms (HNO, 2019). There is a severe lack of specialist mental health staff (0.41 psychiatrists per 100,000 population, WHO 2018). The prevalence of mobility related disabilities was found to be as high as 29% in Aleppo and 22% in Idlib (PRDWG survey, 2018).Half of these cases were a direct result of the conflict. Of all people with disabilities surveyed, over 60% stated that the impact of their disability was exacerbated by the crisis with lack of access to healthcare, including a lack of assistive devices, given as the most common reason.

Implementation of activities

Clinic Rehab: Medair will rehabilitate and re-equip 8 PHC facilities and re-equip a further 2 PHC facilities to ensure the delivery of the Syria BPHS with a focus on maternal and child health (MCH) with integrated nutrition services. Priority will be given to clinics in areas with high needs. Beneficiaries will include host, IDP and returnees; however, Medair is unable to disaggregate returnee data as t his data is included in the numbers for host communities. Training: Medair will implement a training curriculum for staff involved in both the facility-based and community programs. Training topics will focus on meeting key gaps in the current health system: RH (ANC, PNC, and family planning), GBV, IMCI, EPI, EWARS, PFA, mhGAP, disability inclusion, nutrition.PHC Service Support: Interventions will prioritise the strengthening of vital curative and preventative PHC services with a focus on MCH and existing gaps like MHPSS provision. Outpatient curative consultations will be provided for all WGBM, with special focus on vulnerable groups including CU5, PLW, the elderly, people with MHPSS needs and people living with mobility impairment (PLWMI). Initial management of medical, surgical and obstetric emergencies will be provided. Cases requiring secondary care will be directed appropriately. Comprehensive RH including ANC/PNC, family planning, STI treatment and initial management of GBV cases (including PFA and CMR if permitted) will be provided. Screening, diagnosis and management of NCDs will be provided. EPI services will be strengthened. EWARS notifiable cases will be identified and reported to MoH/WHO as appropriate. Protection measures will be taken at each clinic to ensure appropriate privacy for patients and secure access for PLWMI.Mobile Medical Team: To allow for early resumption of PHC services prior to PHC rehab completion, Medair proposes to pilot deploying a Mobile Medical Team (MMT) to a selected clinic intervention area until the clinic rehabilitation is finalised. Selection for this location will be as per clinic rehab selection but prioritised due to lack of access to alternative healthcare providers. A doctor, nurse and pharmacist will triage patients, provide emergency treatment and essential PHC consultations from a community building, container or mobile unit. Appropriate referrals will be made to secondary healthcare facilities. Prescribed medication will be dispensed.Community Health: A community health programme is implemented in every Medair clinic catchment area receiving the training package. CHWs trained by Medair will provide health education (e.g. IYCF, vaccination, RH, GBV awareness) through household visits – sharing specifically targeted messages per HH. The aim will be to improve key health, nutrition and hygiene behaviours and ultimately improve the health status of all HH members.Nutrition: Integrated nutrition activities will be implemented at facility and community levels including screening for CU5 and PLW and IYCF promotion. At PHC facilities, outpatient treatment will be provided for CU5 with SAM and MAM as well as micronutrient supplementation to all PLWs. Any children identified with SAM and medical complications will be referred to appropriate facilities.

Protection Risk Analysis

General• Staff are not aware of protection issues and do not develop programmes to mitigate risks: Internal training undertaken across all staff (male and female) to ensure awareness of protection issues and to improve the project team’s ability to identify and mitigate protection risks. Beneficiary Selection• Aid diversion: Mitigate by ensuring local context well understood and all stakeholders mapped in assessment. Establish strong links with authorities and operators; joint planning and written agreements on intended beneficiaries.• Health facilities are perceived to be inaccessible to particular ethnic groups due to location and/or staff ethnic background: Ensure context well understood prior to clinic selection. Clinic staff to be trained on humanitarian principles if potential issues identified.Gender• Inequity of access to health facilities between men and women: Awareness of wider community context e.g. transport, power availability, main market/shop times. Ensure health facilities and service providers available at times that suit women as well as men.• Head of house reluctant to accept community health initiatives: CHWs are predominantly female (85%) to achieve acceptance for HH visits. CHWs receive training on communication skills.• GBV towards community volunteers or risk of accusations against volunteers: Always make HH visits in pairs, where possible a team of male and female CHWs• GBV survivors may be exposed to family/community punishment due to lack of social and legal support and lack of confidentially during case management. GBV survivors may feel increasingly guilty because of wrong approach of staff involved in case management – increased risk of PTSD and even suicide: Use tools for confidential reporting and ensuring quality of services –confidentiality must be a priority. All staff trained on PFA and support for GBV survivors.• Reluctance of community leaders to accept awareness-raising and empowerment activities, may accept GBV as social norm: Involve influential parties within the communities from the start of GBV programmes. Clearly communicate issues and encourage community contribution. Support social structures.• Inequity of access to health services between boys and girls: Training for clinic staff and CHWs. Monitor data for adverse trends. HH visit and clinic awareness-raising of importance of access for all children.• Head of households reluctant or PLWs unwilling to be screened or access services: CHWs are predominantly female (85%) to achieve acceptance for HH visits. CHWs receive training to effectively communicate with Head of house. Age• Children at risk from accidents during building works: Explanation in advance to beneficiary communities; Contractual requirements for tidy, safe work sites. Medair employed project specific site engineer to oversee contractors and suppliers day by day. Spot checks by Medair project staff.• Inequity of access by the elderly: Clinic infrastructure rehab design ensures physical access e.g. ramps, handles, doorways.MHPSS• Inequity of access by people with MHPSS needs: Training for health facility workers and CHWs.• Stigmatisation around members of families who have MHPSS needs: CHWs build trust with communities and families and have been trained to sensitively engage and contribute to inclusion of the disabled.Disability• Inequity of access by people with disabilities: Training for health workers and CHWs. Clinic infrastructure rehab design takes account of physical access for the disabled e.g. ramps, handles, doorways.• Further stigmatization of people with MHPSS needs or families with disabled family members once those family members access services because of prevalent cultural perceptions: Include wider community in inclusion training/awareness raising activities. Ensuring quality of services –confidentiality must be a priority.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

ASSUMPTIONS- No significant change to the political and security situation inside Syria and the GoS continue to hold control over recently-retaken governorates- No excessive fluctuations affecting value of the Syrian Pound and any inflation/deflation will be able to be offset against hard currency (USD)- Acceptance and participation of authorities, local leaders and communities during interventions- Technical Agreements and/or Memorandums of Understanding (MoU) with PHCs and other relevant actors are adhered to- Equipment in specifications listed by Ministries will be available at the time of procurement- No major deterioration in logistical conditions, supply chain for essential supplies remains reliable, necessary goods approved for transport- MoH approves Medair training plans, allows Medair to conduct assessments - MoH provides timely information/approvals on PHC facilities in need of rehabilitation; needs can be triangulated by MedairRISKS- Temporary suspension of programme if insecurity status is too high, delaying project delivery- Medair cannot secure approvals from ministries within the timeline outlined, delaying project delivery- Pharmaceutical procurement is delayed, due to e.g. difficulties in obtaining quality assurance assessments, and this delays MMT implementation- Medair cannot secure appropriate permissions to transport pharmaceutical products to MMT locations, delaying implementation- Medair MMT staff are not permitted to travel to MMT locations, delaying implementation- Central Bank imposes regulations which constrain Medair’s ability to maintain adequate cash flow to pay programme expenses- Medair staff or contractors do not comply sufficiently with procurement guidelines leading to a loss of financial resource. MITIGATING MEASURES:- Conduct robust, regular context analysis as part of security management- Keep security planning up to date and train a crisis management team for each location - Beneficiaries to be well informed of the level of support Medair provides to manage expectations- Daily, careful monitoring of security situation including close liaison with local stakeholders on security issues - Remain flexible in modality of delivery, i.e. if setting up MMT not feasible within project timeframe due to approval delays, reallocate resources to allow rehabilitation of an additional PHC - Proactive coordination with Ministries, SARC, other agencies and UN to avoid duplication- Maintain good contact with SARC, Ministries, local authorities, communities, UN agencies and other stakeholders- Balance activities for IDPs, returnees and host population to avoid unnecessary tensions, noting that lead factor for beneficiary selection is vulnerability not status- Complete training for all new staff on procurement guidelines, systematic internal and external audits, spot checks for any fraudulent activity.
