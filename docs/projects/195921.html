Project Summary

Standing up For Safe and Protective Education in South Sudan is focusing on improving access to and quality of safe and protective education services for 10,000 emergency-affected IDPs, returnees, and vulnerable host community children in Unity and Western Equatoria states. In response to the drastic lack of sufficient education across these states, our project will focus on ensuring that all children, including children living with disabilities, have a safe place to learn, access to proper sanitation and hygiene, and increased overall wellbeing. We will strengthen accessibility for particularly marginalised groups of children, such as girls and CWDs, through the provision of specialised support like menstrual hygiene management kits, assistive devices, and adapted learning materials. AET will work with local teachers and train them on education in emergencies pedagogical skills and child-centered methodologies, psychosocial support, and wider school governance. To help with present and future cohorts of children and to respond to the increasing protection concerns in the community, we will strengthen school and community level referral mechanisms, which will include appointing child protection focal points at school. We will leverage our 25-year history in-country, our localised approach to strengthen community buy-in and ownership of the project, as well as our vast education and protection expertise.
The intervention would be guided by State Ministries of Education and the community through engagement and participation and would seek to build on already developed programmes and materials and infuse AET experience and expertise in rolling out programmes that would be of quality and bene fit to the marginalized learners in the target regions. AET will seek to build partnerships with other implementing partners and EiE cluster members working in the target regions to ensure project activities are not duplicated and to share lessons learnt during implementation. AET will be able to leverage its learnings, experience and relations from the Girls Education South Sudan (GESS) and the Global Partnership for Education Learning Spaces project in partnership with UNICEF. Through these programmes AET already has current information on the communities that are in need of TLS’s and WASH facilities and will coordinate activities in line with these programmes. Networking through social media and face to face meetings will also be exploited to maximize eficiency, minimize the risk of duplication and reduce program transaction costs

1 - Needs

Out of an estimated population of 12.1 million, around 8.9 million people in South Sudan are currently in need of humanitarian assistance. There are an estimated 2.8 million children out-of-school children across the country and according to the UNESCO Institute for Statistics, South Sudan has the highest rate of primary school-aged children out of school in the world: a staggering 62%. The closure of schools due to Covid-19 lasted 14 months and resulted in an additional nearly 2 million children having their learning severely interrupted. The most marginalized children and youth, including girls and Children with Disabilities (CwDs), face multiple barriers to accessing education—which were only exacerbated by the coronavirus pandemic. The 2021 Education Needs Assessment (ENA) found that young mothers and married learners were the groups least likely to participate in education activities (74% and 72%, respectively), as well as children with disabilities (72%). The lack of enrolment and retention can be attributed to both psychosocial and physical barriers. For instance, traditional gender roles are rigid in South Sudan and practices such as early and forced marriages, coupled with economic pressures (in a country where 92% of the population live in multidimensional poverty) means families de-value the importance of education for their daughters and instead prefer their children to get married to relieve household economic stress through the ‘bride price’ payments. In fact, 52% of South Sudanese girls are married before age 18—which significantly decreases the potential of them finishing school. The expected years of schooling for girls is shockingly low too: just 3.5. If girls do enrol in school, a lack of MHM supplies, dignity kits, and gender-sensitive washrooms discourages them from continuing to attend. Children living with disabilities also face myriad challenges in accessing and staying in school. Social stigmas and lack of awareness around disability, a lack of teachers trained in working with CWDs, and a lack of adaptive learning materials discourage them from attending. Not to mention the fact that the vast majority of schools are inaccessible. Widespread insecurity is also a significant barrier to education and active conflicts in Lakes, Unity and WES have forced schools to close. The 2021 ENA found that insecurity in and around schools was a significant reason for schools to close, student absenteeism and drop-out. For those that are in school, poor school infrastructure, insufficient learning resources and a lack of trained teachers are compromising the quality of education. SAMS data shows that the highest proportion of schools (23.1%) have classes under trees/open air/ or a tent structure. These schools are easily damaged by wind and flooding and lessons are frequently cancelled due to rain and poor weather. For this reason school rehabilitation was cited as the priority intervention to re-open non-functional schools in the 2021 ENA. A recent assessment of WASH facilities conducted by GESS in 2021 found that of those assessed, only 60% have toilets and half of these have separate facilities for girls. One consequence of the school closures due to Covid-19 is that learners were unable to access schools as sites for protection interventions and mental health support, during a time when they faced additional protection challenges. Of the schools assessed in the ENA over 80% reported a rise in child protection cases during these closures and many reported sharp increases in the number of girls dropping out due to early pregnancy. Although schools can be a site for protection interventions, over a third of schools assessed in the ENA reported there being no functioning referral mechanism in place in their school or community.

2 - Project activities

This project will address the existing needs in Western Equatoria and Unity, by implementing a cross-sectoral intervention that targets the most marginalised children. We have prioritised these 2 states given the high level of need in the education sector that has occurred in these areas as a result of flooding, ongoing insecurity and continued displacement of people - in WES it is believed that over 67% of primary school aged children were out of school before the COVID-19 related school closures and in both states the gender disparity in both primary and secondary education is shocking. In order to address these issues we will undertake the following project activities: Objective 1: Improve equitable access to safe and protective education services for 866,000 emergency affected IDPs, returnees, spontaneous refugees, and host community children including children with disabilities and youth (girls and boys). Objective 1 activities: 1.1: Construction of temporary learning spaces and classrooms in line with Inter-INEE's Minimum Standards for Education in Emergencies and Standards; 1.2: Rehabilitation of temporary learning spaces and classrooms; 1.3 Construction of gender segregated latrines and water points; 1.4: Rehabilitation of gender segregated latrines and water points; 1.5: Distribution of sanitation and hygiene materials (hand washing soap, sanitizers, masks, etc.) and PPE for students and teachers; 1.6: Conduct community sensitization sessions and strengthen existing community structures so the community are sensitized on children's rights and the importance of education for all. Objective 2: Improve the quality of gender and disability-inclusive education services and teachers' capacity to protect the wellbeing of all children and prevent the negative impact of the emergencies on learning environments. Objective 2 activities: 2.1: Provision of quality education recreational materials for emergency-affected children; 2.2: Provision of teaching and learning materials for ECD, primary, and secondary school education; 2.3: Training of teachers on EiE pedagogical skills and child-centred methodologies; 2.4: Training of teachers and learners on psychological first aid (PFA), available psychosocial support services, child protection (CP), and existing referral mechanisms that are available to the community including for CP/GBV, health and nutrition; 2.5: Strengthen school and community child protection referral mechanisms through the provision of resources, training of focal points at a community and school level on how to identify, refer and respond to CP incidences and training Community Protection Committees; 2.6: Provision of life-skills and basic vocational training to youth and adolescents (14-17 years); 2.7: Provision of specialised support for particularly marginalised groups of children, such as girls and CWDs, in order to ensure they can access an education (such as provision of MHM kits, assistive devices, adapted learning materials etc). Objective 3: Strengthen the education system’s response capacity of the school community and national/local education actors to mitigate the impact of existing emergencies on learners, teachers, and communities. Objective 3 activities: 3.1: Train SMCs/PTAs and education authorities on school management, governance, psychosocial support (PSS) and Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR)/ climate change; 3.2: Support prevention and mitigation of communicable diseases through training school communities by providing appropriate Information and Education and Communication (IEC) materials (i.e. posters, leaflets); 3.3: Support conducting back to learning campaigns; 3.4: Support technical and institutional capacities of national actors, including finance, procurement, HR, MEAL, governance and other areas.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

This project aims to target the most marginalized members of society and will therefore ensure that they are integrated at all levels of the project cycle. Through establishing a variety of mixed feedback mechanisms, such as feedback boxes and help-desks, we have ensured that they are accessible for all. Feedback boxes offer anonymity, while help desks provide face-to-face places to share input. Help desks are particularly important for those individuals who are not literate as they can verbally share their opinions with our staff at-hand to transcribe without any prejudice or bias. Programme staff will raise awareness about the feedback mechanisms with programme participants to ensure the community is aware of ways to communicate with AET and CHIDDO. We will also hold quarterly feedback meetings in places within the community that are accessible to all so as not to exclude any members. Locations and times of meetings will be selected in coordination with some of the most marginalised and vulnerable members of society to ensure representation and attendance. Additionally, through engaging with a selection of local stakeholders, such as religious leaders and village elders, we will ensure that we mobilise representatives from all members of society. We will work with local stakeholders to ensure that they specifically focus on mobilising the most vulnerable members of society. A key aspect of the feedback sessions will be the importance of inclusion and therefore we will focus on sensitising the community on the ensuring that all groups are included in all project-related activities.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

This project has been designed for and by the affected population to ensure it reflects their needs. This was possible due to AET, MRDA and CHIDDO's strong field presence in the counties across 2 states and their longstanding relationships with the communities, which has allowed us to engage the affected population during project design and will continue to do during all stages of the project cycle. We have held initial community engagement sessions that helped us to not only identify the current needs in the community, but also allowed us to discuss potential solutions that the affected population felt would be appropriate to address their needs. During this needs assessment we specifically sought the views of children who are particularly marginalised, such as girls, young mothers and children living with disabilities (CLwDs). Throughout the project we will continue to provide information, collect, analyse, and respond to feedback and complaints from a selection of beneficiaries. This will involve holding quarterly feedback sessions with children, teachers, caregivers, community members and key stakeholders. This feedback will then be analysed by the M&E team, responded to within 1 month and where necessary or viable incorporated into our programme. An example of this is when we received feedback about out-of-school children struggling to remain in school after reintegrating, we established remedial classes to help them catch up on lost learnings. Alongside hosting these quarterly feedback sessions, we will also erect anonymous suggestion boxes at schools and within the community and establish help desks within the community where we can collect feedback on a daily basis. This feedback is submitted to the field offices and then processed by the M&E team at a national level. Again, feedback is given within 1 month of the feedback received. By offering multiple feedback methods that are accessible to all we ensure accountability to the affected population at all stages of the project cycle.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

AET uses all available opportunities to incorporate awareness and prevention of harm and SEA into its programmes, including staff and partner training, advocacy, and programme activities with affected populations and key stakeholders. Child friendly versions of AET Safeguarding Policy and procedures are made available in all schools in which it works, and teachers will have information and resources to hand for children and adolescents accessing our services. In addition, our accessible feedback mechanisms allow reports of exploitation and abuse to be made. These mechanisms are constantly available, visible, and understood at project sites. Based on feedback from the community and recent reports, as a top priority AET will ensure referral pathways are up to date so that all school and community resources are accessible, with relevant help line numbers provided where possible. Additionally, AET, MRDA and CHIDDO have robust safeguarding policies and practices and arecommitted to the Inter-agency Standing Committee Task Force’s six core principles relating to sexual exploitation and abuse. Safeguarding considerations are integrated into staff training and recruitment. Recruitment interviews include a question on safeguarding, a letter of good conduct is sourced at reference stage and when staff sign their contracts, they are required to sign a commitment to our global safeguarding policy and our child protection policy. To ensure staff’s knowledge surrounding safeguarding is up to date, we offer safeguarding training to all new staff members and then deliver annual refresher training to all staff. This trainingfocuses on the different types of safeguarding incidences, the reporting mechanisms in place, and the available services for the survivors. All staff are aware of the process that needs to be undertaken when raising any issues to the safeguarding board. All staff are required to adhere to the global child protection policy to ensure that any forms of maltreatment are reported to the appropriate authorities within the given locality.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Throughout the project we will organise quarterly feedback sessions and establish various referral mechanisms that are accessible to all so that we can collect feedback throughout the duration of this project and respond to changing needs as they arise. This feedback will be submitted to the field teams and then analysed by the relevant programmes and M&E teams. Any adaptations that are needed will be identified and proposed to the affected populations in a consultative way so that we can ensure that the project continues to meet the needs of the affected population and that appropriate solutions to these problems are identified. Feedback will be provided to respondents within 1 month of receiving the feedback.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

AET will work closely with other actors, local government authorities, local leaders and human rights organizations to guard against sexual exploitation of women and girls (8.2). All the project staff, teachers and CECs will undergo safeguarding and PSEA training so they are able to prevent, respond to and refer any cases that they may identify. Referral mechanisms will be established and existing systems will be strengthened so that girls and women are aware of how they can report any incidences that may occur. A child protection committee will be formed and a focal point person will be appointed at each school so they can monitor and follow up on any issues that may be identified during this project (8.4). Additionally, education is key to reduce gendered forms of abuse, violence and exploitation. We will therefore incorporate sessions during which we explore this topic in the curriculum with children so they are aware of their rights. Alongside this, we will host community sensitisation sessions with key community members so that they are informed about children's rights and are able to identify, respond to and refer any forms of abuse that may occur in the community. The project will involve all targeted communities in project implementation right from the onset (8.5). Community education committees and local leaders will be involved in mobilizing all the target communities. During co-creation and project start-up phase, consultation meetings will be held with stakeholders to ensure the views of minority groups feed into the design. Project review meetings will be held quarterly in the course of project implementation to ensure continuous collection of feedback from all stakeholders. This will ensure that all minority clans/ethnic groups are included in our project. AET will seek the guidance of the communities in developing activity work plans to ensure communities are aware of and take responsibility of the process while ensuring prudent utilization of the available resources. The community will be part of the implementing team and will be charged with the responsibility of developing accountability mechanisms for the resources through consultation with AET and steering committees. Additionally, we will develop a selection criteria tool that will help us to ensure that the most marginalised groups of beneficiaries are included in all aspects of the project cycle. Finally, in order to reduce the interference with beneficiary selection we will establish a strict selection criteria based on the target beneficiaries for this project (8.8). This will help to prevent external interference and will ensure that beneficiaries are selected on a needs basis. This needs assessment has also informed the disaggregated list of beneficiaries that we plan to target, which will guide our beneficiary selection process and reduce the chances of conflict occurring.
