Project Summary

The project aims to enhance the self-reliance of vulnerable communities in Eastern Equatoria State. This is achieved, on the one hand, by increasing their household income and food production and, on the other hand, by facilitating access to drinking water and water for agricultural use. The project thus contributes to removing the constant threat of food and nutrition insecurity.
The project supports farming families to increase their agricultural production in a sustainable way. Caretakers of malnourished children are supported to create kitchen gardens for vegetable production. The project also focuses on the creation of other income generating activities (promotion of fair and inclusive value chains). Furthermore, the project improves access to drinking water and water for agricultural use. It also supports various schools to improve their sanitation facilities and to increase the knowledge about hygiene and environmental protection through the innovative Blue School approach.

1 - Needs

People affected by poverty such as the great majority of people in South Sudan live under precarious circumstances and are particularly vulnerable to shifts and shocks; even small negative changes in their living situation (for example due to natural hazards, climate change or conflicts) have massive effects on their livelihood capacity. The CaCH assessments carried out in the project area showed that the level of resilience of the population is extremely low. The drought in 2018 consumed all financial resources or crop reserves the households (HHs) might have had, forcing them to employ severe coping strategies such as the displacement to refugee camps in Uganda. With an estimated population of 1.097.900 in Eastern Equatoria, it is estiamted that 37.9% will be fall under IPC pahse 3 or worse in the period from December 2020 unti March 2021. TFrom April to July 2021, the food security situation is expected to deteriorate as the lean season progresses, with an estimated 507,000 people (46.2% of the population) likely to be in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity, of which 367,000 people are likely to be in Crisis (IPC Phase 3), and 140,000 people are likely to be in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. During this period, Ikwoto County will be classified in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) acute food insecurity. Food insecurity is expected to be severe among households whose food stocks will have run out and they will be relying on markets for food pruchases. Ikwoto County has 101.982 inhabitants of whom about 5% (approx. 5.099) live under IPC 4 conditions and 25% (approx. 25.496) under IPC 3 conditions . According to reports from farmers in Ikotos County as well as the CAD, no good harvest is expected in 2020 because too much precipitation has fallen, and many farming areas have been flooded and the agricultural crops have not been given good growth opportunities. In addition, high relative humidity coupled with high temperatures offer potentially ideal propagation conditions for the desert locusts and it can be assumed that this threat will also remain real in 2021. The project at hand proposes to reduce the high dependency on external aid by addressing the challenges that communities face to recover their livelihoods. This shall be achieved by enabling the population to have access to safe and affordable water, improving health and hygiene, supporting farmers to increase their agricultural productivity, better post-harvest management, encouraging individual community members to create their own business, creating linkages between farmers and/or business holders with the market, and, last but not least, creating a healthy learning environment for children. Hygiene, safe water, water for agricultural purposes, nutrition and food storage techniques are considered alongside food availability and access to income. It has been generally recognized that investing in a climate-resilient agriculture and access to water is an important step towards building resilient livelihoods. CaCH recognizes the inherent independency between the thematic areas; the different fields of activity shall not be considered as separate activity, but will be planned and implemented in an integrated manner. Only a society with sufficient and safe access to water for drinking will be able to use water for other productive use, including for agricultural activities. Resilience is seen as the greater concept linking all activities within the project. Through this integrated response, all aspects contributing to increasing resilience among the general population and among children will be addressed. Since children are the leaders of tomorrow and the key drivers of change of the future, CaCH considers it important to specifically address children in the intervention.

2 - Project activities

The project of Caritas Switzerland includes the following activities: Outcome 1: Enhance agricultural production and reduce post-harvest losses among farmers, mothers / male caregivers of malnourished children and in schools: Training for (lead) farmers in improved farming methods and post-harvest storage; Creation of farmer associations and 50 farmer field schools with demonstration plots; Distribution of seeds and tools to 1500 farmers; Construction of improved granaries for farmers and post-harvest storage infrastructure for lead farmers; Installation of 30 village nursery beds for vegetable production and facilitation to set up kitchen gardens; Purchase, distribution and planting of seedlings for nursery beds; Training in agro-ecological farming practices, improved seed storage systems and post-harvest handling; Training in nutrition, food preparation, grey water usage, natural resource management and food preservation for mothers/caregivers (including cooking demonstrations); Establish school gardens in all 14 schools and activate school agriculture clubs; Distribution of seeds and agriculture kits, fencing for school gardens; Training of students and teachers in agro-ecological farming methods, nutrition, food preparation, land management, etc.; Construct 3 food storages according to the guidelines of the government; Outcome 2: Improved product value chains and increased income for farmers, mothers and small-scale businesses: Facilitate local farmer shops/cooperatives in 3 locations; Encourage creation of VSLAs and motivate mothers/farmers to become a member; Provide seeds and tools to farmers and mothers/caregivers through private farmers- and spare parts shops; Organize seed fairs; Create linkages between farmer associations/lead farmers with local traders, agro-dealers and wholesalers; Provide vouchers/cash grants to 150 HHs for alternative income activities and provide professional skill training; Outcome 3: Sustainable and affordable access to safe water for consumption and for productive use among farming communities: Rehabilitation of 10 boreholes and drilling of one new borehole; Installing of rain/surface water harvesting schemes, irrigation systems; Construction of solar-run units and solar system for agro-purposes/livestock; Training of village pump technicians, county water technicians and Water Management Committee (WMC) members in O&M (with the Integrity Management Toolbox); Rehabilitate school WASH infrastructure (latrines, hand washing facilities, water storage tanks) and distribution of WASH material for schools (soap, cleaning items etc.); Install drip irrigation systems, rainwater harvesting and water filters in schools; Distribute seedlings for fruit and pine trees.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

To ensure gender mainstreaming, avoid reinforcing or perpetuating gender inequalities and increase protection against PSEA, the gender dimension is taken into account at every stage of the project. Prior to the start of the activities, meetings were be held with the communities in which the representatives of all population groups were asked to participate (elders, PLWD, women, youth, minorities). The project team identified groups that could face difficulties in relation to their participation in the project or access to the project outcomes and put in place measures to ensure that these disadvantaged groups can have continuous access to the project benefits without facing additional barriers. CACH rolled out its new Code of Conduct in November 2020 including an extended and updated chapter on PSEA. All CACH staff in South Sudan received training in the new Code of Conduct and was specifically sensitized on the issue of PSEA. If required, any staff member can provide information on CACH's PSEA policy.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

The FSL activities targets 1500 farmers (815 females, 686 male) and 1000 mothers/caretakers (901 females, 99 males) and thus, feedback from women is always received. The majority is not literate. Some school related project activities address specifically the needs of adolecent girls who will also be encouraged to provide regular feedback. CACH places particular emphasis to a diversified composition of Water Management Committees and School Management Committees in order to cover as broad a spectrum of the target group as possible and take the most divers feedback into account.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Community involvement and participation are incorporated into all components of the project. Prior to the start of the activities, the team informed the village chiefs and HHs about the project objectives, the beneficiary registration and other activities. MoU`s were signed with each boma stipulating the tasks and responsibilities of all involved actors. Community members themselves can decide whether they wish to participate in the farming and kitchen garden activities, if they fulfil the selection criteria. The farming HHs and kitchen garden groups selected a representative to serve as lead farmers and lead mothers who act as direct link with the project team. The representatives of the farming HHs and kitchen garden groups participate in the monitoring activities of the project staff in order to understand the issues of concern and the challenges that arise during the implementation. Feedback sessions are conducted with the group representatives and other members of the communities. Regular monitoring visits in the villages and information sessions held for the villagers give them the opportunity to ask questions and directly contact the project management. The observations will be discussed either individually with the beneficiaries or in group discussions, together with the respective group representatives. The formation of WMCs and SMCs further strenghten direct participation of beneficiaries and create an increased sense for ownership which in turn leads to an increased involvement of the target groups and provide the basis for beneficiary feedback, complaints and/or encouragments.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Feedback sessions are conducted with the group representatives and other members of the communities. Regular monitoring visits in the villages and information sessions held for the villagers will give them the opportunity to ask questions and directly contact the project management. Observations are discussed either individually with the beneficiaries or in group discussions, together with the respective group representatives. Regular steering committee meetings are organised, to which representatives of the beneficiaries are also invited. These meetings offer beneficiaries the opportunity to express feedback of the project measures. The steering committee, consisting of the CACH project management team, representatives of the state and county line ministries and the donor, can thus react immediately to feedback and/or complaints and take countermeasures to adapt project assistance if necessary.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

8.4 The project adopts a ‘do-no-harm’ strategy to assess protection risks as a result of the project interventions. FGDs with women/girls are conducted to make sure that basic protection related information is shared and available. During the FGDs, women are encouraged to act as multipliers and further share the information with other community members. As a minimum requirement for mainstreaming gender (and age) to achieve improved gender and age sensitivity, CACH demonstrates self-assessment using the Gender and Age Marker Approach (GAM). Further, it is critical to understand the basics of local power dynamics, how they can contribute to violence, what can be done to minimise vulnerabilities and who can help when incidents do occur. In this project, CACH proposes capacity development on all levels among actors in the FSL and WASH sector to understand the link to GBV, related responsibilities and what practical actions stakeholders can take. CACH tries to increase the number of women professionals at community level by addresseing specifically female farmers but also through ensuring that project related committees are gender equal, which will reduce the risk of GBV. Community participation in the project design phase and provision of continuous community sensitization is the key for successful gender sensitive access to humanitarian aid. Further CACH facilitates access to information for survivors of sexual violence for instance through specially targeted radio programmes. 8.5 Prior to the start of any activity, the team informs the village chiefs and concerned HHs about the project objectives, the beneficiary registration and other activities. MoUs are signed with each boma stipulating the tasks and responsibilities of all involved actors. When selecting committees, specific attention is paid to a diversified composition in order to integrate representatives from marginalized groups to cover the broadest possible spectrum of the target group and to give everyone the opportunity to actively participate. 8.6 In a region where about 90% of the target group is not literate and has no access to electronic means of communication, alternative ways for the complaint management mechanism have to be found. Therefore, during the project work, regular target group meetings will be held where beneficiaries can share their feedback, which will be recorded and processed by the CACH project team. In addition, there are regular steering committee meetings to which representatives of the beneficiaries are invited. These meetings provide good opportunities to give feedback even beyond the CACH project staff, as the steering committee consists of the CACH project management team, state and county representatives of the line ministries and representatives in observer role of the donor SDC. 8.9 In rural South Sudan, there is mainly a communal land tenure system in place. CACH takes a very participatory approach to site selection for i.e. FFS, Central Kitchen Gardens and water boreholes, in order to avoid such land disputes. This is also in-line with CACH do-no-harm strategy.

12.1.2. Decreased proportion of populations facing IPC 3,4 and 5

The objective of this project component is to empower smallholder farming communities to sustainably manage their natural resources and improve their access to diversified food in Ikwoto County (IPC 3). This increases their ability to better absorb future food shortages and shocks such as droughts and support people in the long run to become more resilient towards climate change and situations of extreme drought (see SDG 13 - Action to combat climate change and its impacts). For a sustainable improvement in terms of food security and self-reliance of the rural population, the project envisages to introduce to smallholder farmers better farming techniques. Through agro-ecological farming methods, farmers will be able to improve soil fertility, address water conservation issues, control diseases and pests and sustain crop yields. CaCH works to increase productivity and diversify the production systems using approaches that increase the smallholders’ ability to manage natural resources in a sustainable manner. Seeds and tools were distributed once to kickstart the assistance to the farmers in relaunching their agricultural activities. For further agricultural training and learning exchanges, farmers are organized in Farmer Field Schools. Demonstration farms are used to promote climate-resilient agricultural practices, show best practices and to teach soil and water conservation. After Covid-19 restrictions will be lifted, farmer field days will be organized, during which all farmers from the area can visit the demonstration plots, learn about cultivation techniques, share ideas and experiences and, if needed, also exchange seeds and tools. 50 farmers were selected as “lead farmers" and are supported with entrepreneurship skills and technical support and to promote natural resource management and address land and water conservation issues with the other farmers. They do have the task to visit the other farmers regularly, to promote better farming practices and improved post-harvest management.

12.1.3. Equitable access to cross-sectoral basic services to vulnerable populations (including women, children, the elderly, persons with disability and older persons)

The project at hand proposes to reduce the high dependency on external aid by addressing the challenges that communities face to recover their livelihoods using a multi-sectoral approach. This shall be achieved by enabling the population to have access to safe and affordable water, improving health and hygiene (WASH), supporting farmers to increase their agricultural productivity, better post-harvest management, encouraging individual community members to create their own business, creating linkages between farmers and/or business holders with the market (FSL), and, last but not least, creating a healthy learning environment for children (Education). Hygiene, safe water, water for agricultural purposes, nutrition and food storage techniques are considered alongside food availability and access to income. It has been generally recognized that investing in a climate-resilient agriculture and access to water is an important step towards building resilient livelihoods. CaCH recognizes the inherent independency between the thematic areas; the different fields of activity shall not be considered as separate activity, but will be planned and implemented in an integrated manner. With water scarcity and lack of proper wastewater management being a hindering factor for social and economic development (see SDG 6), the provision of water is essential. Only a society with sufficient and safe access to water for drinking will be able to use water for other productive use, including for agricultural activities. Resilience is seen as the greater concept linking all activities within the project. Through this integrated response, all aspects contributing to increasing resilience among the general population and among children will be addressed. Since children are the leaders of tomorrow and the key drivers of change of the future, CaCH considers it important to specifically address children in the intervention. As changing behavior of children is easier than changing those of adults, building a future generation with increased resilience capacity is a promising path to building the resilience of the population. The activities carried out on a micro-level in the schools correspond to the activities carried out on a more macro-level in the communities: both the schools and the communities will benefit from improved water infrastructure, better hygiene, improved farming techniques, harvest storage methods and knowledge in nutrition and food preservation. All activities focus on strengthening the most vulnerable within the communities and building their resilience.

12.1.1. Decreased prevalence of global acute malnutrition among children under age 5 in severity levels 3 and 4

The project activitiy "Mothers/male caregivers increase their household food production and income through village gardens and secure seed storage" targets specifically mothers and caretakers with children under five years of age. The objective creates village gardens for mothers and caregivers of marginal families. These gardens mainly grow vegetables and pulses and contribute to increased food diversity and reduced malnutrition in the villages. The mothers/caregivers also receive practical training in land preparation, climate-resilient cultivation techniques, pest and disease control through traditional methods and learn about nutrition-rich vegetables, cooking and food preservation. With the help of cooking demonstrations, the mothers/caregivers are also taught how to prepare nutritious meals for their children and learn the basics of nutrition and food processing. Specifically women and girls face an increased the risk of sexual harassment towards women while fetching water. By drilling new boreholes close to villages and bringing the water closer to the HHs, the project takes this into account and protection risks for women/girls are further reduced.
