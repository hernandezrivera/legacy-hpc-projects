Project Summary

UNHCR’s protection programme for IDPs and IDP returnees will focus on ensuring access to legal and physical protection for IDPs and returnees, with a strong focus on supporting the national authorities to identify and implement sustainable solutions for Iraq’s forcibly displaced population. UNHCR’s rights-based approach to programming will have a strong focus on non-discrimination and ensuring that all Iraqis have access to rights in line with international and national standards. The anticipated transition from humanitarian to development programming in Iraq will focus on advocacy for increased integration and self-reliance through longer-term livelihoods and access to employment, inclusion in national systems (including the national protection and social welfare systems) and other forms of durable solutions for IDPs and IDP returnees. UNHCR will reinforce coordination mechanisms among protection partners, advocate for increased funding for protection and rule of law interventions (particularly with regard to strengthening of national systems and national actors), and continue to focus on mainstreaming protection (including child protection and SGBV) in the humanitarian and development responses. Capacity building on protection, including CP and SGBV and PSEA and community-based complaints mechanisms, will be a key component of UNHCR’s strategy to strengthen national protection systems and national actors, and to strengthen the protection component of other sector assistance programmes. UNHCR will continue to work to reduce protection risks through protection monitoring, resulting in referrals to targeted services and protection interventions and through evidence-based protection advocacy. Legal counselling, assistance, and representation on civil documentation, detention and advocacy for cases of forced returns and forced evictions will be prioritized as will strengthened case management and referrals to specialized services for children at risk and survivors of SGBV. UNHCR will also support feedback and two-way communication mechanisms with IDPs and returnees, including community-based complaints mechanisms and confidential and safe complaints mechanisms for SEA. UNHCR will also continue to support communication with communities (CwC) activities, in particular to support IDPs to make an informed decision regarding solutions to their displacement. UNHCR will work with strategic partners including the Government institutions at Baghdad and KRI level (MoDM, CoMSec, MoI/Civil Affairs Directorate, JCMC/NOC, MoLSA, DCVAW) to ensure a smooth handover and nationalization of IDP/Returnee protection and assistance, and ensuring that national authorities assume their responsibilities for protection

Needs

The declaration of military victory in the last quarter of 2017 led to the decision to close some camps and require populations to return to their areas of origin. However, ongoing conflict in Kirkuk and Salah al Din governorates in 2018 resulted in new displacements, and large numbers of people still remain in camps and informal settlements. According to UNHCR and partner protection monitoring reports and Rapid Protection Assessments, IDPs in Iraq face severe risks, including exposure to violence, human rights violations, including restrictions on access to safety and freedom of movement, forced encampment and forced/premature return, and denial of return to liberated areas. Most IDPs live in sub-standard conditions, often lacking access to basic services of a minimum standard, and facing risk of eviction, often induced as a means to pressure them to return. In these environments, people with specific needs – including women, adolescent girls, unaccompanied children, the elderly, and persons with disabilities – are particularly vulnerable and struggle to access appropriate services. These trends are expected to continue in 2019. With the conflict declared over in December 2017, IDPs increasingly face pressure and even coercion to return to areas of origin. Other IDPs face administrative and physical obstacles in returning and securing their right of return. As documented in UNHCR’s position on protection considerations for persons fleeing Iraq, LGBTI persons in Iraq face discrimination. Availability of land and livelihoods are also two key areas that have had a negative impact on the search for solutions. As documented in the Multi-Cluster Needs Assessment and protection monitoring reports, IDP women and children with perceived affiliations to ISIL/Daesh are identified as the most vulnerable category. Meeting their needs as well as supporting programming which addresses gender-based violence, prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse, access to civil documentation and national services, including government social protection and social safety nets, as well as accountability to affected populations form the core of protection concerns in Iraq. The lack of livelihoods and basic services is forcing 80% of people in need to adopt stress, crisis and emergency negative copying strategies. In line with the Cash Working Group’s (CWG) findings, UNHCR data indicates vulnerable families are accumulating debt to purchase essential items and over 60% live on less than IQD 480,000 per month (approximately USD 397 per CWG’s calculation), which is below the set amount within the Survival Minimum Expenditure Basket . Socio-economic data collected through UNHCR’s Comprehensive Household Assessment Tool (CHAT) show the number of eligible beneficiaries for Multi-Purpose Cash Assistance has increased by 50% compared to last year, indicating that the needs of vulnerable families has extended beyond the emergency phase and increased in severity in the months following displacement and return. This has had a negative impact upon the ability to support themselves and for families to provide a protective environment for girls and boys, increasing the risk of exposure to exploitation, and leading some families and individuals to adopt negative coping strategies, such as child marriage among girls survival sex. As documented in the MCNA, girls and women, particularly those from female headed households also remain at high risk of GBV, such as domestic violence, sexual exploitation and abuse, rape and sexual assault. In addition, a substantial number of boys and to a lesser extent girls, have been arrested for perceived affiliation with ISIL, detained for significant periods without charge, and sentenced to years of imprisonment for affiliation and for acts they may have been coerced to commit. Women, girls and boys associated with ISIL or who lived under its control, have a high likelihood of having witnessed, survived, and been forced to participate in some of the most extreme forms of violence, exploitation and abuse, including sexual violence, rape, and exploitation. According to the MCNA and data from UNHCR’s ASSIST/CHAT database, access to documentation is also a major protection issue for forcibly displaced families. It is estimated that the majority of displaced persons are missing at least one of the four identity documents required for accessing social services (identity card, nationality certificate, residency document, and/or PDS card). These civil documents are a prerequisite to facilitate access to government protection services and non-contributory cash and in-kind assistance, such as the PDS. Access to civil documentation is a particular challenge for persons living in displacement. While mobile missions of Civil Affairs Directorates and mobile legal assistance has increased access to civil documentation, there are still significant numbers of individuals in need of documentation.

Activities

UNHCR will undertake the following activities as part of its 2019 HRP protection programme: (i) protection monitoring using UNHCR’s comprehensive household assessment tool (CHAT) and other situational and community-based assessment to provide an overview of the protection situation (and changing protection vulnerabilities), but also to provide referrals to specialized protection and assistance services (including emergency protection cash assistance) and provide detailed evidence for protection advocacy of UNHCR and the Protection Cluster; (ii) legal assistance - UNHCR will continue to prioritise provision of legal assistance to IDPs in displacement as well in areas of return, which will include awareness of legal procedures to obtain and renew personal documents; and individual counselling and representation to obtain documentation, including through judicial remedies. Legal assistance will also be provided to IDPs in areas of return to address housing, land and property rights, as well as representation of detention cases and for SGBV cases as part of the case management structure.; (iii) supporting issuance of civil status documentation by national authorities - UNHCR will continue to support legal interventions to ensure that affected children are registered and issued with birth certificates and avoid the risk of statelessness, and will seek to collaborate with partners such as UNICEF in order to increase access to civil status documentation, particularly for women and children with perceived affiliation to extremists. (iv) peaceful coexistence activities - Participatory assessments show that community participation and self-reliance activities are required to support community-based protection and peaceful co-existence. As displacement becomes protracted, there is a need to support the active participation of IDPs in identifying sustainable solutions and building peaceful co-existence among diverse communities. Quick Impact Projects (QIPs) will serve as a tool to promote social cohesion while addressing an identified and finite need – primarily with the aim to improve and expand service facilities, generally through construction or refurbishment. As such, QIPs will expand the protection space for persons of concern, reduce the burden on host communities, and promote peaceful co-existence between the communities. QIPs will serve as pivotal component of UNHCR’s area-based approach for out-of-camp IDPs. Every intervention will incorporate a component in line with the C2RB (Conflict Resolution and Resilience Building) project, which promotes conflict resolution and resilience building among the population; (v) community mobilization, communication-with communities and complaint and feedback mechanisms - Community mobilization and capacity-building programs will focus on sensitization and capacity-building efforts in order to equip IDPs and related stakeholders with key community participation concepts to articulate their needs and exercise their rights. CwC initiatives, such as community-based complaint mechanisms, hot lines, and information sharing channels will be strengthened and consolidated. This will also include ensuring that there are safe, confidential and accessible complaints mechanisms for SEA, and ensuring that UNHCR and partners, IDPs and host communities are aware of the complaints mechanisms, and have confidence in reporting and investigation procedures; (vi) support MODM to register and profile IDPs - UNHCR will continue to support further development of the capacity of MOMD to conduct registration for IDPs and returnees and maintain reliable data on their situations. A central element of this support in 2019 will focus on introducing and promoting the use of biometrics in MOMD’s registration processes, through new iris-scanning equipment. While most IDPs are registered with the Ministry of Migration and Displacement (MOMD), there is an ongoing need for registration services due to new displacements and secondary displacements particularly in Anbar and Ninewa; loss of registration documentation; and returns. UNHCR will therefore continue to work with MOMD to register newly displaced individuals, and also record returns to areas of origin; and (vii) targeted protection advocacy on sustainable solutions (and safe, dignified and voluntary returns). UNHCR will continue to strengthen its own internal tools such as ASSIST to ensure a proper enrolment and monitoring of beneficiaries, including to assess multi-dimensional vulnerability (including protection vulnerability) and refer individuals/families for targeted material assistance, such as shelter and MPCA, but also to ensure referrals to specialized protection services, including legal assistance.

Protection Risk Analysis

UNHCR continues to receive complaints of SEA and aid diversion, and continues to investigate all complaints in line with the PSEA Network SOPs and its own internal oversight and control mechanisms. In order to address this protection risk, UNHCR requires that all partners sign the UNHCR Code of Conduct, and UNHCR monitors compliance with the Zero Tolerance Policy on SEA and UNHCR SGBV policies and guidance. As co-chair of the PSEA Network, UNHCR will also actively engage to share key messages on complaints mechanisms and build capacity of partners on PSEA and investigations, while continuing to directly investigate any complaints of SEA, fraud or aid diversion. Through its leadership of the CwC Task Force, UNHCR will support IDPs and returnees to make informed decisions about solutions and to better understand how to access their rights and entitlements, and ensure that they are aware of complaints and feedback mechanisms. UNHCR is committed to localization, and will ensure that national partners are supported to strengthen their capacity to develop and implement protection projects that effectively mainstream prevention of GBV and PSEA, and that they implement a community-based approach that integrated AAP. UNHCR will also maintain regular meetings with partners implementing protection projects, in particular projects that may subject partner staff to harassment and/or threats (e.g. legal aid partners representing persons with perceived affiliation), and follow-up directly any allegations with relevant authorities to ensure that humanitarian protection partners are able to safely support IDPs and returnees.

Mitigation Measures

The Government of Iraq declared in December 2017 it had regained control of all Iraqi territory from extremists. However, the scale and severity of humanitarian needs remain high and returns and rehabilitation have not taken place at the scale and speed anticipated. Many of the 1.87 million internally displaced persons (IDPs) and 4.11 million returnees face constrained access to basic services as well as security and protection risks while contending with destroyed properties and infrastructure, lack of livelihood opportunities and financial resources. This has led to secondary displacement or re-admittance to camps where return was not possible or sustainable. IDPs and returnees continue to face serious protection risks including unlawful and disproportionate restrictions on access to safety and freedom of movement, confiscation of documents, abduction and illegal detention, forced encampment, forced and premature returns, and increased risk of sexual gender-based violence. Some IDPs are barred from returning to areas of origin by security actors, tribal leaders or communities over alleged affiliation with extremists. This will result in challenges in identifying sustainable solutions for IDPs, and may require longer-term engagement of UNHCR and humanitarian partners in the provision of protection and assistance programmes. UNHCR continues to receive complaints of SEA and aid diversion, and continues to investigate all complaints in line with the PSEA Network SOPs and its own internal oversight and control mechanisms. In terms of mitigation measures, UNHCR will undertake the following actions: (i) capacity building interventions for Government and civil society actors on the Principled Returns Framework and the Guiding Principles on Internal Displacement and relevant international and national human rights legal frameworks with a view to strengthening their capacity to implement sustainable solutions in line with the principles of voluntariness, safety and dignity and so that Government actors gradually assume their responsibilities for providing protection to conflict-affected populations; (ii) UNHCR requires that all partners sign the UNHCR Code of Conduct, and UNHCR monitors compliance with the Zero Tolerance Policy on SEA and UNHCR SGBV policies and guidance. As co-chair of the PSEA Network, UNHCR will also actively engage to share key messages on complaints mechanisms and build capacity of partners on PSEA and investigations; (iii) Through its leadership of the CwC Task Force, UNHCR will support IDPs and returnees to make informed decisions about solutions and to better understand how to access their rights and entitlements; (iv) UNHCR, through the Protection Cluster and directly, will also continue to actively engage in protection advocacy with Government actors and the international community; (v) UNHCR will also strengthen its internal tools to gather and analyse protection information through increased IM capacity and through continuous support to partners using CHAT and ASSIST; and (vi) With some multi-year grants concluding in 2018, the UNHCR operation will proactively engage with donors to underscore the necessity of continued support, including looking to non-traditional sources of funding.

How is your project accountable to affected population

UNHCR is a member of the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) Task Team on Accountability to Affected Populations, including Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (the AAP/PSEA Task Team). AAP is reflected in UNHCR's Revised Age, Gender and Diversity (AGD) Policy (2018), which aims to ensure that all sections of populations of concern have equitable and non-discriminatory access to protection and assistance programmes, and a say in decisions that affect their lives. The AGD Policy defines key elements of accountability with respect to affected populations to which UNHCR is committed. They include: communication and transparency; feedback and response; participation and inclusion; and learning and adaptation. The policy also standardizes the language used in reporting; informs priorities; supports assessments of the extent to which UNHCR achieves its AAP commitments; and provides criteria when evaluating learning and improvements. UNHCR’s programmes integrate three key elements that support AAP: (i) an AGD approach: An AGD approach is a rights and community-based approach according to which planning, programming, implementation, and monitoring and evaluation should be guided by principles of equality and participation; (ii) Community-based protection: this requires UNHCR to integrate community engagement in all aspects of its protection work, and recognize the capacities as well as needs of communities in humanitarian crises, in order to avoid harm and ensure that humanitarian programmes do not inadvertently leave people and communities worse off. A core tenet of the AGD policy, community-based protection employs a rights-based approach that puts persons of concern at the centre of decisions that affect their lives; (iii) Participatory assessment: this tool builds partnerships with IDP, returnee and host community women and men of all ages and backgrounds by promoting meaningful participation through structured dialogue. Separate discussions with women, girls, boys, and men, including adolescents, enable UNHCR and partners to gather accurate information on the specific protection risks that different groups face, identify underlying causes, understand community capacities, and hear their proposed solutions. It helps communities to take collective action to enhance their own protection, and makes it possible to implement a rights- and community-based approach. In Iraq, UNHCR will implement the following approach by ensuring integration of the Revised AGD Policy in all of its partnerships, including through the organization of its annual Participatory Assessment to guide participatory planning, and to provide feedback to IDPs and returnees on project implementation. As lead of the CwC Task Force, UNHCR will support partners to integration community-based approaches in its programming and to ensure that two-way feedback is incorporated into protection programmes. In the specific area of GBV, an internal expert is currently deployed in Iraq to assist UNHCR to develop a National SGBV Mainstreaming Action Plan. The plan will include mainstreaming SGBV and PSEA into UNHCR’s sectoral programming and response, including child protection and work with adolescents and youth. UNHCR is also supporting the Protection, CCCM and Shelter/NFI Clusters to integrate SGBV into their planning and implementation. UNHCR will continue to closely coordinate with IDP Call Center to ensure that UNHCR follows up on reported issues, and challenges are addressed and appropriate referrals made. UNHCR also uses PDMs to assess the protection outcomes of our legal assistance programmes, which uses a mixed methodology of household surveys and focus group discussions.
