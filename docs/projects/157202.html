Project Summary

The influx of South Sudanese refugees (SSR) in Sudan continues in 2018 as civil war enters its fourth year. 33,117 additional persons arrived in 2018 bringing the total number of refugees to 852,080, out of which 384,473 were registered with UNHCR/COR while 467,607 were IPP registered and unregistered. Refugees from South Sudan account for approximately 78% of all reported refugees in Sudan, with new arrivals each month. In East Darfur alone, there were 9,353 SSRs arrivals in 2018. Overall, the Government of Sudan estimates up to 1.3 million South Sudanese refugees in Sudan. While the Revitalized Agreement for the Resolution of the Conflict in South Sudan (R-ARCSS) was signed in September 2018, its full implementation will take time and ongoing violations to the agreement are reported, making sustainable return to their areas of origin in South Sudan unlikely in the near future. It is expected that South Sudanese refugees will continue to arrive in 2019.
Inter-agency partners’ capacity to respond to the needs of refugees continues to be stretched, exacerbated by critical funding gaps and Sudan’s ongoing economic crisis, as well as fuel and cash shortages. While progress has been made to meet assistance standards, significant gaps remain, for example: a majority of children remain out of school; water availability and latrine coverage are below standards, high rates of open defecation; access to health services is inconsistent, with supply gaps in basic medicines; high food insecurity and critical malnutrition rates. These issues are compounded by limited access to livelihoods and household income, including increased tensions with host communities, prevalence of child labor, early marriage and other child protection issues, and SGBV risks. Continued investment and scale-up is required in order to better meet the protection and basic service needs of SSRs.
ARC target population in Adila and Abujabra localities will be 24,517 and will provide Primary Health and Nutrition services, which includes general medical consultations and treatment of minor ailments, maternal and child health care (antenatal care, delivery, postnatal care, family planning and referral of complications), community management of acute malnutrition, Infant and Young child feeding, routine immunization support and conduction of outreach health education through established networks of trained community health, nutrition and WASH volunteers. A functional referral system will be established between refugee communities and existing PHCCs. ARC will also provide psychosocial counselling through the services of one Psychosocial counsellor per locality. These counsellors will be rotated in the target communities to identify vulnerable women and children and refer them to ARC's medical services or UNHCR (for protection and legal issues) if required.
Through WASH activities, ARC will cover costs of operating and maintaining two water systems - one in Abujabra and one in Adilla, rehabilitation and upgrade of boreholes to hybrid solar pumping systems, one each in Abujabra and Adilla. There will be regular water quality testing at different points of water-chain usage including storage and corrective measures taken to improve the quality of water. 300 household latrines will be constructed; 100 latrines in Abu Jabra and 200 in Adilla localities in East Darfur. These will be made with concrete reinforced beam instead of wooden pole to minimize the environmental impact. The proposed interventions will promote proper hygiene practices and better understanding of the key public health and nutrition risks associated with water and hygiene related issues. To ensure this integration and complementarity between WASH, health and nutrition services, ARC will transform its previous village health committees and WASH management committees into the integrated Community Relief and Development Committees.

Needs

Despite the lower arrival rates in 2018, the capacity of inter-agency partners to respond continues to be stretched, exacerbated by a critical funding gap as well as Sudan’s ongoing economic crisis. The South Sudanese refugee population in Sudan have an exceptionally heightened vulnerability with over 60% of new arrivals being children, and women and children comprising 82% of the population. The health and nutrition status of newly arrived refugees remains a concern. Recent Standardized Expanded Nutrition Surveys (SENS) conducted indicated critical (>15 per cent) Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) rate and Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) (>3 per cent) among children 6 to 59 months of age. The SENS also indicate high rates of anaemia (>40 per cent) among children and women (aged 15-49 years), as well as low antenatal service and measles immunization coverage among refugees in both states. In mid-2018, average daily water quantities for refugees stood at 10.2 L per person per day, with just 5 l/p/d in some areas. Latrine coverage has worsened in 2018, despite progress made on latrine construction. Heavy rains have caused most latrines to collapse in South Kordofan, East Darfur and South Darfur, due to the reliance on locally-available materials, weak structures, and the use of emergency standards. In August 2018, an inter-agency latrine functionality survey conducted indicated an average latrine coverage gap of 91 persons per latrine. There has been no consistent distribution of personal hygiene kits for women and girls. Children under 5 openly defecate within their environment, which contributes to high rates of diarrheal disease. The adoption of hand washing is quite low across all states due to significant gaps in soap supplies. Collection, transportation and storage of water at household level is also a major issue of concern due to contamination risk from old or misused jerry cans.
