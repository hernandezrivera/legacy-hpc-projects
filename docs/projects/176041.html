Project Summary

Kapoeta has been tattered by war and currently undergoing intense ravage through cattle rustling, this has made any households vulnerable to shocks and stresses especially, communal fighting which has forced tens of thousands to be displaced. The skirmishes of 2020 November and December forced thousands to flee their homes seeking safety in the neighborhood, many properties were destroyed, an estimated 5700 are in crisis. 2071 boys (0-17yrs) and 1780 girls (0-17 yrs.) and 1021 men (above 18yrs) and 828 (above 18yrs). Many across Kapoeta East County especially children and women were smash hard in 2020 by the several shocks of exaggerated conflict and local violence, in the following year there were flooding, and the impacts of COVID-19 related restrictions. Cattle raids are a protracted delinquent in parts of Eastern Equatoria and South Sudan in General, separate from the civil war that killed tens of thousands between 2013.More than 50 people were killed and hundreds of others were displaced by sectional violence in December. However, many children especially from the poor and excluded households do not have access to education at all levels. Based on the most recent Phase Classification (IPC) analysis, it was determined that 5.29 million people in South Sudan (45% of the total population) faced IPC Phase 3 40,000 were in Catastrophe (IPC phase 5) and 1.1 million were in Emergency (IPC phase 4)
Through this proposed intervention, OPRD will strive to provide continued access to protective, quality and equitable education services for South Sudanese boys and girls. This will be achieved through:
1) an increase in the number of suitable facilities offering children and young people upper-primary and non-formal education services;
2) enhanced quality and inclusiveness of teaching methods and school-based protection mechanisms; and
3) efforts to engage communities and institutions in school governance and quality education delivery.

1 - Needs

The provision of education opportunities in the Republic of South Sudan takes place against a backdrop of political instability, a struggling economy, extreme poverty, food insecurity and armed conflict. The lack of basic infrastructure across much of the country and high levels of insecurity impede development progress and the provision of humanitarian assistance. Conflict and displacement - South Sudan is the newest nation in the world, in which it seceded from a unified Sudan. A civil war broke out in December 2013, opposing South Sudanese government forces and opposition forces, which pushed millions of people to flee to other states or neighbouring countries. Mass displacement has had a devastating impact on the social fabric of communities and cohesion of families. It is characterised by poor educational outcomes and high numbers of out-of-school children and adolescents, particularly those affected by conflict and displacement. Economy - South Sudan is one of the world’s weakest and most underdeveloped. The country’s GDP per capita has dropped in recent years, from $ 200 in 2019 to less than $100 in 2020. The oil sector accounts for about 70% of GDP however, on current reserve estimates, oil production is expected to reduce steadily in future years, to become negligible by 2035. Outside the oil sector, livelihoods are concentrated in low productive, unpaid agriculture and pastoralist work. The South Sudanese Pound (SSP) has depreciated. The economy of rapidly against the US dollar in recent years, which has impacted service delivery across all sectors, and notably the payment of teacher salaries. Lack or delays in salary payment is impacting teachers’ motivation, leading to absenteeism and, ultimately, to teachers leaving the profession in search for better economic prospects. Education - Many schools have been destroyed during the conflict and/or have stopped functioning because children and teachers have fled or are too scared to attend, while some parents are no longer able to pay for uniforms and books as they deal with unemployment and the effects of the chronically devalued currency. A recent study on Out of School Children reveals that at least 2.2 million children (75% of whom are girls) in South Sudan are not receiving an education, which represents one of the highest rates of out-of-school children in the world. For those enrolled, up to 86% of learners are at least five years over age for their grade and many are at risk of dropping out if timely action is not taken. The conflict and associated economic decline have eroded the Government’s ability to provide consistent basic services. While they revealed that an additional 6,000 schools need to be built or rehabilitated, the Government of the Republic of South Sudan allocated only 4% of the 2021 national budget to education. Now, thousands of children who fled as refugees are starting to return to the country, increasing the burden on already over- stretched education services. Most vulnerable populations - Children make about half of South Sudan population and bear the brunt of conflict and economic pressures. At least one in two children in South Sudan is affected by the humanitarian crisis. The conflict has prevented children across the country from growing up in a secure and peaceful environment that is conductive to their physical and emotional development. Their families rely on negative coping and survival strategies, including intentional family separation, school drop-out, child labour and involvement in criminal groups or armed forces.Social and cultural norms weigh most heavily against girls. In an often unforgiving patriarchal society, many prefer a boy-child as the heir, who will preserve family lineage and pride. Girls are raised to cater to the needs of their male family members; cooking, fetching water, cleaning and serving in preparation for their pre-destined role as a future wife and mother.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

Accountability to beneficiaries and child safeguarding: OPRD is committed to mainstreaming principles of Accountability for Affected Population (AAP) in accordance with the Core Humanitarian Standard on accountability and quality management. At the community level, OPRD will strive to include members of IDP, host and returnee communities in the design and implementation of the project to encourage a strong community ownership. Continuous engagement and coordination will ensure that the aspirations of the community are being met. OPRD will ensure that target groups and communities have access to information that is relevant to their needs by creating opportunities for those communities (including children and youth) to participate in programming decisions and provide feedback. During implementation, OPRD will ensure that interventions ensure children and communities are listened to including via child and youth protection tools and regular monitoring of our established complaints and feedback mechanisms. During community visits, our staff will use feedback forms to record any issues that require response. OPRD will set up/strengthen the existing complaints and feedback mechanisms to solicit feedback; these will be child friendly, gender sensitive and suitable for adults and children with disabilities. OPRD’s MEAL Officer is field based and will facilitate the collection of feedback and complaints through field visits, focus group discussions and surveys to measure satisfaction levels. Prior to implementation, OPRD continues to engage in community focus group discussions and sensitization meetings to ensure that beneficiaries are well informed of the project before inception. OPRD will also have staff present during the activities that are available to provide information and support beneficiaries if there are any concerns. In addition, Save the Children will use the Safeguarding-in-Programming checklists to ensure continuous analysis of risks. Moreover, there will be a regular orientation on child safeguarding for Save the Children staff and new employees will sign the Child Safeguarding policy and Code of Conduct.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

The project has a feedback mechanism, and will have caseworkers, and focal point persons who will be in direct contact with the community gather data refer and give a response. Routine Data Quality Assurance tool (RDQA): Ensuring data quality is a priority for OPRD, which implies using double counting elimination procedures. OPRD staff will conduct quality checks and validations of data using Routine Data Quality Assurance tool (RDQA). The RDQA will be conducted as per the standard RDQA criteria of data validity, integrity, precision, reliability and timeliness. The MEAL team at field level will undertake the RDQA on a regular basis, which will be embedded in the routine monitoring activities during project timeframe. Findings will be communicated to the program implementation team and mid-level management and serve as a basis for corrective measures on the ground. Recommendations from the assessment will be included in a task tracker to support their effective roll-out. Reporting: OPRD will report to UN- HRP as per the donor’s requirements, demonstrating progress against the indicators in the approved logical framework
