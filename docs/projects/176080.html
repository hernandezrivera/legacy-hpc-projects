Project Summary

According to the IPC report released on the 18th December 2020, 6.3 million people, or 53% of the people analyzed faced high levels of acute food insecurity (IPC Phase 3 or above) from October to November 2020. While 5.8 million people or 48% of the people analyzed will likely face high levels of acute food insecurity (IPC Phase 3 or above) between December 2020 and March 2021 and 7.2 million people or 60% of the people analyzed will likely face high levels of acute food insecurity (IPC Phase 3 or above) from April to July 2021. Intensified conflict and sub-national violence, the second consecutive year of major flooding, and the impacts of COVID-19 played major roles in the worsening food insecurity. Some 1.6 million people remained internally displaced and another 2.2 million as refugees in the region. Insecurity, lack of basic services, and unresolved housing, land, and property issues prevent people from returning home in large numbers. Overall food security worsened and some communities are facing catastrophic needs. 1.4 million children are acutely malnourished than in the past three years. Women and girls continued to face extreme levels of gender-based violence and psychosocial distress. People’s coping mechanisms weakened because of the cumulative shocks, leading families to adopt negative practices such as forced labor and child marriage. Some 8.3 million people in South Sudan are estimated to be in need of humanitarian assistance in 2021. These include 8,000,000 South Sudanese women, men, girls, and boys, and 310,000 refugees and asylum seekers. This is an 800,000 increase in absolute numbers from the 7.5 million people in need in 2020. A total of 72 counties face extreme needs while five are in severe need. Jonglei State is the worst-hit state with 85% of the total population in IPC 3+ requiring humanitarian assistance from April to July 2021. Akobo and Pibor are the two counties in Jonglei classified under IPC 5 catastrophe. Akobo has 90% of its total population in IPC3+ (198,920) with 11,051 (5.6%) of those in need are in IPC5. With a population at famine, the need to respond to save lives is not only a priority but a right, and this project is designed and intended to respond to the crisis in Akobo, Fangak, Pigi/Canal (Jonglei State), Panyikang, Nasir (Upper Nile State) and Twic County (Warrap State)
These targeting locations continue to face inter-sectoral needs in the thematic areas of Food Security, Nutrition, WASH, Health, and Protection. The agro-pastoralist nature of populations in the counties targeted has exposed the population to severe food insecurity and malnutrition due to low food availability, diversity, and access. The high levels of acute food insecurity in the counties targeted are driven by flooding, insecurity, population displacements, declined crop production, diseases and pests, the economic crisis, the effects of COVID-19, limited access to basic services, and the cumulative effects of prolonged years of asset depletion and loss of livelihoods.
The project will target persons through cluster objectives 2 (Enhance and sustain emergency food production through the complementary vegetable and crop livelihood inputs, fishing and livestock support in a way that prevents the spread & transmission of COVID 19 (compulsory use of new SOP & guidelines) and 3 (Reduce dependency on food and agricultural inputs to support and strengthen households’ ability to absorb shocks in a way that prevents the spread & transmission of COVID 19 (compulsory use of new SOP & guidelines). (CAO) plans to reach a total of 27,000 Direct and Indirect beneficiaries, or persons with much-needed food security and livelihood support of these, 60% females; 35% IDPs, 25% returnees, and 50% vulnerable host communities.

1 - Needs

The Food security and livelihoods situation in the counties of Akobo, Fangak, Pigi/Canal (Jonglei State), Panyikang, Nasir (Upper Nile State), and Twic County (Warrap State) continues to decline with projections indicating a worsening situation affecting mostly women, children and the elderly. According to the latest Integrated Phase Classification (IPC) report, in the first projection period of December 2020 to March 2021, an estimated 5.82 million people (48.3% of the population) will likely face Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity. During this period, an estimated 1.79 million people are likely to face Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. In the second projection period of April to July 2021, an estimated 7.24 million people (60% of the population) are likely to face Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity, with 31,000 people likely to be in Catastrophe (IPC Phase 5) acute food insecurity in Akobo County (11,000) in Jonglei State. During this period, an estimated 2.47 million are likely to be in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. The most severe acute food insecurity conditions are in counties where chronic vulnerabilities have been exacerbated by a combination of shocks, including floods, insecurity incidents, the macro-economic crisis, and the effects of COVID-19 measures. In Jonglei State, both Akobo and Pigi are classified as IPC phase 4, with phase 5 pockets anticipated in Akobo County. The high levels of acute food insecurity in the counties targeted are driven by flooding, insecurity, population displacements, declined crop production, diseases and pests, the economic crisis, the effects of COVID-19, limited access to basic services, and the cumulative effects of prolonged years of asset depletion and loss of livelihoods. The continuing currency depreciation and associated impacts on commodity prices and incomes coupled with the effects of COVID-19 continue to erode the purchasing power of vulnerable households who rely on markets to purchase food and other basic needs. Seasonal scarcity of food, the flooding in 2020 (with limited recovery from the previous floods), and the much lower than required humanitarian food assistance in the face of increased needs, will likely result in an increase of acute food insecurity during the projection periods, especially during the lean season. About 1.4 million children under five years are expected to suffer from acute malnutrition in 2021 based on the same season historical data of food security and nutrition monitoring system, SMART nutrition surveys, and admission trends for 2020. This is the highest caseload for acute malnutrition since the start of the crisis in December 2013. Further deterioration of the nutrition situation is projected during the lean season of April to August 2021. The major factors contributing to acute malnutrition include the high prevalence of diseases (up to 36%), poor quality, and diversity of food (Minimum Acceptable Diet: 7%, Minimum Dietary Diversity: 15%). Furthermore, poor access to health and nutrition services due to heightened inter-communal conflict and flooding mainly in the Greater Upper Nile is also contributing to acute malnutrition. COVID-19 related disruptions, including those rightfully implemented to curb coronavirus infection rates, as well as changes in SAM and MAM referral protocols for children have exacerbated the lack of access to services. Low food availability, diversity, and access have all contributed to the worsened food security situation. The majority of the population depends on livestock as a major livelihoods source. Low levels of vaccinations, treatment as well as the loss of livelihoods through rustlings, death, and displacement have all affected community livelihoods. A robust food security and livelihoods response by CAO is required in these counties in order to improve the living standard and well-being of communities.

2 - Project activities

Community Action Organization in line with FSL clusters 2021 Strategic Objectives will conduct the below activities:- • Provision of Emergency food & livelihood support through the distribution of seeds, tools, fishing kits/ vegetable kits; rapid response kits (fish kit/ cowpea & three variety of fast-maturing vegetable seed)• Support to seed fairs using locally sourced seed; seed security & multiplication strengthening/ Support market functionality. • Support value chain addition e.g. through agriculture, fishery, and livestock products/ by-products preservation & marketing• Training & capacity building: both adaptive and absorptive on-farm production (farmer/agro-pastoral field schools, demonstration plots), income generation (business skills), vocational training, etc.• Conduct Emergency food production activities: main season agriculture seeds & tool distribution; fishing, vegetable & crop kits; • Conduct Emergency food & livelihood support activities: fishing kits/ vegetable kits; rapid response kits (fish kit/ cowpea & three variety of fast-maturing vegetable seed). • Training & capacity building: both adaptive and absorptive on-farm production (demonstration plots), income generation (business skills), etc. • Participate in Multisectoral models: livelihoods, nutrition-sensitive food security, hygiene, sanitation• Participate in Reducing pest and crop disease e.g. FAW & Desert locust surveillance, detection, prevention & eradication• Participate in Reduction in post-harvest losses and introduction of appropriate preservation technologies (e.g. hermetic storage bags)• Conduct training and interventions to promote market access. • Conduct meetings to strengthen local peace structures to help resolve conflict.• Conduct awareness sessions to Increase and share knowledge and prompt action on SGBV prevention through working with 3000 women, men, youth, policymakers, and Implementing partners.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

CAO has a Prevention of Sexual Exploitation, Abuse (PSEA) Policy which sets out the expectations, systems, and processes aimed at preventing sexual exploitation of other people we work with such as vulnerable adults and community members, as well as the actions that will ensue if incidents of sexual exploitation, abuse and harassment are identified. This will be shared and read out to beneficiaries at the onset of the project with periodic refresher sessions on what is expected of them.CAO with the help of our protection department will ensure that our strategy will be inclusive and user-friendly for all targeted beneficiaries (Children under 5 Boys and Girls, PLWs and caregivers, and all the men targeted in the community. CAO will continue to communicate and or consult the community through FGDs and KIIs. The stakeholders i.e BHIs, RRC, Administrators, women leads, and chiefs are the bridge between CAO and the beneficiaries to help better the program and ensure PSEA will CAO continue to communicate or consult the community, how will you use the information to better the program implementation

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

In collaboration with the CAOs Protection department, help desks will be erected in FSL sites. Help desks are a good opportunity for two-way dialogue. They allow people to report concerns without risk of stigma from the community (as people might use the help desk to do anything from asking about distribution times to report sexual abuse. Help desks can also build trust if they are frequently open and staff is helpful. CAO staff will be responsible for championing good practices and maintaining an organizational culture that prioritizes and safeguards all against SEA. Managers at all levels (Both Juba and Field level) have responsibilities to support and develop systems that maintain an environment that facilitates implementation of this Policy and which prevents SEA. They must ensure that CAO staff and program participants understand the importance of a working feedback mechanism. Managers must create a safe environment at CAO for anyone to come forward and raise allegations or concerns of SEA and act immediately to respond to any reports without discrimination giving special attention to individuals living with disabilities, and even those who are illiterate both women, men, boys, and girls. FGDs and the use of suggestion boxes with individuals who are not literate, women and girls as well as most vulnerable members of the community will be used to promote inclusivity

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

CAO seeks to be accountable to the women, men, boys, and girls whose lives it aims to improve, and places this responsibility at the core of its humanitarian policy. As a matter of human rights and meaningful programming. CAO as guided by the cluster defines Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) as “an active commitment by the organization to use power responsibly by taking account of, giving account to, and being held to account by the people it seeks to assist”. By being more accountable to affected populations; Men, Women, Boys, and Girls, CAO will do this by increasing Twic, Fangak, Akobo & Pigi canal Communities' participation and feedback in programme identification, design, delivery, and lesson learning. CAO seeks to achieve programmes of higher quality, with greater and more sustainable impact. The project will increase the space for the community to shape their own recovery especially to better deliver against its commitments to stakeholders, including the people CAO serves and the resource partners who make assistance possible. CAO will ensure effective information sharing and communication channels by sharing information about CAO programmes with beneficiaries in a timely, accessible, and inclusive way. This will put the community in a position to understand and shape decisions that impact their lives. CAO is committed to ensuring that people receiving support participate in and influence all steps of the programme cycle, including initial assessment, project design, beneficiary selection, implementation, monitoring, and evaluations. Systems of community representation must be fair and representative, enabling the most marginalized, vulnerable, and affected to have a voice. CAO will use FGDs, using focus groups as a method of participation gives a voice to those in the community who are unable to speak up in a larger meeting or setting. This will be done in collaboration with other sectors in CAO to boost integration.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Projects monitoring systems and reviews that gather information about beneficiaries and their perceptions, views, and responses to the interventions. Such information can be help improve the assistance of both individuals and communities/beneficiaries through a wide variety of data collection methods. Even when the information is not comprehensive, it will undoubtedly help managers in improving the performance and impacts of their projects and programs. It will also help the organization building trust and facilitates effective communication between beneficiaries and the organizations that assist them. C.A.O will organize project performance and quality review meetings with staff, beneficiaries, and partners to assess progress towards the set objectives and work plans, to monitor project performance and quality of activities based on monitoring and beneficiary feedback data, to identify risks and set risk management strategies during implementation. A project evaluation will be carried out at the end of the project and the process used to measure the achievement of project outputs and outcomes then documented for the record.CAO will take immediate and appropriate action upon receipt of any report or complaint about SEA allegations, concerns or suspicions, or retaliation related to SEA.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

•The long-distance traveled by some beneficiaries to receive services at FSL sites exposes them to risks of violence and exploitation. Community Action Organization will be developing several mechanisms to mitigate these risks i.e. advocating for security by spouses to the sites. This will also promote male participation in FSL service delivery at the grassroots level alongside providing security for the women's caregiver. -An available and confidential complaint mechanism will be put to use and made accessible to beneficiaries for inclusivity purposes and as a way of informing CAO of bottlenecks in the project implementation period.• Many beneficiaries are illiterate which reduces the range of modalities available for them to use to provide feedback and could therefore affect feedback quality. For example, feedback presented in a face-to-face session may not be as honest or comprehensive as feedback offered anonymously due to the power dynamics at play. To address this, CAO will use focus group discussions to garner feedback in a less individualized manner. • Some FSL sites have extensive catchment areas. In these cases, mothers, caregivers, and children must often travel or walk long distances to access the sites, sometimes staying overnight in the bush. This journey presents several safety risks, including harassment and sexual violence. In order to identify and mitigate these risks, CAO will conduct regular GBV safety audits at its FSL sites. Feedback from these safety audits will be used to understand the risks that beneficiaries face and therefore find and implement solutions to mitigate them.
