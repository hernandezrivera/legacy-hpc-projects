Project Summary

This project will support the provision of emergency one-off multi-purpose cash assistance to vulnerable Libyan IDP, refugee and migrant communities affected by conflict in Tripoli. This project will support 340 Libyan IDP HHs, 330 refugee HHs and 330 migrant HH, aiming to reach a total of 3721 individuals. NRC’s response will consist of the following activities:
1- Market Assessment.
2- Beneficiary Targeting.
3- Provide one-off emergency cash assistance.
4- Referral to other services.
5- Conduct Post Distribution Monitoring.

1 - Needs

The hostilities starting in early April 2019 has resulted in the displacement of 20,975 families (approximately 104,875 individuals) [data from IOM DTM Flash Update # 20- Libya] from their homes in and around Tripoli, when the eastern based Libyan National Army (LNA) and the western based Government National Accord (GNA) resumed hostiles in and around Tripoli. The current escalation in conflict has continued for months as a result of militia groups based in and around Tripoli defending the city in support of the GNA. With substantial international backing for both parties, the conflict is likely to continue, which will further exacerbate the humanitarian situation for displaced and conflicted-affected communities, including in Tripoli. The overwhelming nature of displacement of Libyans is such that most persons do not relocate to communal shelters, but are moving to reach safety with family or friends in different locations. Displaced families often pass through transit centers where they may remain for 48 hours but generally not longer. At the moment transit centers provide a range of NFIs such as dignity kits, blankets, mattresses. There are however currently 600 + IDP households (HH) in Tripoli currently registered and living in community shelters – who represent some of the most vulnerable IDPs as they are unable to rely on familial or community based support as many other IDPs do and nor is the in-kind assistance sufficient to meet their full range of needs. With a resolution to hostilities unclear, families facing protract displacement in addition to years of instability, economic and political crises, the continued reliance on negative coping mechanisms is sure. In addition to the above, there are approximately 4,900 refugees and migrants who are held in the Department of Combatting Illegal Migration (DCIM) - run detention centres (DCs) and are considered the most high risk of a total estimated migrant population of 650,000. The international humanitarian community considers these detained migrants and refugees to be amongst the most vulnerable persons in all of Libya. Many DCs are only nominally run by DCIM, and are influenced by local militias with reports of human rights abuse, torture, inadequate food, and overcrowding. Reports from international media and human rights group also indicated acitivities related to human trafficking, bonded labor, slave trade and smuggling all occur within DCs. The recent clashes have included greater risks,evidenced by the death of 53 refugees and migrants in Tajoura detention centre (due to the use of heavy weaponry in the area). This incident has led to the evacuation of the Tajoura DC as well as the release of detainees from other DCs in heavy conflict areas. The migrants among those released have been approaching UNHCR for support, however, are considered outside of the mandate of UNHCR and do not received goods, services, or other support (as there is a lack of referral options for migrants). In Libya, UNHCR has reached an agreement with the Libyan authorities to consider individuals from nine nationalities (Syrian, Sudanese, South Sudanese, Palestinian, Eritrean, Somalian, Iraqi, Ethiopian, and Yemeni) as refugees whom they consider PoCs. In rare cases will UNHCR provide support to individuals outside of these nationality groups. The International Organization for Migration (IOM) does provide some services to migrants, but only in cases where they are enrolled in the voluntary humanitarian return (VHR) program. In many assessments of needs in Tripoli, regardless of the beneficiary group, cash is listed as a high priority need. Cash is a versatile modality and proven useful in preventing negative coping mechanisms.In Libya, this takes various forms including taking out loans, borrowing money from friends and family, eating fewer meals a day, spend savings, reduced expenses on health food and education, and to depend on family and friends for support.

2 - Activities

The project will target a total of 1000 households (HHs): 340 Libyan IDP HHs, 330 refugee HHs and 330 migrant HHs. The response will consist of five activities: 1- Market Assessment. NRC will rely on the JMMI, which was created in an effort to inform cash-based interventions and better understand market dynamics in Libya. NRC monitor vendor outlets who have PoS, in areas where there are high concentrations of beneficiaries, to ensure continued electronic functionality and price monitoritng on a bi-monthly basis. 2- Beneficiary Targeting. NRC will receive migrant (urban – those released from DCs) referrals from UNHCR, and referrals from actors managing communal shelters around Tripoli. NRC will assess IDPs against vulnerability criteria based on the CMWG eligibility scorecard while referred refugees and migrants will immediately qualify for assistance as they are deemed very vulnerable. 3- Provide one-off Emergency Cash. For migrants this will happen at registration points with UNHCR. For IDPs this will be done at communal shelters in coordination with the actor who manages the shelter. The transfer amount will be harmonized with the values set by the CMWG. 4- Referral to other services. While assessing potential beneficiaries NRC will make internal and external referrals for other types of services – using established inter-agency referral tools- available to create a further protection environment. 5- Conduct Post Distribution Monitoring. NRC will follow up with cash assistance recipients between 2-4 weeks after distribution to conduct an assessment on sufficiency, use, difficulties and perceived impact of the assistance. The PDM will be harmonised with the CMWG PDM indicators and questions. In addition, the financial services provider offers reports with a breakdown of how the transfer value was spent based on the type of point of sale (POS) grocery stores, medical services etc.

4 - Protection risk analysis

1- Explosive hazards: This risk includes injuries, death, psychological trauma, or material damage caused by IED, AO, UXO, grenades, or landmines, and can affect both staff and beneficiaries.2- Beneficiary or Community Dissatisfaction: Beneficiaries and local communities may perceive that some groups are being given preferential treatment while others are being discriminated against, posing a risk to both staff and beneficiaries. They may also be unhappy with the type/amount of assistance given, or perceive that NRC do not recruit staff in an open and transparent manner.3- Lack of Community Acceptance: As an INGO, NRC may be perceived as a form of outside interference by some elements of the community, while some elements of NRC's work may also be considered controversial (e.g. assistance to migrants, which some may perceive as being a pull factor towards Libya/Europe).4- Sexual exploitation and abuse: SEA could be experienced by both staff and beneficaries and can result in physical and psychological consequences for victims, as well as serious legal and reputational exposure for NRC. SEA perpetrated by NRC staff would also violate the 'Do No Harm' principle.

5 - Mitigation measures

1- Provide staff with adequate training, equipment, and facilities, ensure advisories disseminated to staff. 2- Incorporate conflict sensitivity analysis when conducting beneficiary selections in order to avoid favoring a particular tribe or community segment; Manage expectations for beneficiaries; Conduct regular meetings with local authorities and key community leaders in order to explain recruitment and beneficiary selection processes; Manage crowd dynamics during high stress distributions; First Aid Kit available in project locations; First Aid training; Crowd dynamics training; Clear and transparent recruitment process;3- All staff to respect cultural norms within Libya, staff provided with induction to educate on Libyan culture; staff adhere to NRC code of conduct; code of conduct violators held accountable by line management4- All staff training on NRC SEA policy and Code of Conduct; Clear and easy confidential reporting processes for staff and beneficiaries; Positive investigation policy for all allegations; Demonstrated zero tolerance for SEA amongst staff or partners/contractors; Requirement for partners to have an SEA policy and reporting process; Proactive policy of dip-sampling amongst higher risk beneficiary groups. Managers from outside the program area conducting interviews within vulnerable communities; Case study presentations to be given annually at Area and Country level SMT meetings; Training for managers in how to identify when staff are engaged in SEA; Background and reference check to be conducted for each employee.

6 - How is your project accountable to affected populations?

Common feedback mechanism

6.1 - Other accountability mechanisms

To ensure accountability to beneficiaries, NRC is committed to setting up systematic and effective mechanism that would provide beneficiary and non-beneficiary feedback, and to provide an on-going response to NRC on its projects. NRC takes guidance from the Core Humanitarian Standards definition of Accountability and understands Accountability to Affected Populations as a process of using power responsibly, taking account of, and being held accountable by the people we support. As with other NRC projects, a complaint and response mechanism will ensure that beneficiaries and communities have the opportunity to register feedback and any complaints. In 2020, the mechanism will include a dedicated and centralized complaint and feedback hotline, email, and as previously, allow for face to face sessions between staff and beneficiaries. NRC uses this feedback to resolve any issue that may arise and adjust programming accordingly according to the needs and input of the communities. Furthermore, populations served by NRC staff will be informed on NRC’s Code of Conduct and how to report any breaches, employing the community members and beneficiaries as part of the accountability system.
