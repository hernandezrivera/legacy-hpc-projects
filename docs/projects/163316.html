Project Summary

Due to protracted crisis and lack of capacity, the Libyan health system is almost entirely unable to meet the health needs of the Libyan population. Particularly vulnerable are migrants, IDPs, persons with disabilities, whose access to healthcare is severely obstructed.
The proposed project will improve access to lifesaving and essential primary health care services among the vulnerable migrants, IDPs, returnees and host communities in Libya through a series of measures, ranging from primary healthcare service provision through mobile clinics to populations most in need, MHPSS services, referral to secondary and tertiary healthcare, to support to existing state-run health facilities in order to strengthen their ability to provide health services.

1 - Needs

The protracted crises in Libya has weakened the technical and operational capacities of the health care system, resulting in lack of access to critical primary and secondary medical healthcare. Migrants, displaced persons, refugees, people with special needs including people with mental health problems and rural communities are particularly vulnerable and have even less access to healthcare. Even those who enjoy some access to health services do not have the opportunity to avail from a complete package of services in an integrated manner; one main shortcoming is the shortage of medical equipment and medicines in the health facilities. An earlier assessment (SARA 2) in 2017 has revealed that, 17.5 per cent of hospitals, 20 per cent of Primary Healthcare (PHC) facilities, and 18 per cent of specialized health facilities have been partially damaged or completely destroyed; the situation has not shown an improvement since then. Reproductive health services including ante- and postnatal care, family planning and the management of sexually transmitted infections are almost collapsed, resulting in challenges in healthcare services for women. Immunization program is facing interrupted vaccine supplies; there are reports of measles and mumps outbreaks in 2019. The disease surveillance system is weak and not properly established. Spread of Tuberculosis (TB) and Leishmaniosis has been widely seen in some districts and municipalities.Although noncommunicable diseases (NCD) represent 77% of the burden of disease and are the leading cause of death in Libya, over 75% of open PHC facilities in Tripoli, Benghazi and the west do not provide NCD services. Access of migrants and refugees to health services is restricted by multiple factors including lack of money, distance, lack of staff, and discrimination at the health facilities. Some of them are held in the detention centers in extremely precarious conditions, where sanitary facilities are insufficient, nutritious food is unavailable, and the lack of access to health create undue suffering among detainees. The overcrowded living conditions, poor hygiene practices, unsafe water and inadequate nutrition are contributing to the spread of infectious diseases such as TB. People with mental health issues, HIV, chronic illnesses and special needs are in critical need of healthcare support. The health sector has identified 3,970,842 people in need of health assistance. This includes 1,662,586 people in areas ranked 4 on the severity scale, and 122,485 people in areas ranked 5. A total of 1,785,072 people have acute health needs . 54% and 4% of baladiyas and 41% and 18% of muhallas were classified as severity scale 4 and 5 respectively.

2 - Activities

1. Provide mobile health outreach services through the deployment of mobile clinics in urban locations, providing primary healthcare and referrals to specialized facilities. 2. Deploy mobile healthcare clinics in migrant detention centres providing integrated essential health service package including emergency care, reproductive, maternal, child and neonatal health care, screening and treatment for communicable and non-communicable diseases, health promotion, vaccination, and MHPSS services. 3. Assist in establishing a functional referral system and linkages at different level of health care system including community, primary health care centers and secondary hospitals. 4. Provide mental health services to migrants, IDPs, and vulnerable members of host communities through regular visits by psychiatrists and psychologists to detention centres and urban areas, as well as weekly mental health outreach days through its mobile clinics. 5. Strengthen the capacity of healthcare providers through capacity building trainings on the complete package of primary health care including case management of communicable diseases and specialized emergency medical services through deployment of the emergency medical teams. 6. Support public health facilities that will be used for referrals in different parts of the country to improve delivery of essential services, through supply of essential equipment and needed supplies for diagnosis. 7. Support the ongoing disease surveillance system through strengthening of the existing Early Warning Alert and Response Network (EWARN) and District health Information system (DHIS-2). This will help ensure early detection and response to disease outbreaks in the detention centers.8. In its role as co-chair of the Migration Health Sub-Working Group (MH-SWG), IOM in collaboration with MOH will facilitate coordination of partners’ activities to in order to avoid duplication and promote complementarities.

4 - Protection risk analysis

Protection needs are amongst the most commonly reported needs in Libya for all people affected by the crisis, whether Libyan or foreign nationals. Overarchingly, protection needs are primarily driven by exposure to conflict and violence, human rights abuses, contamination from explosive hazards in urban centers, and major challenges related to impediments in access to critical services and essential goods and commodities. Militias and armed groups, some even having links to competing governments, carry out arbitrary arrest and detention, torture, unlawful killings, indiscriminate attacks, disappearances, seizure of property and forced displacement. In some instances, such as the siege on Benghazi, armed groups have also imposed restrictions on movements, trapping civilians in hostile and unsafe environments without access to basic necessities. (Human Rights Watch country analysis).

5 - Mitigation measures

The project will mainstream protection as an integral part of its overall intervention; IOM will ensure that access to health services for particularly vulnerable groups is prioritized. IOM utilizes DTM’s Mobility Tracking with protection enhanced indicators to identify specific protection risks and needs of vulnerable groups. The findings from DTM will be considered throughout the project cycle including beneficiary selection, service modality selection, and delivery of assistance. The project will pay special attention to the most in need among the vulnerable population including unaccompanied migrant children, victims of trafficking, and survivors of gender-based violence. The project will work in close coordination with the Protection unit to ensure use of already established referral links and pathways between health and protection actors. Whenever cases with protection concerns are identified by the teams providing basic services, they will be referred to IOM’s Protection Team for further assistance or referral. Additionally, the project will be implemented in line with the international humanitarian principles and protection values, including ‘do no harm’, non-discrimination, meaningful access, safety, dignity, participation, empowerment and accountability. IOM will raise awareness and enhance capacities of national actors through various capacity building activities on rights and protection of migrants, refugees, IDPs and vulnerable host communities.

6.1 - Other accountability mechanisms

Accountability to the affected population is a key concern for IOM programming in all its projects, especially in the humanitarian settings. The project will promote a high level of community participation in the implementation of the project including efforts to strengthen the complaint and feedback mechanism for all beneficiaries. IOM’s mobile outreach teams and partner agencies will directly assess the target populations’ needs during the assessment phase. Through a complaint and feedback mechanism that is under development jointly by all project teams, IOM will also collect information on how beneficiaries perceive services provided by IOM and other service providers, and integrate the feedback to adapt and improve project activities during the implementation period. The result matrix and the monitoring framework will be utilized to identify the completion of the activities. Monitoring and supervisory tools will be used to recognize areas if any corrective measures are required. IOM with other partners are involved in assisting the Ministry of Health in extending the District Health Information System and its form/tool will be explored for capturing the data on the interventions, beneficiaries and the referral mechanism.
