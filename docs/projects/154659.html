Project Summary

The project will support improvements in health and nutrition outcomes for new-borns, children under 5, and their mothers in eight governorates (Anbar, Baghdad, Dahuk, Erbil, Kirkuk, Ninewa, Salah al Din and Sulaymaniyah).
Activities will include routine immunization targeting children under 5 in internally displaced people (IDP) camps and in returnee areas (including Mosul City); promoting optimal Infant and Young Child Feeding (IYCF) practices, with special attention to early initiation of breastfeeding and feeding counselling for new mothers; management of malnourished children including screening, treatment and referral; neonatal and maternal health will be supported via a programme of home visits in IDP camps.
Complementary support to systems strengthening will take the form of provision of health and nutrition supplies including cold chain supplies/equipment, nutritional supplementation, and anthropometric equipment, as well as training of medical and paramedical staff on maternal, neonatal, and child health and nutrition (MNHCN) services. Behaviour change will be encourage through targeted social mobilization interventions (‘Communication for Development’ or C4D) that aim to increase uptake of vaccination services and improve knowledge, attitudes and practices in relation to infant and young child health outcomes.
To enable a smooth exist strategy, UNICEF has been focusing since 2017 on enhancing the capacity of different level medical and paramedical staff to provide quality health care services. Also, supported the overstretched health facilities (due to high influx of IDPs and returnees) with basic rehabilitation and replenishment of supplies and equipment (cold chain supplies, anthropometric equipment, incubators, etc). Furthermore, UNICEF re-constructed the vaccines store in Anbar, Salaheldeen and Ninevah. On the other hand, UNICEF has supported the DOHs to do evidence generation (nutrition survey and assessments and barrier analysis studies on IYCF and vaccination services, cold chain inventory assessment).

Needs

Iraq has recently come out of a major, protracted conflict situation. In the aftermath, there is an urgent need to revitalize health systems and services in host community areas, while simultaneously maintaining delivery of critical health services in camps hosting internally displaced populations (IDP) and in returnee areas. Although more than four million individuals have returned to their places of origin, there remain 1.9 million individuals displaced (International Organisation for Migration, Displacement Tracking Matrix, October 2018). Recent Ministry of Health (MoH) and UN assessments (2017), show that many health facilities including hospitals, Primary Healthcare Centres (PHC) and vaccine stores have been damaged, looted and have had their staff displaced. Additionally, health supplies, including vaccines, have been disrupted or are found to be out of stock. Hospitals and health clinics are running short of medical supplies and medical staff do not have access to sufficient items to cope with the needs of the displaced or returnee populations. The general conditions have been worsened by the interruption of electricity and water supplies, a lack which is still prevalent especially in areas of return. Although the Diphtheria Tetanus and Pertussis (DTP3) coverage has increased to 80 per cent and 82 per cent in 2016 and 2017 respectively, about 500,000 children under five years (U5) remain unvaccinated, placing Iraq among the bottom ten countries in the world for this intervention. More than 30 per cent of health districts in Iraq’s 18 governorates are not able to achieve the 70 per cent DPT3 coverage. Since March 2018, increased cases of measles have been noted in many areas of Iraq, with 623 children reported as affected across the country by October 2018. The MOH, UNICEF, and the World Health Organisation (WHO) agreed to conduct a two-phase national measles campaign, however the operationalization of these campaigns have been impacted by lack of funding. Significant disparities have been observed in Anbar, Kirkuk, Ninewa, and Salah al Din which are among the worst conflict-, displacement- and return-affected areas since 2014. Neonatal deaths are 56 per cent of deaths among U5 children in Iraq. In IDP camps, host community areas and areas of return, neonatal facilities are not at adequate standards. Taking Mosul City in Ninewa as an example, there are currently three hospitals providing delivery services; these facilities are old, have been badly maintained and have certain departments which are totally non-functional. In 2016, the UN Global Estimate Report revealed that neonatal deaths increased from 18/1000 in 1990 to 22/1000 in 2016. Geographic disparities are likewise noted for rates of neonatal death, with an MoH household survey in 2016 revealing significantly high neonatal mortality of 23 per 1000 live births nationally, with disparity between different districts from 35 in Al-Madaen district, Baghdad, 30 in Dibis district, Kirkuk and 28 in Zubair district, Basrah and 21 in both Sheikhan district, Ninewa, and Mergasoor district, Erbil. Hence, there is an absolute and urgent need to ensure availability of high quality Maternal and Neonatal Health (MNH) care with special attention to the most vulnerable areas of Iraq including areas affected by high influx of IDPs and refugees and recently retaken areas where infrastructure is damaged or looted. While there are historic disparities between certain areas of Iraq, these existing differences have been exacerbated in the last four years by the presence of Islamic State in Iraq and the Levant (ISIL) and the resulting large-scale military operations, mass displacement of populations including health workers, and damage and neglect of health infrastructure – for example, prior poor record of health and nutrition-related performance has been noted in Anbar, Kirkuk, Ninewa and Salah al Din in particular (Oral Polio Vaccine [OPV3] at less than 60 per cent in Anbar; stunting of 33 per cent in Ninewa; U5 mortality in Kirkuk, Salah al Din and Ninewa higher than the national rate of 37.9/1000 per live birth i.e., at 42/1000, 47/1000, and 56/1000 respectively [Central Statistics Office and UNICEF, Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey 2012]), Infant and young child feeding (IYCF) practices are still very poor with only 30 per cent of babies initiated into breastfeeding within one hour of birth. To meet this need, growth screening, management of malnutrition, IYCF promotion and counselling are critical interventions to maintain children’s nutritional status. Although wasting is relatively low in IDP camps (Global Acute Malnourishment 5.2 per cent and Severe Acute Malnourishment 2.7 per cent) this needs maintaining through an effective nutrition program that includes regular screening for growth, and early management of malnutrition. Continued population movements, especially in the western governorate of Anbar, present significant epidemiological risks

Activities

Output 1: Immunization services for children in re-taken areas and IDP camps revitalized and sustained. Activities: 1. Include all U5 children affected by emergencies within the routine national Expanded Programme for Immunization - EPI schedule. 2. Support capacity development of EPI managers, staff and vaccinators on proper EPI services including appropriate cold chain system and micro-planning. 3. Procurement of cold chain supplies and equipment. 4. Support social mobilization activities for immunization (mobile teams, orientation sessions, group discussion, IEC materials, TV/Radio broadcasting). 5. Provide timely logistic support (contracts for vaccines distribution to- hard to reach areas, mobile vaccination teams, outreach services, supervisory visits, etc.). 6. Basic rehabilitation of damaged/looted health facilities/vaccine stores. Output 2: Improved quality nutrition services (focusing on IYCF services, management of malnutrition and nutrition supplementation) for the most vulnerable children and mothers. Activities: 1. Support, promote and protect optimal IYCF feeding practices with special attention to breastfeeding counselling (Baby Hut initiative). 2. Procurement of nutritional supplementation, and anthropometric equipment. 3. Conduct relevant training to project sites’ staff on proper nutrition services and strengthening referral services and management capacity for children with SAM. 4. Conduct nutrition communication/social mobilization activities (mobile teams, orientation sessions, group discussion, IEC materials, TV/Radio broadcasting). 5. Conduct growth monitoring at the IDPs camps and facility levels for returnees’ children. Output 3: Maternal and Essential Newborn Care and Continuum of Care services scaled up and sustained in the IDPs’ camps. Activities: 1. Conduct DOHs/districts to roll out a system of home visits for new-borns residing in camps and post-natal care for mothers and breastfeeding counselling and care for Low Birth Weight babies. 2. Conduct DOHs/districts to roll out a system of home visits for new-borns residing in camps and post-natal care for mothers and breastfeeding counselling and care for Low Birth Weight babies. 3. Support the provision of quality Essential New-Born Care services at the health facility level in the selected governorates through training and provision of basic supplies. 4. Supervision and monitoring of field self-assessment of Maternal Neonatal and Child Health MNCH interventions using MOH standard checklist for National MNH quality improvement initiative. 5. Provide comprehensive MNCH communication interventions for social and behavior change to improve maternal and newborn health outcomes.

Protection Risk Analysis

Possibility of developing adverse events due to vaccination which might contribute to the vaccine ingredient and compatibility with the vaccine receivers, or the administration methods that might harm the children. Or the contamination due to the weak precautionary measures. All of which can lead to serious complications for the children and negatively affecting the reputation of the vaccination services. Administrating wrong vaccine is also another potential risk, this can indicate false coverage rate and it can cause the vaccine preventable diseases incidences.

Mitigation Measures

Training of vaccinators on correct administration techniques and adverse events management, quality of vaccination services; contamination mitigation measures and correct waste management. Focused supervision and monitoring to ensure high quality of vaccination service and correct administration using global guidelines and standards. Trained supervisors will offer on—the-job training at vaccination delivery points to ensure quality of services and minimize risk of adverse events following immunization.

How is your project accountable to affected population

UNICEF is accountable to women, boys, girls and men whose lives it aims to improve and places this responsibility at the core of its humanitarian policy. UNICEF interventions are informed by needs assessment of affected populations to determine appropriate responses. Participation by IDPs and returnee communities enables them to play a role in decision-making processes that affect them through the design of UNICEF health and nutrition interventions and the review and evaluation of programmes. Dialogue between UNICEF staff and affected populations is facilitated to ensure effective engagement of beneficiaries and timely information provision. UNICEF has a strong working relationship with the Ministry of Health and its Directorates at governorate level and ensures that these bodies remain the key duty bearers, alongside local non-government organisations (NGO) and civil society organisations (CSO), in the identification of services and supplies needed in the local area, and that these bodies remain accountable to the children and families they serve. Furthermore, UNICEF has established a robust field monitoring system which enabled UNICEF and the counterpart to take informed decisions, better targeting and optimal utilization of resources. UNICEF commits to accountable practice through its leadership of the Nutrition Working Group (NWG), and as an active member of the Health Cluster led by WHO, in coordination with Health cluster and NWG partners. Through direct engagement with NGO, CSO, and community-level partners, UNICEF maintains strong and regular lines of communication both with partners and with beneficiaries, ensuring timely feedback into operational and strategic levels of decision-making.
