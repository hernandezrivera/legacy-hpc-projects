1 - Needs

According to the Protection Cluster Analysis (August 2022), 1.021,561 people are in need of humanitarian assistance in the West Benk (454,697 children, 531,212 adults, 35,652 over 65 and 153,234 PwD). The needs continue to be driven by protracted conflict, over 55 years of Israeli occupation, and widespread violations of international humanitarian and human rights laws, internal Palestinian political divisions, and recurring hostilities. The lack of political progress on the Middle East Peace Process, the failure of Israel as the occupying power to comply with its obligations under international law, combined with the physical and political fragmentation of the Palestinian polity have resulted in a gradual deterioration with grave humanitarian consequences. As at June 2022, 60 Palestinians (14 boys, 5 women) were killed by IF, 479 people were displaced due to demolitions, and 327 settler violence incidents resulting in Palestinian casualties and/or property damage were recorded. So far, 2022 has seen unprecedented increase in settler violence with settlers holding numerous demonstrations across the West Bank, with protesters on multiple occasions blocking main roads, throwing stones and impeding movement for Palestinian residents. Israeli forces restricted the movement of Palestinians in several locations across the West Bank, hindering the access to and functioning of essential services and livelihoods: pupils and teachers faced delays in reaching schools, teaching hours were reduced, and, in some areas, classes were cancelled; delays reaching health facilities were also reported. The protection risks identified by Protection Cluster include: General protection and human rights risks, in particular deterioration in physical well-being due to violence and conflict include: killings & injuries resulting from IF use of force and settler violence; violence in Refugee Camps (IF operations & intra-Palestinian violence); restrictions to freedom of association, peaceful assembly and freedom of opinion and expression. Displacement and risk of forcible transfer are due to coercive environment (evictions, demolitions, settler violence, residency rights, restrictions on movement and access to land, resources and basic services). Psychosocial distress and deterioration in mental well-being risks include: political situation, insecurity and violence, including threats of home demolitions, arrests, night raids and settler violence; escalations of violence / prolonged periods of high tensions Child protection threats/ Grave violations against children due to: use of force and violence against children by IF and settlers resulting in killing and maiming; arbitrary arrest and detention of children, incl. ill-treatment; violence against children in the home (abuse and neglect) and impact on mental well-being; negative coping mechanisms (child marriage and child labour) Specific protection risks facing women and girls include gender-based Violence (GBV) in all its form; and child, early, and forced marriages. The restrictive and discriminatory planning regime applied by Israel in Area C and in East Jerusalem, which continues to prevent Palestinians from addressing basic housing, livelihood and essential service needs. Demolitions represent one element of a coercive environment aimed at forced displacement of Palestinians, the others including the promotion of plans to relocate communities to urban townships; restrictions on access to natural resources; the denial of basic service infrastructure; and the lack of secure residency. These practices are often implemented against a backdrop of the establishment and expansion of Israeli settlements. The situation in oPt is affecting women, men, boys and girls differently. The coercive environment in the West Bank has an especially severe impact on Palestinian children, who experience a constant military presence, frequent clashes and acts of violence, restrictions on movement, and home demolition and destruction of infrastructure and property. Since the beginning of 2022, a total of 20 children have been killed in the West Bank and there are currently 56 outstanding demolition orders against schools in the West Bank including East Jerusalem. Reports indicate a high rate of school dropouts, with specific risks of child labour for boys and early marriages for girls. Girls are often taken out of school out of concern for their safety in the coercive environment, while boys drop out largely because of pressure to contribute to the household finances. Boys also face specific human rights violations, including an increased risk of death and injury inflicted by Israeli security forces during clashes, incidents of stone-throwing and protests, as well as incarceration. Women in the West Bank are disproportionately affected by the loss of homes resulting from demolitions and forced evictions and often face more dire consequences due to the denial of residency rights, such as their inability to enter the workforce due to movement restrictions. The cumulative effects of occupation practices, including restrictions on movement, have affected the equal rights of women and girls and impeded their self-reliance. Women and girls are specifically vulnerable to gender-based violence as they go about their everyday activities. Searches by male soldiers and harassment, including at checkpoints, have affected the movements of women and girls and have served to deprive them of equal access to family life, education, health care and employment. Women and girls have also been subjected to harassment and violent attacks by settlers. Victims and witnesses have reported the use of racist and sexist language by male and female settlers and soldiers directed towards them or towards female family members, causing them anxiety, fear and feelings of humiliation.

2 - Activities

In 2023, NRC Information, Counselling and Legal aid team (ICLA) will target 17,583 Palestinians identified as affected by conflict-related violations and protection risks such as conflict-related violence, risk of forcible transfer, restrictions on freedom of movement and access to services, including livelihoods and settler violence. ICLA will prioritize communities living in Area C, Hebron (H2), Nablus, Bethlehem and Ramallah governorates, Jerusalem periphery and East Jerusalem. ICLA will continue to provide legal counselling (including due diligence, risk-assessment and mitigation, individual and community legal advice, supported referrals), as well as legal assistance, including paralegal services, accompaniment, follow up or court representation in order to ensure the best possible individual legal protection outcomes (e.g. ensuring temporary/permanent protection from evictions/demolitions, obtaining residency and family unification, filing complaints re: settler violence, access to socioeconomic rights, particularly of women, people with disability and other vulnerable groups, etc.). Tailored services will aim to reach people in financial hardship, women, youth and children, IDPs and UNRWA refugees, Bedouin / herding communities and people with disabilities, as well as Palestinian workers and small-business owners at heightened risk of negative impact of discriminatory policies and practices. ICLA is currently carrying out gender participation assessment in order to identify needs and gaps in response and design activities that would tailor specifically hard to reach population, specifically women, youth and children. The outreach strategy will be developed during 2023. In the meantime, ICLA has already strengthened the team with engaging women lawyers specialised in inclusion (of women, children, youth, people with disability) and instituting or expanding collaboration, coordination and partnership both internally within NRC and externally with NGO sector (e.g. Maan, WCLAC, HI, SC, etc.) and with the PA with a view to address some of the barriers to participation of the hard to reach population in ICLA response. For example, NRC is implementing a project aimed at enhancing East Jerusalem Palestinian women participation in employment through providing legal aid and advocating for opening daycare facilities for children, delivering occupational training and employment guidance and ensuring equal access to insurance schemes and in the West Bank, following complaints of sexual harassment at a checkpoint, the “women only” lane was secured through NRC intervention. Similarly, in order to reach children and youth, ICLA has worked with NRC Education team to develop age-sensitive materials on life-skills and protection from conflict-related violence. ICLA is also carrying out digital outreach survey in order to assess the different needs, capacities, skills and knowledge of different age and gender groups regarding the legal content, which will inform the development and design of awareness raising and sensitisation activities through social media. To ensure accessibility, ICLA will continue the Mobile Legal Aid Clinic (MLAC) for Wall-Affected Communities and expand MLAC modality for other hard-to-reach areas (e.g. Seam Zone, remote villages/communities). In term of policy change, ICLA will continue to use a range of complementary approaches and strategies to expose, challenge or change unjust laws and policy, including legal research and analysis of domestic laws and international humanitarian and human rights laws, case-based advocacy, data collection, collation, analysis and dissemination, and Public Interest Cases (PIC) to secure individual/HH/community protection and/or temporary solution. ICLA will continue to provide support to national capacities through training, mentoring, secondment, facilitating knowledge network, and provision of material and technical assistance to improve quality of legal interventions. ICLA will also continue to lead the Legal Task Force (LTF) and coordinate activities of legal aid actors by engaging them through regular meetings, workshops, exchange of legal briefs/alerts, research and policy documents, etc. In addition, ICLA will intensify joint response with the West Bank Protection Consortium (WBPC), ensure representation in other coordination forums (East Jerusalem and Area C Task Force, Education Cluster, Protection Cluster, Child Protection Working Group) and proactively seek to engage with other stakeholders in order to improve non-legal forms of protection and assist in addressing the socio-economic needs in targeted areas. In order to ensure an integrated holistic approach that supports and amplifies legal aid, ICLA is working within elaborated referral system jointly with other NRC core competences, West Bank Protection Consortium partners, and UN OCHA, as well as with local authorities and village councils.

3 - Indicators

Outcome indicators: • 97% of each male and female beneficiaries and 97% of households in East Jerusalem and Area C who have eviction or demolition orders remain in their homes due to programme services • 75% of male and 72% of female beneficiaries who obtain permits to access their property in the Seam Zone as a result of programme services • 89% of male and 85% of female beneficiaries who were able to retrieve confiscated equipment • 74% of male and 76% of female beneficiaries who obtain residency status or recognized legal identity rights and access services as a result of programme services • 92% of male and 88% of female beneficiaries who report/are observed taking appropriate actions towards securing their rights as a result of legal counselling services. • 94% of each male and female beneficiaries participating in information sessions who report the information presented will assist them in their daily lives • 84% of male and 86% of female beneficiaries receiving training who competently identify their rights • 73% of male and 71% of female beneficiaries who are able to exercise their HLP rights • 81% of male and 85% of female beneficiaries who report ability to act on HLP rights • 50% of beneficiaries (25% male/25% female; 45% 20-50 years old/5% under 20 years old) reached through consultation in the establishment of community-based complaint mechanisms (CBCMs), awareness activities and community mobilisation interventions on PSEA including how to report SEA-related complaints. Output indicators: • 1,190 HHs/direct beneficiaries (928 male/ 262 female – including 60 elderly, 48 children, 18 with disability, 48 Bedouin/herding community, 129 women headed household, 11 UNRWA registered) and 5,950 indirect beneficiaries, members of the household receiving legal assistance on HLP, residency, access and workers right • 200 HHs/direct beneficiaries/HHs (114 male/ 86 female – including 10 elderly, 18 with disability, 20 Bedouin/herding community, 15 women headed household, 6 UNRWA registered refugees) and 1,000 indirect beneficiaries, members of the households receiving legal counselling on HLP, residency, access and workers right • 130 beneficiaries (101 male/ 29 female) legal awareness session • 32 public interest cases (8,803 beneficiaries of which 4,624 male/ 4,179 female) challenging unjust HLP, legal identity, residency, freedom of movement and other IHL and IHRL issues • 60 (28 male/ 32 female) professionals receiving technical training • 250 (118 male/ 132 female – including 13 elderly, 18 with disability, 80 women headed household) beneficiaries receiving legal paralegal services • 850 technical support services provided to partners, local and national authorities • 35 reports/factsheets on IHL/IHRL violations, conflict-related violence and relevant trends • 50 legal briefings/field visits • 10 consultation sessions with communities exposed to settler violence The programme contributes to alleviating the gender-specific constraints that women and girls face as a result of prevailing gender norms and roles in society based on the premise that meeting women’s specific needs, rights and interests is an important and first step to achieve gender equality. In 2022 the ambition is to drive change by considering specific opportunities for gender equality. NRC has already partnered up with local organisations working on empowering women to access their socio-economic rights. Through focus group discussions, workshops, learning events with female lawyers, universities and institutions working on family protection and other crosscutting areas of law, the programme will embed gender considerations and contribute to changing the legal landscape. Other initiatives include commissioning an IHRL/IHL legal expert opinion on the legality and differential impact of Israeli HLP/residency violations on women and/or a study on women’s awareness of their HLP rights and how involved and empowered they are in joint decision making on issues that affect them (demolitions, evictions, loss of livelihoods opportunities). In 2023, NRC ICLA will work with the Protection and Education Clusters to secure that collective awareness-raising activities are integrated into the PSEA plan to ensure appropriate and consistent PSEA messaging to communities as in community engagement initiatives such as posters and flyers. In addition, ICLA will work jointly with NRC Education programme on incorporating awareness-raising, sensitization and capacity building for parents, teaching and non-teaching staff at school level.

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

Through the Complaints and Feedback mechanism, NRC is committed to strict standards of transparency and accountability across all core competencies where everyone has the right to file a complaint and receive a response. NRC’s CFM focal points in West bank and Gaza respond to all requests/complaints directly or within 14 days as per SOP. The complaints and feedback received by NRC is confidential and closely monitored to be used to improve our working methods making our programs more responsive, effective, and appropriate to the needs of the affected populations. Across core competencies, the CFM team, based on the feedback or complaint received, have the responsibility to inform program teams to respond and act to bring positive change, and continuously inform the community on the appropriate actions taken to respond to the feedback or complaint. NRC CFM contributes to organizational learning, and continuous improvement of accountability and quality of NRC’s work to be gender-sensitive, safe, and confidential, avoiding any risk, harm, or retaliation to our affected population. CFM and M&E units compile, and analyze data collected through various learning exercises and then share the learning and findings with program management. This includes findings and instances of learning from all M&E exercises, field staff observations, outcome surveys, recommendations from evaluations, and recommendations from complaints or feedback received. Program management are accountable to ensure that the learning takes place, and recommendations and findings are incorporated into projects as much as possible.

9.1d - Explain how you have ensured that mechanism are accessible to all population groups?

In 2020, NRC Palestine launched a Digital Complaints and Feedback Mechanism, informative sessions on the use and application of NRC’s CFM were conducted for all staff and targeted populations in WB and Gaza. Soft and hard copies of CFM information cards were distributed to targeted populations in WB and Gaza. Through this digital CFM, beneficiaries can seek information requests, provide feedback and complaints through automated WhatsApp and Phone calls, including a designated CFM email address and complaint boxes at NRC offices to be used by beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries during office walk-ins. The CFM and M&E units collect information from the people we serve throughout distinct phases of the project cycle through different channels such as surveys, key informative interviews, monthly phone calls and FGDs to make sure all targeted community members are satisfied with our CFM channels and are aware of this mechanism, also to capture the impact of NRC’s services on the target population, as well as beneficiaries’ feedback on quality and process of CFM. CFM focal points regularly follow up with beneficiaries after they receive NRC services to measure relevance, timeliness, and effectiveness of interventions including post distribution monitoring, follow up outcome monitoring exercises which are used to capture beneficiaries’ feedback.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

In order to prioritize safety and dignity of Palestinians at risk or affected by forcible transfer over other considerations, NRC ICLA teams, in coordination with the protection staff, will conduct protection risk assessments , adopt conflict and context sensitive approach in dealing with beneficiaries, ensure informed beneficiary consent and acting on instructions of beneficiaries, securing confidentiality and data protection. In addition, ICLA will use additional protection technical expertise to supplement its interventions; coordinate with specialised protection actors to assist with conducting protection risk assessment of ICLA BNFs, carry out combined ICLA/protection campaign around protection issues; in collaboration with gender / child protection actors, conduct joint legal and protection research around a particular protection issue or risk in order to inform programming or recruit staff with expert protection skills in NRC. For example, although the legal framework might enshrine certain rights and entitlements that NRC would ideally like to pursue (e.g. strategic litigation), NRC will not do so, if the process of accessing justice were assessed as likely to do harm to the claimants. Another example concerns the provision of legal aid prior to delivery of humanitarian assistance and includes: 1. a community protection analysis to generate legal vulnerability profiling, which in turn informs the design of the humanitarian interventions including community-tailored legal awareness sessions. 2. Next, legal advice is provided with respect to specific list of activities aimed to be delivered within a specific community. In some cases, the legal advice would be to suspend the implementation of the project as it might pose a risk on the community, or it could have a negative effect on pending legal procedures. NRC’s Protection Policy, Gender Policy, CoC and Child Safeguarding Policy and guidance notes and tools including Core Competency Quality Assessment tools and the Protection Resource Pack all contribute to reinforcing and providing guidance on how to action the protection policy. NRC’s Code of Conduct holds staff, partners and associates accountable, while projecting a common set of standards to the beneficiaries being served. PSAE policies and related mechanisms are included in the project design . At the community level, ICLA will support multi-sectoral, sustainable, and integrated programming with other humanitarian actors (e.g. through WBPC), which will allow people to preserve or increase their resources and opportunities, thus making them more resilient, and more likely to find durable solutions. ICLA will ensure community engagement on different levels of PCM and echoing community feedback. For example, ICLA has ensured sufficient female staff lawyers are in the field in hard to reach communities carrying out information sessions and counselling targeting specifically women on subjects such as access to healthcare, education, freedom of movement, etc. Similarly, MLAC is specifically tailored to address the legal aid needs that affect women in particular (e.g. residency issues) in Wall affected communities. ICLA will conduct needs assessments, service mapping and where relevant, refer beneficiaries to other NRC core competencies or other partners providing specific services. NRC ICLA will support UNOCHA in the assessment, dissemination of information and implement referral pathways in order to ensure coordinated prevention and response to emergency. ICLA will actively and regularly participate in the Protection Cluster, lead Legal Task Force and collaborate with other Clusters and actors on awareness raising, sensitisation, outreach and information dissemination activities to ensure that people have meaningful access to information and feedback mechanisms.NRC Advocacy unit will seek to maintain the humanitarian situation in WB on the international agenda to shape priorities and address the impact of the GoI .

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

RC ICLA programme is designed to ensure that the most vulnerable Palestinians in hard-to-reach areas have access to basic rights and humanitarian assistance, reduce their vulnerability and build resilience. Information dissemination and counselling: ICLA’s main priorities will be to empower women, men, girls and boys to have equal access to information and services. ICLA will develop information and communication products to access basic services, rights and entitlements (e.g. online, social media, WhatsApp etc.) Accurate, timely and relevant legal information and awareness-raising messages will be disseminated through outreach activities, including face-to-face information and counselling by trained field staff. ICLA will also identify and train members of the communities to disseminate information, recognise protection concerns and refer cases to specialised service providers. For example, in 2022 ICLA team has received training in GBV, legal protection of children and the use of Washington Principles. In fact, the programme has already adapted its intake to ensure age, gender and disability/impairment are included and measured. Moreover, ICLA is currently in discussion with other actors such as HI, SC, WLAC as well as NRC Education to seek their support in tailoring ICLA’s activities to the different needs of men, women, boys and girls and people with disability (e.g. developing content or form of legal information material that is AGD sensitive) and work out joint activities and mutual referral mechanism. ICLA will use judicial and administrative remedies to support access to justice for the most vulnerable including women, children and people with disabilities. Approaches and procedures will be selected on a case-by-case basis to allow for a customised response that meets the specific needs and interests of the beneficiary(s). In this respect, carefully selected and trained fe/male officers may provide legal information and advice, facilitate negotiations, and if relevant, provide legal representation. ICLA’s Mobile Legal Aid Clinic is specifically designed to reach marginalized communities. Flexible and creative approaches will be used to address barriers to access to justice such as health, education, costs, distance, etc. ICLA will also provide technical support (sensitisation, training, orientation, etc.) to local NGOs, youth and women leaders and community representatives, as well as humanitarian actors with a view to strengthening their capacity to ensure protection from IHL/IHRL violations. Subject to needs assessment, ICLA may also provide financial and organisational support to implementing partner to strengthen their capacity and ensure sustainability of the intervention.: Advocacy activities aim for the GoI to align its policies and practices in the OPTs with its obligations as the occupying power under IHL and IHRL. The project will apply an IHRL framework to advance a rights-based discourse that both emphasises the duty bearers’ responsibilities under IHL and highlights violations through accessible and compelling rights-based messaging. ICLA recognises the fundamental need to address the different impacts of conflict and disasters on and promote the potential for positive transformation of gender norms. With regard to the situation in the West Bank, the programme contributes to removing the gender specific barriers for women, children and youth in different ways, for example by alleviating the psychosocial impact of the threat of displacement by reassuring the entire family that the demolition or eviction is being challenged, providing at least temporary protection at no cost to the family. Additionally, delays in demolition and eviction can be for many years, allowing families time to plan for a future displacement.
