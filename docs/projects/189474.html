Project Summary

The proposed program will work toward achieving health cluster objective (Reduce excess morbidity and mortality by timely detection and coordinated response to epidemic prone diseases) by protecting the target population from malaria and other Vector Borne Diseases (VBD). This will be achieved through appropriate and timely implementation of WHO approved Integrated Vector Management (IVM) interventions. Indoor Residual Spraying (IRS), mosquito larviciding and fly control measures will be employed as tools to disrupt disease transmission together with educating the community on the diseases itself and how to prevent infection through adopting desired positive behavior. Similarly, to improve the quality of health service delivery, nursing and medical staff will be trained on malaria and other VBD differential diagnosis and case management. MENTOR has been a member of HRP for two consecutive years and recently have successfully implemented the mentioned activities in Bentiu, Renk and Ulang Malakal, Maban and Ajoung Thok with significant results achieved.
This program will aim to support 179,225 individuals which include 112,829 in Bentiu IDP camp, 34,396 In Malakal PoC and 32,000 flood affected community living in makeshift IDPs sites in Bentiu town. 1 - Needs

Malaria is a major public health problem in South Sudan and accounted for 52.6 per cent of all morbidities and 58.2 per cent of all mortalities in 2021 . Transmission season starts in June/ July onwards and is sustained through until October/ November, however it may extend further in case of flooding or prolonged rainy season. Food insecurity is wide spread with an estimated 8.3 million people to face acute food insecurity till July 2022. Food insecurity can cause malnutrition which is closely associated with severity of malaria and diarrheal diseases. There were 835,000 IDPs between May and December 2021 of which 80 per cent were from Jonglei, Unity and Upper Nile states . Malakal PoC in Upper Nile State, is currently hosting 34,396 IDPs while the number of IDPs in Bentiu camp in Unity State is 112,829. Similarly, there are 32,000 people recently displaced by floods and have been residing in four makeshift camps in Rubkona town. The high rainfall and frequent flooding in both sites, together with the cramped and overcrowded setting of the camps, provide optimum conditions for mosquito breeding and high malaria transmission which can lead to major malaria epidemics. The nature of the soil in both Malakal PoC, Bentiu IDP sites and outside in Rubkona town drains water very poorly so small water pools form quickly and rest on the top of the ground. This increases the number of semi-permanent/permanent breeding sites for mosquitoes that remain for weeks/months post rainy season prolonging malaria transmission. Moreover, the Bentiu IDP site is a naturally swampy area and, despite the best efforts of the systematized drainage system, the standing water in channels surrounding the blocks are often found with high concentration of mosquito larvae even in the dry season. In 2018, the local mosquito population in Bentiu was found to be over 90% resistant to the pyrethroids class of insecticides – this is commonly used in the long-lasting insecticidal mosquito nets that are distributed in mass campaigns. Therefore, the recent or future programmes of LLIN mass distribution, although providing personal protection to the user, cannot be considered an effective tool for controlling mosquito populations. Furthermore, sandflies, the vector for visceral leishmaniasis (VL), are smaller than mosquitoes therefore can move more easily through small holes of LLINs reducing the protection of the user.Previous years have proven that late or absence of vector control interventions causes malaria epidemics with devastating consequences. This programme will provide life-saving protection for the IDP community of Bentiu IDP camp and outside in Rubkona town and those in the Malakal PoC by targeting disease vectors and therefore preventing transmission of malaria and other vector-borne diseases

2 - Project activities

The MENTOR Initiative proposes to respond to the health cluster objective, reduce excess morbidity and mortality by timely detection and coordinated response to epidemic prone diseases through:• Indoor Residual Spraying: The control of adult mosquito disease vectors through IRS is key in reducing the risk of malaria infection. MENTOR will procure WHO prequalified insecticides for IRS for use in all the three target locations. Local community workers will be recruited and trained in implementation of IRS by following WHO guidelines. To ensure an optimal level of protection in line with WHO recommendations, MENTOR will aim to achieve at least 85% coverage of shelters prior to the onset of the rainy season. Impact of the IRS intervention will be measured through analysis of weekly malaria incidence reports collected from health facilities in the target locations. Empty sachets/bottles of insecticides will be disposed of properly. • Larviciding for mosquito control: Complimenting IRS, reducing the vector breeding capacity through Larval Source Management (LSM) ensures that the disease transmission capacity of Anopheles, Aedes as well as Culex mosquitoes is limited through preventing larval development. The WHO-recommended larvicide Abate® 500E will be applied to all stagnant water containing mosquito larvae. The prevention field staff will routinely assess all potential breeding sites and 90-95% will be treated in 3 rounds of activity. • Fly Control: Flies can act as mechanical vectors for a multitude of diseases. The WHO-recommended safe chemical Pyriproxyfen will be used for application in latrines, rubbish piles and open defecation sites. The prevention field staff will routinely assess all potential fly breeding sites across the IDP sites and target host communities and 90-95% will be treated in 3 rounds of activity. Staff will also work closely with the WASH cluster and WASH partners to coordinate the timing of intervention. • Capacity building for health care workers: The capacity of health care workers will be improved by providing training on differential diagnosis and case management of malaria and other VBDs. They will be trained on the pathology, symptoms, correct differential diagnosis, and case management of VBDs that contribute to morbidity and mortality amongst the target population. On the job training will be provided to serve as follow-up supervision visits. A module on COVID-19 IPC will also be taught. • Information, Education and Communication: This will be implemented through door-to-door IEC campaigns as well as health education sessions for community leaders. Integrated IEC will focus on delivering a variety of health messages, such as the promotion of bed net usage, signs and symptoms of VBDs and promotion of early treatment seeking behaviours, prevention of mosquito breeding sites, good waste management, good hygiene and sanitation practices to reinforce the relationship between WASH and VBD control, and the importance of adhering to COVID-19 preventative measures. This program will aim to support 179,225 individuals which include 112,829 in Bentiu IDP camp, 34,396 In Malakal PoC and 32,000 flood affected community in Rubkona town. The total beneficiaries (179, 225) are comprised of 16,210 males under five, 15,433 females under five, 26,475 male 6 to 17 years of age, 26,535 females 6 to 17 years, 38,270 male 18 to 60 years, 35,710 female 18 to 6 years, 11,090 males above 60 years and 9,502 females above 60 years. This program is also going to benefit 26,884 people with disability in all the target locations

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The nature of MENTOR activities is community-based. Indoor Residual Spraying, larviciding, fly control and health education sessions are carried out at the community level. Capacity building of health staff and medical commodities are provided to health facilities run by different health partners. Beneficiary feedback is key in all of this and continuously collected throughout the programme. As mentioned before, MENTOR engages with the camp management feedback and reporting structures that enable the target communities to make complaints and / or suggestions without having to meet with MENTOR staff directly. All of MENTOR’s staff are also trained in being focal points for feedback and can be approached at any time during the programme implementation. Any feedback is then immediately fed back into the programme management cycle and used to continuously improve service delivery and impact.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

During IEC/BCC and IRS campaigns MENTOR teams visit community members door-to-door and have the chance of directly meeting and engaging with individuals living with disabilities, illiteracy, as well as old people and women and girls. MENTOR staff also encourages people who are left behind to attend in health education sessions. This ensures that every beneficiary has the chance of voicing concerns and giving feedback as well as suggestions without having to travel or overcome other barriers.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

All MENTOR staff are enrolled in Preventing Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) training and MENTOR will enroll all newly recruited staff in PSEA training as well. MENTOR will make sure to analyze any Gender Based Violence (GBV)-related risks and work with the responsible NGOs, INGOs, governmental bodies to address these risks, and report and refer potential GBV cases to the related stakeholders. Referral to specialized services is essential for cases which are identified through protection monitoring, at the community centres or through other forms of outreach activities. It is also an integral part of case management and is therefore critical despite potential limited availability of services. It is essential that referral pathways and service/s mapping information are regularly updated at local level to facilitate referrals and related activities. MENTOR will also ensure that all beneficiaries are aware that all MENTOR staff have been trained appropriately and that they can be approached at any time with any protection issues. Staff will then refer them to specialized services.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

MENTOR has its own Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) policy that is guided by WHO, the Inter Agency Standing Committee (IASC), and the Sphere humanitarian standards. The essence of MENTOR’s AAP document is that the organisation is committed to: 1. Taking Account – through participation (enabling affected populations to play an active role in the decision-making processes) feedback and complaints (actively seeking the views of affected populations to improve policy and practice in programming) and design, monitoring and evaluation (Design, monitor and evaluate the goals and objectives of programmes with the involvement of affected populations) 2. Giving Account – through transparency (providing accessible and timely information to affected populations on organizational procedures, structures and processes to ensure that they can make informed decisions and choices) 3. Being Held to Account – through leadership and governance (demonstrating their commitment to accountability to affected populations by ensuring feedback). At the beginning of each program, introductory meetings are held with all the beneficiaries. In such meetings, extensive project activities details are shared, which include the project goal, objectives, name of staff, the duration, activities, office addresses, as well as the name and contact details of the program manager and other relevant individual. MENTOR also actively engages with the camp management feedback and reporting structures that enable the target communities to make complaints and / or suggestions without having to meet with MENTOR staff directly. This contributes to ensuring an environment in which target communities feel able to give feedback without any fear of a negative reaction/consequences

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

8.1.1 Likelihood: Medium Impact: Medium MENTOR’s activities are carried out, wherever possible, directly in the communities, exposing beneficiaries to as few risks as possible. Before the start of IRS, Larviciding and Fly control activities, the community workers will be selected from the target communities and then trained on implementation of these activities The training of spray teams will be based on the latest WHO operational manual for IRS for malaria transmission, control and elimination (2015). The training will entail the safe handling of the WHO approved public health insecticides, the correct use of protective clothing and equipment (PPE), the maintenance of equipment, the correct application of the insecticide, waste management, as well as health education topics related to IRS such as the removal of house items before spraying and precautions after the shelter has been sprayed. The training will be conducted for two days just before launching these activities. Each worker will be provided with a standard set of PPE including an over all, gum boots, face visors, base cap, face and cloth wash soap, rubber gloves and face masks. Participants will also be taught about the Covid-19 risk communication strategy which emphasizes the importance of maintaining a physical distance of 2 meters between individuals, avoidance of crowded places, wearing of mask and frequent hand washing with clean water and soap. For communal activities, distributions or IEC messaging, central places in communities are preferred, shortening distances for beneficiaries as much as possible.8.5.1 Likelihood: Low Impact: Medium By their very nature, MENTOR’s programme activities do not discriminate against people based on gender, age or disability. Malaria and most other VBDs affect all ages and genders, so entire communities are targeted in order to ensure maximum protection, reduce the human pool of infection and onward disease transmission. The prevention activities provide benefits to all household members. During IRS, larviciding campaigns and IEC sessions, MENTOR encourages equal participation of all genders, age groups and people with disabilities in planning, implementation and monitoring of core activities. Previous and current MENTOR records indicate a balanced participation of women/men and youths (both boys and girls), and vulnerable groups are identified and prioritized in terms of service delivery.• 8.8.1 Likelihood: High Impact: Medium MENTOR programmes target all beneficiaries in a project area, so that no people will be left out or subject to less services as a result of partial selection and/or selection processes that negate the principle of offering services strictly on a needs basis. MENTOR programmes have experienced the occurrence of favouritism in the past in relation to the selection of daily workers for IRS, larviciding and fly control campaigns, leading to pressure to recruit local staff that may not have high qualifications. This risk is mitigated by closely working together with the local communities and recruiting directly from them. All daily workers also need to meet several criteria to be considered, depending on the prospective activities.• 8.9.1 Likelihood: Medium Impact: Medium MENTOR has a very advanced security system, with which newly arising security threats can be quickly assessed and necessary precautions be taken. Should the need arise, staff can quickly be evacuated and brought to safety. MENTOR also has a track record of delivering programmes in insecure areas to not leave vulnerable people without support, should this be possible under the circumstances.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

Studies have shown a synergistic relationship between malaria and malnutrition. It has been suggested that malnutrition may predispose children to malaria infection or make it more difficult to recover from infection. Different forms of malnutrition have been relatively consistently associated with high-density parasitaemia and anaemia, both associated with severe malaria. Moreover, severe acute malnutrition is believed to be linked to intestinal malabsorption of medicines which can cause sub-optimal drug exposure and contribute further to poor patient outcomes and accelerated selection of antimalarial drug resistance. At the same time, malaria infection may predispose a child to acute weight loss or impair response to standard treatment for malnutrition and previous studies have shown malaria infection to be associated with poor nutritional status. Therefore, Effective collaboration between malaria control and nutrition intervention programmes is essential for proper case management and decreasing the prevalence of global acute malnutrition in children.
