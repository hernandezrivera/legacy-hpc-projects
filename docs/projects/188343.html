Project Summary

More than 11 years of destructive conflict in Syria has hit those who are least responsible the hardest – children. Humanitarian and protection needs are still acute. With basic infrastructure and services decimated by the conflict and 6.7 million people internally displaced, 12.23 million people are in need of health services in 2022, including 4.4 million displaced persons, 1.33 million children under 5 years – including an estimated 503,000 live births expected, and 3.38 million women of reproductive age (15-49 years). Across Syria, 137,000 children under 5 suffer from acute malnutrition (wasting) half a million children are stunted and 41.6% of children under 23 months suffer from anemia, meaning they are unlikely to reach their full potential in life. The current economic downturn and worsening food insecurity across Syria will likely contribute to the deterioration of the nutrition status of both mothers and children (Draft HNO - FSL 2022).
Over the last year in the face of the COVID-19 pandemic, food prices across Syria have hugely increased and the value of the Syrian Pound has plummeted. This has left communities vulnerable to food insecurity amongst many other risks. Food insecurity is directly related with the risk of malnutrition, highest for PLWs and children under 5 year who have an increased nutritional need (Save the Children, Nutrition SMART Surveys Final report – North-east Syria camps, March 2021). Caregivers of children under 2 years and pregnant and lactating persons (CPLPs) will be provided with MIYCN services in the target areas. Children under 5 years will be screened for acute malnutrition and treatment will be provided at the facility or through referral to nearest health facility. SC will follow-up all cases of malnourished children until they complete their treatment and achieve the desired nutritional outcomes.
The nutrition teams will work with the health teams to provide integrated nutrition services – inclusive of screening, treatment, IYCF (infant and young children feeding) awareness, early initiation of breastfeeding, delivering nutrition supplies, and applying CMAM (community management of acute malnutrition) to treat malnutrition cases and referral for complicated cases and acutely malnourished infants below 6 months for inpatient treatment services. There has been an increase in food-insecure populations across Syria. Over 12 million people affected by food insecurity especially where there is protracted displacement. SC will coordinate with the food security and livelihoods (FSL) sector and Nutrition Sector and prioritize cash-voucher assistance to support nutrition-sensitive programs. All PLWs and caregivers of CU-5 within the intervention population will be linked with MPCA support to enable improved dietary practices; particularly, improved dietary diversity for reduction of micronutrient deficiencies amongst infants and young children and PLW, and improved child growth and development. Mothers delivering at the health facilities integrated within the nutrition program will be automatically enrolled for MPCA and nutrition counselling. Mothers requiring PSS and GBV support will be referred to the protection teams for additional assistance.
The target population include mothers, children (aged 6-23 months) as well as children with disability. Centralized Health Information Management system will be used for collecting and compiling all intervention data both internally and also for 4Ws reporting at the sector level. MEAL teams will enable program beneficiaries to share feedback on quality of services, accessibility and available services. The M&E team and health team will ensure protection of health data and control accessibility to only authorized personnel.

4 - Needs

In August 2021, UNICEF estimated that approximately half a million children in NES are suffering from chronic malnutrition, with an additional 137,000 CU5 experiencing acute malnutrition. A recent (unpublished) study by SC and the Nutrition Working Group in Al-Hasakeh, Ar-Raqqa, and Deir ez-Zoir governorates, and the initial results indicate that 20% of the 500 surveyed children aged 6-59 months using MUAC and WHZ scores were found to be severely or moderately acutely malnourished. An additional 9% were in the at-risk category . The same survey also estimated Minimum Dietary Diversity (MDD) in communities surrounding the health facilities through a household survey conducted with mothers of infants and children 6-23 months. Initial analysis suggests that 94% of children were not reaching the recommended MDD, risking their overall development and subjecting them to risks of acute and chronic malnutrition, micronutrient deficiencies and diseases. According to the SMART Surveys conducted in 2019 and 2020 in NWS, the Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) rates soared from 0.7% to 2.5%. Similar data for NES is not yet available, but the Nutrition Working Group is planning to undertake it in February 2022.The lack of integration of malnutrition management at PHCs due to underfunded nutrition services, limited access of humanitarian service providers, and difficulty delivering supplies through cross-border access points, have contributed to poor coverage of treatment and surveillance.

5 - Implementation of activities

The intervention will improve access to quality life-saving nutrition services through integration of nutrition services in static health facilities in hard-to-reach regions of Syria. These facilities will provide integrated nutrition services for communities affected by the water crisis, socio-economic challenges and vulnerabilities related to war and displacement. The nutrition program will systematically prevent, identify, treat, and refer, where required, acutely malnourished children between 6-59 months by providing integrated nutrition interventions of CMAM and MIYCN. Referral and follow-up mechanism will be established for providing specialized care when needed. Special focus will be given to early initiation of breastfeeding and early identification and treatment of acute malnutrition by empowering mothers to use MUAC screening to effectively detect children with acute malnutrition and link to appropriate service points (mother-led MUAC). SC will coordinate with the Nutrition Sector in WoS and other health and nutrition partners for building capacity to integrate on aforementioned interventions across PHCs and avoid any duplication of activities. In addition, community engagement and group counselling sessions will be provided. Field teams will aid in establishing family support groups (M2MSGs/F2FSGs) and encourage family members to be engaged in providing support to women and children in improving the nutrition practices within the family unit.

9 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk 1: Lack of or inadequate collective information provision, feedback and participation of affected communities due to irregular access to the communities and remote management of health services. Likelihood: high, Impact: medium. Mitigation: Establish/strengthen local partnerships, building the capacity of partners to effectively engage with communities, and strengthen community-based health services to facilitate dialogue with communities. Ensuring that community voice and patient feedback are included in the Third-Party Monitoring mechanisms for health programs. Risk 2: Barriers to access or exclusion of some vulnerable and minority from accessing health services for different reasons such as children and adults with disabilities and denial of access to certain areas that have shifted lines of control. Likelihood: medium, Impact: high. Mitigation: Analyze and disaggregate all data by sex, age and disability. Analyze the needs of vulnerable and minority groups throughout the program cycle with identification of risk factors and rights violations impacting service provision for beneficiaries. Risk 3: Breach or leakage of sensitive data and information containing patient names, medical conditions and health care provider names into the wrong hands maybe can put people and communities at risk of targeting, shame, abuse and even death. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: Electronic data and information should be properly protected and backed up.


10 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

Risks: Women and girls not able to access services or participate in income generating activities due to sexual harassment; exploitation and abuse by aid workers; female heads of HHs and adolescent girls in particular risk of sexual exploitation in exchange to services (Voices from Syria, 2022); risk of not reporting SEA for fear of retaliation; lack of awareness on reporting channels; stigma and shame and fear of losing on the services making it difficult for SC to hold staff/partners involved accountable. Mitigations: SC has a Child Safe Guarding policy which includes PSEA. To mitigate the risk of PSEA during the assistance delivery, the following will be included: mandatory training and regular refreshers on Child Safeguarding, Safe programming and PSEA policy to SC and partner staff; working closely with the WoS PSEA TF to strengthen feedback, reporting and response mechanisms for SEA; conducting community awareness and sensitization sessions on PSEA with emphasis on reporting and ensuring safety of survivors through referrals; regular participatory safety audit to identify protection and safety concerns arising as a result of project implementation and developing action plans to mitigate identified risks collaboratively with the community; including PSEA messages and topics in all IEC materials and training sessions conducted under the project; ensuring that centrality of protection is embedded in all projects to mitigate risks and prevent potential harm.


11 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Access and security: SC regularly reviews and updates contingency plans for its operations inside Syria to mitigate against context changes. This has recently been updated for NES and NWS based, including multiple potential scenarios regarding changes in territorial control, continuation or interruption of cross-border access and access to locations of implementation. Fraud Prevention: SC has zero tolerance for interference and/or diversion of aid to any parties to the conflict. All SC staff complete mandatory trainings, which include Fraud, Bribery, and Corruption and Aid Diversion and Financing of Terrorism. Financial, procurement and supply chain procedures further eliminate the risk through multiple vetting processes and checks, including the partners. Risks due to the COVID-19 pandemic: The situation may result in risks to beneficiaries due to lack of access to programming sites and spread of infection amidst beneficiaries and health staff members. Relevant program adaptations have been developed and all COVID19 – health and nutrition sector SoPs are being implemented at all sites. Weak logistics capacity: Field staff have received technical and systems trainings on processes and management and contingency plans are in place in case of loss of access. SC has signed framework agreements with local suppliers in Turkey to be able to deliver aid across Syria in a timely manner.
