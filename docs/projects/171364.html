Project Summary

Somalia continues to face a dire humanitarian situation, with an estimated 5.2 million people in need of humanitarian assistance. This is attributed to cumulative shocks, including erratic 2020 Gu season, widespread and severe seasonal flooding, desert locust infestation, socio-economic impacts of COVID-19 and protracted conflict and flood-related displacements.
According to the 2020 Post Gu Seasonal Food Security and Nutrition Analysis for September (FAO/FSNAU), an estimated 1.3 million people are facing Crisis or worse (IPC Phase 3 or higher) outcomes through September 2020. This number is expected to increase to 2.1 million people through December 2020, in the absence of humanitarian assistance. An additional 2.5 million people are Stressed (IPC Phase 2), bringing the total number of people experiencing acute food insecurity to 3.8 million. In addition, 849,900 children under the age of five years face acute malnutrition over the next 12 months (September 2020 to August 2021), including 143,400 likely to be severely malnourished.
The ongoing Deyr rains in parts of central regions of Galmudug pose a high risk of flash flooding especially in low-lying areas. In Baxda village, Caddado district, Mudug region, one person died following heavy rains that collapsed their house. According to a multi-agency assessment in Adado district in May 2020, about 1,350 IDP households and 350 households from vulnerable host community need food security intervention. Seasonal Hagaa flooding since July has resulted in the displacement of nearly 342,000 people, inundated 294 villages and destroyed property and about 15,000 to 20,000 hectares of assorted crops, mainly in rural areas along the Shabelle river valley according to the UNHCR-led Protection and Return Monitoring Network (PRMN) in Somalia.
Desert locusts continue to cause havoc in different parts of Somalia. In Galmudug, adult locusts continue to spread in rural villages in Galkacyo, Hobyo, Cabudwaaq, Cadaado, and Dhuusamarreeb. The locust problem has a wide range of impacts that worry Galmudug pastoralist as the region’s economic mainstay is livestock rearing. The locust will have a long-term impact on grazing lands and the poor performance of the rainy seasons may lead to depletion of pasture and acceleration of the dry season.
CPD plans to reach 23,400 people facing severe acute food insecurity in Galmudug, Benadir and Lower Shabelle regions, mainly IDPs and host communities affected by the impacts of floods, desert locusts, conflicts and socio-economic impacts of the Covid-19 pandemic by improving access to food through unconditional and conditional transfers. Weak, vulnerable members of the IDPs and the host communities such as women and child-headed households, people with disability and the elderly will receive unconditional support through mobile cash transfers as per the CWG recommended transfer values per region (Benadir $70, Galgadud $110, Lower Shabelle $60, Mudug $80). The men and the youth will be engaged in conditional transfers e.g. cash/food-for-work for livelihood interventions that will support community recover their livelihood and meet their immediate needs. Activities include rehabilitation and establishment of community infrastructure such as water catchments i.e. water pans.
CPD will protect and restore livelihoods related to food and income sources for the target communities through skills training to increase household income; rehabilitation and support for productive capacities of communities through agricultural inputs (e.g. quality seed, farm tools, training, land preparation and irrigation support) and livelihood strengthening and diversification (e.g. fishing).
Project will be inclusive by incorporating people with disability, women, youth and marginalized groups in all project phases in the target areas. Covid-19 measures and guidelines will be incorporated in project implementation.

What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

Flexibility and choice: One size does not fit all! Using cash or vouchers recognises that beneficiaries are best placed to decide what their households need, and gives them the flexibility to choose according to their own diverse needs and priorities. – Dignity: In allowing beneficiaries to control what goods and services their households need, cash transfers are considered by many as more dignified than receiving in kind, and recognise beneficiaries as active participants in providing for the welfare of their families after a disaster. Empowerment: The availability of cash gives households a sense of restored power over their immediate situation. In addition, there is evidence that receiving cash can at least temporarily empower women within the household. Access to financial structures such as bank accounts may empower beneficiaries, in particular women, in the longer-term as well. Supports or revitalises local trade: Injections of cash or the use of commodity vouchers with local traders benefits the recovery of trade and the local economy. Cash-based support for livelihood rehabilitation can potentially also provide a link between relief and longerterm economic recovery and may offer the opportunity for beneficiaries to increase income and create savings. Reduces negative coping strategies: In the modern world, most households depend on cash to some extent. When aid is given in kind, households are often forced to seek cash in ways that may be detrimental to longerterm livelihoods, for example selling productive assets, or giving children to the care of relatives. Evidence shows that cash transfers reduce the negative coping strategies used by beneficiaries. Multiplier effects: By stimulating local markets, cashbased interventions may benefit more than the direct recipients of cash. Local traders and service providers, transport workers and suppliers and transporters of goods may all benefit from households having money to spend. Time and cost efficient for agencies and participants: When goods are available and accessible locally, cash or voucher-based interventions have the potential to be more rapid than bringing supply from out of country. When beneficiaries use local markets, agencies also eliminate logistical and import costs. However the additional staff, security and logistical resources sometimes required to distribute cash or manage a voucher programme must also be factored in.

Explain how the project design considered market functionality ?

The project used the Market Functionality Index (MFI) designed by WFP and is part of the new Business Process Model for cash-based transfers. It is made to support the ‘Market assessment and risk identification’ and 'Market situation monitoring' processes of cash-based assistance operations. Using the MFI, CPD carried out market assessments that tackled;1) Assortment of essential goods, 2) Availability3) Price4) Resilience of supply chains5) Market competition, 6) Infrastructure7) Services8) Food quality9) Access and protection. The project also considers use of the Somali shilling and its acceptance or not in some areas as well as the wide use of mobile money transfer by most of the population in Somalia.

Mitigation Measures

Risk that marginalized groups, persons with disabilities, women, boys and girls, do not participate in consultations or decision-making processes for the project or are excluded from humanitarian services/assistance/activities:• Keep lists of beneficiaries and personal data confidential and safe from unintended use - apply effective data protection measures.• Ensure the location of and access routes to distribution points and services are safe with enough space for orderly distribution.• Identify central locations - beneficiaries should not have to traverse long distances or pass through conflict zones to access distribution points.• Distributions should take place in daylight hours giving beneficiaries sufficient time to travel home before nightfall.• Identify groups at risk of unsafe livelihoods practices (eg. child labour survival/transactional sex) and prioritise provision of alternatives for these groups.• Develop livelihoods programmes in a way that protects and supports household caring responsibilities and promotes family unity. Participation in livelihoods opportunities should not undermine child protection or other caring responsibilities. Consider employing care providers, providing care facilities (nursery) or supporting community care mechanisms to enable women’s participation in such programs.• Promote equitable and impartial access to all• Apply a systematic outreach process in registering beneficiaries – otherwise vulnerable persons, such as older people, may easily be excluded as other more mobile and vocal population groups are registered.• Ensure lack of documentation does not exclude individuals from accessing services• Use this information to inform monitoring activities and identify any barriers to access, discrimination against particular groups or whether assistance is being diverted. Take this into consideration when determining locations for food security and livelihood interventions.• Ensure the location, distance and access routes to the distribution points is accessible to all members of the community, including the most vulnerable groups.• Separate male and female queuing areas and ensure female staff presence at distributions so that they are accessible to women and girls.• Introduce special arrangements for persons who have difficulty accessing the distribution point or unable to carry the rations (such as persons with restricted mobility or marginalised groups) community mobilisation, home/mobile distributions, youth groups assisting older or chronically ill persons. It should not be assumed that friends and family will do it.• Ensure that both men and women have equal and fair access to work opportunities (including cash/foodfor-work schemes), micro-credit, cash transfers, agricultural inputs and skills training and that they receive the same benefits for their input and work. Risk that beneficiaries are not accessing/using complaint and feedback mechanisms• Set up accessible, confidential and well understood feedback mechanisms for suggestions and complaints with a view to improve programming, understand community perceptions, promote beneficiary empowerment and assist in detecting misconduct. Eg. place feedback surveys in food baskets, feedback box at distribution site, phone lines• Set up mechanisms for submitting feedback that do not require the beneficiary exposing themselves to project staff.• RESPOND to complaints, regardless of whether corrective measures can/need to be put in place. • Consider a joint feedback mechanism with other sectors to minimize confusion. Risk of explosive hazard contamination in project target area• Avoid placing distribution points near military installations or threats such as attacks, stray bullets, crossfire or violence. Weapon bearers should not be present inside distribution points.
