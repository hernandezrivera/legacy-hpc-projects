Project Summary

RI’s primary goal is to promote malnutrition prevention and provision of improved treatment of acute malnutrition to promote good health among conflict-affected and vulnerable groups. In 2021, RI will scale up integrated services in the Nutrition, Health, FSL, Protection, and WASH sectors through the implementation of its projects in the Greater Upper Nile State. This strategy will help to address interrelated and under-lying causes of malnutrition, such as food insecurity, suboptimal childcare and feeding practices, and lack of safe water and sanitation
In 2021, RI will increase services for malnutrition prevention activities and provide improved and timely community-based management of acute malnutrition.
RI will support South Sudanese including refugees, internal displaced persons, returnees, and vulnerable populations. Our response will prioritize life-saving treatment services targeting children under five years and pregnant and lactating women, in compliance with relevant national guidelines. These are the most vulnerable groups to malnutrition due to their increased biological and physiological needs. Geographically, our response will continue to prioritize the Greater Upper Nile State (Maiwut, Maban, Nasir, and Longechuk) which are characterized by high rates of malnutrition, food insecurity (IPC Phase 3 and 4), conflict, and internal displacement.
The RI Nutrition sites will act as hubs for integrated programming. RI's health team will provide malaria testing and treatment for severely acute malnourished children being treated at the Nutrition sites, whilst the WASH sector will provide NFI kits to severely malnourished children’s families. The programme will also integrate with FSL by establishing kitchen gardens at nutrition sites to demonstrate recommended feeding practices and provide nutritious vegetables to caretakers of children admitted. The RI nutrition sites will integrate the early childhood development stimulation activities and will be considered as entry points for GBV victims looking for assistance. All nutrition staff trained on GBV referral pathways.
To further localize the RI’s response and to ensure sustainability, RI will strengthen the County Health Department (CHD) responsibilities to oversee the nutrition interventions, to coordinate the response with various stakeholders at the county level, and build stakeholder’s capacity on the new national guidelines of community-based management of malnutrition and maternal, infant, and young child nutrition.
RI will provide technical support to the CHD to review the county level nutrition intervention’s strategy.
RI will utilize the nutrition information system data and data collected at the county and the community level to employ a gender-sensitive approach to mitigate any obstacles compromising women and girls’ ability to receive nutrition support. Women’s participation will be encouraged in program design and feedback mechanisms. RI will also collaborate with existing and planned development interventions on nutrition, such as those funded by the World Bank in the UNS.
RI will have pursued four objectives including:
• Deliver life-saving management of acute malnutrition for the most vulnerable and at-risk U5 children and pregnant and lactating women.
• Prevent under-nutrition by increasing access to maternal, infant, and young child nutrition interventions and VASD campaign.
• Increase access to integrated nutrition, health, WASH, FSL, and protection interventions
• Strengthening capacity development of the CHD and the health and nutrition site staff through holding CMAM and MIYCN integrated training.
- Enhance nutrition situation monitoring, analysis, and utilization of nutrition information for early warning and decision making.

1 - Needs

According to December 2020 IPC, 2,102 million people faced emergency acute food insecurity (IPC Phase 4). Based on assessments already conducted by the food security and nutrition monitoring system, SMART nutrition survey, and admission trends in 2020, 1.4 million children and 483,000 PLWs are expected to suffer from acute malnutrition in 2021. This is the highest caseload for acute malnutrition since the start of the crisis in 2013. Malnutrition in young children negatively impacts their physical and mental wellbeing, affecting their educational outcomes and livelihood opportunities at a later stage. Malnourishment also increases vulnerability to common and infectious diseases such as cholera, malaria, diarrhea diseases, acute respiratory infection, and measles. The proxy prevalence of Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) among under-five children in South Sudan ranges from 1.3 percent to 33.4 percent. In total, 47 counties out of 78 have a proxy prevalence of GAM above the emergency threshold of 15 percent (2021, Nutrition Cluster GAM rate) Malnutrition in UNS remains critical and the causes are multifaceted. Progress is undermined by overlapping shocks and stressors which in 2020 included intensified conflict, widespread flooding, and the COVID-19 pandemic. Needs assessments conducted by RI have determined that the current scale and coverage of the humanitarian response in targeted counties remains largely inadequate to meet humanitarian needs. The continuing conflict has led to the displacement of thousands and has seen a deterioration of the economy affecting food security. Following the signing of the revitalized peace agreement (RPA), local authorities witnessed an increased movement of returnees from surrounding countries, including Ethiopia. Such spontaneous returnees are putting pressure on already-depleted host community resources. Malnutrition is exacerbated through drought and flooding cycles, which are elevated in areas that are difficult to access. The flooding experienced by the targeted counties in UNS constrains vulnerable people’s physical access to basic services and restricts humanitarians’ ability to assess and respond to needs. The persistent insecurity in the targeted counties also greatly contributes to the increase in cases of severe acute malnutrition. RI continually monitors the security situation in the four counties (Longechuk, Nasir, Maban, and Maiwut) and necessary mitigation measures are taken for the safety of RI staff and the continued support to the beneficiaries. Throughout 2020 the insecurity in the targeted Upper Nile States has remained unpredictable. Inter-communal fighting, including cattle raiding, has caused peaks in tensions, displacement, and deaths; and drives the same causes of malnutrition. As the nutrition needs remain acute in UNS, all assessments are indicating an urgent nutrition intervention is required. The population of these counties is highly reliant on natural resource-based livelihoods, including agriculture and livestock rearing, which have been significantly disrupted by conflict, displacement, cattle raids, and climate shocks. Two of the four target counties (Nasir and Maiwut) are in IPC Phase 4 (Emergency) food security conditions, which are expected to persist through at least July 2021. The other two counties, Longechuk and Maban, are in Phase 3 (Crisis), with Longechul predicted to move into IPC 4 in March 2021. Coverage and access to regular health and nutrition services remain challenging due to long distances, remoteness, and floods in the rainy season. Based national nutrition cluster 2021 GAM release, Relief International (RI) counties have the following GAM rates, Longechuk with proxy SAM 6.7%, proxy MAM 12.47%, and proxy GAM 19.17%; Maiwut with proxy SAM 6.7%, proxy MAM 21.94%, and proxy GAM 28.64%; Maban with proxy SAM 2.05%, proxy MAM 15.95%, and proxy GAM 18.0%; Nasir with proxy SAM of 1.57%, proxy MAM of 21.99% and proxy GAM of 23.56%.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

The project will prevent and address Sexual Exploitation and Abuse, with a PSEA communication strategy and appropriate complaints mechanism All RI staff and volunteers who are based in the four locations are trained on RI's PSEA and safeguarding policies, and ways to report incidents, refresher sessions will be conducted for other staff on a bi-annual basis. Each RI field office has safeguarding champions who are given more intensive training on RI's safeguarding and PSEA approach. During project kick-off meetings with communities RI will inform communities of the PSEA policy, who the safeguarding champions are, what constitutes PSEA, emphasize that all project activities are delivered free of charge and that beneficiaries should not be asked to accept anything in exchange for any goods or services. RI will place posters and visuals outlining RI's policy and where community members can report at distribution sites. Additionally, RI will have a help desk manned by RI staff at each distribution site, to collect complaints and feedback and to oversee distributions. Finally, RI's independent MEAL team and project management staff from Juba will conduct ad-hoc monitoring visits to project sites, meeting with community members and will reiterate the training and RI's position on PSEA.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Safety Audits will serve as an additional Feedback and Complaints Mechanism (FCM) modality in our integrated program (Health, Nutrition, WASH and protection) programs. All focus group discussions occurring as part of Safety Audits will ensure provision of an enumerator who is the same sex as the participants. Exit audits will be conducted at the facilities to every beneficiaries and all these will capture feedback from individuals with disabilities and others. As part of the MEAL system participants will be specifically asked to share their concerns with the program and suggest improvements. In each of our project sites, we avail FCM modalities as “minimum standards”: 1) Monthly staff meetings with first-line implementers, 2) Focus Group Discussions with Direct beneficiaries, 3) Individual interview form, 4) Suggestion boxes installed at all supported facilities and communities. The identities of all feedback and complaints mechanism (FCM) complainants will be kept strictly confidential by program management staff when provided. By using a variety of methods to receive feedback, RI will ensure that feedback from vulnerable and marginalized groups is captured. We have standard, context appropriate FCM IEC materials (posters, stickers, flyers), which are displayed at all project sites. IEC materials include images and text in both English and the local dialect to ensure comprehension by a maximum number of individuals. During all community meetings, trainings, and sensitization sessions, a standard script will be used by staff to inform beneficiaries on available FCM modalities. Depending on the urgency, feedback will be delivered to the first line program implementers during the monthly meetings. Specific responses will also be discussed bi-monthly at the beginning of focus group discussions at the site level to inform beneficiaries of what changes are being made given previous feedback, or if any concerns are being addressed at the national level. Urgent issues will be addressed immediately by the project leads through appropriate channels.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

RI will integrate interventions to improve accountability to affected populations in all project sites. RI will conduct sensitization and mobilization before the start of the project, through consultations with community leaders, both formal and informal. Chiefs, local leaders, and women/youth groups will be provided with full details of project activities. The project introductory meetings in all project areas will include explanation and orientation on how to make feedback and complaints and how the feedback and complaints will be analyzed, and when feedback on complaints and requests received will be given back to the community. Moreover; RI staff and local authority will be trained on how to orient, collect, record beneficiaries’ feedback and complaints received, as well as how and when to respond. all staff will be trained on core data protection and confidentiality principles also. RI has already conducted an AAP study in each location proposed which asked a statistically significant sample size what the preferred means of both receiving information and giving feedback were. Overall, individuals felt more comfortable giving feedback verbally through RI staff or community leaders. Interestingly, females preferred female community leaders or direct to RI staff. Further, all participants stated they preferred to have information shared verbally from RI staff, and then community leaders. A significantly fewer cohort stated they preferred giving feedback to local authorities. The verbal nature of feedback and information was emphasized throughout and is unsurprising given the literacy levels of the wider population in all counties, and the limited/no access to electricity and mobile phone connection for more advanced feedback mechanisms e.g SMS, hotlines, or email. RI will ensure to have the following community engagement throughout the project lifecycle:- Project kickoff and close meetings with Community Leaders, and women's leaders, local authorities, - Monthly FGDs with disaggregated cohorts of the target population- in-depth interviews with sample beneficiaries- patient exit interviews to measure satisfaction- case studies. - complaints hot-desk and AAP champions at training and distribution activities Quarterly monitoring will be undertaken to ascertain the quality of service delivery, achievement towards project outcomes, and the impact on beneficiaries.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

RI will use the beneficiaries’ feedbacks to shape and adapt to project design and implementation. RI will receive community-level feedback at the facility level and community level on beneficiaries’ satisfaction with the quality of services provided. Feedback received will then help with course correction of programming. RI will gather beneficiaries' feedback to shape and adapt beneficiaries' feedback in future program design and implementation. Additionally, RI's MEAL team will conduct quarterly FGDs with community members to gain feedback on RI's WASH project activities. RI's response to feedback will be shared back with the community leader. Action plans will be developed with RI's project team and where appropriate actions will be followed up to improve the implementation of the project.Feedback will be received through patient exit interviews, FGDs, community consultation meetings, and feedback boxes.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

RI will conduct safety audits of all locations, interviewing women and girls and other particularly at risk groups in the community, as well as community leaders to identify particular locations, activities and services where protection violations occur. this mapping will identify where RI's assistance will make the most impact and reduce protection violations from occurring. RI has already completed a comprehensive protection baseline in all proposed locations which emphasized a huge intersection between access to health and nutrition services facilities and prevalence of GBV and other forms of protection violations. Risk 8.1 and 8.2 - These two were identified as a key issued affecting women and girls in RI's locations proposed under this action during RIs protection baseline in September 2020, particularly related to violence/sexual violence occurring during transit to access basic services such as health and nutrition static sites. By increasing access to these services RI is reducing the travelling time of women/children particularly accessing these services and thereby reducing the risk of GBV.Risk 8.5: RI will make sure all beneficiaries are consulted and informed about RI interventions through meetings, especially through FGDs with women and girls conducted by RI trained female staff and volunteers to get their perception and contribute to Nutrition design activities. RI will also explain implementation modalities and intervention timelines to target beneficiaries, specifically those designed to mitigate exclusion of women and girls from consultation and decision-making processes. RI’s outreach team will be able to identify cases and encourage household and community decision makers to allow caretakers to take their children to nutrition facilities.8.7 to mitigate the risk of harmful use of sensitive data, RI has conducted extensive training to all nutrition facility and project-based staff on data protection and confidentiality principles. Patient records are never left unattended and are kept locked in a lockable storage cabinet overnight. RI will conduct refresher training with staff to endure that confidentiality principles are maintained.

12.1.2. Decreased proportion of populations facing IPC 3,4 and 5

RI, through it's integrated multi sectorial programming will decrease the proportion of populations facing IPC 3, 4 and 5. The intervention will promote MIYCN messaging, conduct cooking demonstration and initiation of kitchen gardening at the homes of beneficiaries. By integrating with WASH and FSL the intervention will conduct integrated NID with VASD campaigns and promote the usage clean drinking water and clean handwashing.

Beneficiaries will also receiving seeds and tools to tackle the underlying causes of malnutrition.The intervention will also provide caretakers of SAM and MAM beneficaries with WASH messaging including best practices for drinking and storing water, clean hand washing, defecation and health and hygiene.

RI's nutrition intervention will contribute to the decreased prevalence of GAM in children under five in four IPC locations of 3 and 4 by offering services for malnutrition prevention and treatment.
