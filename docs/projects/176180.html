Project Summary

The year 2019 was a rather peaceful year in South Sudan compared to the previous and preceding years though there were sporadic elements of insecurity or fights in different parts of the country.
2020 seem to have been one of the worst years for South Sudanese with no armed political conflict, intercommunal violence took on a different shape escalating to organized subnational violence. Many have been displaced as a result, shelters burnt, lives lost and livelihoods uprooted especially in Jonglei and Greater Pibor Administrative Area, Upper Nile and parts of Central Equatoria State
COVID-19 threatened to wipe out the human race infecting every country in the globe and led to government imposing restrictions that further eroded the little livelihood gained among the IDPs especially those in Urban Juba Collective sites and IDP Camps. Its effects are still felt as more restrictions are imposed.
The unprecedented floods in most parts of the country seemed to have put the last nail on the coffin as it almost uprooted the entire Jonglei State displacing them to various parts of the country, Mangalla in Central Equatoria and Minkaman in Lakes as others crossed the international boundaries to seek asylum as refugees in neighbouring countries. The floods destroyed homes, swept away belongings; others lost their lives in Jonglei, Upper Nile and Central Equatoria.
Due to the impact of COVID-19 and other preceding challenges, the pound dipped against the dollar. Putting up simple shelters or accessing NFIs has become difficult for many in the IDP locations
Despite these challenges, there is high anticipation of returns as reported
The effects of 2020 will be greatly felt in 2021 that is seen with emergencies as well as resilience building and fortunately recovery for much anticipated returns.
The project is to provide emergency shelter and non-food items and shelter solutions to 18,510 conflict affected newly internally displaced persons, returnees, protracted IDPs and affected non-displaced host communities in need of this support in all counties in Central Equatoria (8,765); Juba, Yei, Lainya, Morobo, Terekeka and Kajo Keji. Juba will be through static response while others are mobile response.
In Jonglei (4,120); Duk, Pibor, Bor South and Twic East through static response and Pochalla through mobile response. Due to good number of partners in Jonglei there will be no much mobile response, however HDC in case of serious emergencies and when called upon can scale up to respond through mobile team in other areas within Jonglei.
Upper Nile (5,625) include Malakal and Maban through static and Fashoda, Melut, Baliet and Longochuk through mobile response.
CVA modality will be used in areas where markets are functional and beneficiaries have wide acceptance of the mode. This will be 10% of the total budget proposal
HDC will promote community-led initiatives in reducing natural disasters particularly floods that have uprooted livelihoods in Jonglei and Upper Nile States.
HDC will work closely with local authorities, community leaders and particularly able-bodied youth to support in dykes repairs and construction through cash for work and provision of sandbags to hold sand/soil in place.
Other initiatives of supporting durable solutions will include use of SNFI team with support of HDC HLP legal team to do HLP assessments and work closely with Community Land Committees to assist those displaced in getting back their lands.
For hard to reach areas where HDC does not have static presence, HDC will utilize the existing humanitarian hubs in Kajo Keji and Kodok during its mobile response when interventions are close to these areas. This has already been happening with our Protection Monitoring Team mobile response in partnership with UNHCR in Unity Leer. This initiative will support integrated multisector response with other partners utilize the hubs which are inventions of accessing risky and hard to reach areas.

1 - Needs

The complexity of challenges both natural and man-made have taken a toll on the long-time resilient South Sudanese. The government relies on humanitarian partners to support the most vulnerable, however because of the overwhelming number of needs, there is targeted assistance. The needs in Upper Nile, Central Equatoria and Jonglei have been driven by conflict and insecurity which are further exacerbated by escalated subnational violence, increasing number and geographical coverage of unprecedented floods.COVID-19 has had a significant impact in sharply worsening the living conditions and pounding on the struggling economy. Many have lost their jobs especially those running small-scale businesses like restaurants as the consumer buying power diminished. In Central Equatoria in Reach Humanitarian Situation Monitoring Sept 2020 report indicate that access to reliable shelter remained relatively stable in CES for host communities. However, access to reliable shelter was impacted by the rainy season and associated flooding, particularly in Juba and Terekeka counties. It also shows that the percentage of IDPs living in temporary shelters was high in Kajo Keji and Terekeka Counties. In Upper Nile State, as reported by Reach Humanitarian Situation Monitoring over 90% of the shelters within the host community are tukul while majority of the IDPs 81% live in rakoobas - temporary structures that need continuous repair and maintenance.The same report indicates that mosquito nets and plastic sheets are the most needed items among the population. In Jonglei the key major drivers of needs are subnational violence that has intensely increased in early part of 2020 running through most part of the dry season. The unprecedented floods uprooted almost the entire population of Jonglei with many displaced to Central Equatoria in areas like Mangalla and with Juba.Jonglei and Greater Pibor Administrative Area, Humanitarian Update (as of 8 December 2020) reports that it is likely that subnational violence and floods will again occur in these areas of Jonglei and Pibor in 2021 displacing many more and pushing many further to poverty lines South Sudan Humanitarian Needs Overview 2021 reports that 1.65 million women and children lack adequate shelter and NFIs. These groups especially when displaced face serious risks; health, safety, wellbeing and security, as they are unable to access these needs due to their gender and role in the society. The group unable to meet their basic shelter and NFI needs are newly displaced IDPs, returnees and about 2.5% host community. The report informs that these needs are greatest in Upper Nile, Jonglei, Central Equatoria and other 5 States in South Sudan and will need to be supported immediately to prevent further loss of lives and increase in vulnerability.HDC will target those in collective centers, re-designated IDP camps, newly displaced and returnees coupled with a small percentage of host community that are in the same level of vulnerability.

5 - Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA)
5.1. Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA)?

Yes

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

Only a small percentage of the project will be through CVA modality. Though markets are opening, South Sudan remains volatile and annual rainy seasons make over 60% of South Sudan roads impassable as reported through Log Cluster updates.A mix of modalities of both in-kind and CVA is to be used. CVA is also still new and most of the partners and FSPs are still getting acquainted with the procedures.Only areas where markets are functional will cash assistance be appropriate like Juba.

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

The Joint Market Monitoring Initiative led by REACH reports show more areas are covered and more organizations are joining in the exercise across South Sudan even in the recent flood and subnational violence hit areas of Akobo and Ulang.In the year 2020 there has been a strong lead from the Cash Working Group coordinator to connect humanitarians with Financial Service Providers, many are now seen in regular CWG meeting. This is promoting use of CVA even in remote or hard to reach areas where markets are functional Through experience, we have seen persons of concern sell items to buy what they need most. Provision of cash will provide that choice coupled with dignity. It is also important that CVA is used in areas where markets are functional like Juba to promote the local struggling economy.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

HDC introduced in 2018 a policy that staff should separately sign the code of conduct and contract. Proper orientation is then done by Human Resource Manager.PSEA since its strong focus and emphasis in 2018, HDC staff have received several trainings on the subject and have a focal point on PSEA issues. Additionally the team leader will also be provisionally the PSEA focal point during interventions; will ensure that staff receive refresher briefing or training on the subject and also train the enumerators, community focal points, community leaders, etc on PSEA as these groups are often engaged in our SNFI interventions.Information on PSEA to the community will not only be passed the trained groups in the community but also through IEC materials at every stage from analysis to response to evaluation. This will ensure PSEA messages reach as far as possible into the community as part of prevention and response.HDC staff will also share the toll free number as provided by the office of DRSG. We will also share our toll free line and responsible staff for reporting confidentially issues on PSEA.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Community Focal Points and enumerators will do household visits to reach out to the most vulnerable in order to ensure their views and feedback are also incorporated into the project designFGDs and KIIs will be conducted with these special groups to also ensure their views impact on the project design and implementation

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Meetings with the general community are conducted to inform about the project. After this meeting, gender-represented community focal points (CFPs) are recruited who are trained to be knowledge about project implementation and requirements. They help in dissemination of information to the community and beneficiaries.Information about distribution or other activities are also passed through communication in the churchesSpecific information during project activities are announced using loud speakers by the CFPs or project staff.The local chiefs and authorities are also used as sources of passing information but the above mentioned ones are used to pass the messages beyond them

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The project is kept flexible for issues or feedback from the community that might dictate changes in the implementation or design of the project.Feedback/complaints/challenges from the community will be channeled through to community leaders during meetings and partners during coordination meetings or through monthly reports to the donor.Mid year project revisions will be used to revise the project proposal/design in order to reflect the changes in the project.

Risks that beneficiaries are exposed to violence during registration/distribution as a result of the distance to or location of humanitarian assistance and servicesDuring analysis stage, safety audits are conducted before the assessment of needs to identify potentially safe areas also unsafe ones plus other risks to guards against.The CFPs are also a reliable source of information in finding safe locations for the beneficiaries that are also accessible by those with protection needs especially those living with disabilities.These locations are often as possible close to the beneficiaries; public places, airstrips, local authorities compounds, schools, etc 2. Risk of unsafe and unethical management of data, especially disclosure of sensitive personal data on beneficiariesTrain staff on the importance of safe data management and dangers of it.Use kobocollect to preserve confidential data and as part of proper data managementComputers use passwords to protect information form unintended usersHard files with confidential information are kept away in lockable shelvesSeeking consent of individual cases for any referrals or sharing of information of that individual 3. Risk of interference from external stakeholders in the impartial and needs-based selection of beneficiaries thus negatively affecting social cohesion and sparking intercommunity conflict over access to resourcesBefore the team departs to the field, a detailed and clear targeting criteria is set. This criteria is communicated in advance to the stakeholders.The CFPs and enumerators are also trained on how to identify the persons that the project is targeting.The use of CFPs ensures that the project goes beyond the gate keepers and also any interferences are communicated early in time for a prompt action.The staff are also trained in keeping the ethics and principles of humanitarian work. The CCE/Protection Officer is dedicated to ensure that correct procedures are followed by the staff. 4. Risk of land disputes and tenure insecurity in targeted project areaHDC is HLP partner under Protection Cluster with well-staff legal team that will support SNFI team understand/assess HLP issues or handle referrals during interventions especially integrated responses.SNFI Cluster has also mainstreamed HLP questionnaires into the assessment forms. There is added strength of HLP expert by SNFI Cluster and IOM - it will be easier through HLP trainings in pipeline and existing experience and capacity, to carry out assessments that will adequately obtain information on land rights, existing tenure system and also verify HLP ownership as part of HLP due diligence.The process will be done during desk reviews and analysis stage that is often a two-week period that will ensure the HLP section in the analysis report is addressed and analyzed in order to mitigate any HLP issues that may arise,During the response stage, it will be informed by HLP findings in the analysis report and engage the community leaders and other stakeholders to handle the issues identified in order to avoid evictions or conflict during shelter construction and other HLP related activities.The shelter construction process will involve the legal HLP team to support the SNFI team. Proper consultation after the findings will be done through FGDs and KIIs with community leaders, IDPs, returnees, host community and local authorities. This will ensure those receiving humanitarian support are not put in harm especially when they are settled in an area with HLP issues that are not researched sufficiently and may result in conflict or evictions.
