Project Summary

The proposed project is designed to provide conflict affected populations in Northeast Nigeria with access to safe and sustainable water, sanitation and hygiene services through hardware and software approaches.
The project will build the resilience of the affected populations and complement the efforts of government with regards to resettlements of beneficiaries. The project will improves access to safe and adequate water supply and basic sanitation infrastructure for households, health facilities, schools. The project builds resiliency through complementary hygiene practices to maximize and enhance health-seeking behaviors among the most vulnerable communities.
In emergency context, the project with provided beneficiaries with access to emergency WASH response; provision of emergency water through water trucking, construction of water emergency water facilities, construction of emergency latrines and sustainable and proper hygiene practice.

1 - Needs

Northeast Nigeria is suffering from the worst humanitarian crisis, derived from over ten years of active conflict. Fighting has caused significant damage to essential infrastructure, including water and sanitation systems and health facilities. Access to basic services has been drastically reduced against a backdrop of increasing rates of food insecurity, malnutrition, the reemergence of cholera, and near complete economic collapse. About 18 million people have limited or no access to safe drinking water, sanitation, or hygiene services.According to the MSNA data, the total population in need of humanitarian WASH is 2.8 million people, comprising: 583,000 children (57%) and 200,000 women (20%) with insufficient water, while 1.3 million children and 487,000 women have no access to sanitation and 1.6 million children and 576,000 women have no access to handwashing facilities that have running water and soap. 8.6 - Please describe briefly the modalities your project will put in place to ensure protection mainstreaming (particularly referring to the above questions).

Taimako-CDI is committed to ensuring safe programming and mainstreaming protection at all stages of this project through the following: Ensure teams include men, women and people from minority groups and where possible women to speak with women and men with men at key stages of the programme; Use appropriate participatory methodologies to ensure that those less likely or able to speak are able to contribute to the design of the project and express their views freely; Provide training for staff to learn how to respond to gender and equity issues; Use a range of different techniques to encourage the respect of women as well as men and to strengthen their roles in committees or as managers of water, sanitation or hygiene projects– a range of participatory methodologies will be used for this, as well as using role models, influencing community leaders, use visits to camps where women are more supported, and providing additional support over a longer time period where women are being excluded or ignored; Determine gender and social profiles of communities in which the programmes will work. Understand - the roles and responsibilities of men and women in different social groupings, who has decision making power over what, who owns and controls what, what are the possible cultural norms related to men and women of different social groupings (elderly, young, children, and people from different ethnic groups) including in relation to WASH; Consider women as well as men for technical tasks such as pump maintenance – there is no evidence to show that women are less capable to undertake this task but some arguments that they may have more reason to undertake an efficient maintenance regime; Provide on-going support and confidence building to women on committees – where there are particular problems with women not being respected then increase the frequency of support and over a longer period. Undertake advocacy as part of the support; Ensure that there are a reasonable number of women on mixed committees as well as men, and not just one single member – ideally the women’s representation should be at least 40% and ideally 50% or greater. The number of women on a committee as well as men does not automatically mean their voices are heard, but having more on a committee will help for the women to give each other support and hopefully allow them to have access to information. They may also as a group be able to pressurise the men and the committee to take action when there are problems with sustainability; Engage women and men elders in the project – they may have useful historical knowledge of previous problems relating to sustainability and hence allow the new programme to respond to these, and they may be helpful in conflict resolution relating to stolen money, mismanagement or break down of trust. All TCDI projects are designed following the ''Do No Harm'' principle. To achieve this, TCDI involves the community throughout the project cycle following a bottom-up approach. All beneficiaries receive relevant project information during the inception meeting and are able to provide their input. Beneficiaries are requested to participate in decisions that affect them like the location of water points, bathing shelters and latrines, or the selection of hygiene promoters. Both beneficiaries and local community leaders are directly involved. TCDI staff is trained in Do No Harm Approach and Conflict Sensitivity, which ensures that staff at all levels treat beneficiaries in a dignified manner. The Complaints and Response Mechanism (CRM) acts as a tool to ensure protection related issues are well taken care of and the most vulnerable beneficiaries are not left out of the project.

9.5 - How will your project ensure feedback is collected from the most vulnerable community members?

Women, men, girls, and boys within target communities will be directly involved at all stages of the program cycle in order to encourage ownership and participation in program activities. Community structures, networks, and support groups are fundamental to building resilience and are at the heart of the TCDI’s programme strategy. TCDI recognizes that communities are not homogeneous, and the gender analysis will enable us to jointly design an appropriate intervention, with the community, looking at the needs of all sectors. TCDI is fundamentally committed to ensuring accountability to communities and installing a rigorous feedback mechanism which is part of the program Women, men, girls, and boys within target communities will be directly involved at all stages of the program cycle in order to encourage ownership and participation in program activities. TCDI actively commits to Core Humanitarian Standards (CHS), and pro-actively informs and guides all TCDI staff why and how to be accountable to beneficiaries. Beneficiary accountability is one of TCDI's quality standards and priorities across all humanitarian interventions. TCDI handles all complaints and feedback through its Complaints & Response Mechanism (CRM) system. Each project has a complaint/feedback focal point to coordinate complaints and feedback received. Beneficiaries have access to this mechanism verbally (phone, face-to-face) or non-verbally (email, SMS). Different channels are being used, but experience shows community committees are the most effective way to collect feedback and complaints from the community since most beneficiaries are illiterate or do not have a phone. All feedback is registered by MEAL officer and reviewed by the Programme Manager and Country Director. He/she deals with all complaints and feedback according to TCDI Complaint Handling Procedures.
