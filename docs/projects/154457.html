Project Summary

This project supports conflict-affected individuals in return areas to restore their pre-conflict livelihoods. Livelihoods restoration is focused on beneficiaries who lost their business due to the conflict and have strong pre-conflict experience and skills; the intervention provides an asset recovery grant paired with light mentorship for beneficiaries to adapt to the post-conflict market. Informed by consultations with women and men in affected areas, program activities and approaches will prioritize the safety, access and dignity of conflict-affected families, mainstreaming protection principles.

Needs

In the four years since the Islamic State (IS) first captured territory in Iraq and through the ensuing operations to retake these areas in 2016-2017, families across the country have been deeply impacted by conflict. Today, the number of returnees exceeds the number who remain in displacement. In contexts where return is voluntary, the relief of going home is often tempered by the challenges of rebuilding; many families find their homes severely damaged or completely destroyed, their household items destroyed or looted, and their livelihoods utterly devastated by the effects of war. CRS’ multisector assessment in January 2018 found that families face limited opportunities for – and numerous barriers to – income generation, upon return. Respondents overwhelmingly pointed to a lack of livelihoods opportunities in areas of return. Before the conflict, both men and women in targeted areas practiced agricultural livelihoods, enterprise-based livelihoods such as small businesses or factories, and worked for the government; men also engaged in vocational trades. The conflict disrupted livelihoods through damage to infrastructure, loss of equipment, supplies, and other assets due to the theft or destruction, and the placement of landmines and UXOs; both returnees and conflict-affected families in re-taken areas are affected. Upon return, those who are able to access employment find work inconsistent and insufficient to cover household needs; average monthly income ranges from US $80-280 per household, with monthly expenditures averaging $240. Without remaining savings or income, men and women lack the start-up capital required to restore livelihoods. In some cases, the conflict has also shifted income-generating responsibilities; some families lost their primary breadwinner, with other individuals (often women) in these families now responsible to provide income. Communities reported that those most in need of livelihoods support were female headed households and youth of both genders. During assessments, men and women alike indicated a preference for assistance through provision of capital.

Activities

This project focuses on livelihoods restoration in return areas. Result 1: Conflict-affected individuals resume productive livelihoods in areas of returnActivities: - Individuals receive light guidance on adapting to the post-conflict market- Individuals receive cash grants for asset recovery- Individuals receive light coaching while restoring their livelihood Livelihoods restoration is focused on beneficiaries who lost their business due to the conflict and have strong pre-conflict experience and skills; the intervention provides an asset recovery grant paired with light mentorship for beneficiaries to adapt to the post-conflict market. CRS will use a rigorous multi-stage beneficiary selection process to target the beneficiaries who meet vulnerability criteria, ensure market viability, and verify beneficiary commitment to proper use of funds. CRS will invite selected applicants to a Livelihoods Restoration Workshop. During this two-day workshop, CRS will coach applicants in preparing a business plan, which forms the basis of their grant application. The workshop will focus on supporting applicants to adapt their pre-conflict business to the post-conflict market and use best practices to prepare their business plan. After the workshop, applicants will submit their business plan as a grant application. CRS will provide a cash grant for asset recovery of up to $1500 USD based on their livelihood type, demonstrated asset recovery needs, and market assessment of asset costs. CRS anticipates supporting all applicants that demonstrate viability, cost effectiveness, and commitment to proper use of funds to receive asset recovery grants. CRS will pair the grant with ongoing support through Restoration Coaches (“Coaches”), who follow up with beneficiaries over the course of six months, the standard timeframe for a business to be sustainable based on CRS experience. CRS will monitor use of grant after 2-4 weeks of grant receipt. CRS utilizes its agency protection mainstreaming approach when designing and implementing programming, which puts top priority on the safety, dignity and access of conflict-affected people, including the special needs of persons with disabilities, survivors of SGBV, and other persons of concern.

Protection Risk Analysis

CRS has identified the following as potential protection risks: 1. Women may face barriers to participation due to the public nature of businesses (due to social norms and perceptions related to security) as well as the household’s prioritization of male household members as breadwinners. 2. The project may impact intra-household dynamics, especially when women take on the role as primary breadwinner, or inter-group dynamics between ethnic/religious groups in the society. 3. Cash distributions may present protection risks if not well organized with consideration of specific needs of vulnerable groups. 4. Internal or external actors may purport themselves as having influence over the project’s selection process in order to exploit applicants or beneficiaries.

Mitigation Measures

As is true across all CRS programming, protection is mainstreamed throughout project activities following CRS Global guidance and best practices. CRS monitors protection issues through both its feedback mechanisms as well as through ongoing beneficiary follow-up and program monitoring; CRS will regularly review this information and make program adjustments to reduce existing and newly identified risks. Specific mitigation measures for those risks identified above are as follows: 1. CRS mitigates women’s barrier to participation in various ways. CRS defines business broadly to include home-based businesses or small neighborhood-based businesses (i.e. selling kubbeh to neighbors) more accessible to women. CRS also encourages female applicants through outreach and prioritizing female applicants for consideration. CRS has a gender balance in its staff, and ensures that local Restoration Coaches are both male and female. In each target area, CRS inquires about appropriateness of gender segregation in activities, adjusting to the local context as needed. In livelihoods restoration, CRS considers that women may have less experience in their pre-conflict livelihood than male counterparts; CRS has lower minimum experience requirement for women and offers additional coaching if needed. 2. CRS mitigates risk of impacting intrahousehold dynamics or intergroup dynamics through clear and consistent messaging about the project to the community and beneficiaries, ensuring strong understanding of the project selection criteria, activities, and goals in the community. CRS also mitigates this risk by staying in consistent contact with its beneficiaries and using its various feedback mechanisms to stay aware of and responsive to related protection issues. The intervention is sensitive to complex relationships between social groups and, to the extent possible, utilizes a social cohesion approach designed to serve as a catalyst for cooperation. 3. CRS conducts cash distributions in a manner that considers the particular needs of vulnerable populations. Livelihoods cash distributions are organized in a safe, accessible location with small groups receiving at one time. Beneficiaries wait to receive their grant in a comfortable room with seating for all attendees. If an individual is unable to attend the distribution due to age, gender, illness, disability, or other limiting factors, they can nominate someone to receive on their behalf. CRS promotes its hotline through business cards and posters at distribution sites as well as manages a helpdesk on site. 4. CRS mitigates the risk of internal or external actors using perceived influence to exploit applicants or beneficiaries through many mechanisms. Internally, CRS and its partners have a Code of Conduct and protection policy for all staff. Staff are trained on these policies and how to report incidents. CRS also separates responsibilities and includes many checks in the selection process to avoid undue influence of one person. Externally, CRS uses messaging on all its materials and forms affirming that aid is free, project participation is based on criteria, and any exploitation should be reported through the CRS hotline. CRS uses a free hotline, among other feedback mechanisms, and widely advertises this hotline in its target communities.

How is your project accountable to affected population

The CRS Livelihoods project is designed based on review of secondary data sources, coupled with primary data collection during which the affected population are directly engaged to identify priority needs within their community. Based on the priority needs identified by the affected population, this project has been tailored to address the unique needs of conflict-affected Iraqis while also ensuring the design is responsive to the identified needs of vulnerable populations especially women, girls, youth, the elderly, and those with disabilities. In project-start-up, CRS will also consult with the community to inform targeting criteria, specifically related to viability and vulnerability criteria, as well as details of activities design, including considerations such as gender segregation, timing of activities, and other sensitivities or adaptations needed. CRS-Iraq takes a client-oriented, active approach to accountability. In addition to the feedback collected during regular program monitoring (post-distribution monitoring, satisfaction surveys, etc.), CRS Iraq has developed inclusive feedback mechanisms (responsive to gender and other vulnerability considerations) based on input provided by affected communities on preferred methods of communication. CRS ensures that 100% of feedback is addressed either real-time when the feedback is received, or through appropriate follow-up by CRS staff; most cases are resolved within one week of receipt. This two-way communication ensures transparency throughout project design and implementation. The MEAL Technical Support Unit (TSU) is responsible for managing all feedback mechanism inclusive of activities implemented directly by CRS or by local partner Caritas Iraq. CRS utilizes stakeholder feedback to support adaptive program management. The CRS MEAL TSU shares all feedback to program staff on a weekly basis and aggregate information is compiled into a feedback dashboard to identify trends across projects and activities. During quarterly programmatic reviews aggregated community feedback from the quarter are analyzed, informing a “learning-to-action” discussion for programmatic design and implementation adjustments.
