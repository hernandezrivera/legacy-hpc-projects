Project Summary

The proposed project will respond to the protracted Rohingya crises in terms of supporting the vulnerable Rohingya community and host communities through ensuring their food security and sustainable livelihood and resilience building. Considering the multi-vulnerabilities factors like economic crisis, price-hike, and pre-existing natural hazards in the Rohingya context, WVI will provide support through food and cash-based interventions to improve their livelihoods and reduce vulnerability and up liftmen of the quality of living. Hence, the activities will be inter-linked with environment and climate change issues where relevant. The project will employ most relevant and feasible options for building self-reliance of Rohingya refugees and marginalized host communities.
For the Rohingya refugee population (Food Security Obj_02), the project will implement several key initiatives. An extensive awareness-raising campaign will engage a significant 226,000 refugees, focusing on imparting crucial knowledge about food security within the context of climate-smart practices, utilizing resources amounting to $2.5 million. Additionally, a 'Cash for Work' program will provide 5,600 refugees with opportunities for employment, injecting a substantial $105.0 million into supporting their financial stability. Complementing these efforts, 13,150 refugees will be assisted in cultivating climate-resilient crops, with an investment of $29.8 million. Moreover, services aimed at strengthening food security will be extended to 5,600 refugees, backed by a $20.0 million investment.
Simultaneously, interventions targeting the host communities (Food Security Obj_03 & Obj_04) will be set in motion. A multifaceted awareness-raising initiative will reach out to 20,688 individuals from these communities, channeling $2.5 million to enhance their understanding of agricultural-based livelihoods. Alongside this, support for crop agriculture will be extended to 20,688 individuals, with a dedicated investment of $5.0 million to fortify crop resilience in the face of climatic uncertainties.
Recognizing the significance of fisheries and livestock activities, 2,000 individuals from host communities will receive substantial support amounting to $30.0 million. Furthermore, cash assistance in both unconditional ($55.0 million) and conditional forms ($60.0 million) will be directed towards 1,000 and 500 individuals, respectively, significantly aiding their economic stability. Service provisions will be extended to 100 individuals, backed by a noteworthy investment of $735.0 million aimed at supporting the host communities.
Lastly, efforts will be dedicated to enhancing the capacity of host communities in natural resource management and disaster risk reduction, with an investment of $15.0 million. Additionally, $12.0 million will be allocated to cash-for-work programs aimed at community development and disaster risk reduction. Collectively, these concerted efforts will not only address immediate food security concerns but will also promote resilient agricultural practices and elevate the socio-economic status of both Rohingya refugees and host communities.
Social cohesion stands as a vital element within the project, focusing on the inclusion and mobilization of youth and women into groups to foster social unity. This initiative will also integrate principles of PSEA (Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse), gender equality, and deliberately aim to incorporate individuals with disabilities. Notably, the project aims for 60% of its beneficiaries to be women. All together, the project will reach a total of 226,000 individuals across 13 camps, specifically targeting camps labeled 8E, 9, 10, 11, 12, 15, 17, 18, 19, 20, 20xt, and 24. Additionally, the project will engage 5,295 individuals in selected areas within 11 unions spanning Teknaf and Ukhiya Upazila.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

Access to fresh and nutritious food continues to be an obstacle for the refugee population residing in Cox’s Bazar camps and host community areas due to the ongoing Rohingya crises. The refugees generally have no regular income opportunities and lack of technical skills that would allow them to adapt quickly to change in their situation. The total number of Rohingya in Bangladesh has increased from 767,324 (154,264 households) after the August 2017 influx to 965,467 Individuals (200,560 Families) (UNHCR population data, September 2023).Refugee Influx Emergency Vulnerability Assessment (REVA-6) Report, June 2023, WFP has shown that in 2022 overall vulnerability continued an upward trend in both the Rohingya and Bangladeshi communities, resulting in much higher prevalence than in pre-pandemic years. For the Rohingya, 95 percent of households were found with moderate to high vulnerability, much higher than the 55 percent Bangladeshi community level. The proportion of highly vulnerable Rohingya households (83 percent) increased by 1 percentage point compared to 2021, largely driven by economic shocks such as inflation, from the second quarter of 2022, impacting overall vulnerability. REVA-6 Report, June 2023- stated that, since 2020 a total of 74 percent of Rohingya households in a panel retained their previous vulnerability levels, of which 70 percent fell into the high vulnerability category in 2022. The shift was most likely due to the high price hikes and livelihood opportunity, which negatively benefitted the Rohingya. REVA-6 Report, June 2023, WFP has shown that among Rohingya households, the following demographic characteristics were found to contribute significantly to high vulnerability; households with 3 or more members with a disability, households with children under 5 years old, households with three or more children (< 15 years), households with adolescent (10-19 years), households with 5 or more members, high crowding index (> 2 people per room), and high dependency ratio (> 1.5). Heads of households with below-primary schooling or no education at all had a high probability of having less income, causing the high vulnerability both in Rohingyas and Bangladeshi communities6. The absence of active working members in a household was also a predictor of high vulnerability. Other hand among the Bangladeshi community, women-led households with three or more children, households with a high crowding index, and households with a high dependency ratio7 remained highly vulnerable. Other determinants which contributed to high or moderate vulnerability were: households with three or more children under 15; households with six or more members; households with a head with below primary or no education, land occupancy less than 5 decimals, and households without a male member of working age (i.e., 15-49 years old) REVA-6 Report, June 2023, WFP has shown that in 2022 overall expenditure at household-level increased significantly in the face of the global economic crisis and its overarching impacts on Bangladeshi markets. The average monthly expenditure for cash purchases increased by BDT 486 and BDT 162 per capita within the Bangladeshi community and Rohingya households respectively as compared to 2021. By adding the imputed value of humanitarian assistance, aggregated expenditures for Rohingya households rose to BDT 2,834 (US$27.50) per capita per month and 3,294 (US$32) for Bangladeshi community households, which demonstrates the importance of humanitarian assistance in supporting the essential needs of vulnerable households. Approximately half of the assessed households in camps not having an acceptable food consumption score, and reporting challenges related to their food assistance; The most worried issues are that still 10% HH are poor in FCS (Ref: J-MSNA, 17 Oct’2021).

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

a) Camp-based coordination: WVI will facilitate Food Security and Life Skill development initiatives in both the camps and host communities. By offering multiple services within the same camp, WVI will enable an integrated approach across sectors, ensuring seamless coordination among various stakeholders. Active participation in inter-agency clusters, led by UN agencies like UNHCR and WFP, alongside government bodies, will help identify gaps and prevent duplication. Regular updates on activities through clusters will contribute to the comprehensive "Who Does What, Where and When" database. b) Government relations and linkage building: Establishing robust relationships with the Government of Bangladesh at national and Cox's Bazar levels, WVI will maintain strong ties with ministries, local authorities, RRRC, and DC. Active participation in meetings and sharing program reports with relevant authorities will continue. WVI will aim to scale up production and create market linkages for long-term food security and sustainable livelihoods, incorporating activities such as school feeding, fresh food system enhancement, and Cash for Work initiatives for future risk reduction. c) Coordination with the FSS sector: To enhance collaboration in 2024, WVI will engage in FSS sector meetings actively. These meetings will serve as platforms to align strategies, foster cooperation, and exchange vital information. Participation in relevant groups will ensure smooth transitions and focused discussions on sector-specific challenges. Timely sharing of reports and knowledge dissemination will remain a priority. Active representation in Strategic Advisory Groups will influence higher-level decisions. With an aspiration to lead within the FSS sector in FY 2024, WVI will contribute expertise and strategic direction toward achieving sectoral goals and priorities.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

WVB will ensure mainstreaming of protection throughout the implementation of the activities. “Do no harm” is the guiding principle for implementing the activities for the vulnerable people. WVB incorporate protection principles in designing and developing program interventions in Cox’s Bazar to ensure that all activities target the most vulnerable individuals, enhance safety, dignity, and promote and protect the rights of refugees, encouraging inclusiveness without contributing to or perpetuating discrimination, abuse, violence, neglect, and exploitation Privacy and confidentiality of the beneficiaries will be considered while designing and implementing the project. Beneficiaries will also receive awareness raising session (GBV awareness messages, prevention messages, referral mechanism, menstrual hygiene management and safe access of women and adolescent girls) to protect them from any unwanted incidents. WVB will conduct PSEA training for all staff and volunteers those will be engaged in the project implementation. As a part of inclusion, WVB will establish child friendly space, male & female separate toilet facilities and breast feeding corner at each facility inside the camp and host community Multi-Purpose Facility Center (MPFC). WVB keep the Complaint and Feedback Mechanism (CFM) box in the center and display the hotline number to reach out by all in any issues. WVB will provide assistance and services according to needs and not on the basis of age, sex, gender, nationality, race, ethnic allegiance and so on. Services and assistance are provided in an adequate scale, within safe and easy reach, are known by the affected individuals and accessible by all groups including medical cases, disabled individuals, discriminated groups etc.

3.3 - Age & Disability

Considering diversity, the project will target the vulnerable community members irrespective of their gender, disabilities, social status, race, age, religion, ethnicity, sexual orientation etc. During the participant selection process project team will involve and ensure those diverse group of people has been targeted and engaged in the program WVB commits to creating and maintaining a safe organizational culture where all those whom WVI serves and who work for WVI feel empowered to insist on non- discriminatory and respectful behavior from each other, where poor behavior is not accepted, and where power is not abused. The safeguarding of children within WVB`s work is all our responsibility and must be considered in every aspect of our work. A commitment to good practice is expected of all WVB’s employees. WVB is open to feedback, continual learning, and improvement to ensure that safeguarding children is at the project location of our work.

3.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

World Vision Bangladesh (WVB) recognizes that the essence of accountability is to respect the needs, concerns, capacities and disposition of those with whom we work and to answer for our actions and decisions. WVB’s priorities and approach to AAP is outlined in the Protection and Accountability Policy (2020), elaborated in the Community Engagement for Accountability Strategy (2021 – 2026) and mutually reinforces the Gender Policy (2022) and the Disability Inclusion Roadmap (2020). WVB has established a Community Feedback and Response Mechanism (CFRM) which includes a Hotline, help desk, and complaint boxes, for receiving feedback and complaints from the targeted populations. Protection assessments and the CFRM process protection issues in the community by collecting community feedback and responses in an organized manner. WVB will do capacity development training for Staff to enhance their understanding on protection and AAP. Our intent is to contribute to changes that result in the improved well-being of children and communities. WVB works with the most vulnerable to identify critical needs and seeks to ensure their right to receive assistance and protection based on their informed consent. We recognize the importance of community involvement and participation in all activities and plans that affect the lives of communities. We reaffirm our commitment to receive and respect their opinions and ensure timeliness and appropriateness of our interventions. World Vision’s preferred role is to work alongside children and communities and in partnership with governments, churches, civil society organizations and donors. WV affirms our role as a citizen of the humanitarian and development community and respects international laws and treaties as well as national laws.

3.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Yes. Through diverse environmental and climate resilient intervention at the targeted areas, the project will enhance environmental protection, and Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) along with improved communication facilities for the people in the climate vulnerable and disaster-prone areas of refugee and host community. For instance, climate resilient and adaptive Homestead gardening, Cash for Work (e.g; Waste management, Tree plantation) will contribute to environmental sustainability building in the targeted location and reduce future climate risks. The project will support Landslide and early warning system for the both Rohingya and host communities that will address contingency plan, disaster risk reduction preparedness plan, disaster responses, community awareness and campaigning program with government, civil societies and development partners.
