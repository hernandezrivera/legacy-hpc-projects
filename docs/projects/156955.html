Project Summary

The IRC’s theory of change related to people meeting basic needs outcomes posits that the primary reason that vulnerable households employ negative coping strategies is in order to ensure adequate access to income or goods and services to meet their and their families’ survival needs. If people have sufficient purchasing power to buy basic necessities according to households’ diverse, self-identified needs, and local markets are functional and accessible, then people will be able to meet their basic needs and avoid negative coping strategies that may be damaging, unsafe, exploitative and/or undermine future income earning potential. Through Multi-Purpose Cash Assistance (MPCA), households are empowered to address their unique and self-identified immediate basic needs that they deem to be essential to their survival.
The IRC will continue to provide unconditional MPCA to the most vulnerable households in Northeast Syria, including recent returnees, newly displaced and protracted IDP households as well as to highly vulnerable host community members. The justification for targeting both host and IDP communities is to avoid creating tensions by only supporting one group, and out of a recognition that host communities in Northeast Syria are often equally, if not occasionally more, vulnerable than IDPs, ensuring a needs-based rather than status-based approach to assistance.

Needs

The conflict has resulted in large-scale disruption to livelihoods and an estimated 69 percent of Syrians are currently living in extreme poverty (less than $1.90 per day). Combined with a sharp increase in prices and currency depreciation, purchasing power of Syrian households has been greatly reduced. Decreased purchasing power inhibits vulnerable households in Northeast Syria from meeting basic needs, forcing households to resort to negative coping strategies to survive. IRC’s August 2018 livelihoods assessment in Deir-ez-Zor revealed that the majority of IDPs surveyed reported having insufficient ability to meet basic needs, including food (88.1%), NFIs (80.7%),%) and shelter provisions (78.3%).%), while returnees reported needs for employment opportunities (77.1%), food (63.8%) and NFIs (63.1%).%). Most respondents accessed food and NFIs through purchasing from the local market using cash (48.3% and 63.1%) and credit (42.6% and 34.4%) respectively. However, most households were characterized by low purchasing power due to disrupted livelihood opportunities and lack of reliable sources of income. Most respondents (78.6%) had incurred debt of some sort for survival, and 95.8% of the respondents resorted to one or more negative livelihood coping strategies to meet their basic needs, including relying on less preferred or less expensive food (85.2%) and reducing the portion size of their meals (62.3%). The IRC’s baseline survey conducted in Ar Raqqa governorate identified that 7.2% of households reported having no main income earner; in 27.7% of households the main income earner was elderly; in 24.1% of cases the main income earner had a chronic disease; and in 13.9% the main income earner had a disability. Approximately 63% of assessed households’ financial resources were spent to buy food, leaving only 37% of financial resources for non-food related basic needs. The average household dietary diversity scores were 5.3 out of 12 possible, indicating very low dietary diversity and reliance on cereals, oil and sugar, without proper sources of other required vitamins and nutrients. Measurement of household hunger score showed 46.4% of households in Ar Raqqa had moderate or severe hunger during the 30 days prior to measurement. The average Reduced Coping Strategies Index (rCSI) was reported as 20.7 out of 56 possible, indicating high reliance on coping strategies, in particular, relying on less preferred and less expensive food, reducing number of meals per day, as well as reducing portion size of the meals. 62.8% of households reported having to borrow money to ensure their households’ basic needs were met during the 30 days prior to survey, and 42.3% reported they had to withdraw children from school to send them to work. On post-distribution monitoring exercises for cash programming, however, there is a significant improvement of household hunger score, allocation of resources to non-food related basic needs, as well as household dietary diversity scores and reduced coping strategies index. In the PDM report for distribution in Ein Issa (Ar-Raqqa) conducted in September 2018, the rCSI reduced to 9.6, selling productive assets is reduced to 3.5, and there were marked improvements in other indicators as a direct impact of one round of distributions.

Implementation of activities
Protection Risk Analysis

In designing its MPCA program, the IRC has made every effort to address protection mainstreaming in the technical design. The IRC understands that MPCA and the selection of beneficiaries is a potential cause of conflict and takes steps including transparent targeting, household verification and public communications to reduce this threat. In all aspects of the project, from design to implementation and monitoring, the core protection principles of ‘do no harm’, meaningful access, safety and dignity, and participation will be mainstreamed. To ensure the centrality of protection within economic recovery and development activities, the ERD team has dedicated Protection Mainstreaming Officers who work under the technical supervision of the protection team. This staff helps the distribution teams to identify specific needs of at risk groups, including women and girls, as well as protection risks and proposed mitigation measures in consultation with beneficiaries. Community outreach will include messaging on prevention and mitigation of sexual and gender based violence. All IRC staff working on the project will be trained on core protection concepts, gender mainstreaming, and referral procedures. The IRC will integrate activities within its well-established referral system, allowing the team to refer potential protection cases to the protection team and, in turn, protection teams to refer protection cases in need of basic needs assistance to the ERD department. The IRC will maintain and regularly update a protection mainstreaming action plan and seek to improve the program processes including gender-equitable community participation, information dissemination, and provision of inclusive infrastructure for persons with disability and the elderly implemented by and in coordination with the Protection department. Post-Distribution Monitoring (PDM) include questions around safety during distribution, any possible risks or tensions in the household or community as a result of receiving assistance, and equitable access to and use of distributed items within the household. If alarming trends are noted, delivery modalities will be adapted immediately to ensure no further harm is caused. All field-based distribution staff will receive information about available protection and GBV response services in order to make safe and ethical referrals upon beneficiary request as well as receive service mapping from the protection team on a monthly basis, to ensure the relay of information on access to services if/when asked during household visits. IRC’s ERD, WPE and Protection teams have established a formal referral mechanism to link vulnerable individuals and families with cash provided under this program as well as to ensure the timely referral of individuals to protection, gender-based violence and child protection case management services. Although MPCA can be an effective tool for providing aid, the lack of gender considerations in program design can sometimes put women at risk such as exposure to extortion, intra- or inter-household violence, and lack of control of resources within the household or the community. Therefore, it is of paramount importance to thoroughly assess and understand the gender dynamics in the targeted community to identify potential risks and adopt adequate mitigation measures. The IRC’s Livelihoods Assessment in Deir-ez-Zor revealed that the majority of respondents preferred cash assistance (92%) when compared to vouchers (3%) and in-kind assistance (5%) and cash was the most preferred among both male and female respondents, with 96.8% female respondents preferring cash respectively, because of its flexibility to purchase self-identified basic needs.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks and Mitigations i. Market stability: While the market in Northeast Syria remains vulnerable to contested governing authorities and/or possible closure of international and regional supply lines, the markets in the governorates of Ar Raqqa, Deir-ez-Zor, and Al Hassakeh governorates have remained functional, accessible, and resilient in terms of maintaining a supply of basic needs items and prices. It is unlikely that Ar Raqqa, Deir-ez-Zor, and Al Hassakeh will experience a wide-spread and total market collapse.ii. Security: While the operating environment in Northeast Syria is relatively stable, the programming during the award period is likely to face a higher level of instability due to the December 19, 2018 announcement of the U.S. intention to withdrawal from Syria and ongoing statements from the Government of Turkey on an intended military incursion. Additionally, concerns remain over the presence of IEDs in Ar Raqqa and Deir-ez-Zor governorate. Therefore, there must be a close working relationships with other actors in Northeast to monitor the situation and coordinate on operational and programmatic responses. Additionally, IRC will work closely with the Mine Action Group and other actors to ensure safe programming and to prevent pull factors towards areas of potential harm.iii. Access: IRC endeavors to have unhindered access in all of the communities. However, in preparation for severe security deterioration or changes in the control in the areas where IRC manages programs, has developed Syria-specific remote management guidelines, in-line with IRC’s Global Remote Management Guidelines. Based on level of access and capacity of staff, IRC in NES is able to provide remote control over programs, where highly skilled national staff implement with decisions from international managers located apart from programs, or remote delegation, where there is partial or temporary delegation of authority to national staff at project sites while other staff are in a separate location. In case expatriate staff have to relocate to Iraq during a full military operation and manage remotely, our national staff are capable of implementing emergency programs in NES and will be supported with technical guidance from the expatriate staff working out of Iraq. Additionally, key positions such as of Field Coordinator (FC) have been nationalized and the FC will be the focal point for the emergency response.v. Community members and local stakeholders accept and are allowed to participate in project activities: The success of this project depends on active participation of community members and local stakeholders in all facets of the program. However, the IRC has existing strong ties to the local community, given its experience implementing previous and ongoing cash and protection programming in these areas.
