Project Summary

The project aims to ensure the community’s access to timely and lifesaving information on different thematic areas and allow different age, gender, and diversity groups to participate in decisions that affect their lives through effective community engagement and community participation practices. The project will engage the religious and community leaders, who are trusted to the community. Imams can work for alleviating social stigma and influence the community through risk communication. The project will also enhance the capacity of humanitarian actors in community engagement and accountability, ensuring that the needs of the affected population are at the centre of every intervention. Additionally, projects will be implemented through local partners, with adequate capacity building and mentoring support. The project will track feedback, complaints, and rumours or misinformation through digital complaints, feedback mechanism called COMPASS.
The project also seeks to draw from learning in the response through surveys that will inform on community preferences for information and feedback, awareness of their rights, community participation and feedback mechanisms. The findings from the surveys will inform the partners, sectors and the larger response on appropriate and relevant approaches and strengthen Community engagement and Accountability practice in the Rohingya and the host communities.
Also, the project will disseminate audio messages from a mobile loudspeaker or hand mike considering the needs of different age, gender, and diversity groups, who face barriers to access the information. The project will engage Rohingya volunteers for disseminating messages in their language. The project is targeting both Rohingya and host communities, to share lifesaving information and bring both the communities together for ensuring social cohesion. It will do some research and assessment to involve faith leaders in community engagement and assessing the effectiveness of feedback channels. The project will disseminate emergency messages during the monsoon and cyclonic season to reduce the disaster and environmental risk.

1 - Identified Needs

Rohingya women preferred door-to-door visits. Some female key informants noted that women also receive information from male relatives. Men preferred loudspeaker announcements which are easier for them to hear as they have more access to public spaces. Technology is the preferred and most trusted way to receive information for host communities. Both men and women in host communities overwhelmingly trust news received via television. Women also preferred social media and loudspeaker announcements, while men preferred radio and loudspeaker announcements. The fact that 68% of women (ISCG, October 2020) in host communities prefer social media is significant as the risk of fake news does exist.Limited access to timely and lifesaving information, especially for women, girls, and elderly in camps and host community, due to cultural movement restrictions as well as for the poor mobile connection (J-MSNA, 2020) restricting Rohingya people to receive information. In some cases, whilst information is available, literacy levels and language barrier making the problem to understand the information (J-MSNA, 2020). There is a need to disseminate audio/video and pictorial print information (for display) to be provided in various ways, relevant to the community context. Women and girls need to be provided with information in settings culturally accessible to them. Who people trust to receive information and updates from depends on gender and age. Younger Rohingya women (18-35 years) largely trust volunteers and community leaders, while older women in the 36-60-year category trust community leaders and service providers (ISCG, October 2020). With most community leaders, such as religious leaders or Majhees, being men, this creates a barrier for women and girls who may feel less comfortable raising their personal issues with them. Rohingya men predominantly trust community leaders, followed by camp authorities, such as CiCs and police. Host community women trust the local authorities to receive information, although younger women trust volunteers more compared to older women, who favour community leaders. The local authorities are also the most trusted source of information for host community men of all ages, although younger men also trust relatives and older men trust both their relatives and religious leaders.According to the ISCG Gender Impact assessment, Rohingya men reported that humanitarian actors had consulted them to understand their needs, concerns and perceptions on the COVID-19. Over 80% of men reported had been consulted at least once by humanitarian actors on COVID-19, among whom 72% were consulted multiple times. Younger men (aged 18-35) were slightly more likely to be consulted (94%) compared to those aged 36-60 (89%). Seventy percent of Rohingya women reported that humanitarian actors had consulted them on their needs and concerns around COVID-19 at least once, of whom 59% were consulted multiple times. However, age was a factor: women aged 18-35 were more likely to be consulted at least once (73%) compared to women aged 36-60 (58%). Host communities reported that they were much less consulted in comparison, especially women. Two-thirds of men in host communities reported that humanitarian actors had never consulted them on the COVID-19. The corresponding figure for women was 82%. Both younger men and women were slightly more likely to be consulted. Considering the different experiences and needs of host community women and men, it is essential that community engagement is prioritised, and more consultations held with the host communities to tailor a response to their needs. Not surprisingly, when asked in what manner they wanted to be more involved in the COVID-19 response, 72% of host community women said they wanted to be consulted about their needs, while only 39% of men reported the same (ISCG, October 2020).
