Project Summary

After six years of armed conflict in Ukraine, the Minsk Agreements, which outline the basis for peaceful settlement of the conflict, remain unimplemented. As long as the armed conflict continues, along with its consequences of civilian casualties, damage to housing and critical infrastructure, contamination of mines/UXOs, significant restrictions on the freedom of movement and disruption to normal life, people living along both sides of the line of contact will have emergency humanitarian needs, especially for shelter repairs, NFIs, protection, health, heating and water. While many prefer to remain in their homes, regardless of the ongoing conflict, those most at risk from the conflict must be offered reasonable alternatives so that they can live in greater safety. The main protection needs relate to physical protection from the effects of armed conflict, access to social and economic rights, documentation, HLP rights, as well as SGBV and child protection.
In line with its Protection mandate, Multi-Year Multi-Partner Protection and Solutions Strategy for Ukraine, and Country Operations Plan UNHCR will continue to address the protection needs of IDPs and persons, affected by the conflict, with particular focus on the persons residing on the both sides of the contact line. In doing so, UNHCR will partner with government, NGOs and communities. With its leading role in Protection and Shelter, UNHCR will continue to contribute to the broader humanitarian response within the framework of IASC for the entire crisis-affected population.
In the protection sector, UNHCR will conduct protection monitoring, particularly along the contact line and at EECPs. UNHCR will provide protection counselling, legal assistance and representation. UNHCR will support community-based groups and build the capacity of local protection actors, such as community centers and providers of social and administrative services, to deliver support to conflict-affected populations and to build partnership with community-based organizations, with a focus on persons with specific needs. In 2021, UNHCR will mobilize community participation in SGBV prevention and response. Persons with specific needs will receive individual protection assistance, using a cash-based modality wherever possible; as a component of comprehensive individual case management, including social accompaniment to assist persons access public services. Persons with psycho-social assistance will receive psychological assistance. UNHCR will advocate for the adoption and implementation of laws and policies that protect the rights of IDPs, as well as conflict-affected persons living in areas along the line of contact, and in NGCA. ; In addition, UNHCR will support measures that ease movement across and along the line of contact. Protection by presence will be provided through a robust field presence along the line of contact and in NGCA.
Throughout the response, particular attention will be given to minorities, older persons (who are disproportionately women), as well as persons with disabilities, since their needs receive less attention from other protection actors.
UNHCR will operate a hotline for IDPs and conflict-affected persons. Hotline operators will provide protection counselling and make referrals to services. All complaints will be forwarded to UNHCR for follow-up. UNHCR will continue to assess all partners’ capacity to prevent and respond to cases of SEA, and support partners to implement best practices. As part of AAP, UNHCR will conduct a participatory assessment with IDPs and conflict-affected persons in 2021 to assess both protection risks and community capacities.

1 - Needs

UNHCR’s Participatory Assessment and regular protection monitoring show that protection risks that IDPs and conflict-affected population face vary greatly by location. The protection situation is particularly dire for persons living within five kilometers of the contact line (in both GCA and NGCA) where ongoing armed conflict affects their daily lives. In isolated areas along the contact line (in GCA where data is available), settlements have a high concentration of vulnerable persons: 41% of household members are over the age of 60, and 13% had a disability, and 60% are women. Many household members have overlapping vulnerabilities, particularly relating to older people with chronic illness, but also older people with disabilities. Residents experience high levels of fear due to the armed conflict; this causes psychosocial distress, with older persons reporting heightened anxiety and a sense of loneliness; men resorting to alcohol abuse; and children desensitised to violence. Additionally, many conflict-affected persons cannot access basic services (such as medical care) because of poor infrastructure and disrupted transportation links. In NGCA, persons along the contact line face similar challenges relating to ongoing violence. Persons in NGCA report challenges with employment and meeting basic needs, lack of access to civil documents. They face legal difficulties in accessing pensions and social benefits, and obtaining identity and civil documentation, including birth certificates. A major concern is the situation at the five EECPs with GCA, which registered a million crossings per month before the onset of COVID-19 pandemic in the country. Even before the outbreak, the waiting time remained long, and people face risks to physical safety due to shelling and UXO contamination.. Crossing the EECPs is is particularly difficult for older persons who travel mainly for family reasons or to obtain access to their pensions – almost a half of all those who were crossing. All interviewed groups identified several problematic areas like lack of psycho-social assistance, obstacles in accessing medical services, problems in payment of pensions and other social benefits.In focus group discussions UNHCR conducted for the High-Level Panel on internal displacement, IDPs underlined importance of sustainable housing for them to integrate in their new communities. Stable official employment and full access to services are important as well. IDPs with specific needs (including those with disabilities, residents of collective centers and single parents) mentioned additional challenges in accessing care and treatment for their chronic problems, as well as difficulties in accessing administrative, medical and rehabilitation services, particularly with limitations imposed during the quarantine.COVID-19 restrictions have imposed a considerable burden on vulnerable individuals and caused deterioration of the protection environment. During the quarantine, many people lost jobs, while those dependent on social services or pensions could not access them in many cases. The pandemic also led to abrupt closures at the EECPs. During these closures, people got stranded at EECPs or in the vicinity after the failed attempt to cross. Many were separated from family members and stranded without means of subsistence. In some instances, they were exposed to the danger of physical harm and other risks. Closure of EECPs during the pandemic overall increased protection and humanitarian needs. The pandemic negatively affected delivery of administrative services.In 2021, it is expected that vulnerable people will be in need of additional social accompaniment to access these services after the quarantine period.
