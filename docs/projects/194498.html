Project Summary

Despite a relative stability in the country over the past years, the malnutrition and food insecurity situation across South Sudan remains critical. According to the current IPC projection period of April to July 2023, an estimated 7.76 million people (62.7% of the population) will likely face Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity, with 43,000 people likely to be in Catastrophe (IPC Phase 5) acute food insecurity in Akobo, Canal/Pigi and Fangak counties of Jonglei State; and Leer and Mayendit counties of Unity State. During this period, an estimated 2.90 million people are likely to face Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. The major factors contributing to acute malnutrition include high prevalence of diseases. 62.5% of children reported to have been ill two weeks prior to the assessment. Diarrhea, fever, and cough were the leading symptoms reported. Existing poor sanitation conditions are likely contributing to the high incidences of illness, with open defecation reported by as high as 50% of households in 56 counties.
Between January and December 2023, an estimated 1.4 million children under five years are expected to suffer from acute malnutrition based on analysis and estimations from the results of the SMART nutrition surveys, Food Security and Nutrition Monitoring System (FSNMS) and program admission trends. Approximately 345,893 will need SAM treatment while another 1,057,703 will need MAM treatment. Acute malnutrition burden will be highly concentrated in the four states of Jonglei, Upper Nile, Unity and Warrap in about 61% of the total burden. As for the severity of the situation, during July and October, 30 counties are classified in IPC AMN Phase 4, 15 counties in IPC AMN Phase 3, 19 counties in IPC AMN Phase 2 and 13 in IPC AMN Phase 1 (Acceptable). During the post-harvest season, unlike in the past, not much improvement is expected. Instead, the situation is expected to deteriorate in 50 counties. This is the highest number in three years and related to increased food insecurity and the decrease in coverage and uptake of nutrition services due to conflict, flooding and impacts of COVID-19. Lack of nutrition in young children results in negative impacts on their physical and mental wellbeing, affecting their educational outcomes and livelihood opportunities at a later stage.
UNCDR will provide timely and quality treatment of acute malnutrition to children 6 - 59 months and Pregnant Lactating Women (PLW) reaching at least 85% of SAM with the expectation of 10% of which will be referred and managed at the stabilization center (SC) for medical complications, 80% of MAM children 6 -59 months and 55% of PLWs. Prevention activities will also be incorporated in the response to reduce incidence of acute malnutrition and promote good health. The project is GBV and FSL-sensitive, ensuring UNCDR addresses the drivers of malnutrition; food insecurity, sub-optional child care and feeding practices, morbidity, lack of safe water and sanitation, internal displacements. The response has prioritized uncovered hard to reach locations in conflict prone areas where population especially target group experience high rates of acute malnutrition. The proposed Nutrition in emergency project for the year 2023 will reach out to 3,333 beneficiaries (891 SAM new admissions, 1,107 MAM new admissions and 1,335 PLWs) through both static and mobile approaches, working to ensure value for money with the 166,024 USD fund support if granted. UNCDR has existing GBV and CP projects already running with support from SSHF, UNICEF & IOM. All activities throughout the project will be shared with stakeholders and beneficiaries through an existing mechanism for transparency. As required UNCDR will ensure accountability of resources as well as proper Monitoring and evaluation of activities and reports in Ayod, Canal/Pigi, Maiwut and Renk Counties is well implemented.

1 - Needs

The most food insecure states between October and November 2022 where more than 50% of their populations are facing Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity, Jonglei (68.0%) and Upper Nile (58.5%) are included. At national level, MAD was 4.6%, MDD was 18.1%, and MMF was at 25.2%. Only 4.6% of children aged 6-23 months are receiving the required food quality at the required frequency. Limited access to health is a major contributing factor, especially in locations with flooding and conflict (Jonglei, Upper Nile and Unity states) where coverage of health services remains low. Acute malnutrition, one of the top nutrition related causes of death in children U5, continues to affect children U5 and PLW. The prevalence of GAM among children U5 was standing at a very high level of 23.1%, according to the latest, lean-season FSNMS+ survey conducted in Feb 2022. Access to a backyard kitchen garden is only available in 65% of the nutrition sites. Furthermore, the roll out of the nutrition and GBV action plan will help address the GBV related concerns raised through the GBV safety audit that was performed in 64% of the nutrition sites. UNCDR proposes to bridge the gap in some parts four Counties will be uncovered in 2023 as partners’ exits. UNCDR if funded, which has capacity to deliver will continue treating/supporting the existing OTP & TSFP sites in the above mentioned locations. MIYCN activities will also be incorporated into the proposed programming as a means to prevent malnutrition. As part of enhanced nutrition situation monitoring, analysis and utilization of nutrition information for early warning timely coordinated response decision making, UNCDR will also participate in SMART and FSNMS surveys in addition to the routine data collection in the nutrition sites and ensure timely reporting to the cluster. There is a great need to increased access to integrated nutrition, health, WASH, FSL and protection interventions in proposed locations to promote access and optimize basic services and enhance community resilience. According to recent data on MUAC screening conducted by implementing partners, these counties are recorded the PLW MUAC prevalence from 17.9% - 32.7% which is very high (Feb 2022 HNO/FSNMS+). The high levels of acute malnutrition could be attributed to high morbidity, poor access to health, nutrition and WASH services are assumed to play a role, particularly outside Renk where access to health care and safe drinking water is more difficult to reach. Several actions both immediate and longer term were recommended by nutrition cluster and nutrition information working group including conducting a coverage survey, mass MUAC screening, and joint field mission with WFP UNICEF, MEDAIR and nutrition cluster. Nutrition interventions accompanied by further surveillance should be undertaken to avoid a worsening nutrition situation that is likely to be accompanied by under-five mortality. These four counties were classified as Critical with proxy GAM rate U5 in Ayod is 4.8% (SAM of 3.3 and MAM of 11.5%) and PLW of 20.2%, Canal is 19.0% (SAM of 4.1% and MAM of 14.9%) and PLW of 17.9%, Maiwut is 16.1% (SAM of 2.6% and MAM of 13.5%) and PLW of 32.7% and Renk is 13.5% (SAM of 2.7% and MAM of 10.8%) and PLW of 21.8% (Feb 2022 HNO). UNCDR through an intensive geographical needs assessment across four Counties and its neighboring counties have great capacity to implement nutrition projects with the aim of helping already existing partners bring down drivers of malnutrition (Jan, 2021 SMART). This will be done through both static and mobile approaches so we ensure no beneficiary is missed in our quest. There is huge need on ground to address other sectors into nutrition programming and as UNCDR shall work in line with the cluster and MOH. UNCDR will ensure with support from UNICEF & WFP maximum treatment and prevention of SAM & MAM children under 5 Boys and Girls and PLWs.

Admission and malaria testing for children under 5 boys and girls with SAM. • Provision of LLITNs to children under 5 girls and boys admitted with acute malnutrition in nutrition sites for malaria prevention. • Maternal, Infant and Young Child Nutrition counselling to primary caregivers of children boys and girls aged 0-59 months • WASH Kits distribution to children under 5 boys and girls admitted with severe acute malnutrition. • . Provision of clean water to people in IPC 3 & 4 and ensuring accessibility. • Ensuring nutrition sites in IPC 4 have access to water facilities. • Initiation and management of Kitchen Gardens for demonstration in Nutrition sites. • Conducting Gender Based Violence safety audits at least once to nutrition sites in our areas of operation. • Supporting and referral of GBV survivors for GBV Response services through nutrition sites.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

UNCDR staff will be responsible for championing good practices and maintaining an organizational culture that prioritizes and safeguards all against SEA. Managers at all levels in Juba and the Fields have responsibilities to support and develop systems that maintain an environment that facilitates implementation of this Policy and which prevents SEA. They must ensure that UNCDR staff and program participants understand the importance of a working feedback mechanism. Managers must create a safe environment at UNCDR for anyone to come forward and raise allegations or concerns of SEA and act immediately to respond to any reports without discrimination giving special attention to individuals living with disabilities , and even those who are illiterate both women , men , boys and girls . FGDs and use of suggestion boxes with individuals who are not literate, women and girls as well as most vulnerable members of the community will be used to promote inclusivity.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

UNCDR seeks to be accountable to the women, men, boys and girls whose lives it aims to improve, and places this responsibility at the core of its humanitarian policy. As a matter of human rights and meaningful programming. UNCDR has guided by the cluster defines Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) as “an active commitment by the organization to use power responsibly by taking account of, giving account to and being held to account by the people it seeks to assist”. By being more accountable to affected populations; women, girls, boys and men, UNCDR will do this by increasing Communities participation and feedback in programme identification, design, delivery and lesson learning in Ayod, Canal/Pigi, Maiwut and Renk Counties. UNCDR seeks to achieve programmes of higher quality, with greater and more sustainable impact. The project will increase the space for community to shape their own recovery especially to better deliver against its commitments to stakeholders, including the people UNCDR serves and the resource partners who make assistance possible. UNCDR will ensure effective information sharing and communication channels by sharing information about UNCDR programmes with beneficiaries in a timely, accessible and inclusive way. This will put community in a position to understand and shape decisions that impact their lives. UNCDR is committed to ensure that people receiving support participate in and influence all steps of the programme cycle, including initial assessment, project design, beneficiary selection, implementation, monitoring and evaluations. Systems of community representation must be fair and representative, enabling the most marginalized, vulnerable and affected to have a voice. UNCDR will use FGDs, using focus groups as a method of participation gives a voice to those in the community who are unable to speak up in a larger meeting or setting.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

UNCDR has a PSEA Policy which sets out the expectations, systems and processes aimed at preventing sexual exploitation of other people we work with such as vulnerable adults and community members, as well as the actions that will ensue if incidents of sexual exploitation, abuse and harassment are identified. This will be shared and read out to beneficiaries at the onset of the project with periodic refresher sessions on what is expected of them.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

To ensure that PSEA allegations are reported efficiently and with consistency, UNCDR has in place a complaints and reporting mechanism a formal and confidential process for stakeholders to report their concerns. The complaints mechanisms allow for the identification of problems which might otherwise not surface, and for subsequent corrective action to be taken. The mechanism will provide a range of contact options and all methods should will be carefully considered, depending on availability of appropriate infrastructure among communities. The mechanism will allow for the submission of complaints anonymously and be auditable and transparent to ensure reported cases are handled efficiently and effectively. An effective complaints mechanism has the potential to strengthen the organization’s credibility and reputation which in turn shapes the project lifeline. All beneficiary feedback will be taken into account to better achieve the project set outcomes and objectives.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

The long distance traveled by some beneficiaries to receive services at nutrition sites exposes them to risks of violence and exploitation. UNCDR will be developing several mechanisms to mitigate these risks i.e. advocating for security by spouses to the sites. Many beneficiaries are illiterate which reduces the range of modalities available for them to use to provide feedback and could therefore affect feedback quality. For example, feedback presented in a face-to-face session may not be as honest or comprehensive as feedback offered anonymously due to the power dynamics at play. To address this, UNCDR will uses focus group discussions to garner feedback in a less individualized manner. Lastly, some nutrition sites have extensive catchment areas. In these cases, mothers, caregivers, and children must often travel or walk long distances to access the sites, sometimes staying overnight in the bush. This journey presents several safety risks, including harassment and sexual violence. In order to identify these risks, UNCDR will conduct regular GBV safety audits at its nutrition sites. Feedback from these safety audits will be used to understand the risks that beneficiaries face and therefore find and implement solutions to mitigate them.
