Project Summary

The purpose of this project is to ensure women and girls in Syria are safe in their homes and communities and receive support when they experience harm. The IRC’s strategy is to meet the immediate protection needs of the most vulnerable populations through the provision of specialized and non-specialized GBV services.

Needs

As the Syrian conflict heads into its eighth year, 13.1 million people are in need of humanitarian assistance, with over 5.6 million living in acute need. Individuals in Syria face increased protection risks in their daily lives while the capacity to positively cope with these risks continues to be stretched. Indiscriminate and targeted attacks on civilian areas and facilities, grave rights violations, denial of humanitarian access, and violations of international law persist. The humanitarian situation in NWS continues to deteriorate due to the scarcity and shortage of humanitarian services. GBV, and in particular domestic violence, is widespread and one of the more pervasive and detrimental results of the conflict. Recent developments highlight the criticality of improving GBV response services and contingency planning. Given resource constraints and humanitarian access challenges in some areas, mobile teams are essential to immediately reach populations and provide lifesaving emergency GBV response during transit and after displacement and host communities. Findings from 2018 HNO and VOICES show that women and girls are disproportionally affected by GBV in Syria, such as early marriage, sexual violence and domestic violence. Adolescent girls are also particularly affected by sexual violence, early marriage and movement restrictions in this context. Fear of kidnapping and sexual violence furthers these restrictions, often leading to families preventing their daughters from going to school. Disabled women and girls were identified as particularly affected by GBV in Syria and yet those services (that do exist) often remain inaccessible to them and their needs remain unmet. Syrian service providers are uniquely positioned to reinforce and create norms, policies and laws to promote a protective environment for the most vulnerable members of society. However, if the capacity of service providers fails to meet minimum standards, services risk not only being inaccessible but may cause harm. Many local NGOs are newly established, with little experience implementing protection programming in line with global case management standards. While specialized direct service delivery by international organizations remains important, strengthening the knowledge, skills and resources of community-based stakeholders to implement accessible and responsive protection services is a priority.

Implementation of activities

Two GBV mobile teams which will deliver response and prevention services. Mobile teams’ locations will be determined based on displacement /population density and needs of women and girls and will provide immediate lifesaving services to women and girls newly displaced and in the host communities. Prioritised GBV services include: PSS, safety audits, problem management sessions for individuals, GBV risk mitigation, referrals, coordination, awareness raising/information sessions on topics women and girls request including CSD, mine action and early childhood development. IRC’s teams will run age-appropriate group support structured PSS sessions for women and girls in semi-static locations provided by local councils or rented buildings by IRC. Structured sessions at these locations will include stress management, dealing with emotions, relaxation techniques, and ‘my safety my well-being’ (MSMW) sessions for adolescent girls. PSS structured sessions are designed to build coping skills and resilience among women and girls, support social networks. Dignity kits will be provided for adolescent girls attending MSMW. Women who participate in new mothers support groups and PSS activities will be introduced to early childhood care content to help them provide stimulating activities with their children, while 3-6 years old children will participate in early childhood education activities in the safe spaces. IRC will continue to support 12 local Syrian partners in northern Syria to strengthen their GBV case management programming by building capacity of staff knowledge, systems and management structures. The intervention will follow three phases: Phase 1: Strengthening knowledge & common understanding through Trainings on core competencies and specialized case management and workshops based on gaps identified in the post training surveys and baseline assessment. Phase 2 Practice: coaching for supervisors and caseworkers through observation of case management session; case file review; group and individual coaching sessions; Phase 3 Handover: Coaching and quality assurance to be handed over to partners and a final workshop to provide IRC with feedback to inform future project cycles.IRC will partner with Ihsan, a local Syrian organization with a strong track record of delivering quality GBV prevention and response programs, to support the continuation of services from the Women’s and Girls’ Safe Space (WGSS) Anjara in Western Aleppo. WGSS are places where women and girls can feel physically and emotionally safe and receive protection services. At the safe spaces women and girls can re-build their social networks; acquire skills such as life skills or/and vocational skills; access safe and non-stigmatizing multi-sectorial GBV response services (psychosocial and medical); case management; and receive information on issues relating to women’s rights, and health and other available services. Through this project, IRC will deepen its support to Ihsan, through technical support and direct support for case management by increasing the number of case workers at the WGSS and their capacity to deliver other priority services, such as PSS. GBV case management supervision will be provided through one-on-one support, in groups, through on-the-job observation and coaching in accordance with GBV case management guidelines and minimum standards. IRC will technically support Ihsan to conduct service mapping and make safe referrals to available services such as health, psychosocial support, recreational and livelihoods activities including activities within the WGSS. IRC will provide capacity assessments, training and coaching materials and monitoring and feedback tools. IRC will also distribute 6625 dignity kits in targeted communities.

Protection Risk Analysis

Protection is a core objective of all humanitarian work in crisis affected areas. Given that affected populations heavily depend on humanitarian services for survival, it is crucial that services be delivered in a safe and dignified manner. Due to access constraints in Syria, many humanitarian actors provide remote service delivery from Jordan. It requires actors to carefully consider protection-specific needs and how their humanitarian interventions do not cause harm to beneficiaries, including the prevention of exploitation and abuse. Services will be open to Syrian locals, IDPs, and returnees, but will be targeted based on need and vulnerability, prioritizing women, children, PWD, and the elderly. The protection monitoring team will collect data directly from vulnerable communities to allow IRC to understand beneficiary needs, gaps in service provision, and context specific protection risks related to aid provision, deliberately ensuring that all vulnerable groups can access services. IRC will also conduct regular project review and reflection workshops and utilise child-led clubs in SHLS to assess progress, document learning, and promote children’s participation. The Humanitarian Access Unit (HAU), work closely with program leads and field management to identify risks and trends that may impact IRC to manage, mitigate and prepare contingency plans. The Syria country program developed a Security Management Plan (SMP) and Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) for northwest Syria to guide IRC operations and staff, to enable them to provide lifesaving assistance using the safest methods for IRC staff and beneficiaries. These policies are updated regularly or when necessary. The HAU, keep monitoring the conflict situation and provide analysis and scenarios for potential risks to inform the contingency planning through Bi-weekly situation updates, quarterly humanitarian access updates and the daily security situation reports including one-on-one meetings for specific risks management. These practices gives the IRC the ability to ensure the safety and security of staff, beneficiaries and assets including the IRC obligation in regards to duty of care and staff welfare. The IRC also uses the Feedback and Accountability Mechanism (FARM) to ensure beneficiary accountability and ensures that beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries alike are able to provide feedback to an independent Accountability Team. The FARM is promoted through fliers, posters, in-person presentations, videos, and more. Upon reception, feedback will be categorized for priority and complaint type, logged in an online information system, and instantly uploaded to a dashboard for trend analysis. Feedback will be routed based on their activity and priority level to the appropriate focal point and responses, fact-finding, or corrective action will be taken based on the content of the complaint. The FARM, coupled with monthly satisfaction surveys, recurring focus group discussions, and other ad-hoc studies will enable beneficiaries and non-beneficiary community members to provide regular feedback, complaints, and recommendations to IRC before, during, and after implementation.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risk: GBV Survivors not arriving at clinics for timely treatment (especially within 72/120 hours) due to access barriers and stigma of help-seeking for GBV; Likelihood: Medium; Impact: Medium; Management/mitigation strategies: Ensure quality and confidential services that are responsive to survivor’s needs which will serve as motivation for survivors to seek services. Credible and safe access to services will be provided through IRC’s awareness raising/prevention efforts. Risk: GBV survivors’ confidentiality being maintained and their data security and ownership being respected; Likelihood: Medium; Impact: High; Management/mitigation strategies: Provide means and trainings for data protection. Risk: GBV service provider’s safety and security because of their role and potential impacts of secondary/vicarious trauma (eg. PTSD); Likelihood: High; Impact: High; Management/mitigation strategies: Provide self-care training for staff. Risk: Lack of quality services and referral expose survivors to harm, enhancing risk of stigmatization reprisal and further trauma; Likelihood: Medium; Impact: High; Management/mitigation strategies: Service mapping and liaising with GBV SC on new services. Risk: Lack of quality services also lead to fewer disclosures and lack of trust in service; Likelihood: Medium; Impact: High; Management/mitigation strategies: Service mapping and liaising with GBV SC on new services. Risk: Resistance or backlash from male community leaders for providing GBV response services to women and girls. Likelihood: High; Impact: high; Management/mitigation strategies: Work with the male leaders and local councils to introduce services.
