Project Summary

In Iraq, local non-displaced populations, internally displaced people (IDPs) and returnees are considered to be people in need; each of which has their own specific profile and challenges. NRC’s Shelter programme will principally aim to support shelter-vulnerable populations, prioritising those unable to meet their basic needs due to limited access to adequate and stable means of income. Target populations in 2019 must be ‘shelter
vulnerable’, meaning living in a shelter which is inadequate in terms of providing safety, security of tenure, privacy, dignity, protection from the climate, sufficient indoor space and access to WASH facilities. Consideration of both socio-economic criteria and standardised protection criteria will determine target beneficiaries, with a particular focus given to marginalised groups such as female-headed households, suspected IS affiliated households, people with disabilities and families blocked from returns.

Needs

Though 2018 was marked by a significant number of returns in Iraq, the number of returnees across the country remained below those projected within the 2018 HRP. During the first seven months of the year, the International Organisation for Migration (IOM) recorded 736,428 individuals as returnees; however, nearly two million individuals remain recorded as internally displaced. 64% of the IDP population currently reside in five key governorates; Anbar, Baghdad, Dohuk, Kirkuk and Ninewa, with Ninewa being the recorded destination for 74% of returnees during the same period. As of 18th November 2018, Ninewa accounts for approximately one third of IDPs within Iraq according to IOM, hosting 584,520 individuals - of which 98% are originally from the Ninewa governorate. As well as the high level of IDPs, IOM recorded 523,158 returnees during 2018, of which99.9% stated that they had returned to their habitual residence. Many of these families now face challenges with regards to safe and adequate shelter when they return to their habitual residence. Areas such as Zanjili in Mosul City reported 79% of structures were damaged during the conflict, with 25% destroyed. Many families remain displaced (both within and outside of Ninewa governorate), stating a reluctance to return to areas of origin(AoO). The top three obstacles to enabling returns are lack of safety and security in AoO, lack of basic services in AoO and finally the need for shelter reconstruction/rehabilitation. Whilst many governorates in Iraq are witnessing IDPs leaving or returning, the situation within Dohuk is distinctly different. This lack of reported returns (13,116 individuals) from Dohuk suggests protracted displacement will continue throughout 2018 and into 2019, with 84% of IDP families living in camp settings and 86% of families living outside of camp settings stating that they were not ready to return. Primary reasons for both groups reluctance to return at present are poor security in areas of origin followed by lack of services and damage to properties. Dohuk governorate also hosts 88,342 Syrian refugees with 60% currently living in four different camp locations. Of the remaining 40% living outside of camps, estimates show that 79% live in non-shared houses/apartments, 10% in shared accommodation and 11% in unfinished/non-residential accommodation. With over 93% of both IDPs and refugees nearing four years of displacement, the incidence of negative coping mechanism use in order to maintain adequate shelter continues to rise. While displaced populations are reluctant to return to their areas of origin in Dohuk, the contrary applies to displaced families in Kirkuk. In the town of Hawija in Kirkuk governorate, the return process has been restricted with families not being able to obtain the multitude of security approvals from various security actors. Key factors in obstructing the return process include perceived affiliation with IS and lack of documentation. For IDPs out of camps living in Kirkuk there are reports of increased threats of eviction due to lack of ability to pay rent, exploitation by landlords and political factions using evictions as part of their campaigns. Considering the current obstacles to return, movement towards camps or use of sub-standard accommodation types will likely increase. Social cohesion challenges are evident in areas of returns to levels that restrict the ability of minority groups to return. For example within Sinjar, groups who identified themselves as Muslim are unwilling to return due to fear of reprisals from local communities or security actors. In the Al Qaim and Rawaa towns of Anbar governorate, pull factors of improvements to security situations compounded with the push factors of high rental costs for sub-standard accommodations led to families returning. These returns occurred despite substantial damage to buildings in both locations with over 20% of buildings reported destroyed and a further 15% severely damaged in Al Qaim alone. Security of tenure remains a challenge for families wishing to return, and achieving formal rental agreements similarly affects families in areas of displacement. 25% of returnees approaching NRC for assistance in Mosul City reported lack of documentation as restricting access to assistance. Finally, as humanitarian actors and donors increase their focus on areas of return, the risk of further reductions to assistance for refugees and IDPs in protracted displacement increases. Families that are unable to return due to economic, legal, protection or other challenges will be in a higher echelon of vulnerability.

Activities

NRC will continue to position itself to respond to emergencies that require the provision of shelter/NFI assistance in response to a variety or emergencies that may result from such issues as mass movements or severe weather. This flexibility and pre-positioning through establishing long-term framework agreements will enable NRC to respond in a timely and efficient manner reaching vulnerable populations in the different geographical contexts within Iraq. Provision of assistance, whether in-kind or through cash, will be followed up with technical support where necessary with cross-sectoral referrals carried out as appropriate. By providing timely and contextually appropriate Shelter/NFI assistance, NRC aims to address the critical household needs and provide immediate protection from the elements whilst establishing strong referrals to Information, Counselling and Legal Assistance (ICLA) and Camp Management teams reducing the risk of eviction and further displacement. For vulnerable displaced populations out of camps facing extended periods of displacement living in substandard shelters, NRC will continue to provide assistance upgrading shelters to minimum standards. When upgrading shelters, NRC will follow the model used in 2017 for MSUs with upgrades linked to security of tenure for beneficiaries. Through negotiation with property owners, target beneficiaries will receive agreed rent free, freeze or reduction agreements in exchange for upgrading the property. In areas of return, NRC teams will conduct household level assessments using the socio-economic vulnerability tool developed by Iraq’s Cash Working Group along with NRC’s existing social assessment. Using a combination of the two assessments (expected to be unified in early 2019 at cluster level) extremely vulnerable households living in war-damaged houses (cat 2 properties as detailed in Shelter Cluster Guidelines) will be identified as eligible for emergency repairs to properties. Through the provision of high quality shelter assistance that reaches the most vulnerable NRC aims to stimulate and support vulnerable households to begin the process of early recovery. Understanding the community level priorities outside of habitual residence such as education and livelihoods are critical to achieving durable shelter solutions and will be addressed utilising NRCS UDOC centres as focal points for community engagement. Targeted communities will be, engaged at all stages of project implementation increasing ownership and awareness of project objectives and processes. By implementing through this community lens NRC, aims to enable families to achieve safe and secure living free from threats of eviction and persecution whilst enabling access to essential services and livelihoods required to maintain healthy living. Through all stages of implementation, collaboration with NRC ICLA teams will be essential in ensuring security of tenure and ensuring legal access to compensation programmes for beneficiaries. Monitoring and evaluation of activities are, adjusted according to the modality with findings shared at cluster levels. Household level satisfaction surveys with beneficiaries of rehabilitation related works will be, conducted approximately 3 months after completion of works. Surveys will look to understand the impact of assistance on beneficiaries on changes to living standards both inside and outside of the habitual home.

Protection Risk Analysis

Protection risks for IDPs out of camp particularly affect families that are unable (perceived affiliation to extremist groups or blocked due to tribal tensions) or unwilling (lack of security, services or livelihoods) to return to areas of origin. For many, their standard of shelter is reliant upon economic standing or access to extended family, with vulnerable families resorting to sub-standard or non-accommodation shelter options. Females within this target group face disproportionate restrictions to live their lives in protection, safety, dignity and privacy in comparison to males, due to restrictions to independently access public spaces, services and goods and most generally, to livelihood opportunities. For IDPs in camps who are not able to return and who are facing extended period of displacement, families that are unable (perceived affiliation to extremist groups or blocked due to tribal tensions) or unwilling (lack of security, services or livelihoods) to return to areas of origin are also particularly at risk of protection concerns. As the government’s drive to close camps continues, IDP families that remain are likely to remain at situations of increased vulnerability. Continued restrictions on freedom of movement and access to services will see further decline in living standards and likely lead to increased early and unsustainable returns. For returnees, protection risks affect families that have returned voluntarily or forcibly to governorates of origin. When safe and voluntary return or relocation does become possible, returnees require special assistance to rebuild their lives. People forced to return may face further challenges in reintegration to the community due to discrimination and competition in accessing resources. Returnees will face particular protection needs, most notably in regards to HLP rights such as property and land restitution. With regards to local non-displaced populations, this sub group will include those who are hosting IDPs displaced in their governorates of origin but unable to return to their original area or home as well as those that, although not hosting IDPS, are marginalized, in extreme poverty and unable to access livelihood opportunities or have been affected by conflict in their place of origin. Unexploded ordnance remains a risk for all target groups and responding partners. Risks are higher in areas that are becoming accessible more recently and where mine actors have yet to begun or finalized clearance.

Mitigation Measures

NRC’s program will prioritize safety and dignity, and avoid exposing conflict-affected individuals to further harm by ensuring that the proposed locations for shelter activities are, considered in terms of threats of physical attacks and threats to safety such as areas contaminated with UXOs. Moreover, due consideration to tenure arrangements will be made, with the ICLA’s Housing, Land and Property (HLP) teams support in mitigating risks related to secondary occupation and land tenure, and NRC’s assessments taking social cohesion into consideration to asses where shelter may be a source causing tensions or conflict. NRC‘s ICLA staff will be able to support in mitigating those risks by support with Conflict and Dispute Resolution, by building the skills of community leaders to solve disputes related to property rights. Meaningful access will be promoted through the development of appropriate response to target the most vulnerable groups, paying particular attention to female-headed households and assistance needed by persons with disabilities and elderly persons to ensure construction of accessible shelters.To mitigate the risks due arising from high presence of UXOs and IEDs, NRC will work with demining partners (DDG, MAG and UNMAS) to promote safe environment and risk mitigation though awareness campaigns. This will be even more critical in rural areas of return where only informal demining activities have reportedly taken place, where risks are considerably higher.

How is your project accountable to affected population

NRC believes that the most important aspect of humanitarian interventions is that the assistance is actually accountable to the people it aims to support. This accountability – both in terms of quality of assistance and ensuring that it addresses real needs – is critical for humanitarian actors to maintain their relevance to affected communities. NRC understands that Quality and Accountability (Q&A) of our intervention can only be realised through vigilant and conscious efforts at community and organizational level. Throughout the implementation stages of the response phase, the application of Sphere Standards, the Accountability to Affected Population (AAP) Framework, and the NRC Code of Conduct are emphasized. During the assessment and design stage, NRC ensures active participation from the affected community through consultations, meetings, interviews and focus group discussions and integrates findings into the design. NRC has several accountability mechanisms in place to ensure that the affected communities are well informed and aware of modes for approaching NRC with feedback at all stages of project cycle. These enable and empower communities in holding the organization accountable towards the humanitarian principles and code of conduct. Throughout all programming, NRC shares planned project activities with communities through community committees that are established when the organisation initiates a project. These community structures facilitate transparent communication directly with the organisation, and enables NRC to respond directly to feedback and improve programming accordingly. NRC also establishes and runs Community Feedback and Response Mechanisms (CFRM) as per well-defined Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs). NRC shares information about the CFRM through banners, flyers and community-based awareness raising. The affected population are able to provide complaints and feedback through dedicated hotline numbers, staff field visits, complaints and suggestion boxes and via email. NRC uses this mechanism to resolve any issues arising and adjust programming plans to better suit the local context. In the SoPs of CFRM, clear and well-defined guidelines are available for sensitive and serious issues including issues related to protection. Trained and dedicated staff managed these issues effectively and timely. Dedicated accountability staff reports directly to senior management. The organization applies participatory approach within its Monitoring & Evaluation (M&E) process, based on the principles of Accountability i.e. confidentially, accessible, safe, transparent, response, feedback and records. NRC’s feedback and information mechanisms ensure that communication with communities is carried out in the language, formats and media that are easily understood by the communities, respectful and culturally appropriate of different members of community especially vulnerable and marginalized. Our staff pays particular attention to the gender, age, and diversity issues of collecting, responding to, and analysing feedback.
