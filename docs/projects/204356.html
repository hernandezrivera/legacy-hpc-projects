Project Summary

The armed clashes that erupted on 15th April 2023 between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) have had a devastating impact on people and infrastructure throughout Sudan. The conflict that started in Khartoum, soon spread to other states – namely those in Darfur, North Kordofan and Marawi in Northern State. The IOM-DTM estimates that approximately 843,130 individuals (168,354 households) have been displaced internally as a result of the conflict. A further 253,591 individuals have fled neighboring countries, notably Egypt, Ethiopia and Chad. There remains a substantial number of people who are unable to leave their homes because of the continuous fighting and insecure routes.
This a lifesaving multi-sectorial project for WASH, Education, FSL, Protection, NFIs and shelter targeting 50,000 affected populations (18,000 women, 10,000, 9000 boys and 13,000 girls). 50,000 (18,000 women, 10,000, 9000 boys and 13,000 girls) in Khartoum and Algazeera states.
Under WASH 50,000 will be having access to safe water through provision of water trucking to 50,000 individuals, distribution of chlorine to 4000 HHs, rehabilitation of 4 water yards, rehabilitation of 20 institutional latrines, distribution of soap to 4000 individuals, construction of 20 hand washing facilities, implementation of waste management and garbage collection and providing hygiene kits to 2000 HHs, conducting of hygiene and cleaning campaigns.
Under Emergency Shelter and Non-Food Items, 21,000 Individuals will be targeted, of them 1100 HH will be provided with NFI kits, 850 HH will receive tents, 4000 will be provided with cash/vouchers for NFIs, additional 4000 will be provided with cash/vouchers for emergency/repaired shelter, and 4000 HHs will receive ash/vouchers for rent. In protection, 6,000 individuals will be targeted with GBV activities and 6000 will be provided with child protection inputs and 5000 will receive general protection inputs. Under Education, the project targeted 13,000 individuals (6240girls,4140boys,1300male,1300 female) of them 5000 girl and boys supported with recreational materials, and 5000 girls and boys will have access to safe drinking water in schools, 2000 girls and boys will be supported with teaching and learning materials and 100 Teachers\ facilitators will be supported with increased knowledge on psychosocial skills. In FSL the project will reach 30,000 people of them 5000 people will receive in-kind food assistance (Half Ration), 5000 people will receive Cash/Vouchers food assistance [Full Ration], 5000 will receive Cash/Vouchers food assistance [Half Ration], 5000 will receive seeds/tools as agriculture support, 5000 people will benefit from Livestock support, and 5000 people will receive vouchers/cash assistance for agriculture support.

1 - Needs

Clashes between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) that erupted on 15 April 2023 are having devastating consequences for civilians. The conflict also spread in other parts of the country (such as Darfur and North Kordofan). As of May 4th, at least 736,000 people have reportedly been displaced inside Sudan since 15 April, according to estimates by IOM’s Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM).Education:The conflict has severe impacts on learning opportunities. Schools s in Khartoum and in Greater Darfur are either not functioning or closed. Some schools in safer areas such as White Nile, South Kordofan, and Gezira are currently occupied by families fleeing Khartoum and Darfur regions, thus schools cannot be used for learning purposes. Examinations were interrupted in certain states due to the occupation of schools as well as conflict restricting movement and access to schools. Forced displacement of teachers and children. Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH) Lack of safe drinking water has been reported in all areas experiencing conflict, increasing the risk of water-borne diseases, including cholera and mosquito-borne diseases (dengue fever and malaria) due to negative coping mechanisms such as drinking unsafe water, poor hygiene practices such as handwashing, clean food chain, etc. Water quality treatment units, drilling rigs, and other equipment have also been destroyed, exposing people to untreated drinking water Newly displaced people find themselves in crowded IDP camps experiencing shortages of water and latrines, further increasing health and GBV risks. Increased pressure on the use of the already insufficient water and sanitation infrastructure has led to increased breakdown of the existing and functional water points and yet travel and movement restrictions have made it difficult to move spares and labor to repair the damaged water and sanitation facilities. Lack of basic hygiene and Sanitation supplies such as soap, MHM materials, and handwashing facilities is highly impacting the social well-being and the health of the affected communities. Long queues at water points due to the increased population in safe locations have led to scramble and violence at water points. Food security and Livelihoods Acute food insecurity in Sudan is escalating rapidly and worsening the already high burden of food insecurity before the conflict. According to FEWSNET, the ongoing conflict has caused a swift and unanticipated disruption to trade and market functionality, household mobility, humanitarian assistance, and basic service provision, including healthcare, banking, electricity, transport, and communication, has left millions of people facing critical shortages of food, water, and basic supplies, including in dense urban areas and in greater Darfur, which hosts a large share of displaced and acutely food insecure people. The ongoing conflict is a major protection crisis: among the killed and injured, many are children. Indiscriminate attacks have been reported against civilian populated areas and infrastructure, including schools and hospitals.Displaced and conflict-affected women and girls face an increased risk of GBV, including sexual violence during displacement and in temporary accommodations, exacerbated by the increased presence of armed actors. Domestic violence risks have increased with limitations on mobility, increased stressors, and decreased access to basic needs, as well as vulnerability to sexual and other forms of exploitation.According to the figures stated in the HRP 2022, the number of people in need in Khartoum is 3,474,616 and in Aj Jazirah is 1994523 people, these figures including IDP, returnees, resident and refugees.

5.2 - Impact on environment/natural resources

Due to our experience and lessons learned in the multi-year DFID-funded IWRM projects in the Red Sea and Darfur, IAS uses IWRM principles to enhance the environment in the design, implementation, and closing of our WASH humanitarian projects. For 2022, IAS will be implementing Hafirs using the community-involved catchment approach, the assessments will also be based on catchment while coordinating with WES the decisions governing all things related to the intervention, including environmental actions. Rather than continuing to use up existing precious water by drilling more boreholes, this approach guides rainwater (90% of which is normally lost) through the use of sand dams and other subsurface dams to replenish existing wells. The environmental impact of safe water provision is naturally very positive in terms of supporting healthier lifestyles, a better quality of life, and supporting plants and agricultural projects, etc., but with the use of cement and construction materials, great care will be given to cleaning up after each project site; that each water point intervention area will be cleaner than before IAS commenced the project. Inclusive community/ main actors project design, initiation, and planning meetings will be the stage for environmental protection and planning advocacy. Beneficiaries will be tasked with developing a long-term environmental plan and will sign off and be accountable to the authorities for their progress. For every water point, IAS will conduct Environment Impact Assessments (EIAs). Taking the data from these surveys, we educate targeted beneficiaries to take the necessary steps to improve the environment. Communities will be involved in cleaning up water points and latrine construction sites. Fences and shrubs will be set up around the area. Conservation of natural resources, especially water, will be a central theme in WMC meetings throughout the project, including close-out activities, including inspection, ensuring that the environment activities were carried out and the environment enhancement plan was completed for the future of each beneficiary community where IAS is intervening. As well as budgeting for water point maintenance needs, WMCs will be requested to work with the community to budget environmental enhancement activities. Finally, those receiving environmental advocacy and planning training will be encouraged to bring their knowledge to the local schools.

6.2 - Explain how your project aims to ensure protection mainstreaming during its design and implementation

IAS, will apply different standards and best practices in emergency response including SPHERE standards, LEGS, Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR), Natural Resource Management (NRM) and "Do No Harm'' approaches in the implementation of this project activities. It will be taken into consideration that the transfer of resources (project inputs and services) into a resource-scarce environment can trigger conflict in the communities. Careful assessment of the local context, examining how humanitarian assistance would interact with the current situation in the target areas, and the creation of options for project revision to improve its quality will be carried out. this assessment will include analysis of: (i) dividers and sources of tensions between groups: systems and institutions; attitudes and actions; values and interests; experiences; symbols and occasions; etc.; (ii) connectors across sub-groups and the local capacities for peace; (iii) the project in terms of why; where; what; when; with whom; by whom and how and its impact on dividers/tensions and connectors/local capacities for peace. The partners will work together to ensure that the project is implemented in a way that can strengthen local capacities for peace, build on connectors that bring communities together, and reduce the divisions and sources of tensions that can lead to destructive conflict. To ensure that no negative impact should result from this project the following will be undertaken: just distribution of resources, creating or strengthening networks of relationships across divisions, strengthening good governance, the use of participatory processes for decision making, supporting traditional or indigenous mechanisms for conflict resolution and reconciliation, inclusion of diversity of ethnic or religious groups, gender, and youth in project activities and leadership structures. Selection of beneficiaries will be performed with due consideration of “Do No Harm” principles, ensuring fair representation of different vulnerable groups regardless of their ethnicity, religion or gender. Criteria for selection of beneficiaries will be socialized with communities and ample opportunity will be provided to raise concerns or complaints. Selection criteria for all project activities will focus on female-headed HHs, child-headed HHs, orphans, elders and persons with disabilities. The population of the targeted localities; Al Ganab, Dordeb and Jobayt Elma’aadin in Red Sea state Al Kurmuk, At Tadamon, Baw, Ed Damzine and Wad Elmahi in Blue Nile state include a high proportion of vulnerable individuals; targeted HHs might have lost a family member during the conflict, mainly men, which explains the considerable number of women-headed HHs among the targeted communities; those women have to act as the breadwinners for their children and often their elders on their own. Enabling them safe and dignified access to basic services is part of the protection in this program.

7.2 - Please outline how the project or your agency will inform and interact with inter-agency approaches to accountability to affected people and feedback mechanisms, including SEA and abuse complaints?

Affected populations will be involved in all stages of this project. For example, when the project is approved, engagements will be held with affected populations, including women, boys, and girls to share about the objectives, the duration, and the key activities. IAS will also disseminate the complaints reporting mechanisms to ensure that the affected persons are able to understand how to raise complaints, but also know what to expect from IAS when a complaint has been raised. The targeted communities will participate in Project Focal Persons and selecting of water management committees. In addition, the community members’ views will be sought during project monitoring visits to ensure that the project results into the desired outcomes. IAS will continue to popularize the whistleblowing and complaints response mechanism with the community members to enable the communities to raise complaints or give feedback on the projects or conduct of the IAS staff or the staff of implementing partner organizations. Quarterly project reflection meetings will be held in which affected populations will provide feedback on what is working, what is not working, and solicit feedback from the targeted communities on the changes, both expected and unexpected caused by the project. Project monitoring tools will also be designed to capture feedback from the affected populations to ensure that the project is effective in respond to the needs, challenges, and feedback from the community. The most appropriate mechanisms will be established in consultation with community members, taking into consideration literacy rates, accessibility to communication devices and the presence of suitable sites for establishment of complaint/suggestion boxes; to ensure accessibility of all community members (men, women, children, elders and disabled people) and provision of timely response. Good internal procedures and confidential reporting systems will be implemented to ensure the confidentiality and safety of all community members. The communities’ feedback will be acquired and verified through different approaches including regular monitoring activities and feedback, complaints and response mechanisms including CFRM-Complaints Feedback Response Mechanism, focus group discussions, semi-structured interviews, key informant interviews, community meetings, establishment of complaint/suggestion boxes in project sites and communicating the partners’ contacts and addresses with community members and committees,
