Project Summary

This shelter support and NFI intervention will provide life-saving assistance to 47,880 conflict affected individuals in Iraq, including those facing prolonged displacement, returnees, and vulnerable host community members.
Informed by consultations with men, women, boys and girls in affected areas, program activities and approaches will prioritize the safety, access and dignity of conflict-affected families, mainstreaming protection principles. Through shelter upgrades and repairs to war-damaged shelters, CRS will ensure that conflict-affected households have access to quality living space that is appropriate to their context in either displacement or return. NFI distributions, consisting of both cash and in-kind items, will enable targeted beneficiaries to restore their essential relief items and household assets. Across both shelter and NFI components, where markets are functioning and accessible, CRS will utilize market-based approaches to support local markets and uphold beneficiaries’ dignity, agency and choice.

CRS will implement the project in Baghdad, Anbar, Duhok, Kirkuk and Ninewa governorates, working in partnership with Caritas Iraq for implementation in Baghdad and Anbar. CRS has funding for education, livelihoods, and social cohesion programming that will complement that activities proposed for shelter and NFI assistance.

Needs

Across Iraq families continue to face notable humanitarian needs. Whilst the number of internally displaced persons (IDPs) returning home (4.1 million) exceeds the number who remain in displacement (1.8 million), in contexts where return is voluntary, the relief of going home is often tempered by the challenges of rebuilding; many families find their homes severely damaged or completely destroyed and their household items destroyed or looted. In cases where return has been premature, secondary displacements are being recorded. These secondary displacements are further straining the protracted displacement across Iraq, in which an estimated 30% of IDPs are in camps, 8% in critical shelters, and 62% in private settings. In 2019, protracted displacement is expected to continue. At the time of the REACH survey in August 2018, the vast majority of in-camp and out-of-camp IDPs (68% and 66% respectively) reported that they had no intention of moving to a different location within the following three months. For many families living in protracted displacement, the quality of basic items accumulated during displacement have deteriorated over time, but families are unable to replace them due to exhausted savings and poor economic opportunities. Limited access to financial capital or income generating opportunities leaves also displaced families especially vulnerable to new shocks like the seasonal extremes of cold winters and dry, hot summers. In the summer, CRS needs assessments have consistently highlighted the need for fans and food and water storage. Through assessments in IDP camps in Ninewa, Kirkuk, and Anbar, CRS has found that newly displaced families need core NFI items, alongside seasonal items such as blankets, heaters and fuel storage in the winter. A notable percentage of out-of-camp IDPs remain in tenuous housing conditions that fall below Sphere standards. Many out-of-camp IDPs reside in “critical shelters conditions,” which include partially constructed houses and unfinished buildings that are typically concrete block structures with only a skeleton of walls, floors, and stairs, and without windows, doors, or other finishing various across locations; for example, one recent CRS assessment in Kirkuk governorate found that the vast majority of out-of-camps IDPs were living in unfinished or informally built houses. These shelters leave their occupants with little protection from the harsh climate or privacy from the hundreds of other families in the same shelters. Over 33% of out-of-camp IDPs highlight lack of privacy and overcrowding as key shelter concerns. This can be especially restrictive for women and girls who may not be able to be in same room as non-familial men. Similar privacy concerns are present in areas of return where, because homes were damaged or destroyed, many families are cohabitating in small spaces.

Activities

CRS will coordinate with other actors to ensure that the most vulnerable households are selected for assistance, using or adapting tools such as the Cash Working Group's socioeconomic vulnerability assessment tool as appropriate.NFI Activity Descriptions#1: Provide highly vulnerable IDPs living in camps with NFIs – 13,860 individuals / 2,520 householdsTo ensure IDPs in camps can maintain adequate living conditions, CRS will provide a basic NFI kit or equivalent cash transfer value, where market assessment show cash as an appropriate modality. In addition, as some families are entering their fourth year of displacement, in many cases the basic NFI assistance that they received upon initial displacement has been worn out or damaged, especially in cases where multiple families are sharing the items. This is particularly true for families dealing with the harsh living conditions in camps. When needs are identiﬁed, CRS will conduct an assessment to determine an appropriate response, including which items require replacement, to provide basic NFI top up kits, in kind or through a cash modality. Top-up kits could include speciﬁc top-ups for seasonal assistance.#2: Provide highly vulnerable IDPs living out of camps with NFIs – 15,000 individuals / 2,727 households.For displaced families living in out of camp environments, CRS will provide a basic NFI kit or equivalent cash transfer value, where market assessment show cash as an appropriate modality. As per Activity 1, when needs are identiﬁed, CRS will conduct an assessment to determine an appropriate response, to provide basic NFI top up kits, in kind or through a cash modality. Top-up kits could include speciﬁc top-ups for seasonal assistance. For all NFI assistance, CRS will determine if a cash-based response is appropriate to give families greater agency to address their highest priority needs. CRS’ In-Kind and Cash Distribution SoPs will be utilized for all distributions.Shelter Activity Descriptions#1: Carry out shelter upgrades that increase the security, privacy, and basic protection from external elements for conflict-affected families living in critical shelters in out of camp environments - 2,310 individuals / 420 households. Following coordination with local stakeholders and other humanitarian actors, and detailed needs assessments, CRS engineers will conduct assessments to ensure targeted buildings are structurally sound. If the building is safe for families to occupy, the assessment will determine what upgrades are needed, taking into consideration the unique needs of occupants (i.e. elderly, the disabled). CRS engineers will translate assessed needs into a detailed Bill of Quantity (BoQ) for inputs needed for first-line (i.e. plastic sheeting), and where possible, for second-line (i.e. PVC) upgrades. CRS staff consult with shelter occupants during the technical assessment, to explain the upgrades process and seek their input in placement of window openings and internal doors and partitions. #2: Carry out shelter upgrades that increase the security, privacy, and basic protection from external elements for returnees living in critical shelters - 14,400 individuals / 2,618 households. Using the same process as outlined in Shelter activity #1, CRS will upgrade critical shelters inhabited by returnees.#3: Undertake repairs to residential buildings that have been damaged by armed conflict, military operations, or occupation by armed actors - 2,310 individuals / 420 households.Shelter repairs will target families living in sub-standard damaged housing, or whose return from displacement is hindered by inability to safely inhabit their home. Shelter repair activities will target buildings that have not sustained any structural damage and are within Category II of the Shelter Cluster’s Categories of War-Damaged Buildings in Iraq. War-damaged shelters will be assessed by CRS engineers. CRS will utilize the Guidelines on Emergency Repairs of War-Damaged Buildings to determine the scope of repairs that are needed to create a “core” safe and habitable living space for affected households; the repairs are not intended for the entire house. Based on the technical assessment, CRS will develop an individualized BOQ for each shelter. CRS will follow the Shelter Cluster’s recommended value of $1,500 to $5000 per shelter for repairs assistance to minimum standards. Repairs will also include basic household-level water and sanitation repairs or installation, including latrines, showers and water tanks. To implement repairs, CRS may utilize either a direct contracting modality, a cash grant modality, or both.

Protection Risk Analysis

CRS has identified the following as potential protection risks: 1- NFI distributions are non-responsive to gender norms or those who are differently abled, limiting beneficiaries’ access to receive assistance.2- In-kind NFIs cause harm to beneficiaries through unintended misuse, poor quality or faulty items. 3- Beneficiaries in receipt of NFI assistance are threatened or harassed- when others in the community did not receive, or received alternative, assistance.4- IDPs or returnees are evicted from houses following CRS upgrades or repair works.5- Shelter upgrades or repairs unintentionally expose vulnerable groups, such as women and girls and those with disabilities, to harm.

Mitigation Measures

As is true across all CRS programming, protection is mainstreamed throughout project activities following CRS Global guidance and best practices. CRS monitors protection issues through both its feedback mechanisms as well as through ongoing beneficiary follow-up and program monitoring; CRS will regularly review this information and make program adjustments to reduce existing and newly identified risks. Mitigation measures for those risks identified above are as follows: 1- CRS provides separate lines for men and women during distributions. Elderly persons and those with young children are invited to the front of the queue, and chairs are provided for them to sit and wait. If a family is unable to attend the distribution due to age, illness, disability, or other limiting factors, they can nominate someone to receive on their behalf during the registration process. Similarly, if any female-headed households do not feel safe attending the distribution, they can also nominate someone to attend on their behalf. These measures are contained within CRS’ Cash and In-Kind Distribution SoPs. 2- CRS has robust procurement procedures which include receiving samples as part of the tendering process and checking vendor warehouses in the day prior to distribution to spot check samples to ensure quality standards. CRS will provide informational materials to beneficiaries such as stickers or leaflets with items to support correct use. 3- CRS closely coordinates with the S/NFI Cluster to ensure coordination of assistance in geographic locations, to avoid duplication of effort, and to ensure clarity of message for communities. Based on needs assessments and conflict analysis, CRS will determine whether blanket or targeted assistance is appropriate; each distribution has a communication plan detailing how, when and what messages are communicated to community stakeholders about upcoming assistance provision. For in-kind distributions CRS stipulates to vendors that items should be the same specification to ensure all households receive comparable items. 4- For war-damaged shelter repair, CRS will ensure that the tenure of the beneficiary can be confirmed either through property records, or through signed testimony from local community leaders in cases where documentation has been lost or destroyed. The agreement that is signed between IDPs and homeowners for second-line upgrades is designed to give IDPs basic protection from eviction. 5- CRS staff are trained to be cognizant of the protection and privacy needs of men, women, boys, and girls, and assess shelters accordingly. This results in consideration of additional partitions or internal doors, to ensure separated space is created between households sharing shelters, and a separated space for women and girls. Similarly, BOQs can be tailored to unique needs of occupants who are disabled or elderly, such as the inclusion of ramps and handrails when necessary.

How is your project accountable to affected population

CRS Shelter and NFI projects are designed based on review of secondary data sources, coupled with primary data collection during which the affected population are directly engaged to identify priority needs within their community. Based on these needs, activities are designed to address the unique needs of conflict-affected Iraqis, while also ensuring responsiveness to the distinct needs of specific groups such as women, girls, the elderly, and those with disabilities. CRS takes a client-oriented approach to beneficiary accountability by actively seeking out feedback and ensuring transparency through two-way communication channels. CRS has developed inclusive feedback mechanisms based on input provided by affected communities on preferred methods of communication. CRS Iraq operates free hotlines on the phone networks maintained by CRS accountability focal points in the CRS MEAL Technical Support Unit (TSU) during business hours. The hotline numbers are advertised to beneficiaries and community members via signage and the distribution of ‘hotline cards’ which contain full hotline details. To complement the hotline, CRS establishes help desks in/outside of distribution sites made up of a team of MEAL staff (one male, one female) to accept any feedback or complaints during distributions. Whether received in person or via phone, CRS ensures that 100% of feedback is addressed either real-time when the feedback is received, or through appropriate follow-up by CRS staff; most cases are resolved within one week of receipt. CRS utilizes stakeholder feedback to support adaptive program management. The CRS MEAL TSU shares all feedback to program staff on a weekly basis and aggregate information is compiled into a feedback dashboard to identify trends across projects and activities. During quarterly programmatic reviews aggregated community feedback from the quarter are analyzed, informing a “learning-to-action” discussion for programmatic design and implementation adjustments.
