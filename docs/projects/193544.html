Project Summary

This multi-sector Project will support the West Bank Protection Consortium (the Consortium) to deliver assistance to 43783 Palestinians at risk of forcible transfer in the West Bank, including East Jerusalem due to recurrent violations of international law. Building on previous risks and vulnerability analyzes, the Action addresses the protection needs of persons and communities of concern through a multi-sectoral approach that reduces vulnerabilities, builds capacities and responds to threats.Specifically, the Project aims at: (1) preventing and responding to protection incidents (demolitions, settler-related, military operations) with emergency assistance (cash and in-kind), protective measures, and mental health and psychosocial support interventions; (2) mitigating protection risks for highly vulnerable communities (172) through both legal aid and synergizing community-based protection (CPA)and the provision of basic and social infrastructure, including . the delivery of WASH and health infrastructure, hygiene and RCCE awareness materials, education infrastructure, as well as livelihood support for women and men; and (3) promoting increased respect for IHL and IHRL and accountability for violations through coalition advocacy and stakeholder mobilization undertaken with Third States, International Organizations, Palestinian authorities and media outlets. The Project provides a mechanism to respond to OCHA triggers for demolitions and settler-related incidents. Shelter, WASH, Education, MHPSS, FSL, legal aid and emergency responses and advocacy will be coordinated through the relevant Clusters/WGs. A protection response plan is updated yearly in each of the 172 community where the consortium implements the CPA approach, which allows the prioritization of activities and monitoring of progress in community members’ safety and access to basic services. Groups traditionally excluded from decision-making (women, children, PWDs) are given particular attention and responses are designed to fit their specific needs. The plans describe what communities need to be better protected from forcible transfer it the 16 sectors (Access, Access to services, Civil Society presence, WASH, Education, Land Status, Protection, Health, Transportation, Livelihood, Gender, Shelter, Energy, Demography, Relation with PA and Settler violence). Legal needs relate to supporting and advising communities and individuals to prevent demolitions and confiscations (including in the cases of demolition orders against schools, such as Ein Samiya and Um Qussa); as well as challenging the increasingly restrictive legal framework underpinning the coercive environment Consequently, the Project is envisaged to contribute to the realization of the following HRP objectives: 1. Increased respect for International Humanitarian Law (IHL) and International Human Rights Law (IHRL) and accountability for violations; 2. Protection response mechanisms are in place to prevent and mitigate the effects of the occupation and conflict related violence. Implementing partners will also submit projects as individual agencies, which will include some of the same activities (including legal aid services; shelter upgrades, WASH and other projects). Some of these projects will complement this Project’s assistance but will respond to a logic of ‘contiguity’ rather than ‘continuity’, since they are informed by different rationales and approaches. For this reason, financial resources will be kept separated. Our planning, monitoring, and reporting systems prevent any duplication.

1 - Needs

The situation in the WB remains a protracted protection crisis, driven by a lack of compliance with IHL and IHRL. According to the draft 2023 oPt HNO, there are 800,000 Palestinians in need of humanitarian aid in the WB, including 224,000 in Area C, Hebron (H2) and EJ, the target areas of this intervention. In Area C and EJ, 27% of the population is assessed as being in severe need.Settler violence continues to be perpetrated in the WB with impunity: Between 1 January and 29 August 2022, OCHA recorded 481 incidents of settler violence resulting in injury and/or property damage, representing a 44 percent increase compared to the same period in 2021. 2022 will constitute the sixth year of consecutive annual growth in the number of such attacks. The 2022 MSNA also states that those HHs that reported having experienced violent settlers acts in the 6 months prior to data collection, 54% reported having engaged in negative coping mechanism in response to such acts, including changing livelihood and withholding children from travelling (which in many cases resulted in dropping out of school).After a slowing in the rate of destruction of Palestinian property in the first half of 2022 compared to 2021, this rate has increased in recent months: it is predicted that 2022 will constitute the fifth consecutive annual rise in destruction of Palestinian property. Demolitions don’t affect only private residential or livelihood structures, but also public infrastructure such as schools: as of October 2022, 6 schools in Area C, have pending demolition orders and are therefore at imminent risk of demolition. Safe access to education is further compromised by a series of factors, according to the most recent MSNA, including settler violence and harassment on the way to school (31% of cases), traffic hazards/crossing roads (41%), delays on checkpoints (29%) and military presence around school (22%): these data matches with the data collected on the field by the WBPC through the CPA, which reports that when a school is not present inside a community, the threats for the commuting girls and boys increases exponentially, with drop out rates also increasing (especially for girls). The project will support safe access to education both through the rehabilitation of school building in areas where the absence of a school would put girls and boys at risk, and through legal aid provision to schools at risk of demolition. Throughout Area C, the coercive environment created by Israel deprives Palestinians of their homes and results in disruption to livelihoods, a reduced standard of living, limited access to essential services and reduced capacity to upgrade one’s shelter to minimum standards, due to building restrictions and systematic lack of building permits. The project response is informed by the annual needs assessment conducted in the months of October and November, targeting 172 vulnerable communities in Area C, H2, and EJ peripheries utilizing the CPA tools. This process utilizes the MQ tool to survey and record all the humanitarian needs in these communities, including women, girls, boys, elderly and persons with disabilities in the process. The data collected feeds into the Ranking tool which prioritizes the interventions in each communities and defines the needs that require urgent response. The shelter rehabilitation was ranked as a top priority among many of these communities. The major shelter needs were noticed in communities located close to Israeli settlements, check points/ barriers, military areas, or bypass roads. The needs were higher for those which present metallic sheds as type of shelter. Other needs included lack of privacy due to extremely small size of shelter, scares winterization preparedness (leading to water leakages in the house), and overall substandard condition

3 - Indicators

The WBPC has developed a specific Log-frame and its related indicators to monitor its integrated protection approach to counter prevalent violations of IHL and IHRL that give rise to forcible transfer. As a key component of the Consortium's MEAL system, the MEAL framework has been updated and incorporated the recently added indicators to collect high quality data to monitor program progress, making adjustments, and evaluating results. Under the Consortium Emergency response, Post-response monitoring ensures that quality standards are met, measures beneficiary satisfaction, monitors displacement and identifies issues arising from the response. In addition, the monitoring is used to ensure that sufficient attention is paid to the four elements of protection mainstreaming throughout the implementation of the action and that corrective actions/measures are identified and implemented. In line with IASC commitments on accountability to affected people/populations and ECHO guidelines, protection principles have been integrated into the Consortium's MEAL framework. To do this, a methodology and tools were designed for each partner to integrate the four elements of protection mainstreaming into existing monitoring and evaluation activities, and disaggregation by gender and age is always ensured. Under the Consortium Resilience building component, the Community Protection Approach incorporates field-based participatory monitoring of the actions undertaken in targeted communities, combining quantitative data collected and qualitative data stemming from focus group discussions with beneficiaries. The Protection Vulnerability Index, Protection Response Plans and the community profiles are updated on an annual basis to support trends analyses of vulnerability in the target communities since 2015. Findings contribute to the final reporting of the impact of the Action on the overall resilience of the community. On completion of basic and social infrastructure projects, a structured handover process is conducted involving an evaluation to ensure quality standards are met. 5.2 - Please briefly explain why the specific modality/ies for this response were chosen.

The project implements a range of activities under a common protection goal. Legal assistance and other community engagement and support are implemented through service delivery modality, while for material response the in-kind modality is the preferred option to ensure quality response. The cash modality is used where in-kind support is not possible due to security, safety and other constraints. This is particularly the case in East Jerusalem, but also in situations where the presence of outside actors(such as NGO staff or contractors) might negatively affect the safety of community members and/or the provided assets.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

Do no harm principles are mainstreamed in all aspects of the Action. These principles are translated operationally as follows: Vulnerability-based targeting: The communities targeted for resilience-building support under this Action were prioritized due to the severity of the protection risks they face, assessed through multi-sectoral, mixed-method protection vulnerability assessments. Likewise, the planned activities in these communities are selected based on protection-sensitive assessments. Since 2020, the Consortium introduced vulnerability assessments for its emergency responses.The Action integrates IASC commitments in relation to AAP through policies and tools that ensure a people-centred programming cycle. Beneficiaries are involved at all stages, from design to evaluation, and play an active role in decision-making through community-driven processes. These processes are designed to be inclusive, enabling groups traditionally excluded from decision-making (women/girls, youth, elderlies, persons with disabilities) to secure adapted responses to their specific needs and priorities. The project also has a dedicated AGD WG.Mitigation of risks: Participatory risk analyses inform all programming decisions, based on informed beneficiary consent. The Consortium model synergizes risk awareness activities with operational risk mitigation measures and preventive legal action to mitigate assistance-related protection concerns. Prior to the implementation of rehabilitation or construction activities, detailed risk and technical assessments and legal analyses are conducted with beneficiaries to determine whether the activity should proceed and on what basis in order to ensure do no harm and informed consent. Beneficiaries are involved not only in activity design but also as implementers of substitution actions in order to reduce potential security risks while enabling beneficiary families and broader communities to supplement their income. The technical designs ensure privacy, safety, dignity and accessibility for men and women, boys and girls with or without disability. Implementation modalities (contractor, community or beneficiary implementation) are selected in consultation with beneficiaries, based on do no harm principles, and aim at enhancing local capacity, ownership and social cohesion – including through cost-sharing.The implementing agencies also take seriously into consideration the possible negative impact on the environment that the project activities may have (especially in the Shelter and WASH sectors), so from 2023 the ECHO minimum environmental requirements (MERS) will be applied to the project.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

Through the systematic mainstreaming of Age, Gender and Diversity (AGD) considerations in its operations, the Consortium ensures that all persons of concern enjoy their rights on an equal footing and are able to participate fully in the decisions that affect their lives and the lives of their family members and communities. An AGD Working Group has been established within the Consortium’s management structure to further mainstream AGD across its programming by following a four-pronged approach: (1) reinforcing sex/age/diversity-disaggregated data (SADD) collection and analysis in order to ensure AGD sensitive response; (2) providing assistance tailored to the needs of the different vulnerable sub-groups of the target population; (3) ensuring that CRM and other monitoring mechanisms are accessible and safe to all; (4) implementing policies and practices in the Consortium’s partner organisations that promote inclusion and diversity. Principles of safety, dignity, accessibility and participation shape the design, implementation and monitoring of the Consortium’s activities and serve as compass to sustain its efforts towards greater AGD mainstreaming compliance. Significant improvement has been made in the systematic and consistent collection of disaggregated data, the composition of field teams has been adjusted to be more gender-sensitive, and technical designs and responses have been adapted to better include the needs of women, children and PWDs. Furthermore, the Consortium will continue to ensure the systematic, consistent and effective referral of cases that fall outside the scope of intervention of the Consortium, particularly protection cases, will be undertaken in close collaboration with the humanitarian cluster system. A key element of this consolidated referral system is the effective functioning of complaints response mechanisms (CRM). Under this action, each partner will have his own Feedback Response mechanism, including methodology and SOPs, in place, with demonstrated improvement during the previous actions.Referral mechanism: The WBPC, through a multi-sectorial integrated approach, prevents and responds to protection risks associated with forcible transfer and coercive environment, including forced evictions, demolitions, and settlers’ violence. During fieldwork, the WBPC and its partners come across a wide range of needs, in and out of the Consortium’s scope of intervention. This is why, an inter-agency referral system within the WBPC has been developed to ensure harmonization of practices and processes across partners, to track trends and gaps, and to set minimum standards and principles for the timely identification and referral of individuals and families with urgent needs. Part of the clusters and the national referral systems, the WBPC referral mechanism ensures that all agencies can conduct safe and quality referrals and adhere to the minimum standards. Hence, the AGD WG also organized a training on "Basic GBV concepts and safe referrals for non-specialized GBV actors".
