Project Summary

South Sudan is one of the poorest countries in the World. More than 83 percent of the population resides in rural areas. And poverty is endemic with at least 80 percent of the vulnerable population living an equivalent of less than $0.99 per day and more than one third of the population lacks secure access to food, clean water and poor infrastructures such as roads, electricity, health centers and schools etc.
Ten years after independence and three years after the signing of the revitalized peace agreement, people in South Sudan continue to face deteriorating humanitarian conditions. Their situation is worsened by endemic violence, conflict, access constraints and operational interference, public health challenges such as direct and indirect effects of COVID-19 and climatic shocks resulting in the dual phenomena of extraordinary flooding and localized drought, which have a severe impact on people’s livelihoods, hampers access to education and water, sanitation and hygiene and health services.
Most South Sudanese rely directly or indirectly on subsistence agriculture and livestock nurture, their livelihoods are currently concentrated in low productive, unpaid agriculture and pastoralist work in the country, however, the prolonged conflict that began in December 2013 is causing widespread destruction, death and displacement. In the IPC analysis period of January 2021, 5.29 million people (45.2% of the population) are estimated to have faced Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity, of which 1.11 million people faced Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. A collapsing economy, reduced crop production and dependence on imports seriously undermine people’s ability to secure sufficient nutritious food all year round, putting millions of vulnerable lives at risk when it is critically needed; however, hunger and malnutrition levels are at historic high levels. As of January 2020, 7.1 million people in need of humanitarian assistance. A record number of people - 6.9 million or 60 percent of South Sudan's entire population - do not know where their next meal will come from as hunger peaks during the lean season from May to July 2021, Women and girls suffer disproportionately from hunger and food insecurity, cultural norms and decades of violence – including rape as a weapon of war – underpin deep gender inequalities. Men control most productive assets and positions of power. With 80 percent of the country’s women are illiterate, domestic violence and early marriage, teenage pregnancy is common place among vulnerable women and girls.
CODAID will implement the coordination and implementation of this project with major focus on food security. Food Security and Livelihood interventions that address the livelihood needs of the affected Households through agricultural livelihood support so as to boost own food production as well as enhance access to emergency food supply will also be implemented
CODAID will deliver its activities and ensure program quality and robust objectives through close monitoring of the activities, accountability to affected populations, and gender mainstreaming and effective mechanisms for humanitarian protection. Project beneficiaries will be selected through a community-based targeting process with due emphasis to vulnerable groups such as female and child headed households, people with disability, the elderly, returnees and IDPs. Protection and gender sensitive approach will be applied during site selection such as for water points, WASH facilities and distribution sites for FSL kits by carrying out GBV risk analysis and focus group discussion to safeguard beneficiaries from risk of GBV and sexual exploitation.
CODAID will also work closely with the community and local government Institutions in order to effectively deliver the planned response and avoid duplication of resources.

1 - Needs

According to IPC of December 2021, more people are likely to experience severe food insecurity in 2022. An estimated 8.3 million people, including refugees, are expected to experience severe food insecurity at the peak of the 2022 lean season (May-July). This represents a 7 per cent increase from the 7.7 million in 2021. The County is categorized within the highland forest and sorghum livelihoods zone (FEWSNET, 2018). Agriculture and pastoralism are the primary livelihoods in Budi, with sorghum, maize, millet, sesame, and beans being the main crops grown in the area. A 2018 report estimated that 85% of households engage in agriculture (FAO & WFP, 2018). Cattle grazing and displacement in the area has also impacted the quality of arable land, which has led to farmers cultivating in areas where the soil quality is lower. Additionally, successive droughts have impacted locally sourced food supplies.The upper land of Budi County indicted to be food secured by last year/2021 (unless with the misuse of food) with no much registered cases of malnutrition as well, most especially in the upper lands (Kibongorok, Kilanya, Nagishot, Kadumakuch, Tulugi,Kerewan, Charit and Ngatuba ) which harvested a promising yield, with exception of Ngarich, Loriyok, Kikilai, Lorema, Piobokoi and Kimatong bomas which seems to not been food secured (FSNMS+ ROUND-27 report 2021). These are influenced by several factors including unpredictable insecurity caused by cattle raiding, crop and livestock pest, parasite and disease incidences, poor sanitation and feeding practices and poor social and economic conditions to mention. Agricultural practices in Ngarich, Loriyok, Kimatong and Greater Lotukei was poor from the period of March - October 2021 and could aggravate the food security situation within these areas. Despite the various interventions in place to counter the deteriorating food security situation and nutrition in some parts of Budi County may remain vulnerable to food insecurity and malnutrition as vulnerability levels increases by day with lost hopes of enough food. Budi generally, Households main source of food consumption is dominated by the households’ own crop production and staple food from markets. Supply to the markets comes both from local production and imports, mainly from neighboring countries like, Kenya, and Uganda. Households depend more on their own production between October-December (post-harvest period), and dependence on markets increases and reaches a peak in the lean season (June-August), as the household’s stocks are exhausted. After the main harvest, the availability of food commodities in the market is less problematic, but prices are at elevated levels. However, with the poor / no harvest during the same periods 2021, most households relied on borrowing and wild fruit consumptions especially areas like Kadumakuch, Tulugi, Kerewan and Charit with poor crop yields. (FSNMS+ ROUND-27). Therefore, at least 98% majority of the households relay primarily on own production and wild food gathering. Wild fruits and vegetables like” Lalop”, “pumpkin seeds”, and green. CODAID is seeking HRP funds to deliver response to improve on food security situation and resilience in Budi County. The project will support the FSL interventions that will focus on the provision of emergency livelihood inputs complemented with skill building and technical assistance to enhance animal production, emergency food production and build community resilience through stimulating crop, animal, vegetable and Vegetable/kitchen gardens which will be integrated with health and nutrition centers targeting mothers with children in curative centers and PLWs. The increased availability of vegetable will improve access to a diversified and nutritious diet. The adoption of better farming will enhance access to immediate food supply and have long-lasting impacts on food security with implication for food self-sufficiency and reduced dependency on food assistance

2 - Project activities

The project will have the following activities that will be undertaken during the project implementations period: Conduct an inception meeting to inform key stakeholders of the project scope and process, including the FSL, cross cutting related activities, i.e GBV, protection, Covid-19 related activities, and the basis for targeting. The meeting will also be an opportunity to make the community own the project and receive feedback from key stakeholders and develop an action plan.  Verification of the registered beneficiaries to confirm that the vulnerable group were targeted based on the plan. Conduct community engagement sessions to ensure the targeted communities understand the roles of the project management committees (PMCs), and the importance of having women driving the community development initiatives. This will ensure acceptance from local leadership; the project management committees will be informed to liaise very closely with and update local leadership often on the project’s progress. Distribution of improved seeds, vegetable kits and tools with the resolved of increasing local food production that will assist households to cut back on food assistance. Establishment of nutrition kitchen gardening at nutrition and health centers for growing of nutritious vegetables and training of caregivers. Training of vegetable (seeds) kits recipients on best gardening practices including climate smart techniques, and marketing. Households that qualify for receiving the vegetable kits will be trained on best gardening practices at the demonstration/kitchen garden sites. These sites will be negotiated with the facilities (OTP/Health). All demonstration/kitchen garden sites will be owned by a household or two for sustainability as CODAID will not continuously support any institutional learning sites. All produce harvested from the learning/demonstration sites will remain the property of their respective owners for consumption and income generation. For ease of travel, CODAID will conduct these trainings for cluster of beneficiaries closer to the site nearest to them. Hence, CODAID will encourage/support many community members to establish gardens in their areas that could potentially be used as learning sites for surrounding beneficiaries.  Training on animal management to the pastoralists and, to equip them with needed skills and knowledge in animal management and immunize and treat animals. Train the selected beneficiaries on good farming practices and food management As cross cutting issues, Gender and protection related activities will be also implemented in the community through an integrated approaches that seek to address the priority needs of vulnerable women, men, girls and boys and promote actions which help reduce violence including GBV and its impact on communities.  The project will update the accountability to affected people (AAP) framework/ work plan at planning stage. This will ensure all activities and interventions related to protection and genders are captured as part of core activities. CODAID staffs will be responsible for establishing daily routines, and site monitoring to ensure that activities are on schedule.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

As of December 2021, an estimated 5.3 million people were reached with some form of humanitarian assistance. More people are likely to experience severe food insecurity in 2022. An estimated 8.3 million people, including refugees, are expected to experience severe food insecurity at the peak of the 2022 lean season (May-July). This represents a 7 per cent increase from the 7.7 million in 2021. The counties of most concern are in the states of Jonglei, Lakes, Unity and Warrap (specifically Greater Tonj), with the most extreme case being Fangak County in Jonglei State, where the situation is comparable to Pibor County in 2021. Food consumption gaps are extreme, and people have largely exhausted their emergency coping strategies. In 2022, the precarious situation is expected to be further exacerbated by the country’s increasing humanitarian caseload, the expected increase in the duration of needs in the most affected areas and the projected further reduction in humanitarian assistance across the country. Food assistance is expected to decrease by 10 to 20 per cent from the already reduced levels in 2021. The current economic crisis is impacting people’s ability to access markets, while conflict, flooding and seasonal effects are limiting the flow of both commercial and humanitarian goods and services The situation continues to worsen, and although famine was officially declared over in June 2017, the overall food security situation continues to deteriorate. In Eastern Equatoria State, is still facing food insecurity and food security remains concerning in 5 counties, including Kapoeta North, Kapoeta East, Budi, Kapoeta South and Lafon/Lopa which are classified in Crisis (IPCPhase 3) or worse due to high food prices, inter-communal conflicts and cattle-raidingStudy on community self-protection and coping strategies in Budi County indicates that there are limited positive coping mechanisms in accessing basic services. A great number of households have limited access to food and they have resorted to eating wild fruits like the case of greater Lotukei and greater Kimotong. Most people are planning to go to camps if no food assistance is advanced to them. Over half of households in the lowerlands of Budi are food insecure and may are borrowing food or relying on help from a friend and relatives, and lacked enough food or money to buy food (CODAID, August 2021). Women and girls are typically more vulnerable to food insecurity than men, often eating last and least. Cultural and social roles dictate that they should care for their families, feed them, and even sacrifice their own portions when necessary. Men generally eat first and receive the largest share, with children and/or dependents eating next. Women and girls are often tasked with collecting food. This is an extremely physical and intense activity that can take an entire day to complete, which puts women and girls at risk of being attacked, raped or killed whilst carrying out activities just to put food on the table.This project will directly improve the food security in the county by training people/ community on improved farming and animal management, providing improved seeds and tools. This will contribute to food security in the area such that by the lean season of May to June 2023 households will have enough food for their household consumption and even a little for market.
