Project Summary

The intervention aims to provide a wide-ranging support from emergency to regular food assistance as well as household and community based agricultural, livestock and livelihoods assistance. The project aims to target populations who are both foods insecure as well as at risk of food insecurity through multiple activities but mutually reinforcing activities to ensure that all four pillars of food security related to access, availability, utilization and stability of food are adequately addressed through lifesaving, life-sustaining, appropriate resilience, and early recovery interventions.

Needs

Eight years into the crisis, the main drivers to food insecurity are prolonged displacements, loss and lack of livelihoods and reduced production capacity which in turn resulted in limited physical and financial access to food, high prices and inflation contributing to reduced purchasing power and continuous livelihood depletion of the most vulnerable populations. The main drivers of food insecurity in Syria are a set of interrelated factors related to the deteriorating economic environment as well as food insecurity stemming from massive displacements. Almost 40 percent of the surveyed households reported to be an IDP or returnee of whom 74.5 percent reported to be displaced for more than 12 months. Other key drivers include loss of livelihoods and reduced production capacity, limited physical and financial access to food, high prices and inflation, reduced purchasing power and continuous livelihood depletion of the most vulnerable populations. As per World Bank from 2011 – 2016, the cumulative GDP loss in Syria is estimated at $226 billion out of which $16 billion in losses and damages attributable to agriculture sector alone. Overall from various sources 83-89 percent of Syrians are Below Poverty Line. As per ESCWA, an estimated 83.4 percent of Syrians live below the upper (moderate) poverty line which is an increase from 28 percent in 2010. Purchasing food on credit continues to be adopted by every second household and is more common among IDPs, returnees, female headed and rural households. An estimated 40 percent of Syrians spend more than 65 percent of their expenditure on food, 57.2 percent of food insecure households reported to have outstanding debt and 65 percent of food insecure people are adopting crisis or emergency negative coping strategies. Lack of information or access to food security related can cause household tensions leading to negative coping mechanisms including tensions and early marriage. Qualitative and quantitative data provides support to the vulnerability of female-headed households as prevalence of food insecurity from the assessed households indicate that 28.1 percent of the male-headed households are food insecure, compared to 32.4 percent of the female headed households. Furthermore, divorcees or widows have difficulties to access assistance because of customs and traditions at times and harassment may occur at distribution points. In some areas, females’ salaries working as agricultural labor are lower than males’ earnings (CFSAM). Child labor and early dropouts of school continues to be a major coping strategy of farmers and displaced households. Availability of food in Syria has also been hugely impacted by the crisis as well as the drought. The CFSAM 2018 reports that at 1.2 million metric tonnes, wheat production in 2017/2018 winter season is the lowest it has been in 30 years, also lower than the previous year at 1.8 million metric tonnes and only 30 percent of the pre-crisis average of 4.1 million metric tonnes. In addition, barley production at 390,000 metric tonnes is the lowest since 2008. Large areas of rainfed cereals failed due to an extended dry period early in the planting season, and irrigated cereal yields were reduced because of unseasonably late heavy rains and high temperatures. The implication of lowest recorded production in the last three decades is that the availability of wheat and barley, major staples, are further constrained and there is a significant shortfall of wheat and barley to meet national cereal requirements even after considering potential government, commercial and food assistance imports. The major constraints to production being weather or dependency on rainfed farming in drought prone areas and availability on agricultural inputs. Additional constraints affecting agricultural sector are high production costs, lack of quality inputs and damaged or destroyed infrastructure (primarily irrigation).

Implementation of activities

All the project design and activities will be finalized after close consultation with the beneficiaries' families with all age segregation male and female and local councils. The basic aim of this intervention is to Improve the food security status of families and their youth girls and boys and engage them to effectively attempt to be self-reliant in the best possible way. The intervention plan anticipates that this will lead the families and their youth to think constructively and positively to work hard and be self-reliant in medium to long term. The intervention will target all population of IDPs, returnees, and host communities focussing on IDPs and returnees taking into consideration most vulnerable people, the women headed families, elderly headed family, child-headed family on the food needs of lactating women, elderly people, girls and boys, disabilities, people have and people with special health conditions.Following are the activities which will be carried during the course of the project:1. Improve the food security status of assessed food-insecure people through life-saving and life-sustaining food assistance by distribution 30000 food basket, 10000 RTEs, 60000 cooked meal, and support 57500 individuals by bread distribution on daily basis for 10 months. 2. Support self-reliance of affected households by protecting and building productive assets and restoring or creating income generating opportunities to save and sustain lives through A-distributing agriculture kits for summer crops to the farmers (seeds, fertilizers, pesticides) in addition to the technical support and agricultural extension. B-Support to small-scale food production through distributing laying hens (10 hens +1 rooster) with fodders sufficient for 6 months. C-Support to livestock asset building and protection through support livestock herders by fodder distribution, animal treatment services and vaccination, where the project aims to distribute 375 tons of fodders, Serve 9000 sheep and 3500 cows by screening and treatment through a mobile clinic, and provide 201000 vaccine doses ( 180000 for sheep and 21000 for cow). D-Support Income generating activities including vocational training through providing technical training for women headed of households on food processing and dairy production and small grants to initiate their income generation activities. 3. Improve communities' capacity to sustain households’ livelihoods by improving linkages with value chain through the rehabilitation/ building of productive infrastructure as well as supporting services, early warning and DRR systems through: A-rehabilitate one mill to support the wheat value chain by buying the wheat from the farmers to support wheat marketing, produce the flour and bran, out of flour bread will be produced to be distributed to the beneficiaries with a subsidized price, bran will be included in fodder distribution activity. B- Establish three livestock disease laboratories (ELISA lab) to conduct a periodic diseases survey covering all NW Syria areas. The lab will support early warning for animal treatment and vaccination in addition to specifying the animal diseases in the NW Syria and help in intervention planning.

Protection Risk Analysis

During the project implementation, the following protection risks may arise. Possible actions to mitigate the risks are also outlined.1. Risk of sexual exploitation by humanitarian workers (risk of harassment) of women and girls, the risk will be mitigated through establish a confidential complaint mechanism, train staff on a code of conduct, and code of conduct in all staff contracts Address any SEA complaints. Recruit female staff and train them The field MEAL staff with monitor the situation by conducting Post monitoring feedback.2.Risk of violence from beneficiaries among themselves/with humanitarian workers when huge onset occurring (crowd control), the risk will be mitigated through identification of sites/environment first; set up several distribution spots on sites; do joint distribution with another partner 3.Diversion of aid, as mitigation mechanism meetings with community leaders/gate keepers/local councils on targeting criteria etcCash/voucher overly spent on non-food items, thus negatively impact food security outcomes. As a mitigation mechanism, provide vouchers or e-card not cash, vary the payment days. In addition to that the MEAL staff will continue vendor monitoring systems (i.e. the selected shops).4.Targeting for IDPs versus Host communities (in general), the risk will be mitigated through local beneficiary selection committees with equitable representation of both IDPs and host communities and clear/precise guidance to the CPs on the beneficiary selection process and criteria. 5.Access to land/assets Risk to secondary rights holders e.g. female headed households, as a mitigation mechanism engage with local authorities to verify the status review appropriateness of the activities versus the dynamic pertaining land rights6.Diversion of assets for communities’ infrastructures as a mitigation mechanism asset monitoring by CPs, keep close coordination and cooperation with local councils, establish governance committee to manage the community’s assets.In addition to this, Do No Harm and humanitarian principles will be fully complied with during detailed project planning,implementation and evaluation. Also, to ensure alignment with the best practices of humanitarian action, corehumanitarian standards will be carefully taken care of across the project management cycle.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

The current security situation that tends to be stable in most areas inside Syria will facilitate the intervention implementation to achieve the planned impacts. in addition to the good understanding and cooperation from most of the local stakeholders.Some risks beyond of QC control may affect the intervention implementation:1. A huge number of IDPs may reach the targeted areas if the East Euphrates operation started, so the demand on the food will be increased suddenly as a mitigation mechanism keep close coordination with the local councils and active organizations in the targeted area in order to harmonize the intervention and advocate through FSL cluster to the donors to address the gap and big demand for the food.2. Delayed of the deliveries due to closure of borders or close internal roads between targeted areas for unforeseen reasons. As a mitigation mechanism QC will keep close coordination with Turkish government since the humanitarian aids have exception, otherwise the procurement will be shifted to Syria, close coordination with the local councils and mean actors on the ground in order to facilitate the aids movements between the different areas and arrange to provide warehouses in the different targeted areas.3. New conflict may start in Idleb areas between Syrian regime and other army groups or between different army groups itself, as a mitigation mechanism, QC has a safety and security team in Gaziantep and the field inside Syria they will keep following up and evaluating the security situation in the implementing area continually. The safety and security plane will be developed and keep updated on daily basis according to the real situation in the ground in order to keep staff and beneficiaries safe.4. Community tension arising from the beneficiary selection. The mitigation mechanism community-based beneficiary selection where all stockholders will be engaged in the developing selection criteria. and the criteria will be published for all beneficiaris to be aware of it. Community feedback mechanisms will be available for all.
