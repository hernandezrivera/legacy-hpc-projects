Project Summary

Syria ranks among the world’s most severe humanitarian crises, and life-saving assistance is particularly critical as it confronts widespread hunger and disease within the context of a conflict. The need for humanitarian aid is overwhelming: UN-OCHA estimates that 15.3 million Syrians are in need of humanitarian aid in 2023.
This project builds on the ongoing initiatives being implemented by CARE Syria along the emergency, food security, livelihoods, WASH and protection sectors in Northeast Syria. The aim is to continue, expand and scale-up the current programs in order to cover the increasing needs and gaps, and reach more people-in-need, particularly in the host communities and displacement camps of Al-Hassakeh, Deir-ez-Zor and Ar-Raqqa governorates. This integrated, multi-sectoral assistance targets 291,629 vulnerable and conflict-affected people in the governorates of Areesha, Al Hol and Al-Hasakeh sub-districts (Al-Hassakeh governorate); Kisreh and Sur sub-districts (DRZ governorate), and Ein Issa sub-district (Ar-Raqqa).
CARE Syria’s ‘Impact Hypothesis’ for this project is in line with the HRP Strategic Objectives of ‘saving lives’ (SO1), ‘enhanced protection’ (SO2) and ‘increased resilience and access to services’ (SO3). “If the targeted households have immediate access to emergency NFI kits [emergency/NFI]; If they have improved access to cash assistance, livelihood inputs and capacity strengthening activities [FSL]; If they have increased access to safe and adequate water, sanitation, and hygiene services and improved hygiene practices [WASH]; and If protection risks to affected persons is mitigated and women and girls’ vulnerability to gender-based violence is reduced [Protection]; THEN vulnerable households and communities in NES can be able to increase their long-term resilience, reduce their vulnerability to future shocks and disasters, and improve their overall wellbeing.”
This project traverses the continuum between the provision of humanitarian aid, early recovery approaches and resilience building, with the ultimate goal of seeking sustainable and longer-term solutions to Syria’s most immediate and pressing needs. Specifically, this project is grounded in several elements including:
Building on a nexus approach to incorporate life-saving assistance while laying the groundwork for early recovery and resilience: All four sectors are focused on providing life-saving assistance given the gravity of the humanitarian crisis in NES as well as in line with HRP’s priorities, but also laying the groundwork for early recovery and resilience through: income-generating activities; skills development and entrepreneurial initiatives; agricultural/livestock inputs and trainings; market linkages; upgrades to critical livelihoods and water infrastructure and more reliable sources of water supply; and, enhanced skills for women and girls to have more control over their lives/ability to make informed choices.
Applying a combined focus on IDPs and host communities: This project will serve both communities in an effort to reduce tensions between the two groups and recognize the host communities’ vulnerabilities so they remain resilient and able to continue to host IDPs.

Integration between sectors and humanitarian actors: CARE Syria has deepened its integration approach across all its programs so that the activities in the sectors are mutually reinforcing and have an amplified effect on the outcomes of the other sectors. For example, WASH, health and nutrition awareness will support one another to promote uptake of behaviors which will have a combined improvement on the overall well-being of the beneficiaries. FSL and protection are linked whereby women who seek livelihoods assistance but also require GBV response will be supported through survivor-centered approaches to enable access to life-saving services and strengthened referral systems for additional support.

3 - Needs

According to Syria HNO 2023, almost seven out of ten people in Syria need assistance. Majority of Syrians are worse off in 2023 than in 2022. The overall PIN has increased from 14.6 million in 2022 to 15.3 million in 2023, including 5.3 million IDPs, 9.9 million residents and 52,700 returnees. Across NES, 99% of the population are living with less than USD 1.90 per person per day, meaning that almost the entire population is classified as living in extreme poverty. Households are using various negative coping mechanisms to endure this untenable situation, including child labor and child marriage and the sale of productive assets – all of which increase protection needs and/or reduce households’ capacity for self-sustenance in the future. As household resilience decreases, their humanitarian needs increase. Reliable access to sufficient and safe water across NES remains a major challenge. 60% of communities in NES have poor access to safe water. In early September, a cholera outbreak was announced by the WHO, with more than 23,000 suspected cases reported in NES so far. In terms of protection concerns, the top three of most commonly reported protection priority needs are (1) special assistance for vulnerable groups, (2) specialized child protection services, and (3) psychosocial support. The HSOS report also indicated that hazardous child labour is a protection risk across 32 communities in NES. Access to psychiatric and psychosocial support remains very limited.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

CARE’s recent RGA revealed that GBV is a dominant feature of the Syrian humanitarian crisis. Women and girls, especially the displaced, are subjected to increased denial of economic resources and education, movement restrictions, exploitation, forced and child marriage, intimate partner and family violence, and physical, psychological, emotional, sexual, and social violence. CARE’s core programming ensures the integration of gender, protection & inclusion across all projects guided by the ‘Gender in Emergencies’ framework. The different needs and impacts for women, men, boys and girls are continuously assessed and interventions are tailored accordingly. Gender-disaggregated data is collected in order to measure impacts and changes. CARE also implements protection-focused activities that are designed to directly address gender & protection concerns through comprehensive GBV and general protection services including case management & referrals, psychosocial support, skills development, and other risk mitigation efforts. Other specific ‘Safe and Inclusive Programming’ activities include: • Community-based protection monitoring• Training of staff & partners on Protection Mainstreaming Principles and Practices, Social Inclusion of Vulnerable Groups, and Safe Identification & Referrals• Training on Safeguarding to staff, partners, BNFs• Mapping of the available services in each field location• Close coordination with protection coordination mechanisms in NES


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

CARE has a zero-tolerance approach toward sexual harassment, exploitation and abuse. CARE carefully examines allegations and investigates, and takes appropriate disciplinary action where needed, taking into consideration the rights and interests of the survivor, consistent with CARE’s ‘survivor-centered approach’. This approach is aligned with Core Humanitarian Standard and IASC principles related to sexual exploitation and abuse. CARE Syria has a robust Community-based Feedback and Response Mechanism to solicit feedback & receive complaints. Its CFRM procedures provide guidance on: (1) effective community consultations – building upon community preferences on how to raise concerns & provide feedback; (2) detailed steps on managing feedback and complaints; (3) Clear informed consent – explaining people’s rights and entitlements, as well as key steps that CARE takes to keep confidentiality of sensitive complaints, and (4) Routing and management of sensitive complaints, including SEA and safeguarding, as well as protection measures and investigation pathways. In NES, CARE has already an established CFRM channels that provide safe & fair venue for all staff, partners, BNFs and community members to share feedback and complaints, including those related to SEA. The mechanisms include both proactive and reactive channels. Eg: hotlines, email, help desks, suggestion boxes, and through individual/community meetings. Both staff & BNFs are sensitized on the available channels


9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

CARE Syria is well-positioned to undertake this project due to its strong presence in the target communities, and local and global expertise in the core program areas. This relies on several critical assumptions: • Security situation allows access to beneficiary populations in the targeted areas• HAO, local councils and community leaders provide approvals in a timely manner• Local markets/prices remain stable enough for procurement and delivery of project supplies/materials• Local community continue to accept CARE’s work in the targeted geographic areas• CARE maintains strong relationships and trust with host and IDP communities • CARE is able to develop and deliver programming in such a way that CARE’s attention to women and girls does not result in backlash among men• CARE is able to provide adequate service to women and girls to overcome conflict and displacement challenges• Adequate funding is secured• No major disease outbreaks, or natural disasters CARE maintains a risk register for all its programs to identify and mitigate risks from the project’s onset. The risk register is reviewed and updated throughout the project implementation. Gender-sensitive community-based targeting mechanisms are developed to ensure that spaces are created where marginalised or excluded community voices are heard and their needs addressed.
