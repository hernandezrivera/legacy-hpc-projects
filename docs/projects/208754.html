Project Summary

An estimated 1.47M OOSC children have never enrolled or enrolled and disengaged or dropped out from primary education across Liberia, North-East Nigeria and Sierra Leone [GoN 2018; SL MICS 2017; UNICEF 2017]. All three countries are off track towards targets for Sustainable Development Foal 4: Quality Education, and Liberia is on track to be the last amongst all countries to achieve Universal Basic Education [UBE] [GEM Report, 2016].
The COVID-19 crisis has compounded the challenges faced in accessing education in emergencies and low-resource environments – school closures have had a catastrophic impact on access. By May 2020, 66%, 51% and 71% of children in Liberia, NE Nigeria and Sierra Leone respectively had no access to any alternative learning through a sustained period of school closure [Street Child 2020]. Schools have since opened to a sharp fall in attendance and enrolment rates. The longer that these children are out of school or learning spaces, the greater the risk of disengagement and dropout and the lower the likelihood they will ever return and reintegrate, and the greater the risk of long-term loss of learning for an entire generation of learners.
Street Child specialises in support children to be safe, in school and learning in low-resources and emergencies. In partnership with Educate a Child, Street Child aims to support 96,000 OOSC who have (i) never enrolled (ii) dropped out [not enrolled in the most recent academic year]; or (iii) enrolled but aren’t attending [within the last month] to be enrolled and retained in education. A strategic selection of four interventions is designed in direct response to the four obstacles to access –
(i) Intervention 1: Social Support targets the low prioritisation of education and exposure to risks in low-resource environments and emergencies; (ii) Intervention 2: Economic Support targets low levels of income and loss of livelihoods that increase the costs [including opportunity costs] of schooling; (iii) Intervention 3: Infrastructural Support targets the lack of accessible, safe, secure and sufficient spaces for learning: and (iv) Intervention 4: Instructional Support targets the lack of trained teachers and teaching and learning resources that lead to low enrolment, attendance and retention. The integration of these interventions targets the nexus of demand and supply side obstacles for out of school children, towards sustainable change.

1 - Needs

Street Child has selected the three target countries due to the high number and proportion of Out of School Children [OOSC], a lack of resources required to tackle the schooling crisis, and a lack or limited progress towards SDG 4 [in particular, SDG Target 4.1 and Target 4.5]. Nigeria has the world’s highest number of OOSC, at 13M [UNICEF, 2018]. 6M out of these 13M OOSC are in Adamawa, Borno and Yobe – conflict affect affected states in North East Nigeria [UN OCHA 2019] - where Boko Haram insurgents have mounted attacks on education over the last 11 years. Liberia is on track to be the last amongst all countries to achieve Universal Basic Education [UBE] [GEM Report, 2016]. 21% of children [aged 6 to 11] in Liberia and 18% of children [aged 6 to 11] in Sierra Leone are OOS [UNESCO 2017]. The COVID-19 crisis has compounded the challenges faced in accessing education in emergencies and low-resource environments – school closures have had a catastrophic impact on access. By May 2020, 66%, 51% and 71% of children in Liberia, NE Nigeria and Sierra Leone respectively had no access to any alternative learning through a sustained period of school closure [Street Child 2020]. Schools have since reopened and observed a sharp fall in attendance and enrolment rates: for example, in Sierra Leone, one in ten children across surveyed schools did not return to school. As school closures coincide with a loss of income in families, caregivers have been forced to choose survival over schooling, have prioritised survival over schooling, with children with disabilities, girls and other marginalised groups bearing the brunt of these difficult decisions by being deprioritized [Save the Children, 2020; UNESCO, 2020]. The longer that these children are out of school or learning spaces, the greater the risk of disengagement and dropout and the greater the risk of long-term loss of learning for an entire generation of learners. The implementation will focus on the Nigeria context and locations for 2024 response plan.

8.6 - Please describe briefly the modalities your project will put in place to ensure protection mainstreaming (particularly referring to the above questions).

The project will mainstream child protection and GBV prevention activities into all activities, in line with both organizations’ child-focused status. Safeguarding and protection risk assessments will be developed and regularly reviewed and updated, in order to provide a best possible protective environment within the communities of implementation. Such assessments will, for instance, consider the varied protection concerns of women, men, boys, girls, elderly people, people with disabilities, survivors of protection breaches or GBV, former CAAGs, and other diverse or intersectional populations with additional protection needs. Education and health stakeholders will be encouraged to join CBCPC structures and meetings, in order to build linkages between community protection mechanisms and adjacent services

9.5 - How will your project ensure feedback is collected from the most vulnerable community members?

A focus on strengthening of community mechanisms will also contribute to overall accountability and participation of community members. Protection and Education structures such as SBMC, CBCPC's will receive additional sensitization on project design and activities, with bi-weekly meetings between Street Child and Partners and these structures to gather feedback provided by the community. To facilitate the highest possible level of engagement, CBCPC's will be staffed by both women and men including people with disabilities, with trusted CRM focal points selected by beneficiaries in the community. Street Child and Partners are furthermore implementing in areas where at least one partner has prior programming experience and commensurate community links and positive reputation, which can be leveraged by each organization, as a further added value of the integration. The common project implementation spaces will also be CRM/AAP hubs, with prominent displays of IEC inside the structures displaying complaint and feedback referral mechanisms. Anonymous complaint boxes will also be installed, and face-to-face complaint desks will be provided at in-person activities. Street Child is taking overall responsibility for developing, implementing, monitoring, and evaluating the AAP and CRM frameworks.
