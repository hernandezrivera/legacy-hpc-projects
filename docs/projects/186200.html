Project Summary

To address the underlying power imbalance between males and females; promote psychosocial support to people who have been affected by the NWSW crisis and to boost the confidence of women to be able to champion the promotion of improved energy use, We respond in three steps, We engage, connect and empower.
We engage and connect women via organizing female football matches or games, this process promotes social cohesion while creating a foundation for them to meet monthly and discuss their common challenges, and share best practices. Within this process we have dialogues with the women and other local stakeholders on topics relating to women's empowerment. This makes women understand their rights and the population to understand the effects of using harmful fuel products as well as recognize that, for change to be sustainable, we must transform the barriers of unequal power relations and structures and support women to take leadership in addressing an environmental challenge that is affecting them most, affecting the families and yet made worse by the conflict and COVID-19. With women at the lead it will be easier to impact on the community cheaply and faster.
We strives to break down traditional gender stereotypes and empower crisis affected women to aid their communities. We accomplish this by teaching displaced rural women and youths how to design, build, and maintain solar systems that supply energy and water to their communities. we recruit, and provide them with a 2 weeks comprehensive training package and ongoing mentoring on business, technology, and leadership skills necessary to kickstart a sustainable clean energy business. They learn about clean energy solutions and how to build a sustainable business around the opportunities these new technologies afford. The training covers business skills like networking, marketing, and record-keeping. And also life skills such as leadership and goal-setting.
We deliver to them a diverse portfolio of quality-assured clean energy products and services to sell directly to people without power. Every entrepreneur receives a Business in a Bag start up kit to get them off and running. This includes key tools for accounting, running a business and marketing.
Women become their own bosses and build long-term businesses as they rely on their natural networks of family, friends, and neighbors to reach out to rural and hard-to-reach customers. As she turns over her inventory, an H4BF Entrepreneur produces a chain reaction of social impact by leveraging the power of the marketplace.
We continue to support them via friendly phone calls, and sometimes expert advice on how to handle product warranties, most times we provide psychosocial support, considering we work with displaced women affected by crisis and have lost love once and livelihood.
On the demand side, we have a hybrid and diverse value chain on the supply side, where we work with a basket of the most creative, high-quality suppliers who deliver the biggest bang for the H4BF buck.
We believe renewable energy technology brings the healthiest, safest, most sustainable, ultimately, and cheapest way to power people’s lives and improve wellbeing as supported by Facts from UN Secretary-General Climate

1 - Besoins / Needs

Access to energy is fundamental to achieving several SDGs: alleviating poverty, advancing health, improving, and more. However, 5.6 million people in Cameroon, with 270,326 from North West, and 152,735 from South West have no access to electricity (PAOP Cameroon Market Assessment). Last-mile settlements have yet to be reached by traditional centralized power systems.Due to the anglophone crisis plaguing the two English sections of Cameroon, ENEO technicians (main energy distributors), cannot access most communities for routine repair due to fear of being kidnapped or killed by armed groups. This put major cities like Nkambe, Kumbo etc for several months in blackout. 4.4 million people are affected (Cameroon Humanitarian Needs Overview 2021), and relying on harmful fuels for primary cooking. Globally, each year, close to 4 million people die prematurely from illness attributable to household air pollution from inefficient cooking practices (WHO fact-sheets household-air-pollution-and-health). Cameroon women are the backbone of the economy —they are in charge of farming, preparing produce, among others, yet they wake up and go to bed without reliable electricity. The lack of a safe and reliable source of energy exacerbates the existing power imbalance between male and females in Cameroon. Women are overwhelmed with heavy domestic roles left to them such as cooking among others. These roles could be performed well and much easier with reliable energy. In addition, the women purchase candles and kerosene to use at night, they pay some money to the power owners to be able to charge phones, while children do revision at night using candles, all these deter effectiveness and safety. The inadequacy of power contributes to women's vulnerability to family violence and limits effectiveness in their roles as home caretakers, yet they are the ones running small businesses and as mothers and frontline community members, they are in a unique position to interact with communities and hence should be at the center of the efforts to improve energy improvement. The women could even do better if they worked together as teams to challenge these setbacks.If women are engaged, network and given the power to earn income while mitigating the impacts of climate change, it will allow her to increase her economic potential, invest in her family's health and well-being, and elevate her social status. Families thrive, communities gain, and nations grow when more women work. H4BF works with women in crisis-affected communities in Cameroon's north and south west. Only 5% of families have access to power, indicating widespread energy poverty. The majority of the households are subsistence farmers, earning less than $2 a day. The majority of the women are first-time entrepreneurs, eager to make money to help support their families but in need of support and training in order to succeed. Many of the women are single mothers including persons with disability who rely on this as their only source of money to support their children. Some are teachers or healthcare workers, augmenting meagre and occasionally erratic wages. Our entrepreneurs are our primary beneficiaries; therefore, we are deeply ingrained in our communities and able to reach out widely to provide renewable energy technologies to their entire communities. Our customers include people who live in off-grid, last-mile villages and are also our target beneficiaries.
