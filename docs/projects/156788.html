Project Summary

The purpose of the intervention is to support the conflict-affected population of Nashabye sub-district of Rural Damascus, of the sub-districts of Hrak (Izra, district, Dar’a Governorate) and Shaqa (Shahba districts, As-Sweida Governorate), by providing psychological first aid, psychosocial support (PSS), and referral services. These activities will be targeting vulnerable people (especially children, women, elderly, people with chronic illness or disability) and they will provide support to avoid harmful coping strategies. The intervention will complement the food security and livelihoods activities already ongoing (Nashabye) or planned in these areas. This will provide an opportunity for qualified Social Workers and mobile teams to conduct outreach activities as well as identifying needs. A space will be identified in each area of intervention (for a total of 3) and rehabilitated following protection standards, which will serve as a static facility to provide a certain range of protection services with a focus on community-based approaches. The facility will especially support the families at risk, provide psychosocial support (PSS) and will serve as a place to meet, obtain information and discuss community issues and initiatives. Due to the presence of explosive hazards in several areas targeted by the project, risk education campaigns will contribute to the achievement of protection objectives, to be conducted both during food and livelihoods kits distributions and also through ad hoc risk education initiatives.

Needs

The effects of an almost 8-year long crisis are still spread across Syria. In Rural Damascus, Dar’a and As-Sweida, the population is still facing lack of services, lack of economic opportunities and the threat posed by the presence of explosive hazards. According to the United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA), the protection needs are especially very high, with an estimated 390,214 people in need in the district of Douma, Rural Damascus, a total of 193,309 people in need in the district of Izra, Dar’a, and 47,304 people in need in Shahba district, As-Sweida. All these areas have a protection sector severity rank of 5, according to the protection Sector information as shared with partners. The most vulnerable segments of the population are children, women, elderly and people with disability and chronic illnesses. According to information collected by COOPI in the sub-district of Nashabye, Douma, the population is still heavily reliant on aid, and psychosocial support has been identified by the beneficiaries as one of their needs. In Dar’a and Sweida governorates, the military offensive of June 2018 and the residual presence of groups affiliated with the Islamic State of Iraq and the Levant, has led to displacement, followed by a fast return to areas that suffered extensive damages. The protracted crisis and the last spike in hostilities as also contributed to increasing the presence of explosive remnants of war. They caused contamination of residential and farming areas and put the lives of civilians at risk, particularly some categories such as children, farmers, herders. The exposure to hostilities, the prolonged displacement and the return in less than favorable conditions has contributed to create a range of protection risks affecting the entire population, and particularly the most vulnerable segments such as women, children but also the youth, with high level of psychological distress and anxiety.

Implementation of activities

COOPI will consult with local authorities and communities to identify three suitable spaces (one for each sub-district) to be used as a static facility for the delivery of basic protection services in the project areas. COOPI will rent the spaces and provide light rehabilitation according to international protection standards, such as accessibility, safety of the premises, confidentiality, privacy, accessibility for disability, segregation of washrooms and disabled-friendly washrooms. The facilities will focus on provision of PSS to persons of concern (POCs), mainly adults. The facilities will employ 4 Social Workers (two in Nashabye and one for each facility in Hrak and Shaqa), who will be in charge of receiving the POCs, provide psychological first aid if needed and analyse their needs for referral to the proper services within the project or externally. The Social Workers will especially refer the POCs to the PSS Case Managers (two for each facility) and PSS Activity Facilitators (two for Nashabye, one for Shaqa and one for Hrak) if they need PSS, or externally to other organizations providing medical, legal or general humanitarian assistance. PSS Case Managers and PSS Activity Facilitators will use the static facility to analyse and follow the cases, provide counselling, refer POCs with serious mental health conditions to specific Mental Health and Psychosocial Support services (MHPSS), if needed, and support families at risk of adopting negative coping strategies. Depending on the specific needs, the facilities will also provide group sessions. The PSS team in the static facilities will be supervised by a Mental Health Specialist who will provide technical guidance to the PSS Case Managers, will supervise cases, in the field or remotely as well as organize individual and group case conferences at least once a month. The Mental Health Specialist will also provide capacity building on PSS for specific relevant protection issues identified in and by the community. In addition, training for the staff will include gender-based violence (GBV), peer support for some staff to help the rest of the team manage stress and encourage self-care. COOPI will capitalize on the presence in the field through other planned and ongoing food security and livelihoods interventions, to reach out to the most vulnerable segments of the population and identify POCs who can be referred to the facilities. Moreover, the project will support POCs attending the facilities to access the food security and livelihoods interventions based on the severity of their protection risks. Finally, COOPI will employ mobile teams (a team of 5 in Nashabye, and two teams of three in Hrak and Shaqa) who will support the identification of POCs in coordination with the facility as well as deliver awareness sessions. POCs with limited mobility will be reached by the Social Workers and the Case Managers through home visits . COOPI will liaise with UNICEF for capacity building on mine risk awareness as well as receiving the risk education materials already approved by the concerned Ministries. The mobile teams will then be trained and they will deliver the mine risk awareness campaign for the beneficiaries during distributions and the outreach activities, reaching a wider range of beneficiaries, including adolescents and youth. Risk education messages will also be delivered by the Social Workers and in the facility. Finally, constant communication with communities and local leaders will inform the design of the plan for the awareness activities, ensuring that venues, schedules and media are suitable for the needs of the communities.

Protection Risk Analysis

Protection risk 1:The population targeted by the intervention is put at risk as a result of location of facility;lack of consideration for accessibility;lack of consideration of contextual information on the location and the local dynamics. Likelihood: Medium. Impact: High. Mitigation measures: 1.Liaise with the community and with SARC to ensure the space is located in a suitable place and it is compliant with the minimum standards for protection mainstreaming 2.Consider mobility patterns and contextual analysis as part of decisions for establishing a static facility or a mobile service provision or combine both 3.Conduct safety audit to ensure safe access (route and location) by men, women, boys, girls, elderly and populations with decreased mobility (due to physical, psychological or other impediments) 4.Engage with local authorities on safety and security in the community 5.Engage with diverse members of community on safety and security in the community (to ensure diverse perspective and perceptions are considered) 6.Select staff for all activities (especially outreach volunteers) with adequate knowledge of the community, but also respecting balance in gender, age, social profile 7.Consider the provision of transport costs for the beneficiaries who have difficulties in reaching the PSS space 8.Ensure regular outreach activities by the Social Worker. Protection risk 2: Exploitation or abuse by staff members/service providers when accessing assistance and services. Likelihood: Medium. Impact: High. Mitigation Measures: 1.Establish a specific complaint and feedback mechanism 2.Conduct sensitization activities to reinforce the message that assistance and services are free 3.Provide training to staff on Code of Conduct and ensure newly recruited staff receives and understands the Code of Conducts and PSEA. Protection Risk 3: Social Workers might encounter survivors of gender-based violence or people needed specific psychosocial support, who might require special medical attention and could face consequences for having reached out to help and face stigma. Likelihood: High. Impact: High. Mitigation measures: 1.Refer survivors and people with serious mental illness to relevant actors operating in the area 2.Ensure the space can provide comfort and confidentiality, in the spirit of the ‘Do no Harm’ principle. Protection risk 4: Content of campaign messages (protection and risk education) are not fully adapted to the social and cultural environment and create misunderstanding or even tensions and had repercussions on the organisation implementing the campaign. Media chosen is not the most effective in the specific environment/community. Likelihood:Medium. Impact: High. Mitigation Measures: 1.Develop clear communication to manage expectations and receive feedback on the quality and usefulness of the information provided 2.Recruit staff who is familiar with the project area. Protection Risk 5: The referrals might not be successful as other humanitarian actors might not have the capacity to assist additional beneficiaries, which would prevent the beneficiaries from accessing the needed support. Likelihood:Low. Impact:High. Mitigation Measures: Constantly liaise with other humanitarian actors in the areas through the Protection sector, to ensure coordination on referral pathways. Protection Risk 6: Familiarisation may cause the civilian populace to become overconfident or complacent to the threat of explosive hazards. Likelihood: Medium. Impact: High. Mitigation Measures: 1.Tailor the risk education messages to the change in behavior 2.Monitor the change in behavior through focus group discussions. Protection Risk 7: Mine risk awareness activities might not reach women or elderly, who have mobility issues. Likelihood: Medium. Impact: High. Mitigation Measures: 1.Ensure gender and age balance in the team 2.Liaise with community and local stakeholders to form mobile teams who can bring the risk education messages to the communities.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Among the most impactful context-related risks, COOPI identified lack of safe direct access to the target areas and beneficiaries, which will be mitigated by constantly communicating with SARC and in-country security monitoring networks. Moreover, the presence of UXO might hamper the ability of the beneficiaries to reach protection facilities, which will be mitigated by the risk education activities conducted within the framework of the project and by liaising with SARC to receive information on progress of clearance activities. The project is based on the assumption that other humanitarian actors are present in the field and they are regularly providing assistance, for the success of the referral system. In case of disruption of such assistance, COOPI will advocate with the relevant sectors for filling this gap. In addition, the referral services can be granted if referral pathways are ready and updated through participation in the activities of the Protection Sector at different levels.
