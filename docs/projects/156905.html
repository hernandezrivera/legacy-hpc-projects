Project Summary

This project is designed with the aim of improving school-aged children’s access to safe, inclusive and quality education, both formal and non-formal, and psychosocial support in order to foster the resilience of the Syrian population. The project will provide a package of integrated support addressing both the demand for and supply of education services in conflict affected areas of Idlib and Aleppo Governorates. This multifaceted approach aims to both increase enrollment of out-of-school children, as well as create a valued learning environment to discourage drop-out and increase meaningful learning. Basic access to education will be ensured through repairs to school infrastructure including access to proper WASH facilities, provision of basic learning materials, and covering school operational costs in order to secure functioning of schools and to create safe and child-friendly environments. Awareness and outreach campaigns will also be conducted to reach out-of-school children, with focus on increasing enrollment and attendance of the most vulnerable. Considering already overstretched formal education infrastructure and number of out-of-school children, PIN also plans to implement self learning program (non-formal education) through supporting Learning centers for out-of school children, who miss out a schooling for more than a month and they have no access to schools in their community. The locations of the Learning Centers will be strategically placed close to formal schools PIN supports so transition from non-formal to formal education system can be supported. Much needed structured psychosocial support activities will be conducted with children, teachers and parents to promote psychosocial well-being and resilience both in and out of the classroom, thereby removing a key barrier to educational achievement.
In addition, quality of education will be improved through contextualized and targeted teacher training and further support to the professional development of teachers within each school, with the collaboration of teachers, school management and relevant education authorities. Monthly incentives will be provided to all educational personnel and other staff in both formal and non-formal education facilities.

Needs

Prior to the conflict, Syria had near-universal primary education enrollment - 100% of male and 98% of female students were enrolled in primary education before 2011, and 67% of both male and female youths were enrolled in secondary education. Syria is now considered to have the second worst enrollment rate in the world. With only 20% of requested funding allocated to education in recent years and only a third of targeted education beneficiaries reached, there is a significant need for increased attention to education in order to prevent a “lost generation” of children from being created. According to current estimates, about 6.1m of Syrian school-aged children (5-17 years old) need education assistance and 2.08 million children are currently out of school compared to 1.75 million in 2017. Aleppo and Idlib governorates, where PIN has been operational, have the highest numbers of IDPs in Syria. In March 2018 alone, 94,914 IDP arrivals were reported to assess communities in Idlib, Aleppo and Hama governorates. Assessments conducted by PIN in March, 2018 suggest that approximately 90% of IDP children arriving in areas in the most recent waves of displacement remain out-of-school, with the vast majority having been out of school for more than one year. The number of students who have been out of school for more than 5 years is also on rise. Considering these high numbers of newly arriving out-of-school children, and the expectation that displacement into PIN’s operational areas is likely to continue for the near future, there is a significant need for the provision of non-formal education support to limit the gap in education provision for vulnerable IDP and host populations. Whilst there is willingness from host communities to support education needs of IDP children, school capacities are in many cases far overstretched. Besides that, direct attacks on schools and education personnel in Syria are not rare. Hostilities have seen over one in three schools damaged, destroyed, no longer accessible or occupied for shelter and other purposes. Schools were reported as the most common building type used for IDP sites in Syria, which further exacerbates the overstretched education infrastructure. Overwhelming schools’ capacities to meet education needs through expecting them to accommodate new influxes of IDP children poses the risk of both undermining quality of teaching and learning, as well as potentially ‘doing harm’ through raising tensions between vulnerable IDP and host populations. During the course of the conflict, the Syrian population have been exposed to unprecedented levels of violence and trauma, creating significant psychological, health and social threats to entire communities and particularly children. The integration of protection related services is, therefore, increasingly required. Almost all children (98%) have been reported to show deterioration in their psychosocial well-being , while around 50% suffer from emotional or behavioral problems, with high levels of students showing raised scores on the emotional problems scale. Psychosocial changes affect both boys and girls in equal measures, with boys more likely to display aggressive behavior including the desire to join armed forces and armed groups, and girls more likely to show more self-harm, fear, being unable to sleep and showing antisocial behavior. Unwillingness to go to school is reported as a major response for both boys and girls, with caregivers often exacerbating the problem by limiting children’s mobility outside the home as they lack relevant coping mechanisms and support networks and suffer from high levels of stress themselves, often due to the constant struggle to access basic needs and safety for their families. Both boys and girls are at high protection risk due to increasing use of child labour, early marriage impacting girls, and targeting for recruitment into armed groups and kidnapping impacting boys.

Implementation of activities

The overall objective of the project is to provide access to inclusive, safe and quality learning environments for conflict-affected children. PIN will target school aged conflict affected girls and boys visiting formal education facilities. Under non-formal education component, the activities will target the most vulnerable out-of school boys and girls who miss out on schooling for more than one month; boys and girls who have no access to school in their community, including those in hard-to-reach and besieged areas, regardless of displacement status. PIN looks actively to include IDP children in programming through self-learning OOSC in camps and informal settlements. Provision of educational support to vulnerable OOSC also reduces the impact of missed education and provides a stronger platform for children to be able to reintegrate into the formal education system. Activities: 1. Increased access to education and psychosocial support for conflict affected children - Provide support to formal general schools and establish Learning Centres providing non-formal education - Conduct BTL campaigns (media outreach, radio programs, awareness raising activities, community mobilization and community outreach) - Rehabilitate classrooms in line with INEE standards - Provide schools with school furniture (desk, chairs, blackboards, etc.) - Provide students with education kits - Provide structured PSS and recreational activities in formal schools and in Learning Centres using PIN’s Activity Handbook that provides a reference resource of basic PSS principles and specific ideas for conducting PSS activities - Provide maintenance and running cost (such as fuel for heating) in learning centres and schools - Construct, rehabilitate or improve gender-sensitive and disability sensitive WASH facilities2. Enhanced capacity of education actors to provide quality education for conflict affected children - Train teachers and education personnel on child centred and protective pedagogy (life-skills and citizenship education, gender-sensitive inclusive education, classroom management, codes of conduct and anti-bullying) – Train teachers and education personnel on psychosocial support and referral mechanisms for children with special needs - Train teachers and education personnel on Education in Emergencies (EiE) and national/INEE (Inter-Agency Network for Education in Emergencies) Minimum Standards (MS) - Provide teachers and education personnel with monthly incentives – Provide teachers and education personnel with teaching resources, kits and guidesFollowing needs assessments and programme design, prior to the start of any intervention, PIN holds community stakeholder meetings to provide an open forum and opportunity for the community and individual households to raise concerns, ask questions, and ensure greater decision making in programming. The meetings also explain the use of Complaints Response Mechanisms, empowering target communities to monitor the implementation of the project and promptly flag any concerns. Throughout the project, PIN will recognise and address the different barriers faced by, and needs of, girls and boys in accessing, participating and achieving in learning. Recruitment of educational mentors and staff will also consider the need for gender equity and inclusivity and aims to accommodate an equal number of male and female centre staff. All data collection will be sex disaggregated and will, where appropriate, include further disaggregation by vulnerability status (residency status, disability status etc). Community Education Committees consisting of selected teachers, parents and LCs will be established to act as a coordination and decision making body within the school and support all school related activities including assessment of needs, communication with PIN and education authorities regarding needs, issues and ideas for implementation of education or social related activities within the school or local community.

Protection Risk Analysis

PIN programming in Northern Syria are inherently driven by protection considerations. Within implementation and regarding the specific activities related to the Education sector, the following protection risks may arise with the following measures to be taken: 1/Specific vulnerable groups/individuals are excluded from accessing education and psychological support. Mitigation measures: CRM mechanisms, participatory approach and consultation with local stakeholders, conducting community events that help to understand excluded groups especially vulnerable out-of school displaced children. In addition Conflict Sensitive teacher trainings include topics on creating a disability sensitive classroom aimed at facilitating the smooth integration of learning abled-physically disabled children with their peers and to reduce the negative social effects of their injuries, such as stigma and bullying 2/Additional threats to children’s well-being, such as child labor, early marriage, recruitment into armed groups, and other negative coping strategies, are also relevant. PIN addresses these wherever possible through holistic approaches to targeting vulnerable communities. Mitigation measures: Wherever possible PIN integrates FSL programming carried out in the same target areas through the FSL cluster. Programs include Food Vouchers, Cash for Work, Food Basket distributions and bread distributions, and are aimed at minimizing barriers to education through reducing vulnerable households’ resort to negative coping strategies. 3/Behavior of educational personnel threatening children’s well-being and protection – Mitigation measures: to ensure accountability and quality, mentors and center staff sign a Code of Conduct (CoC) and participate in a workshop detailing CoC requirements as well child protection best practices. Training will be also conducted to all educational and PIN staff PSEA policy. Any breach of the CoC will be investigated and result in disciplinary project up to and including suspension from the project without pay, and as necessary, termination. 4/Overstretched education physical infrastructure posing a threat to children’s safety – Mitigation measures: PIN plans to conduct necessary rehabilitation, which will take into consideration gender, age and disability. All schools rehabilitation are conducted in line with INEE standards and schools/learning centers are supported to develop their emergency and safety manuals. The core tenets of PIN's approach to protection mainstreaming include:1. Rigorous communication and feedback loops to ensure that the most vulnerable are able to voice their preferences and concerns at all phases of programme cycle management;2. Protection Mainstreaming initiatives to enhance accessibility to assistance of those most vulnerable, including development of new dedicated protection strategy for Northern Syria3. Capacity Building of staff to enhance protection awareness and ensure policies and strategy are fully assimilated into daily work4. Continuous M&E activities carried out throughout all project, including:Participatory field-based M&E, with capacity building for local actors: regular school visits by PIN's education department focus on ensuring quality, gathering feedback and building capacities of stakeholders to take over monitoring activities for the future. Findings are fed back into management plans to ensure methodologies and approaches are adapted accordingly.Results-Based Monitoring: A RBM tool developed by PIN based on objectives, indicators, targets and project time plan ensures the project is implemented on time and is reaching defined targets;Complaints Response Mechanism (CRM): CRM (through email, Facebook, phone number, whatsapp) is incorporated into design of all activities, providing a safe, accessible and effective channel for beneficiaries and stakeholders to communicate feedback, and for an appropriate and transparent response to be provided by PIN)

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1. Inaccessibility to target areas due to insecurity, large scale military offensives, or change in territorial control with safety and security of staff/beneficiaries being compromised. PIN would triangulated information with available sources, use rigorous monitoring mechanism, suspend; relocate activities to areas where access is upheld; shift program resources to other areas; switch to remote management; potentially through pre-identified local partners. 2. Borders are temporarily closed. PIN would suspend activities; advocate with UNOCHA and local authorities to facilitate access and operations; purchase locally if possible; eventually resort to remote cash-based programming in extreme cases. 3. Legal/administrative constraints – including refusal of required operational permissions and visas – delay or prevent aid delivery in countries from where cross-border operations are conducted. PIN would adopt a phased approaches to withdrawal. 4. Inflation of the Syrian pound / Exchange rate fluctuations / Local markets are not able to meet the increased demand for goods use available in-kind commodities through food basket/bread/RTER provisions; switch allocations to increase in-kind assistance provision; reshape programming by revising targets; switch to food basket distributions with items procured in neighboring countries. 5. Restricted cash liquidity inside Syria/international bank transfers rejections. Cash modalities would be suspended whilst the finance department assess and monitor market situation/development; switch to in-kind modalities; envisage back up CTA. 6.Syria offices/warehouses/assets are raided. PIN would temporarily suspend activities; carry out internal/external investigations; advocate for dialogue with relevant actors; inform relevant counter-terrorism agencies/authorities/partners of the incident; seek to relocate the intervention to other areas. 7. Local Councils or other relevant local stakeholders stop collaborating with partners or fail to apply agreed methodology PIN would temporarily suspend activities; engage dialogue to re-sensitize on humanitarian principles of engagement/PIN policies; relocate resources to other target areas. 8. Aid is perceived as politically motivated and/or PIN are subject to intimidation from military groups or local authorities. Activities would be suspended; re-sensitization/dialogue; reallocate funding to alternative areas. 9. Aid is diverted for personal or political gains. PIN mitigates aid diversion through a comprehensive set of rigorous management policies PIN would suspend activities; carry out pertinent investigations; advocate for dialogue for return/replacement of diverted aid; re-sensitize concerned actors on humanitarian role of PIN where possible; reallocate funding to alternative target areas
