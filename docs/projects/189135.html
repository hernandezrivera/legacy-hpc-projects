Project Summary

The emergency this project will address is the U5 GAM rate of ≥19% in underserved payams in Fangak and Nyirol counties rated at the severity of need classification 5 for nutrition (SS HNO 2022 pg 63). Projection to May 2022 shows food insecurity to reach the severity of need classification 5 in Fangak and 4 in Nyirol (SS HNO 2022 pg 30). In Fangak, the payams of Manajang, Greater Pulita, Mareang and Phom with a population of 112,524 will be served. In Nyirol, the payams of Chuil, Pultruk, Thol, Nyambor, Pading and Waat with a population of 113,041 will be served.
The impact of flooding has been severe. A total of 5 nutrition facilities in Fangak (3 in Manajang, and one each in Phom and Mareang payams) and 2 in Nyirol (Chuil payam) were destroyed causing disruption of nutrition services for 1 to 3 week periods. To effectively respond to this emergency, this project aims to provide a package of nutrition interventions that improves diets, services and practices for better nutrition outcomes targeting the most vulnerable children U5, PLW, elderly and the disabled.
The project will use a combination of approaches to strengthen sustainable nutrition outcomes:
• Preventative actions that reduce the rate of malnutrition and improve the sustainability of treatments;
• Community engagement by taking services closer to households, through the roll out of the revised CNV guidelines;
• Facility-based data analysis and feedback information to improve overall performance of service delivery and timely coordination of response decision making;
• Integration of nutrition with different sectors by gap analysis and coordination at county levels for programming with co-located partners in FSL with NPA, ADA and Oxfam, in Protection with FCA and NP and in WASH with ACF, SI and Oxfam. At all sites, nutrition services will be integrated with health services of CMA in Nyirol and HFO in Fangak.
The project will use a combination of response modalities - RRM/ICRM, static, outreach and mobile approaches with nutrition survival kits to ensure timely response to emergencies. Services will be delivered from 24 static OTP-TSFP sites, and these sites will support mobile outreach services to cover displaced populations in 23 additional sites. The response activities will cover (1) case-finding screening using the Family-MUAC method from static units and mobile outreaches to IDP locations; (2) admission of acutely malnourished U5 children and PLW to OTP and TSFP services using expanded criteria where needed including malaria testing; (3) provide SC services for SAM children with medical complications; (4) deliver expanded MIYCN services to PLW and care-givers of children under 23 months through counselling PLW, care-providers and MtoM support groups, including vitamin A supplements and de-worming.
The critical outputs will be treatment of:
• U5 SAM children – Fangak 2109, Nyirol 3024 (Total 5133);
• U5 MAM children – Fangak 3553, Nyirol 4952 (Total 8505);
• MAM for PLW – Fangak 1855, Nyirol 3684 (Total 5539).
The project will mainstream protection and disability inclusion and emphasize gender and age considerations. To improve sustainability, this project will strengthen local capacity of CHDs, project staff and CNVs through job-relevant training. To address insecurity risks for PLW and U5 children, CMA will collaborate with the targeted communities to implement protection practices to reduce risk of SGBV. By applying IASC guidance, specific needs of men, women, boys and girls will be identified and addressed through a gender mainstreaming approach.
In relation to AAP, mechanisms will be provided for affected populations to receive information they need to make informed decisions on accessing nutrition services, provide feedback on the interventions, share concerns and submit complaints. CMA will work with local authorities, leaders and beneficiaries, to ensure their inclusion in decision-making throughout the program cycle.

1 - Needs

The emergency this project will address the U5 GAM rate of ≥19% in underserved payams in Fangak and Nyirol, Jonglei State. SS HNO 2022 shows projections to the May to July period for food insecurity to reach IPC 5 Catastrophic in Fangak and IPC 4 Extreme in Nyirol. Trends show malnutrition is “significantly concentrated in the 6-29 month” age group (NC Needs and Objectives, Slide 4, 10 Feb 2022). It is estimated that a child with SAM is twelve times more likely to die than a well-nourished child (Khara, T. & Dolan, C. Technical Paper: ENN, June 2014). The catchment area population covered by this project is 225065 (Fangak-112024, Nyirol-113041) (NC PiN Data for 2022). In the payams covered, the PiN data for children 6-59 months is: SAM - 3295 for Fangak and 4725 for Nyirol (total 8020); MAM - 14100 for Fangak and 14228 for Nyirol (total 28328). The MAM PiN for PLW is 5299 for Fangak and 7269 for Nyirol (total 11609) and the MAM burden for elderly and disabled is estimated at 1100 for Fangak and 1250 for Nyirol (total 2350). The underlying drivers of the crisis are inter-communal conflict and the economic crisis. Over the past 3 years, shocks from seasonal flooding and the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic have contributed to worsening the crisis. Flooding has caused displacement, loss of food-crops and damage to facilities causing service disruptions and access barrier for all those seeking nutrition services. Fangak is among the 5 worst flood affected counties rated as Extreme Concern with 115000 affected (REACH_SSD_FactSheet_IPC_Flooding_Dec2021). Juaibor in Manajang Payam, Keew in Pulita Payam and Nyadin in Mareang Payam have experienced severe flooding for 3 consecutive years. Data shows Fangak and Nyirol are among the 9 worst affected counties with a GAM prevalence of 19.0% (SAM 4.1%) for children 6-59 months (HNO Nutrition Situation Preliminary Overview - NC Jan 2022, slide 9). The SS HNO 2022 (pg 65) shows GBV ranked severity 4 Extreme in Fangak and 3 Severe in Nyirol. The drivers of GBV remain unchanged from past years. Young children are vulnerable because of their age. Women and adolescent mothers are vulnerable because of their gender. The principal threats to women, children, the elderly and disabled are inter-communal clashes and flooding. GBV mainstreaming is essential to counter the risks noted above and provide unimpeded access to services, gender-balance in frontline workers and provide for the safety and dignity of those who most need nutrition services. These efforts are essential to improve gendered quality and safe access to nutrition services. In addition to OTP/TSFP services, there remains the need for MIYCN services along with vitamin A supplementation and deworming to improve maternal and young child feeding practices. There is also need to strengthen nutrition monitoring to achieve early detection of emergencies and service gaps. The project will use a combination of responses with nutrition survival kits to ensure timely emergency response. In the context of displacement due to conflict and flooding, the displaced have been force to move away from locations where static nutrition services are provided. There is need utilize mobile approaches to reach these IDPs – vehicle, boat, motorcycle, bicycle, canoe and walking, to link affected populations to nutrition services. In the context of COVID-19, there is need for continued training on guidelines for infection prevention and control and training mothers/caretakers to monitor their own nutrition status by conducting Family-MUAC screening and self-referrals. When the core drivers of malnutrition are taken with food insecurity, lack of WASH infrastructure, poor hygiene practice, high prevalence of disease, the threat of GBV and insecurity, it is clear that a multi-sector approach with nutrition integrated with health, FSL, Protection and WASH interventions is needed to effectively combat the current emergency.

2 - Project activities

This project will focus on the implementation of a minimum package of actions to improve diets, services and practices for better nutrition outcomes. The project will provide for a gender-balanced team of frontline workers where 42% are female. Field personnel have been trained on CMA’s PSEA policy and all are expected to sign a PSEA code of conduct. The activities to achieve output targets are defined below. Objective 1. To increase equitable access and utilization to quality preventative nutrition specific services for children, adolescents and women in prioritized locations.Activities:1.1 Prevention of malnutrition through behavior change communication focused on caregivers on feeding hygiene, food quality and diet diversification;1.2 Prevention of micronutrient deficiencies through vitamin A supplementation for children 6-59 months;1.3 Prevention of wasting with deworming treatments for children 12-59 months with Albendazole tablets; 1.4 On-the-job training of frontline nutrition and health workers on the MIYCN counselling including COVID-19 protocols. Objective 2. To increase equitable access and utilization of quality lifesaving nutrition services for the early detection and treatment of acute malnutrition for girls and boys under 5 years of age and PLW affected by acute malnutrition in prioritized locations. Activities:2.1 Rebuild flood damaged facilities and maintain these facilities for delivery of quality services;2.2 Screen for early detection and treatment of acute malnutrition for children under 5 years and for PLW;2.3 Scale-up capacity of CNVs and care-givers by training on the Family-MUAC screening approach and encourage CNVs to make home-visits;2.4 Scale-up capacity strengthening by training nutrition workers on management of SAM and MAM to attain high quality in case management;2.5 Scale-up demonstration kitchen garden at facility level and introduce household gardens;2.6 Conduct monitoring and supportive supervision to improve performance and impact of the project;2.7 Promote community engagement for responsive service delivery by raising awareness on beneficiary rights/entitlement and by establishing a community complaints and feedback mechanism in OTP/TSFP sites;2.8 Conduct spot exit interviews to assess the level of satisfaction of nutrition service delivery.Objective 3. To enhance nutrition situation and program monitoring, analysis and utilization of nutrition information by the end of 2022.Activities:3.1 Through monthly meetings, conduct ongoing consultation, review and evaluation of nutrition program performance with local authorities, community leaders and affected beneficiaries;3.2 Engage with communities to conduct MIYCN barrier analysis and adapt MIYCN program accordingly;3.3 Improve facility-based data collection with on-the-job training of staff on preparation and analysis of monthly NIS reports and impacts on affected populations. Objective 4. To increase equitable access to nutrition sensitive interventions from health, WASH, FSL, Education and Protection sectors through enhanced coordination and joint programming targeting vulnerable groups in prioritized locations.Activities:4.1 Provide WASH NFI kits to children with SAM;4.2 Sensitize households on the correct use of aquatabs; 4.3 Sensitize communities with WASH messages;4.4 Test and treat for malaria and provide LLITNs for children under 5 years of age;4.5 Establish and maintain demonstration kitchen garden at OTP/TSFP sites and engage caregivers of children 0-<23 months to develop household gardens;4.6 Integration of child stimulation and ECD activities for children treated for SAM;4.7 Mainstream child protection and GBV risk mitigation through training of workers on SGBV;4.8 Conduct safety audits at least twice per year to identify and address women and children’s protection/safety needs;4.9 Collaborate with health and provide Zinc and ORS for children with diarrhea.

7.4. Community Engagement Strategy

Yes

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The Facility CMAM Supervisor will be responsible for coordinating the engagement with communities and actively seeking their feedback. The consultation activities will be recorded and reported monthly to CMA’s County Officers, the M&E and Nutrition officers of the CHD and CMA project management team, highlighting what new actions (if any) need to be taken. These measures will ensure that an effective system of representation of affected populations is in place for the purpose of improving project results. Further, the Project Manager will visit and supervise the project (once per quarter) to conduct monitoring and evaluation so that regular assessment of beneficiary knowledge on project progress and their satisfaction with project performance is collected and new measures put in place as required in response to beneficiary feedback. Further, the Project Manager will conduct meetings with local leaders of host and IDP communities and local authorities to ensure effective implementation and robust monitoring of the complaints mechanism is in place so as to achieve effective accountability to the populations being served.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Under the direction of the facility CMAM Supervisor, CMA’s on-ground personnel undertake patient exit interviews, focus group discussions (FGD) and key informant interviews (KII) as part of the planning and monitoring processes to ensure ongoing effective feedback. The CMAM Supervisors ensure that the most vulnerable disabled, illiterate, women and children are included FGD and KII sessions. At all sites, the affected population groups that CMA consults in the planning and feedback processes are as follows: (1) local women and men leaders, focus groups and key informant women and men; (2) Mother-to-Mother Support Groups, other social groups representing the most vulnerable including individuals living with disabilities and (3) discharged patients women, men, girls and boys including individuals living with disabilities.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

CMA has completed the rollout of its revised PSEA policy with training of all management at Juba and County levels and the facility based frontline personnel. Additional PSEA training is planned for CNVs and refresher on-the-job training will be conducted for all facility-based and mobile teams of frontline workers during the coming years. A core piece of the training covers dissemination of information about PSEA. The dissemination will be conducted through meetings with community groups, Mother-to-Mother Support Groups and local authorities. The dissemination will be conducted by the CMAM Supervisor at the facility level. Further, SC nurses, Nutrition Assistants and MIYCN Counsellors will ensure the patients they are treating are thoroughly informed about PSEA. Beneficiaries are advised on: (1) the content of the PSEA policy; (2) available channels for reporting abuse; (3) confidentiality rules in reporting abuse cases; and (4) the mechanisms and locations available for victims of abuse to access support and treatment.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Needs assessment on entry to new locations and on an ongoing basis thereafter ensures that project activities are tailored to the different needs of community members’ especially adolescent mothers, new IDPs, the elderly and disabled. Information is disaggregated in respect of gender, age and the needs of different population groups to improve suitability of planned interventions. In locations already served and new locations, CMA will apply its established strategy and model to ensure accountability to affected populations. The structures and mechanisms established enable affected populations to participate actively in planning services, learn their rights and entitlement with respect to nutrition programs, receive information they need to make informed decisions on accessing services, provide feedback on interventions, voice and submit complaints and participate in program decision-making. CMA’s programs are implemented in collaboration with local authorities and leaders of host communities and IDPs. At all locations, monthly meetings with leaders, Mother-to-Mother Support Groups are conducted to report on project output and obtain community feedback. The Facility Clinical Officer or CMAM Supervisor will conduct discharge interviews with a 10% sample of the patients to obtain feedback. These consultations provide the most vulnerable of affected populations full awareness and opportunity to utilize the complaints mechanism, the opportunity to actively participate in planning and monitoring the project and to learn their entitlements for accessing project services and render complaints. CMA’s on-ground teams will ensure that at least 75% of affected populations are aware and able to utilize the complaints mechanism by practicing the principles of transparency, communication and provision of information linked to the complaints mechanism, and engaging the target populations to proactively seek out their feedback

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

8.1 High Risk Planned Mitigation Measures: Beneficiaries face exposure to violence while accessing facility-based services because of long distances to locations where project services will be centered. To mitigate this risk, CMA will employ mobile and outreach approaches to go to the locations where at-risk populations live in order to reach beneficiaries in a safe way. Further, the project will promote community-based strategies and practices to provide protection for the most vulnerable community members (children, PLW, IDPs and disabled). The project will engage men and women leaders to take responsibility for mobilizing protection for disadvantaged and vulnerable individuals, so that they have unimpeded access to services. To adhere to the principles of “Do-No-Harm”, the project will strive to deliver services in a balanced manner so that IDP and host community populations will have equal access to project services. To achieve this balance, CMA will implement a strong awareness campaign to engage both IDP and host community leaders.8.2 High Risk Planned Mitigation Measures: Women, girls and boy with and without disabilities feel unsafe and are at risk of sexual violence and abuse. CMA will conduct community assessments for delivery of services from static, mobile and outreaches, and apply the “do-no-harm” approach to reduce the risk sexual of exploitation and abuse. The project will conduct safety audits at all static and mobile services, and implement all recommended measures to improve safe access to services. The safety audits will be led by CMA Safety Audit Focal Person, and implemented by female staff members in respect of common protection protocols. These measures will ensure access and minimize exposer of women and girls to violence, including sexual exploitation and abuse as they access to project assistance.8.4 High Risk Planned Mitigation Measures: To address the need for available and effective protection assistance and referral services for abuse survivors, project sites will be linked to health services where CRM service will be provided for victims of SGBV including psychological support for the trauma experienced by child survivors of conflict and of SGBV. Through promotion and awareness sessions, both those in treatment and the wider community will be made aware of the appropriate referral pathways for the services that survivors need. Further, local authorities and community leaders will be made aware of available protection assistance and the GBV referral pathway for CMR and psychological support for survivors of SGBV.8.5 High Risk Planned Mitigation Measures: Exclusion of marginalized communities/minority groups from consultations and decision making on project approaches and interventions will be mitigated by adopting strategies to ensure communities: (1) have information they need to make informed decisions on accessing services, (2) can provide feedback on the project intervention and submit complaints. As women, girls, boys, the disabled and elderly are primary targets of this project, and to ensure they are all included in consultations and decision-making, CMA will organize specific forums (KIIs, FGDs) and meetings to engage these vulnerable groups. This will include engaging women’s groups, and women community leaders in consultation and decision making in the planning, throughout the implementation and at evaluation and this will also include discharge interviews. To reach the disabled and elderly, CMA will conduct case seeking interviews and consultation with these groups to ensure their situational context, needs and limitations/barriers are understood and accounted for in project planning and implementation decision-making.

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

At present, Fangak is rated at severity of need level Extreme and Niyrol is rated at severity of need level Severe for GBV. Displacement caused by flooding and intercommunal conflict are the main causes of the high severity for GBV. In relation to provision of the minimum package of services and activities for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV to achieve safe and timely access to services for case management and psychosocial support including capacity to provide such services, this project will directly contribute to mitigation of GBV in the 4 following ways:• Rollout and on-the-job training of frontline nutrition workers on CMA’s policy and practices for PSEA, and implementation of this PSEA policy at the facility level;• Training of frontline workers on how to recognize victims/survivors of GBV, and the referral pathway for survivors to access appropriate care including the clinical management of rape, psychosocial support and counselling;• Conducting safety audits of nutrition service delivery from facilities at a minimum of once per year, to help provide safe and accessible nutrition services for the mothers, women, girls and boys requiring treatment for acute malnutrition and to adjust facility structures and/or approaches to ensure patients feel they can access services safely;• Conduct evaluation and safety audits of the methods and approaches to nutrition service delivery and reach unserved patients through mobile approaches and help establish community-based protection mechanisms to minimize the risk to vulnerable female clients when they are required to cover long distances to access services. The measures set out above will be implemented in 24 locations (11 in Fangak, 13 in Nyirol) with a catchment population estimated at 225065 (Fangak 112024, Nyirol 113041) which is about 2% of the national population and provide support to the vulnerable women and children who need to access nutrition services the most.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

At present, both Fangak and Nyirol counties are classified in IPC 4 with Fangak projected to deteriorate to IPC 5 by May 2022. Toward reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero), this project will make 5 direct contributions:• Curative treatment of children 6-59 months for SAM - 6416 and MAM - 15584 (51% female), which is 2.4% of national target;• For prevention of malnutrition, provision of VAS to children 6-59 months - 54690 and Albendazole - 50641 to children 12-59 months, (51% female), which is 1.8% of national target;• Treatment of pregnant and lactating women for MAM - 11609, which is 2.1% of national target;• Provision of MIYCN counselling to pregnant and lactating women and the caregivers of children under 23 months - 24307 (90% female) to improve food quality and hygiene, and dietary diversification as means to prevent acute malnutrition, which is 1.8% of national target.• Establishing demonstration kitchen gardens and undertaking cooking demonstrations reaching an estimated population of 35000 (70% female) with new knowledge of the nutritional value of a diversity of foods and how to produce and prepare a more nutritional diet for young children and pregnant women.
