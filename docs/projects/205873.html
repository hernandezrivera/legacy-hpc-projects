Project Summary


NCA is actively engaged in addressing gender-based violence (GBV) in emergency situations in South Sudan, showcasing a robust and continuous response. The specific project focuses on key areas, including comprehensive case management for women and girls, tailored psychosocial support (PSS), targeted prevention and awareness initiatives. Furthermore, there is a dedicated emphasis on prevention, awareness, and PSS for returning men and boys. The project also introduces innovative approaches to menstrual hygiene management (MHM) and implements inventive incontinence management strategies for women and girls in safe spaces. Through these strategic interventions, the project aims to make a meaningful impact on the well-being and safety of the affected populations.

1. Needs

A decade after gaining independence and three years following the latest peace agreement, the people of South Sudan continue to face increasingly challenging humanitarian conditions. These hardships are deeply intertwined with various factors, such as conflict, local violence, public health issues, and climate-related disasters. However, amidst these struggles, one pressing concern that stands out is the pervasive issue of Gender-Based Violence (GBV).In South Sudan, the impact of ongoing conflict and other crises has left a significant mark on the lives of its citizens. The statistics are sobering: an average life expectancy of only 55 years, limited access to education with an average of just 5.5 years of schooling, and an annual income of only USD 768 per person (as of 2022 according to the World Bank).The scale of the crisis is enormous. Approximately 9.4 million people in South Sudan, including 2.2 million women, 4.9 million children, and 337,000 refugees, are expected to require humanitarian assistance and protection services in 2023. This represents a substantial 76 percent of the country's population, marking a troubling 5 percent increase from the previous year (source: OCHA, 2022). In the disputed Abyei Administrative Area, an additional 212,000 people are believed to be facing humanitarian and protection needs (source: OCHA, 2022).Persistent conflict coupled with severe flooding has resulted in a large-scale displacement crisis. South Sudan currently hosts 2.2 million internally displaced persons (IDPs), and more than 2.3 million South Sudanese have sought refuge in neighbouring countries (source: IOM DTM, 2022). Tragically, this crisis of displacement has also contributed to high levels of conflict-related sexual violence and gender-based violence, making South Sudan one of the most severe protection crises globally. Additionally, South Sudan bears the ignoble distinction of hosting the world's fourth most neglected displacement crisis, with over 2.2 million internally displaced individuals within its borders, including more than 36,000 residing in the Malakal Protection of Civilians (PoC) site. This situation also represents the largest refugee crisis in Africa. A recent in-depth assessment by the Norwegian Church Aid (NCA) conducted in September 2023 brought to light the poignant reality of protection issues in South Sudan, with a sharp focus on the states of Upper Nile, Warrap, and Bahr El Gazal. The findings of this assessment shed light on the stark and distressing situation, particularly in the context of Gender-Based Violence (GBV), which has emerged as a burning concern for the people of these states.Furthermore, the outbreak of conflict in Sudan in mid-April 2023 has led to a significant influx of people fleeing the turmoil. More than 75,000 individuals, including 90 percent of South Sudanese nationals, have crossed through key border entry points along the Sudan-South Sudan border as of May 24, 2023. The actual numbers are likely higher, as some people entered the country without formal registration. On average, 3,500 individuals arrive daily. The repercussions of the Sudanese conflict have already impacted South Sudan's economic outlook, particularly in the northern states that rely heavily on imports from Sudan. This has led to increased food prices and fuel costs, potentially exacerbating protection risks and vulnerabilities for the population in the northern states and among returnees from Sudan. It also raises concerns about food insecurity, negative coping strategies, and urgent humanitarian needs. Of the 22 monitored points of entry (PoEs) since the start of the crisis, over 99 per cent of the arrivals have come through just three PoEs – with 83 per cent arriving through Wunthow/Joda (near Renk) in Upper Nile, 16 per cent through Panakuach in Unity, 1 per cent through Kir Adem in Northern Bahr el Ghazal and the rest (less than 1 per cent) through Western Bahr el Ghazal and the Abyei.
