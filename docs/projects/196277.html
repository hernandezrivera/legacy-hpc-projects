Project Summary

According to HNO 2023, at least 12.1 million people are estimated to be food insecure, including all 2 million people living in camps who are deemed to be 100 percent food insecure. Seven in ten Syrian families are food insecure with the highest concentration in Idleb (74 per cent) a d in Aleppo it is 61 percent, with access to food as one of the most pressing needs.
Syria’s multiple crisis have severely affected the agriculture and agri-food sectors and supporting its resilience has become an urgent national priority. It is important to note that the sharp decline in crop production and the entrenchment of food insecurity during the 2021-2022 cropping season was not only driven by weather anomalies, but also a result of issues with water scarcity and availability, damaged infrastructure and productive assets, disrupted services, and scarcity of energy resources, especially
diesel and electricity. In addition, the high prices of agriculture production inputs, especially fertilizers, herbicides, and others, continues to limit agriculture input use by farmers and adherence to standard seed and dose rates. This is resulting in reduced actual yields, reduced crop productivity and reduced food availability.
The project aims at assisting 400 HHs who are living in camps in NW Syria to enhance their protection, food security and food sufficiency by growing their own crops in a sustainable and organic approach.
SDI conducted research for an innovative approach to enable families to cultivate the small pieces of land in front of their tents/homes for high production at low cost. SDI also conducted an assessment in Maarrat Tamisrin, Afrin and Azaz and found that there are suitable IDPs sites that are based on agricultural lands so that we can use the areas between the tents (or a nearby land outside the camp in some cases) that can be cultivated for food production and providing each family with a good range of food diet and we made sure that there are existing water sources that can provide enough water (which will ensure through our WASH programs).
The project will use the "Square Foot Gardening" and "Companion Planting" approaches which will allow high production from a small area of land. 2.4 Square meters will be dedicated for each family and it will divided in 26 square feets. Different plants will be used in each square as follows

Each family will receive an agriculture guide, a detailed agricultural timetable, agricultural inputs, fertilizer, pesticides and tools as necessary. The project will aim for organic sustainable agricultural production a will provide training and coaching to beneficiaries to be able to apply the correct agricultural cycle, produce and use organic fertilizers (compost and vermicompost) and pesticides alternatives (mechanical combating, natural predators).
A small henhouse with 10 hens will be established in each camp to train beneficiaries on how to raise domestic poultry, using their waste in agriculture and eggs in the food and a greenhouse for preparing seedlings, conducting training and storing needed items.
Social workers will provide GBV and PSEA awareness-raising sessions and will help in assisting the targeted women.

3 - Needs

According to Syria Humanitarian Needs Overview HNO 2023 report, the continued deterioration of the food and nutrition situation and the increased reliance and adoption of negative coping strategies has impacted the resilience of households. As many as 77 per cent of households reported buying less expensive and less preferred foods resulting in poorer diets. According to FSA/FSLA 2022, 64 percent of households in Syria reported never having consumed “HIron” rich foods in the 7 days that preceded interviews, further deteriorating the quality of diets as well as increasing risks of nutrient deficiency and increased rates of malnutrition for many Syrians especially children under 5 years and pregnant and lactating women. There is a need for emergency provision of agriculture production inputs for vulnerable farmers and livestock breeders affected by the decline in agricultural production during the last two seasons, including training on climate-smart agriculture approaches, within the Agro-stability zones 2 & 3 with crop inputs and access to irrigation water. Such interventions include support towards own agriculture input production e.g. vegetable seedlings, compost, food etc). Furthermore, there is a need to support early warning systems and Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) approaches. The rehabilitation of irrigation systems and other important agriculture community assets, restoration and enhancement of the national bread value chain remains fundamentally important.

4 - Implementation of activities

SDI has already identified several camps that have agricultural soil, enough water sources (solar panels for water pumping will be installed if needed within our WASH programs) and enough space either inside the camp or in adjacent land that can be cultivated for this purpose. SDI has already conducted research on more productive methods and "Square Foot Gardening" and "Companion Planting" and how they can be adapted to the Syrian context in line with the cluster guidelines and recommendations. SDI will start with beneficiary selection based on questionnaires showing the beneficiary's will to participate in the program, agricultural background, and taking into consideration vulnerability and gender inclusion in addition to having a suitable piece of land. Each land of 2.4 Square meters will be dedicated for each family and it will be divided in 26 square feet. 1 to 4 square feet will be dedicated to each kind of crops (pls check the crops types and numbers in the summary). A greenhouse of 6*4 meters 24 square meters will be established in the camp or in a nearby land to be used for production and preparing seedlings to distribute them to beneficiaries in addition to the necessary training, tools, guidance, a detailed timetable showing the needed steps and dates, and materials, including agricultural inputs, fertilizer, pesticides and tools as necessary so that they produce their food in an organic, sustainable manner under the supervision of SDI's technical team.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk: violation of property rights, hindrance to activities or beneficiary eviction. Likelihood: moderate; Impact: high; Mitigation: Conduct HLP due diligence according to clusters guidelines. Risk: Child labor, risks on workers' safety and health. Likelihood: high; Impact: high. Mitigation: zero tolerance to child labor and requirements to provide safety equipment to laborers will be included in the code of conduct and contracts signed by contractors and followed up. Risk: Explosive remnants of war and mines. Likelihood: moderate; Impact: high; Mitigation: selected sites are declared as clear. Awareness banners and awareness-raising sessions will be included. Risk: The spread of Covid19 across NW Syria. Likelihood: high, Effect: high. Risk mitigation3: SDI will take Covid19 prevention measures according to Clusters and SCHF guidelines such as social distancing, using PPE and proper hygiene for all staff and workers...etc in addition to awareness-raising of IDPs in sites. Risk: Lack of participation and accountability to beneficiaries; Likelihood: moderate, Effect: high. Involving the local authorities, camp management and IDPs representative committees including women and children (and forming committee if they are absent) in all stages of the projects, including needs assessment, designing the project activities, and HLP verification in addition to active information sharing through meetings and focus group discussions.


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

Beneficiaries are at risk on being exposed to any sexual misconduct by the humanitarian workers and they might not report the misconduct due to the following reason: 1. lack of knowledge of reporting mechanisms, especially for people with disabilities and women living in far away areas. 2. feeling afraid of retaliation. 3. fear of stigma. 4. fear of deprivation of assistance. 5. being unable to understand written banners for being illiterate and/or for having a visual disability. 6. for children, language might be complicated To mitigate these risks, SDI will : 1. aim at clearly announcing the hotline number and email specialized for PSEA of SDI and UN numbers. through many means, like, banners, social media, and messages to beneficiaries phones through telegram channel to ensure reaching to the biggest number of beneficiaries including women living in far away areas and people with disabilities. 2. Conduct PSEA training for all staff members and contractors/suppliers dealing with beneficiaries and make them read and sign CoC that state Zero Tolerance towards SEA violations. 3. Conduct awareness raising sessions on PSEA for beneficiaries to encourage them to report any violations and stress on the confidentiality and on eliminating any other fears that they might have. 4. Conduct awareness raising sessions for children in an appropriate language. 5. complaint boxes 6. Using PSEA field and organizational checklists tools.
