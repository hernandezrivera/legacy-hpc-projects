Project Summary

In line with the South Sudan HRP 2022, the project will save lives by addressing the acute severe food insecurity of 23,745 households belonging to internally displaced people (IDP), returnees and host communities in Tambura, Nagero and Ezo Counties through distribution of food vouchers to 3,562 HHs and enhanced and sustained food production through distribution of crop and vegetable seed kits, agricultural tool kits, fishing kits and provide agronomic training to 20,183 HHs. Overall, SLDA aims to reach severe and acute food insecure 142,472 people in IPC phase 4 and 5, including 35,618 men; 35,618 women, 35,618 boys, and 35,618 girls. The purpose of the project is to enhance emergency food production, diversify food consumption and strengthen the resilience of targeted communities.
The project will implement activities that will prevent famine and improve food consumption, dietary diversity and coping strategies of target beneficiaries in IPC 5 and 4. The project provides food support for the protection of lives and human capital of IDP, refugee returnees and host communities. The project will specifically focus on three main areas of support: (i) providing food assistance to prevent famine and improve food consumption, dietary diversity and coping strategies for vulnerable populations; (ii) assisting farmers through complementary vegetable and crop livelihood inputs, fishing and livestock support; and (iii) reducing dependency on food and agricultural inputs to support and strengthen households’ ability to absorb shocks in a way that prevents the spread and transmission of COVID-19.
SLDA will adopt a mix of intervention approaches including but not limited to unconditional and General Food Distribution (GFD), cash transfers, complementary livelihood support, capacity building (e.g. on agronomic practices), and resilience building at the household level. The project will support: (i) purchase and distribution of agricultural inputs and planting materials such as seeds and tools; and (ii) rebuilding or distribution of assets for postharvest handling and food storage for example household granaries and food storage bins. Similarly, the project will support to unconditional food transfers and re‐engagement in crop and livestock production.
The project provisions farmer extension services, establishes farmer field schools and vegetable gardens in schools and nutrition centers. Diet diversity will be achieved by increased consumption of vegetables, increased availability of fish at household level and local markets as well as through targeted sensitization on food preparation (cooking demonstrations), maternal nutrition and suboptimal infant and young child feeding (IYCF) and basic household hygiene.
The project seeks to enhance emergency food production and contribute to the reduction of dependency on food and agricultural inputs. In support of nutrition centres, kitchen gardens will be established to improve the nutritional status of people. This will allow severe and acute food insecure people in IPC phase 4 and 5 to meet their immediate food needs whilst allowing them to rebuild their assets. In addition, by improving people's food security, acute malnutrition will be reduced. Mitigation measures to reduce and prevent the potential the spread of COVID-19 will be incorporated in all interactions with beneficiaries, including training and general awareness of beneficiaries. The project will improve accountability to affected populations (AAP) in a more holistic way by integrating four core people-related issues: age, disability, gender and protection. Similarly, protection will be mainstreamed throughout the project cycle to ensure beneficiary safety and dignity, avoiding doing harm as result of the intervention, providing meaningful access to all sets of beneficiaries and ensuring all groups are actively involved and participate in the project in an inclusive manner.

1 - Needs

Based on the most recent food security analysis in December 2021 for the Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO), it was determined that 8.3 million people are expected to face severe food insecurity at the peak of the 2022 lean season in South Sudan. The main drivers are: (1) covariate climatic, conflict & economic shocks such as floods and dry spells, high food price rises, and the continued disruption of livelihoods due to violence or the fear of violence; and (2) household level idiosyncratic stressors such as death of bread winner, gender based violence (GBV), and morbidity and mortality due to poor hygiene and lack of access to essential basic services. 2022 situation exacerbated by the increasing caseload and further expected reduction in home food availability (HFA); likely reduced by 10 – 20%, as compared to the already reduced levels in 2021. The level of severe food insecurity has seen a 7% above the 7.7 million people in need in 2021. The main contributing factors are floods and dry spells, inflation and high food prices, and the continued disruption of livelihoods due to violence or the fear of violence and household stressors, which are greatly amplified by the continuous effect of shocks. Children, persons with disability, older persons and women headed households are identified as the most vulnerable to be affected by increased food insecurity (HNO 2022). Tambura County experiences super charged sub-national violence and inter communal tensions exacerbated; internal and external displacement; human rights violations; fear of return; disrupted livelihoods: cultivation, trade, wild food collection and HA access issues. Similarly, Nagero County is known as the IDP recipient county with small population. This County is historically isolated and under-served that experiences the effects of conflict shock (REACH Assessment). Thousands of people who escaped violence in South Sudan's Western Equatoria state are starving as humanitarian aid in camps in Ezo town is insufficient to meet their basic needs (Benjamin Takpiny, 2021). People in Need (PiN) in Ezo County are: 106,200 including Extreme phase 19,900 and Catastrophic phase 13,300. Similarly, PiN in Nagero County are: 31,000 including Extreme phase 5,800 and Catastrophic phase 3,900. Likewise PiN in Tambura County are: 95,300 including Extreme phase 17,900 and Catastrophic phase 11,900 (HNO 2022). SLDA conducted a field-based Initial Rapid Need Assessment (IRNA) on March 01 – 07, 2022 in Tambura, Nagero and Ezo Counties confirms that all the selected locations are affected by different natural and man-made disaster, showing high and multiple vulnerabilities and are priority areas as they are in IPC 4 or worse. The IRNA shows that main drivers of vulnerability and food insecurity in target Counties include: (1) natural disaster e.g., floods and droughts; (2) inter communal violence; (3) low agricultural production and productivity; (4) low asset ownership; (5) soaring of food price; and (6) COVID 19 pandemic such as disruptions to supply chains. Physical access is a challenge in target Counties with many of the roads and infrastructure damaged by flooding. There is an urgent need to provide integrated support including food and livelihoods assistance and protection services to the most vulnerable people including children suffering from severe acute malnutrition and pregnant or breastfeeding women who are acutely malnourished. Food insecurity in the target counties has also been compromised by prevalence of localized conflicts, flooding and the impacts of COVID-19 on the socio-economic set up and national economy that continues to constrain availability of income for most households. HHs are demonstrating multiple and high levels of vulnerabilities increasing their protection risks. The project will target these most vulnerable groups through inclusive assistance, and will ensure that protection, gender and COVID-19 are mainstreamed in the project activities.

2 - Project activities

The project will save lives by addressing the acute severe food insecurity of 23,745 households belonging to IDP, returnees and host communities in Tambura, Nagero and Ezo Counties through distribution of food vouchers to 3,562 HHs and enhanced and sustained food production through distribution of crop and vegetable seed kits, agricultural tool kits, fishing kits and provide agronomic training to 20,183 HHs. SLDA aims to reach severe and acute food insecure 35,969 people in IPC phase 4 and 5, including 35,618 men; 35,618 women, 35,618 boys, and 35,618 girls. The project will include a number of activities designed to address the key objective of reducing food insecurity in Tambura, Nagero and Ezo Counties, including 1. Distribution of food vouchers or cash for food: SLDA will distribute unconditional/restricted cash or food voucher to vulnerable HHs in areas in IPC 4 and 5 in Tambura, Nagero and Ezo Counties. The project will support the immediate nutritional needs of vulnerable households (HHs) through the provision of food and/or cash transfers. Beneficiaries will receive USD 100 per HH per month for a period of 3 months. 2. Distribution of emergency livelihood inputs (vegetable, crop seeds and fishing kits): The project will distribute sorghum, ground nuts and sesame which are the main staple foods of the target group. To increase food production among the target group, SLDA will support identified vulnerable households, with locally purchased crops seeds during the cultivation season of 2022. SLDA will support vulnerable HHs with assorted vegetable seeds comprised of tomatoes, onions, kales, spinach, eggplants etc. SLDA will also support to establish kitchen gardens in nutrition centres. SLDA will distribute rapid response kits cow pea and three variety of fast maturing vegetable seed. Potential fishing groups will be supported with fishing kits distribution. Fishing kits will be distributed, each kit consisting of twine (2 spools), monofilament (1 coil) and hooks (2 boxes), following FAO guidelines. 3. Sensitization on basic nutrition IYCF: This will focus on topics such as food hygiene, nutritional value of different foods, cooking classes and nutrition requirements of children and pregnant and lactating women (PLW). 4. Distribution of fuel-efficient stoves: The project will distribute fuel efficient stoves to vulnerable households. Fuel efficient stoves will help to reduce the amount of firewood used for cooking. This is also expected to contribute to promote gender equality by saving time to cook and collect firewood for girls and women. 5. Livestock treatment and vaccination and disease surveillance: The project will facilitate Community Animal Health Workers Training and inputs support, cold chain establishment and maintenance, prevention & treatment especially zoonoses e.g. RVF. 6. Training and capacity building: Beneficiaries will receive training and extension services on a range of topics including but not limited to: training on fishing best practices on proper fish preparation and storage; food utilisation, including hygiene promotion and appropriate feeding practices; vegetable growing/production; adaptive and absorptive on-farm production (farmer/agro-pastoral field schools, demonstration plots); business skills development, entrepreneurship and financial literacy; and accountability to affected populations (age, disability, gender and protection). This will improve technique, increase production and income. SLDA will establish Village Savings and Loan Associations through awareness and guidance. 7. Construction and rehabilitation of rural infrastructure to promote livelihood-generating activities: SLDA will help for the construction of multipurpose water points for livestock and to enhance crop production; and post-harvest processing and storage facilities. After distribution a PDM will be conducted to obtain feedback from beneficiaries on the items distributed and the trainings conducted.

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

Cash transfers are a cost-efficient and effective way of accelerating progress towards the Sustainable Development Goal of Zero Hunger by 2030. SLDA believes that using cash or vouchers recognises that beneficiaries are best placed to decide what their households need, and gives them the flexibility to choose according to their own diverse needs and priorities. Similarly, in allowing beneficiaries to control what goods and services their households need, cash transfers are considered by many as more dignified than receiving goods in kind, and recognise beneficiaries as active participants in providing for the welfare of their families in an emergency situation, like South Sudan. Likewise, the availability of cash gives households a sense of restored power over their immediate situation. In addition, there is evidence that receiving cash can at least temporarily empower women within the household. Access to financial structures such as bank accounts may empower beneficiaries, in particular women, in the longer-term as well. Furthermore, injections of cash or the use of commodity vouchers with local traders benefits the recovery of trade and the local economy. Evidence shows that cash transfers reduce the negative coping strategies used by beneficiaries. When goods are available and accessible locally, cash or voucher-based interventions have the potential to be more rapid than bringing supply from out of country. When beneficiaries use local markets, agencies also eliminate logistical and import costs. SLDA is opting for cash and voucher approaches because: 1. Cash can be used for multiple purposes by communities thus improving livelihoods beyond the food dimension. 2. Use of cash and vouchers avoids transportation and handling costs of materials with the possibility of utilizing the cash earmarked for such costs on other activities. 3. SLDA has standing agreements with cash transfer agents which ensures that target groups benefit from pre-negotiated exchange rates. Use of cash and vouchers build local traders and rural economies. Local procurement of seeds stimulates production.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

There are key factors relating to food supply and distribution which may ultimately influence market planning and infrastructure provision:- increased food crop production, leading to surpluses being available for sale and to a greater demand for marketing facilities in rural areas;- changes in consumption habits creating the need for new cold storage facilities and food processing industries;- poor conditions in markets are common. Demand for stalls in many markets is high and stall space is often limited. Produce is frequently displayed directly on the ground and exposed to the weather, resulting in high losses. Facilities may be limited, usually with no main market sheds and no provision of water supply or latrines; and- increased availability of different types of transport, such as small vehicles or trucks. SLDA will distribute cash for food, as the market will be able to meet demands. SLDA conducted market assessment to measure market functionality, prices and feasibility to meet increased demand. The market assessments show that although markets are moderately functional, they are not able to cope with large or increase in demands in target Counties. SLDA is proposing to use food vouchers in combination with local vendors who procure and distribute the food. Local vendors will be selected through tendering (including but not limited to visits and quality checks). These vendors will procure and supply the food to selected beneficiaries. This way market functionality will not be jearpordized, and markets will not suffer from the food distribution and will benefit from the assistance as well. Beneficiaries will be encouraged to use the money for food. Overall, local traders will benefit from the cash assistance.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

SLDA recognizes the right of beneficiaries and community members to give feedback and seek response from projects affecting them. SLDA engages with the target community and relevant state authorities during the needs assessment, project design and monitoring to seek their feedback and adapt project assistance/delivery accordingly. The broader consultations with the community during the assessment will inform about the project design, priority activities and modality of assistance. Consultative meetings will held with communities and local leaders. The beneficiaries and community members will be encouraged to give feedback and seek responses from the projects affecting them. They will be sensitized right from the start of the project through inception meetings and community consultation workshops on key deliverables including community roles and responsibilities. Community members will participate actively, transparently and without discrimination in analysis, planning, design, implementation, monitoring and evaluation of all interventions proposed. The farmers’ groups, cooperatives, VSLAs and communities including representatives of vulnerable groups will be supported to prioritize and plan food and nutrition security to ensure safe, effective and equitable delivery of services. Beneficiary feedback will be collected through the feedback mechanisms and directly during deliberately organized feedback meetings at community level. The feedback from affected populations will be used to inform decision-making on SLDA's programme delivery. The CFM Mechanism is crucial al as it is used consistently to improve programme implementation and increase accountability to targeted populations. Periodic reviews of beneficiaries’ feedback by managers, and interactive team sessions at the field level are done with documentation of all information from CFM data collection process. This provides critical information and learning that can be used to understand how the programme activities are being implemented in the community. Apart from addressing concerns of current issues of beneficiaries, the feedback are used to improve project implementation, generate learning and as inputs for the design of future projects. Based on the cluster reporting system, the project will put in place and implement and monitoring and reporting system, progress, best practices and lessons learned will be presented in cluster meetings based on the 5Ws formats developed by the cluster.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

In this response, the CFM will be set up and made functional at community level. The CFM will deliver a mechanism that enables the beneficiaries and community members to provide feedback and seek responses in relation to activities of this response in a manner that is safe, non-threatening and accessible. SLDA will consult with the target community, the local chief and state authorities to understand the overall target population profile, access to communication channel and nature of vulnerabilities. SLDA will ensure multiple channels of communication including hotlines, complaint boxes and complaint desks at all project activity sites will be availed to communities. These initiatives will promote beneficiary accountability and feedback and response mechanism to other humanitarian actors. The project will additionally organize regular feedback meetings for special interest groups (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls) at community level. Project staff will be trained on how to handle special interest groups’ complaints with staffing for complaints desks and hotlines being made gender and age sensitive. While establishing the CFM mechanism, SLDA will consider such vulnerabilities and access constrained therefore the target population will be consulted on their preferred mode of complaint/feedback channel. Different mode of feedback such as complaint boxes, phone numbers, email, verbal feedback/complaint during SLDA monitoring visits, in-person to SLDA field office and Help desk at distribution points/beneficiaries registration points will be used to avoid exclusion of access as for as possible considering the operational challenges in target locations. SLDA will use both pictorial, written and verbal channels in local languages to communicate the feedback mechanism to target beneficiaries. The individuals living with disabilities, illiterate, women and girls can also share their complaint/feedback through their local representative and relatives. SLDA will maintain a help and feedback desk at distribution points. In addition, during monitoring and support visits FGDs will be conducted separately with women and men to seek feedback and provide opportunity to discuss any concern/issues or complaint. SLDA has strong principle of Do No Harm; programme staff are trained on ensuring that humanitarian interventions do not put beneficiaries at risk and does not discriminate service delivery on basis of tribes and sex. Similarly, SLDA’s CFM ensures that voices of targeted communities are included in the programme. The feedback is collected through farmers groups, cooperatives and VSLAs, and community elders. SLDA ensures the safety and wellbeing of women and children who come into contact with the organization is promoted through the promotion of a zero tolerance policy towards the abuse and exploitation of women and children during our programing. SLDA is accountable to ensuring all staff, partners and volunteers on this project receive adequate training on PSEA, child protection and safeguarding.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

SLDA will mainstream Protection by taking measures to safeguard beneficiaries from violence, coercion, deprivation, and discrimination to ensure the full respect for the rights of the individual. SLDA will ensure that project activities will be implemented in impartial and non-discriminatory ways that promote the safety, dignity and integrity of the people receiving assistance. Projection will be mainstreamed in all four phases of the project cycle including assessment, programme design, implementation, and monitoring and evaluation. The project will develop a checklist for protection mainstreaming: (1) Avoiding causing harm and prioritize safety and dignity; (2) Ensuring meaningful access; (3) Practicing accountability; and (4) Promoting participation and empowerment. SLDA will commit to ensuring that mechanisms in place to prevent and respond to sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) are strengthened and meet required minimum core humanitarian standards. This project will promote quality programming with a focus on inter-sector synergies and integrated programming to mainstream protection, gender, age, disability and accountability to affected populations. The project ensures that all social mobilization, community engagement and community feedback, complaints and response mechanism will be developed and implemented in conjunction with women’s groups. The project prioritizes the mitigation, response and prevention of gender based violence (GBV) and Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA). During project inception and regular consultations with the beneficiaries and relevant stakeholders, SLDA informs them about their right to assistance, entitlements, right to appropriate behavior including SLDA’s commitment to zero tolerance on sexual exploitation and abuse by humanitarian workers. The PSEA mechanism is designed exclusively to address sexual exploitation and abuse allegations; hence reported immediately (less than 24 hours) in a timely manner to Project Manager in a way that the investigations are started straight away, and the victim is updated on progress of investigation and given timely response. The CFM mechanism is mainly meant to address issues related to project implementation, but it can also pick feedbacks and allegations sexual exploitation and abuse. The project will train women’s groups on the requirements to promptly refer survivors of SEA for assistance through existing GBV pathways.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

SLDA will integrate four core people-related issues: age, disability, gender and protection to improve accountability to affected populations (AAP) in a more holistic way. SLDA will strengthen accountability to people with disabilities and take advocacy and capacity-building measures on disability across the project response. Drawing on local knowledge from diverse groups creates solutions that are both practical and effective. Local communities are creators and owners of the project and catalysts for change rather than simple beneficiaries of programming. Through community engagement, project beneficiaries play a meaningful role in the deliberations, discussions, decisions and implementation of the project activities. SLDA will design a community communication and engagement plan. Project inception meetings to be attended by communities and stakeholders will be held at all locations. Issues to be covered in the meetings will include explanation of project objectives, methodology, activities and beneficiary endowments, responsibilities, and the rights thereof. Participants will additionally be sensitized on the project complaints and feedback mechanism and its application. Contacts details and reporting tools for the mechanism will be shared and their use explained. Due to limited phone/internet possibilities and illiteracy, most of the information will be provided verbally through: (1) Inception meeting. (2) HH assessments. (3) Help Desk. (4) PDM. Community feedback, complaints and response mechanism (CFM) will be established and strengthened in all field locations, and all complaints and feedback are logged, tracked, and reported on regular basis. This will enable SLDA to identify any trends or issues, track progress and future plans, and challenges to improve on effectiveness during project implementation. All feedbacks and complaints lodged are dealt in a timely manner, according to the project’s guidelines, and provided timely clear outcomes, referrals, responses, and thereafter closed.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

A project risk matrix will be completed identifying operational, political, programmatic, reputational, financial and organizational risks. The risk matrix will be reviewed and risks closely monitored. The below identified risks are part of SLDA programmatic risks: 8.1 SLDA will ensure that all registrations and distribution sites are safe, nearby people’s dwellings and easy accessible for all. People will be hired for crowd control and when desirable, separate male and female distribution sites will be organised. Sites will be selected jointly with the community. 8.2 SLDA will inform communities about policies and procedures in place such as code of conduct and PSEA. All SLDA staff are trained in these procedures and policies. This will explain expected behaviour of SLDA staff and how to report misconduct. To prevent sexual violence by community members in distribution sites will be safe and nearby people’s dwellings. Distribution times will also be convenient to make sure that people are home during day time. Community leaders and communities will be asked to protect their women, girls and boys as well. 8.3 SLDA has child safeguarding policy in place and staff are trained on these procedures. In addition, in the event children are on their own, SLDA will make sure they are connected with local leaders and make sure communities look after them. 8.4 SLDA already has a list of referral service providers for protection issues in all the locations of operation. SLDA will establish a referral with these organisations. This information will also be shared with communities and community leaders, raising awareness on available services and referrals. 8.5 Targeting criteria to be developed at project inception will ensure inclusion marginalized groups. Beneficiary targeting criteria including the importance of including marginalized groups to be explained to communities before selection process commences. 12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

SLDA implements integrated programme including provision of nutrition, health, WASH, FSL and protection services. The beneficiaries will receive integrated assistance as far as possible. SLDA will also coordinate with other partners to ensure that the gaps are filled and the targeting include most vulnerable. At the start, SLDA will map protection and other actors in the project areas and establish a referral system. Referral pathways and linkages to other service providers including those in protection, education, health and nutrition will be created. SLDA will refer vulnerable groups of women in Women and Girls Friendly Spaces (WGFSs) in target locations. This initiative will allow outreach to communities to prevent and mitigate GBV, provide clinical and psychosocial care to survivors and support women and girls to know and access their sexual and reproductive health and rights. All referrals will be logged and followed up on, to make sure that people multiple needs, especially protection needs, are met. Factors contributing to inequalities will be addressed in trainings and community level meetings to be organized by the project. Communities will additionally be supported to address access challenges through mainstreamed sensitization on existing services, how and where to access them.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

The flood destroyed homes and livelihoods of people - damaged settlements, agriculture (crops and cattle), horticulture, and market access which are crucial for peoples’ livelihoods in South Sudan. 40% of the crops and vegetable gardens in SLDA operational areas have been affected. People are also displaced and feared to turn to negative coping mechanisms such as informal borrowing, selling productive assets, and depletion of working capital which might be used to protect from the impact of the disaster. The flooding crisis came at the back drop of very grim hunger situation in these areas, where already many people were suffering from acute and severe hunger. This situation was further impacted by the current economic downturn and higher inflation rates, in addition to food price hikes exacerbated by the impact of COVID-19 pandemic. The floods severely affected the opportunities for income generating schemes leading to loss of household income and inability to afford food and meet basic needs. Negative coping mechanisms of poor and most affected households have been reported, such as taking fewer meals per day (mostly amongst women and girls), selling assets or borrowing money at high rates to purchase food items, causing unmanageable debt. The flooding also resulted in increased incidences of human diseases such as malaria and acute watery diarrhoea that will affect productivity and deplete savings in the form of increased medical expenses – this will further contribute to household vulnerability and reduce resources available for purchasing food. SLDA is requesting access to the HRP to cover the immediate needs of vulnerable individuals severely affected by the flooding. Prior to the current flooding SLDA programme has been implementing various flood risk preparedness and mitigation activities including: dissemination of Early Warning Information, and development of an early response strategy to mitigate the impact of flooding, stakeholders coordination, formation of flood response committees, identification of most vulnerable people and high-risk areas; distribution of empty sacks to aid dyke construction and monitoring implementation of flood response actions. This request for HRP is sought as the scale and magnitude of the current flooding and livelihood crisis it has compounded requires additional interventions to help vulnerable households meet their immediate needs, reducing the hunger gap and mitigating negative coping mechanisms. This intervention will also provide support to host communities that had to cope with people displaced.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

The underlying causes of undernutrition are often directly linked to food insecurity. The project aim to improve food security to provide access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food that meets affected population dietary needs and food preferences thus contributing toward decreased prevalence of global acute malnutrition. The project is addressing people’s immediate food needs whilst also providing them with inputs to overcome the lean season and reducing dependencies on humanitarian assistance. This will improve people’s overall food situation and as a consequence will reduce the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero). Although children under 5 will not be specifically targets, they are included in the food assistance and will benefit from the livelihood assistance and a more stable food and nutritious intake. This is likely to decrease acute malnutrition among children under 5. Additionally, PLW will be targeted and they will receive additional food. This will have a positive impact on malnutrition among new-borns and children under 2. The project will target households hosting children under 5 years who are affected by severe malnutrition, levels 4 and 5. Initial selection will involve collaboration with nutrition service providers and will be focused on those admitted to treatment. Such households will be enlisted in cash and voucher assistance to improve market access while also being supported with inputs to engage in own production. The households will be further supported to grow vegetables or engage in fishing as a way of diversifying their diets. Care takers of severely affected children will additionally undergo targeted IYCF trainings that shall include cooking demonstrations, breastfeeding and household hygiene. Reduction of the proportion of populations facing IPC 3, 4, 5 will be addressed by providing inputs and training to enable them to restart household food production. Additional measures will include sensitization on climate change and community-based disaster risk reduction.
