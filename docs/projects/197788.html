Project Summary

Syria remains a complex humanitarian and protracted emergency characterized by over ten years of ongoing hostilities and the resulting long-term consequences, including widespread destruction of civilian infrastructure, degradation of basic services, explosive ordnance contamination, and the largest number of internally displaced people (IDPs) worldwide, all of which is further compounded by an ever-worsening economic situation. In 2022, 14.6 million people remain in need of humanitarian assistance, an increase of 1.2 million from 2021. Climatic-induced and human-caused shocks affecting natural resources, particularly water, continue to intensify and exacerbate the humanitarian situation. Insufficient and poorly distributed rainfall, severe drought conditions combined with low water levels in the Euphrates River, regular and sustained cut-off of Alouk water station, and damaged water infrastructure have not only reduced access to safe water for drinking and domestic use for millions of Syrians but also triggered substantial harvest and income losses. Activities under this plan seek to address the immediate health needs stemming from this outbreak and highlight response priorities across all pillars of the health sector. WHO aims to assist up to 137,000 of the 162,000 people estimated at risk to be affected by Acute Watery Diarrhea (AWD)/Cholera. The overall requirements for this three-month plan amount to $17 million USD.
On 10 September 2022, the Syrian Ministry of Health declared 15 cholera laboratory-confirmed cases in Aleppo governorate and one death. As of 12 November 2022, a total of 8 380 AWD cases associated with 49 deaths (CFR=0.58) were reported by MoH and EWARS teams in 14 governorates, of which MoH reported 5 655 AWD cases and confirmed 1 351 cholera cases, while EWARS teams in NES reported 2 725 AWD cases; of which 187 were confirmed by the RDTs in Deir-ez-Zor, Ar-Raqqa, and Hassakeh.
Project goal;
Reduce avoidable morbidity and mortality due to AWD/cholera, reduce transmission of the disease in affected areas, and prevent/minimize the risk of introduction of the outbreak to other high-risk areas.
Objectives :
1 (Leadership and Coordination) – Maintain effective and integrated coordination and leadership for the AWD/cholera response.
2 (Disease Surveillance) – Enhance timely detection, confirmation, and investigation of AWD/cholera alerts to support rapid implementation and prioritization of response activities.
3 (Laboratory) – Strengthen laboratory capacity for the rapid diagnosis of cholera cases in support of timely and effective epidemiological investigations and response activities.
4 (Case Management and IPC) – Increase access to treatment for suspected cholera patients and strengthen the capacity to manage cases at all levels through the implementation of standard management protocols for reducing morbidity due to cholera and enhancing infection prevention and control practices in treatment facilities.
5 (WASH) – Ensure appropriate lifesaving WASH prevention and response measures to control the spread of AWD/cholera, with special attention to the at-risk populations and improving IPC interventions at health facilities.
6 (RCCE) – Strengthen risk communication and community engagement and provide key messages to vulnerable communities for AWD/cholera prevention and response through reliable channels of communication and community feedback.
7 (Operations Research and Knowledge Management) – Generate knowledge to improve implementation of preparedness and response interventions, and to document lessons learned during the cholera response.
8 (Logistics, Equipment and Supplies) – Ensure procurement, delivery, management, and coordination of essential health and critical WASH supplies to sustain preparedness and response activities.
9. Vaccination with Oral Cholera Vaccine

3 - Needs

Since Aug 2022, an array of gaps and needs has emerged, to effectively respond to the current cholera outbreak:1. Planning for multi-pillar response with an assumption of attack rates of: Rural, open settings: 0.5%; Open, urban settings and closed settings (e.g., refugee and IDP camps/sites): 1% 2. Training of healthcare staff on cholera case management, including ToT and cascade training3. Equip health facilities with necessary materials to manage 25-30% of cases that will develop severe dehydration, 30-40% with some signs of dehydration, and 30-40% will experience no dehydration4. Build the capacity of laboratories for timely cholera confirmation5. Strengthen surveillance at health facilities and community levels.6. Continue to strengthen inter-cluster coordination, especially among Heath, WASH and Nutrition 7. Procure cholera kits and other relevant supplies 8. Improve the status of WASH in Health Facilities.9. Roll out Oral Cholera Vaccine to slow transmission while other public health measures are strengthened The above needs have been determined through health / inter-sector/ cholera task force meetings and discussion, regular sitreps and reporting from field colleagues and partners; ongoing data collection; and epidemiological deep-dives.

4 - Implementation of activities

A Whole of Syria Cholera Response Plan has been developed to ensure a coherent, unified approach to cholera response in Syria. The plan articulates responsibilities for health, WASH, RCCE and nutrition actors across the response. Additionally, a hub-specific response plan is the guiding approach within each response area. Dedicated task forces, together with sector- and inter-sector coordination bodies ensure harmonized approaches while regular reporting against a set of KPIs ensures accountability and monitoring. Furthermore, ongoing work through RCCE listening and KAP surveys enables continuous adaptation of the response to address barriers to adopting preventive practices and/or vaccine uptake. Technical officers within each hub utilize the Incident Management system within WHO’s Emergency Response Framework (ERF) to ensure a targeted, prioritized approach to WHO Cholera response activities

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

There is a protection risk for health care workers who may be exposed to disease infection, physical security/ violence in insecure areas. Likelihood: Medium. Impact: Medium Mitigation measures: a) Assess and analyze the security situation of the concerned areas before planning field visits/investigations, b) Ensure the use of secured transportation routes and vehicles for field investigations, c) Build relations with local communities to increase acceptance of humanitarian workers, d) support the HCW teams with required personal protective equipment PPE. 2. Disclosure/mishandling of patient names and information leads to stigma and discrimination against patient/family/community. Likelihood: low. Impact: Medium. Mitigation measure: use unique codes for cases to ensure the confidently of data, 3. Nature of HCW workers jobs could lead to stigma/discrimination. Likelihood: low. Impact: Medium. Mitigation measure: engagement of community leaderships to advocate the tasks of HCW. 4. Relatedly, for Health care workers: burn out/stress. Likelihood: high. Impact: high. Mitigation measure: periodic breaks and adequate staff

9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Considering the current situation, outbreak epidemiology, geographic expansion and response capacity, the overall risk assessed to be very high at the national level, due to: 1. Outbreak affecting densely populated areas across 14 governorates and cases detected in IDP camps. 2. Limited accessibility of the general population, including in some IDP camps and sites, to safe drinking water and WASH facilities. 3. Presence of more than 6.9 million internally displaced people in Syria7 . 4. Limited accessibility of the population to adequate health care. 5. Limited surveillance capacity and the ability to detect and confirm cases by culture. 6. Limited availability of supplies and lack of expertise in cholera response among the main actors. 7. Limited accessibility to some areas due to hostilities and/or security concerns.
