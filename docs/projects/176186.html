Project Summary

In 2021, 2.3 million children will be at risk of violence, exploitation, abuse, and neglect in South Sudan. The situation is particularly dire in counties hit by multiple shocks like in the States of Jonglei, Upper Nile and Unity where children were expose to different protection risks including recruitment by armed groups, family separation and psychosocial distress due to both natural and man-made disasters such as the severe floods, the outbreak of inter-community violence and the consequently massive displacement. The overall protection risks for children were also exacerbated during 2020 by the covid19 restrictive measures that closed schools and hence reduced the overall protective environment of children. COVID19 had also a negative impact on the economic well-being of households, with food prices skyrocketing in markets and children not receiving meals at school anymore. This led families to rely on negative coping strategies affecting children directly as seen by the overall increase in child labor and early marriage, visible in the last months 2020.
INTERSOS has been implementing protection projects in the counties with most severe needs including Akobo, Ayod, Nyriol (Jonglei state) and Malakal (Upper Nile state) and Mayendit (Unity state). Through its protection interventions, where Pibor was reopened and recent needs assessments conducted in January 2021 also in the counties of Pigi/Canal, INTERSOS has been able to identify over 600 unaccompanied and separate children in need of Family tracing and reunification services and over 1,000 vulnerable children in need of immediate material assistance in the areas of intervention.
To answer to the child protection needs, INTERSOS proposes a holistic intervention to prevent and respond to the most severe and common protection risks children face in Pibor, Ayod, Nyriol, Lankien, Pigi/Canal, Malakal and Mayendit through the establish of static and mobile protection teams providing family reunification services, case management and material assistance for most vulnerable children including CAFAAG and UASC, Psychosocial support. Additionally, to strengthen the protection environment of children, INTERSOS will promote capacity building of local authorities and frontline staff, as well as of community through the establishment of community committees formed by parents, teachers and community leaders among other to monitor the child protection situation and raise awareness on the main preventive measures and risks mitigation to reduce protection risks directly affecting children.
Through this response INTERSOS expects to target 71, 016 people (12,788 W; 12,788 M; 22,720 G; 22,720 B) in six locations considered in severity 5 (Pibor), 4 (Akobo) and severity 3 (Ayod, Canal/Pigi, Malakal, Mayendit) where the overall humanitarian response to child protection remains low.

1 - Needs

In 2020, the government of South Sudan re-committed itself to address the six grave violations against children. However, the children continued to disproportionately suffer by the crisis and the exposure to multiple shocks including above all inter-community violence, floods, and the negative impact of COVID-19. The situation was particularly dire in the States of Jonglei, Upper Nile and Unity. In Pibor the situation has reached a catastrophic level due to the consequences of inter-communal fighting and floods that left a significant part of the area under water. This, consequently led to the separation of children from their primary caregivers/ parents, a series of inter-communal abductions and constant sexual assaults, mainly targeting girls. In parallel the overall situation also caused massive displacement to Pibor town and Gumruk, where families have been sheltering in public places such as schools and churches. This took a high toll on the mental and emotional health of children, recent FSNMD assessments showing at least 30% of children had behavioral changes, showing signs of distress due to repeat exposure to conflict and shocks. In Akobo, the situation is not different, recent needs assessment performed by Solidarité Internationale and INTERSOS in January 2021, show 63% of respondents having heard about child protection cases once a week on more frequently. The main protection risks highlighted by the assessment included: the presence of at least 100 UASC across the 4 Payams assessed (Gakdong, Dengjock, Nyandit and Bilkey), physical and psychological domestic violence, sexual violence, child marriage and child labor. One of the explanations of the increased cases of child marriage and labor, directly correlates with the high food insecurity in the area, families having to engage in said coping strategies to mitigate the high prices of the market and the overall lack of food. In Ayod and Mayendit, the floods heavily impacted the overall protection situation, access to the area by humanitarian actors being hampered since the mid of 2020 and remaining a challenge even in the first months of 2021. The floods carried high influx of displacement and the areas, consequently increasing the number of UASC and the need for family tracing and reunification services. INTERSOS registered 166 UASC in Ayod of which 143 still need reunification with their primary caregivers. In Mayendit, no child protection partner has been in the area for the last months, INTERSOS only implementing GBV response.Movement population also characterized the situation in Malakal and Pigi/Canal, where the last needs assessment estimates 300 UASC children identified by the State Ministry of gender child and social welfare in Malakal. A stagnated number since INTERSOS closed its child protection operation in Malakal PoC due to lack of funding. In Pigi/Canal, the estimated number is of around 200 UASC, with most cases present in Khorfulus. The high number is explained by the movement of people from Malakal to Canal and pendular movements from Alela, Chuei and Wunanguei caused by the armed conflict. The situation of children within the household remains worrying with all respondents of the assessments recognizing daily use of domestic violence towards children. In all locations, the degradation of the protective environment was marked by the closure of schools that has led to an increase use of negative coping strategies directly affecting children and mainly early marriage and child labor as mentioned before, but also has added safety risks for children. In all areas suffering floods there was a specific concern brought by the fact children spend their time either fishing or playing near water, when most of them don’t know how to swim. This has consequently increased dramatically the number of children drowned in the area. Additionally it has also created an increase of snake bites in children, in locations such as Akobo, Canal/Pigi and Mayendit.

2 - Project activities

1. Capacity Building for frontline staff: 1.1 Training of project staff on CPIE, Case Management and PSS handbook and SOPs, information management FTR for UASCs, and Referral pathways. 1.2 Training of teachers and local authorities on the basics of child protection risks identification and response 2. Provision of Family tracing and reunification (FTR) Services and of foster care system2. 1. Identification and registration, documentation, referrals, FTR for UASCs, 2. 2. dentification and training of potential foster families. The identification of foster families starts by a community mobilization aiming at raising awareness on the importance of having a foster care system and find volunteer families that would be interested in becoming foster caregivers. The selection and identification is performed in close coordination with community leaders and women’s groups to ensure the household environments is safe enough for children. After potential foster family’s haven been selected, they follow a standard training curriculum including appropriate care of infants and children, positive discipline, parenting skills, Psychosocial first aid and foster caring. It is only after successful finalization of the training that they will be identified as a foster family. It is only at this stage, that identified UASC will be placed in their care after a matching process lead by the case workers. 2.3 Provision of interim care for the unaccompanied children 3. Provision of case management 3.1 Identification of vulnerable children including orphans, children with disabilities, children suffering or at risk of suffering domestic violence, sexual abuse, early marriage and/or child labor. 3.2 Establishment of referral pathways to specialized services including primary health care, Mental health and education services. 3. Provision of material assistance for the UASC and other vulnerable children to reduce their immediate protection risks. This support can be given in-kind or in-cash depending on the location and the protection risks identified. Cash will only be given when the assistance can be managed by an adult caregiver. If the child needs to be given the assistance directly, in-kind will be prioritized. This kind of assistance can include assistance to support access to food, provision of school kits, and cash for referral to a specialized service. 4. Provision of Psychosocial Support Services including through mobile teams. 4.1 Provision of focused and non-focused PSS activities at CFC aimed at using play as tool to address emotional distress and promote life skills the four general types of play will be used: physical (football, running), constructional (clay, shapes), role-playing (drama plays) and instructional (ball games)4. 2. Peer to peer PSS sessions facilitated by facilitators aimed at understanding and learning how to deal with emotions and feelings. 4.3 To ensure the support continue even in case of increased COVID19 restrictions and/or floods leading to the closure or impossibility to perform activities at CFS, a specific PSS kit will be provided to guarantee access to the services at household level, followed with individual visits on a regular basis. 5. Provision of Awareness and community mobilization 5.1 Community engagement through outreach activities through awareness raising sessions, and FGD. 5.2 Dissemination of protection messages as well as availability of protection services.6. Community Based Child Protection networks (CBCPN) 6.1 Establishment of CBCPNs formed by members of the community. The aim of the CBCPN is to strengthen in a sustainable way the protective environment of children at a community level. Through these networks it is expected the community itself will start identifying and referring child protection cases to protection services while at the same time raising awareness on the main child protection risks faced in their communities. 6.2 Training of CBCPN on child protection basics and PFA.

3 - Project indicators

Measuring the impact of the child protection intervention will be done by a robust MEAL plan developed to measure both outcome and outputs of the project in a quantitative and qualitative way. The measure of outcome results will include the collection of a baseline, in order to have a base against which to measure the success of the intervention. Additionally, indicators measuring overall satisfaction of beneficiary will be measured on the regular basis through PDM exercise and quality surveys performed quarterly in all locations. The MEAL plan will include above all the main indicators: 1. -% training participants showing an increase knowledge on child protection topics.# of women and men trained on child protection in emergencies including case management and PSS - 2. # of unaccompanied and separated girls and boys identified, documented, and receiving family tracing and reunification services.- # of girls and boys released from armed forces and groups and provided with care and reintegration services 3.% of children having received case management service that report satisfaction with the service provision at the closure of the case. - # of girls, boys, women and men with protection concerns supported through case management and referral services- # of girls, boys, women and men supported with cash based Individual Protection Assistance as part of case management and referrals - % of beneficiaries reporting 4. - individual, group, specialized PSS and other related services (e.g. recreational activities, livelihood services, parenting skills) - # of women and men who receive psychosocial support and participating in parenting sessions 5. % of surveyed population in target locations that demonstrate an increase in knowledge of a specific child protection issue as a result of awareness-raising campaigns and messaging. - # of girls, boys, women and men reached through awareness raising activities

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

For complaints related to PSEA and CP policy issues, two members of each CRM committee (one men and one women) will be selected by the community as PSEA / CP focal points. They will receive specific sensitivity training and direct communication line (phone and airtime) with the PSEA focal point in mission in Juba. The focal point will then follow INTERSOS PSEA protocols and liaise directly with the internal audit.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Ensuring most vulnerable beneficiaries and communities have access to safe complaint and feedback mechanisms is one of the first steps in mainstreaming protection across the activities of the project. For complaint and feedback on the implementation of activities (including for example delays of implementation, complaints about selection of beneficiaries, etc.), a Complaint Response Mechanism (CRM) committee in each static location will be selected with the communities directly and ensuring all groups of the society and particularly women, children and people with disabilities have a representative. Each CRM committee will be trained in the standard procedure for taking complaints and feedbacks both in written and oral forms for the literate and illiterate respectively. Program staff and particularly community volunteers will also receive CRM training and will be available to receive complaints and feedback during collective activities and household visits. Additionally, in areas of static activities including WGFS, CFS, offices and distribution sites complaint boxes and desks will be set up. A hotline is not relevant in the areas as network is absent. On a regular basis, the CRM committee will gather the complaints received by this mechanism. One or two members of the staff will be named CRM focal point and will support the work of the CRM committee by gathering complaints received by the community volunteers, and by ensuring all “program” complaints and feedback reach the managers of each location and the response reaches the beneficiaries. Each CRM committee will share on a regular basis directly with the consortia manager and the coordination office of all three partners the database with all the complaints and feedback received as well as the measures taken at field level to be remediated. For the complaints made in terms of PSEA and CP policy, two members of each CRM committee (one man and one woman) will be selected by the community as PSEA focal points.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

The project will use several channels to ensure proper communication with beneficiaries. First, the project will include a strong awareness and mobilization component where community mobilizers will be in charge of reaching all the community using door to door methodologies to comply with COVID19 regulations and FGD in small groups to present the project, the selection criteria and the activities in general. Additionally, the project will work closely with local authorities to ensure they also promote the project within the community. To ensure beneficiary perception of the project is gathered on a regular basis, satisfaction quality survey questions will be included in the PDM exercises done after every distribution and training. The project will also include a final satisfaction survey at the end of the project, aimed at determining the impact of the project in the overall food security situation of beneficiaries. INTERSOS will ensure that complaint and feedback mechanisms are in place throughout the implementation period, aiming at safeguarding proper accountability and program quality, For complaint and feedback on the implementation of activities (including for example delays of implementation, complaints about selection of beneficiaries, etc.), a CRM committee in each location will be established by ensuring all groups of the society - and particularly women, children and people with disabilities - have a representative. Each CRM committee will be trained about standard procedure for treating complaints and feedbacks. Additionally, complaint box will be established at every distribution point and training venue, and the CRM committee will also be in charge of collecting feedback provided by this mean.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

On a monthly basis, program staff on the ground will meet the CRM committee and add to a pre-established matrix all feedback received by the beneficiaries will the measures to be taken to address said feedback. This matrix will be shared with INTERSOS coordination team in Juba that will do a strict follow up of the measures taken during field visits. For the PSEA and CP complaints, a separated matrix will be kept by the focal point where all feedback receive will be stored as well as the measures taken. This matrix will be shared on a regular basis with the internal audit.
