Project Summary

The purpose of this project is to ensure that boys and girls in Syria are safe in their homes and communities and receive support when they experience harm. The IRC’s strategy is to meet the immediate protection needs of the most vulnerable populations through the provision of integrated specialized and non-specialized protection services protection services. IRC will directly provide specialized and non-specialized child protection services covering three towns in Darkoush and Salquin sub-districts in Jisr Ash-Shugur and Harim districts including one Safe Healing and Learning Space (SHLS) in Salqin town. The SHLS services include provision of structured psychosocial support (PSS), Social and Emotional Learning (SEL), and parenting skills sessions for caregivers to prevent violence in the home. Case Management (CM) will be a core aspect of IRC’s CP intervention with a particular focus on child labour, violence and abuse in the home, psychosocial distress, and responding to the needs of unaccompanied and separated children (UASC). Support will also be provided for restoring family links and ensuring appropriate referral through trained community outreach volunteers. IRC will increase the number of case workers providing specialized child protection services in NWS from four to six and establish two new CP committees (CPC).

Needs

Individuals in Syria face protection risks in their daily lives and have a diminished capacity to mitigate these risks. Indiscriminate and targeted attacks on civilian areas and facilities, grave rights violations, denial of humanitarian access, and violations of international law persist unabated. Violence is pervasive, and Protection issues—such as child labour and child recruitment, early marriage, intimate partner violence, sexual violence, economic exploitation and family separation, among others—are alarmingly prevalent and increasing. Community resilience is tested due to disruption of livelihoods and psychosocial distress resulting in the use of these harmful negative coping strategies. The 2018 HNO states that four million children are still in need of protection services in Syria with 82% of surveyed communities reporting occurrence of child labour preventing school attendance, 69% reporting incidences of child marriage, 52% reporting family separation, 47% reporting child recruitment into armed forces, 27% reporting sexual violence, all contributing to the trauma and psychological distress of children. According to Save the Children (2017), at least three million Syrian children under the age of six know nothing but war, and millions more have grown up in fear under the shadow of conflict e.g. 84% of adults and almost all children say that ongoing bombing and shelling is the number one cause of psychological stress in children’s daily lives. IRC’s PM also showed that young children are being employed for hazardous work inappropriate for their age such as construction, carpentry, agriculture, and blacksmithing resulting in significant physical and psychological health consequences. Syrian service providers are uniquely positioned to reinforce and create norms, policies and laws to promote a protective environment for the most vulnerable members of society. However, if the capacity of service providers fails to meet minimum standards, services risk not only being inaccessible but may cause harm. Many local NGOs are newly established, with little experience implementing protection programming in line with global case management standards. While specialized direct service delivery by international organizations remains important, strengthening the knowledge, skills and resources of community-based stakeholders to implement accessible and responsive protection services is a priority.

Implementation of activities

Through the provision of specialised case management services targeting 550 boys and girls, IRC will identify vulnerable children, provide immediate protection and ensure that their individual needs are assessed and addressed. IRC aims to increase its current case management workforce in Idleb Governorate, Jisr Ash-Shugur and Harim Districts, and expand its services to include new IDP sites in Jisr Ash-Shugur District. IRC will target boys and girls who show signs of psychological distress, children who have experienced abuse, unaccompanied and separated children (UASC), children engaged in worst forms of child labour and cases of child marriage. IRC will continue to run one SHLS in Harim District reaching 600 boys and girls through Social and Emotional Learning (SEL), 3,240 through outreach PSS and 1,500 caregivers through parenting education. The SHLS is a unique approach designed to increase humanitarian capacity to provide timely and holistic psychosocial support to children in a secure, caring and predictable place where children and adolescents living in conflict and crisis settings can learn, develop and be protected. IRC will use Community Action as the foundation of its engagement with targeted communities, helping communities to actively address the protection and social needs of their children. IRC will continue to support and build the capacity of a child protection committee (CPC) with 15 members in Salqin and establish similar structures in two other towns with 30 members to promote community-led child protection responses. The IRC will also support the child protection committee to promote community action in Salqin and to further strengthen its community engagement. The IRC will train 400 community leaders and groups to identify risks and strengthen access to local child protection services. IRC will also train 15 community child protection focal points and 200 service providers including teachers on core child protection principles, psychological first aid (PFA), and safe referrals. IRC will continue to capitalize on the existing high capacity of local organizations, especially in less accessible areas of Syria, to support 16 local Syrian partners in northern Syria to strengthen their CP case management programming by building capacity of staff knowledge, systems and management structures. The intervention will follow three phases: Phase 1: Strengthening knowledge & common understanding through Trainings on core competencies and specialized case management and workshops based on gaps identified in the post training surveys and baseline assessment. Phase 2 Practice: coaching for supervisors and caseworkers through observation of case management session; case file review; group and individual coaching sessions; Phase 3 Handover: Coaching and quality assurance to be handed over to partners and a final workshop to provide IRC with feedback to inform future project cycles. For IRC direct implementation, protection beneficiaries will be measured using case management files, attendance sheets, cash distribution forms, and household visit forms that are managed under strict confidentiality rules by the protection team. Recipients capacity strengthening will be captured using attendance sheets and post tests will determine changes in knowledge and attitudes. Beneficiary satisfaction and impact of assistance, including wellbeing and coping strategy scores, will be tracked through satisfaction surveys. IRC’s MEAL team also conduct spot checks at IRC and partner service outlets (except for specialized services) to confirm delivery and quality of service using agreed program quality checklists. IRC monitoring will assesses equitable access and potential negative effects and solicit feedback. During analysis of data, all information will be disaggregated by gender and where appropriate, disaggregated by age.

Protection Risk Analysis

Services will be open to Syrian locals, IDPs, and returnees, but will be targeted based on need and vulnerability, prioritizing women, children, PWD, and the elderly. For CP, IRC will follow vulnerability criteria developed and agreed by the CP sub-cluster which will be constantly updated and contextualized to the situation in targeted communities. IRC’s child protection team together with the protection monitoring team will collect data directly from vulnerable communities to allow IRC to understand beneficiary needs, gaps in service provision, and context specific protection risks related to aid provision, deliberately ensuring that all vulnerable groups can access services. IRC will also conduct regular project review and reflection workshops and utilise child-led clubs in SHLS to assess progress, document learning, and promote children’s participation. The Humanitarian Access Unit (HAU), work closely with program leads and field management to identify risks and trends that may impact IRC to manage, mitigate and prepare contingency plans. The Syria country program developed a Security Management Plan (SMP) and Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) for northwest Syria to guide IRC operations and staff, to enable them to provide lifesaving assistance using the safest methods for IRC staff and beneficiaries. These policies are updated regularly or when necessary. The HAU, keep monitoring the conflict situation and provide analysis and scenarios for potential risks to inform the contingency planning through Bi-weekly situation updates, quarterly humanitarian access updates and the daily security situation reports including one-on-one meetings for specific risks management. These practices gives the IRC the ability to ensure the safety and security of staff, beneficiaries and assets including the IRC obligation in regards to duty of care and staff welfare. The IRC also uses the Feedback and Accountability Mechanism (FARM) to ensure beneficiary accountability and ensures that beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries alike are able to provide feedback to an independent Accountability Team. The FARM is promoted through fliers, posters, in-person presentations, videos, and more. Upon reception, feedback will be categorized for priority and complaint type, logged in an online information system, and instantly uploaded to a dashboard for trend analysis. Feedback will be routed based on their activity and priority level to the appropriate focal point and responses, fact-finding, or corrective action will be taken based on the content of the complaint. The FARM, coupled with monthly satisfaction surveys, recurring focus group discussions, and other ad-hoc studies will enable beneficiaries and non-beneficiary community members to provide regular feedback, complaints, and recommendations to IRC before, during, and after implementation.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Assumptions:- IRC can continue to access targeted areas. - Communities will be willing to engage in child protection activities. - IRC can retain sufficient trained CP staff. Risks:- Security prevents access.- Sensitivity and hostility around child protection topics.- Limited specialized organizations to which referrals can be made, and limited local CP capacity. Mitigation strategies:- Remote and on-site training and support for IRC team members. - Careful introduction of CP activities at the local level, taking a do no harm approach. - Engaging with partners supported under capacity-building program and continuing with strong existing IRC CP staff.
