Project Summary

Protracted conflict and other emergencies such as Famine and floods have continued to weaken the socio-economic foundation of South Sudan for years. Since independence, the country has continued to face both man-made and natural disasters that have has affected the populace in all facets of life almost to a point of no return yet. These broad challenges coupled with the uncertain political environment has created an environment of despair as seen in the free fall of the value of the South Sudanese pound over time. Inflation has taken a toll on the populace, purchasing power has diminished significantly and many people have been left without adequate safety nets to support their basic livelihoods.
All these continue to happen at a time of global crisis on COVID-19 that has further put immense burden on public health needs across the country. The humanitarian services continue to face headwinds on this matter especially because of low testing capacity in locations outside Juba. The global guidelines adopted to deal with the effect of COVID-19 are also not without their shortfalls such as missed beneficiaries who would otherwise be admitted into the program after doing physical anthropometric assessments.
At the national level, the latest IPC report October 2020- July 2021 released on 18th December 2020 juxtapositions the deteriorating situation in the country. The report highlights that in the projected period between April to July 2021, there will be 7.24 million people with severe acute food insecurity and hence in need of urgent action. This is an increase from the 5.82 million in the same phase for the post-harvest period of December 2020 and March 2021. 21 counties were classified to be in IPC phase 4 between Dec 2020 and March 2021 and this figure goes up for the period April-July 2021 where up to 45 counties are classified in phase 4.
The contributing factors to the bad food security situation is attributed to disease prevalence, poor food diversity and quality, inter communal fighting that further hinders humanitarian access and floods that further complicated access but also destroyed crops. COVID-19 also contributes to the complicated operating environment. On the flip side of it, IPC for acute malnutrition also presents a gloomy picture of the situation as it may unfold in 2021 up to July 2021. 53 counties were expected to be in phase 3 and above between November 2020 ND March 2021. This figure goes up to 57 between March and July 2021. Due to lack of surveillance data, some of the counties across the county were classified based on recent FSNSM reports. In the areas targeted by Nile Hope with the 2021 HRP, previous survey data indicated worsening trends of malnutrition. SMART survey in Pigi by IMC in April 2019 revealed a GAM of 24.3%, Fangak was 16.2% according to AAH in September 2019 while a 2018 SMART survey for Akobo East by IMC indicated GAM of 14.2%. The Leer SMART survey was last done by Medair in April 2019 and the GAM rate was 14.3%. These rates of malnutrition depict a bad situation requiring immediate interventions to the vulnerable. Lack of 2020 surveillance data due to COVID-19 situation in a way indicates that the situation could have gotten worse than it was back then especially in the greater upper Nile region where access, conflict anf floods have dominated the period.
Using the conceptual framework on the causes of malnutrition by UNICEF, the situation in the areas where Nile Hope operates depicts a malnutrition burden that can easily be attributed to disease and inadequate consumption and hence immediate causes. The MDD and frequency of meals as shown in the food security component of the recent IPC report clearly support this. The disease burden is also clearly elucidated in the evidence provided. These are mostly because of the fragile health and economic infrastructure in the upper Nile region where access to health facilities is still a problem.

1 - Needs

As indicated with the proxy GAM rates released from modelling of the existing data and conditions due to absence of SMART survey data in 2020, the locations targeted by Nile Hope in this HRP have a worsening nutrition situation. Akobo is indicated to have a proxy GAM of 25.24%, Pigi 24.55%, Nassir, 23.56%, Fangak 20.27% and Leer at 12.58%. Akobo has had a majority population in phase 4 of the IPC classification based on acute malnutrition and was also affected by floods that displaced populations and destroyed farms and other means of livelihoods. Malaria and acute watery diarrhea are prevalent in Akobo and gets worse during the rainy season. Accessibility in Akobo East has improved over time including the opening of mobile Telephony but geographically, the locations towards the border with Pibor such as Burmath still remains a challenge to access. These areas are also prone to cattle rustling and fear of child abduction that occasionally drive out communities from their inhabited areas to the river banks, hours away. This means people’s livelihoods continue to be disrupted throughout the year and this puts a strain on the available resources for health and nutrition safety nets too. Such disruptions also means that children enrolled in the program could be missed or those in need of services are at risk of not being admitted in time except with adequate resources to track and provide dynamic services. In 2020, Pigi county witnessed increased hostilities and inter clan fighting that resulted in many fatalities around areas where Nile Hope works. The situation resulted in displacements, destruction of property and looting of essential commodities used in treating malnourished children and women. Access remains difficult in parts of Pigi and malnutrition is heavily contributed to by disease burden owing to poor WASH situation in the county with swamps and no boreholes for drinking water. Most of the land mass in Pigi county is not arable and the community are reliant on food aid with small scale farming in the forest areas. Fangak on the other hand continue to experience challenges similar to the ones in Pigi with disease burden mainly from malaria, diarrhea and acute respiratory infections. The GAM rates indicate that the malnutrition is above the emergency threshold and efforts must be channeled towards solving the rising cases of malnutrition. The situation in Leer has improved but still remains unpredictable. Service delivery will help a majority whose crops were destroyed by floods in late 2020. The location is prone to conflict that eventually makes provision of essential services difficult. The county is also having populations mostly in IPC phase 3. In all these locations targeted by Nile Hope, there are inadequate feeding practices for children and therefore, through this project, behavior change communication and empowering existing community structures such as the office of the CHD and women in the community will continue as part of imparting useful knowledge on child feeding practices and changing aspects of culture that hinder progress in this area will be key. The trade routes have opened in most of these locations but not optimally. This means the market structures are still weak and most people don’t have enough variety of products to choose from in the market. The purchasing power is also very low since a majority don’t have jobs, there are elderly population at home who cannot engage in strenuous economic activities and because a majority have been traumatized by the protracted violence and suffering over the years. From program data in 2020, Nile Hope was able to admit 20, 842 children under 5 with acute malnutrition (MAM and SAM cases) and 12,480 Pregnant and Lactating women with acute malnutrition for treatment in the 5 counties. In most of these counties Nile Hope works with other partners except Akobo And Pigi but the beneficiaries treated are indicative of the malnutrition burden in South Sudan.

2 - Project activities

This project will seek to implement the following activities;• Active case finding through MUAC screening at the community level• Treatment of SAM without medical complications in OTPs• Treatment of SAM with Medical complications in the stabilization center in Leer• Treatment of MAM for children under 5 • Treatment of PLW with MAM• Malaria testing and treatment for all children in OTP and symptomatic cases in TSFP• Provision of preventive package (soap, mosquito nets, water purifiers) to caregivers and PLWs• Vitamin A supplementation through NIDs to children 6-59 months• Deworming for boys and girls 12-59 through NIDs• MIYCN Counseling and Behavior change communication through Mother support groups, group and one-on-one counseling sessions at the facility and community.Monthly data capturing and reporting through the NIS for all sites (Static and outreach) providing nutrition services across the 5 counties. Community engagement and utilization of feedback mechanism to improve community participation and accountability to affected populationImplementation of the minimum package for integration of Nutrition, WASH, FSL, Health, Education and Protection.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

All staff working in this project will sign codes of conduct with safeguarding policies as well. Staff will be trained on PSEA and will sign commitments to uphold the PSEA principles. Through the staff and community workers, the beneficiaries will be sensitized on their rights and the existence of the policy to protect them from sexual exploitation and abuse

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Through the use of community nutrition volunteers, vulnerable members of the community will be asked to feel free and discuss the issues affected them with the people they trust the most. They will also be urged to seek assistance to write and drop their complaints anonymously in the suggestion boxes provided. Meetings at the community level, facility and during stakeholder meetings will also be used to get feedback from the community.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

There will be project implementation committees in each county drawn from the community. Through these people, feedback from the community will be received and acted upon. At the onset of the project, the project initiation workshop will involve the breadth and depth of the community to ensure that all interest groups are involved. Details of the project will continuously be disseminated during community meetings so that all people are in the know about what the projects seek to achieve. There will also be suggestion boxes in the facilities for collecting anonymous feedback. Finally, the project teams will conduct at least 1 quarterly meeting with stakeholders to review various aspects of the project using a locally constructed satisfaction scale but pegged on the sphere standards

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Feedback will be assessed for accuracy and desire action assessed further for feasibility. In situation where the feedback does not result in significant change of the project design, internal deliberations including with the respective donor(s) will take place and agreed actions implemented. Feedback that require support beyond the project scope will be referred to appropriate entities for action.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

To mitigate against the risks, CP will work closely with the authorities to monitor the security situation and where applicable, suspend services if it cannot guarantee the safety of staff and beneficiaries. Staff and community workers will sign commitments that they will never try to use their positions of influence to take advantage of the women and children served in the project. The local authority will always be kept in the picture to ensure that there is no suspicion on the foregoing within the projects that would necessitate interference. The gravity of the roles of staff to the community will be explained so that leaders do not seek to push for the employment of less qualified staff. Using community nutrition volunteers to report will enable the community members to open up to people they trust since they live in the same locations. However, where discretion is needed, they will be urged to ask persons who can write to write their issues and place in suggestion boxes.In recent years, flooding has impeded service delivery by limiting access and has led to delays in supply deliveries to those regions most affected. To mitigate against this, dry season prepositioning shall be done in all areas as the CP has central storage stores capable of holding adequate stock that would last for more than 3 months. Additionally, designating areas on higher and drier grounds where the facilities can be relocated to to ensure minimal interruptions to service delivery. The COVID 19 pandemic has had an adverse effect on regular nutrition programing that has led to the adoption of a revised set of guidelines aimed at minimizing/curbing the spread of this deadly virus. Nile Hope shall strictly adhere to this guidelines during service provision and ensure at least one facility staff is oriented at least twice in a year and ensure each facility has at least one hard copy of the guidelines for reference. Additionally, Nile Hope will ensure that the staff shall have adequate PPEs (face masks and hand sanitizers) that will protect them from contracting COVID 19. Regular sensitization campaigns on COVID 19 will be conducted in the community by CNVs.
