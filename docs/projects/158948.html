1 - Needs

In the West Bank, including East Jerusalem Israeli settlement expansion has continued, and spatial plans were advanced at a rate unseen since 2002. Settlement expansion, emboldened by the position of the US administration led to an aggravated coercive environment for Palestinians residing in their proximity, driven by destruction of property, including humanitarian relief, unlawful changes made to local legislation and settler violence, ultimately leading to the forcible transfer of Palestinian households, and an increased risk of mass displacement. While the overall number of Palestinians being killed or injured by Israeli forces decreased as compared to 2018, 21 Palestinians have been killed and 2,598 injured during the year according to OCHA. Most of the fatalities occurred in Jerusalem and Nablus governorates; whereas most of the injuries were recorded in Hebron city, followed by Al Eizariya and Al Isawiya (Jerusalem Governorate), in/around Qalqiliya (Kafr Qaddum and Azzun village). At the same time, Israeli forces carried out 3,328 search and arrest operations in the West Bank, the majority of which were in the Ramallah, Jerusalem and Hebron governorates. So far in 2019, OCHA recorded 243 incidents where Israeli settlers killed or injured Palestinians or damaged Palestinian property, which is a mild increase compared to the same period of 2018 (213) but almost double the figure for 2017 (124). Importantly, citing the lack of Israeli-issued building permits, the Israeli authorities demolished or forced people to demolish 283 structures in Area C and 173 in East Jerusalem, displacing 287 Palestinians in Area C and 261 in East Jerusalem. The number of structures demolished so far this year across the West Bank represents an almost 40% increase compared to the same period in 2018. In Area C in particular, we are seeing +30% increase in structures demolitions and +51% in displacement compared to 2018; 1/3 structures were donor-funded, for a total of EUR 300,000. Geographically, Hebron, Bethlehem and Tubas governorates are particularly targeted (particularly, military/firing zones and communities close to settlements). GoI measures to increase court fees and transfer jurisdiction for demolition and planning issues for Area C from the Israeli High Court to the Jerusalem District Court, coming into effect of the MO 1797 (new structures) and increased use of the MO 5753 (mobile structures) have already impacted Palestinians’ access to court. The first half of 2019 has seen intensified advancement of the settlement enterprise in the Holy Basin (Silwan - Kedem Compound, Jerusalem Cable Car and City of David) and Hebron H-2 area (Decree 1789 concerning the civil services administration), including increased settler violence with the departure of TIPH. The restrictions on freedom of movement have a disproportionate impact on “wall-affected” communities, Seam Zone farmers, the population of the H-2 area of Hebron, influencing livelihoods and every aspect of daily life. Access to land by Seam Zone farmers have been severely limited by an increased rejection of request by the military on the grounds that land registration records issued by the PA for private land in Area C were “invalid” and recently introduced standing order that sets the “enabling agricultural cultivation” (as opposed to the 2017 designation “preserving connection to the land”). In addition, 2019 standing order restricts the number of entries into the seam zone according to the size of the plot and the type of crop (for example, 40 days per year for olive cultivation); previously, the permits were issued for a 2-year period for entry on specific days.

2 - Activities

Any of the aforementioned changes could compromise the ability of the international community to provide legal protection from forcible transfer. This project is designed to challenge these policies, through employing a four-pillar approach, comprising of: 1.Immediate legal response; 2. Preventative legal response; 3. Policy change response; and4. Stakeholders’ capacity-building PC continues to engage in the 1st pillar of immediate legal response on behalf of Palestinian HHs and communities before Israeli courts to challenge policies that are absolutely proscribed in international law. The aim is to assist Palestinians to challenge demolition orders or other violations through the court system in order to provide beneﬁciaries with temporary reprieve, and allow for additional measures to be considered. This is done with the hope that respect for international law will pervade in the meantime and the violations will be suppressed. Furthermore, the Israeli judiciary system is the only option for domestic recourse to justice and international law which prescribes that one must exhaust all domestic legal remedies prior to litigating cases on an international level. As a result of PC’s ICLA programme, in 2018 so far approximately 95% of beneﬁciaries in Area C and East Jerusalem have remained in their homes due to legal representation provided through PC. Under the 2nd pillar, preventative legal response, forced displacement is challenged through consultations and the delivery of legal information to communities and beneﬁciaries. This response focuses on the identiﬁcation of beneﬁciary needs and pro-actively addresses obstacles towards access to justice. The 3rd pillar aims to achieve a policy change response through humanitarian focused advocacy, public interest cases (PICs), research, and engagement with UN mechanisms. Ultimately, the aim is for international law to be applied to Palestine in a way that harmonizes Israel’s current discriminatory policies and laws with IHL and IHRL norms.Speciﬁcally, the success of PICs is inter-dependent upon the inﬂuence exerted by the UN, EU and Third States to obtain favourable statements and resolutions founded on IHL and Human Rights principles, which in-turn inform the stance of the parties to the litigation. PICs consequently, and due to their principled nature, have a signiﬁcantly longer duration than individual cases, often lasting upwards of 10 years without a ﬁnal ruling; thus, preserving the status quo for Palestinian communities and avoiding large-scale eviction, demolitions and conﬁscations pending resolution of complex issues of IHL and human rights law by the court. PC is currently supporting 104 PICs in the WB, including East Jerusalem that challenge 45 principled issues pertaining to the Palestinian peoples’ fundamental human rights. These cases aim to establish positive precedent in Israeli courts, which would assist pending cases with slim margins for successful closure. Of the 19 PICs closed in 2018, 8 had successful ﬁnal outcomes, meaning that 42% of these HLP and Residency cases were closed with favourable rulings that did not result in forcible displacement / transfer. Through the 4th pillar, PC contributes to the Palestinian Authority (PA) capacity building and the coordination of the legal aid activities with legal professionals and local communities. Ultimately, ICLA’s work is aimed at contributing to the development of a sustainable legal aid system for Palestinian legal actors. This will be done by enhancing the capacity of the PA to work on the legal response in Area C through the Anti Wall and Settlement Resistance Commission; in East Jerusalem through raising the coordination capacity of Jerusalem Governorate, complemented by the PC co- chairing a sub-working group of the Protection Cluster, the Legal Task Force (LTF), which is the main coordination platform for the legal responses of local and international actor in the ﬁeld of legal aid.

9 - Protection mainstreaming
9.1 - Do No Harm

In order to prioritize safety and dignity of Palestinians at risk or affected by forcible transfer over other considerations, ICLA will conduct protection risk assessments (Do no Harm), adopt conflict and context sensitive approach in dealing with beneficiaries, ensure informed beneficiary consent and acting on instructions of beneficiaries, securing confidentiality and data protection. In addition, ICLA will use additional protection technical expertise to supplement its interventions; coordinate with specialised protection actors to assist with conducting protection risk assessment of ICLA beneficiaries, carry out combined ICLA/protection campaign around protection issues; in collaboration with gender / child protection actors, conduct joint legal and protection research around a particular protection issue or risk in order to inform programming or recruit staff with expert protection skills in PC. For example, although the legal framework might enshrine certain rights and entitlements that PC would ideally like to pursue (e.g. strategic litigation), PC will not do so, if the process of accessing justice were assessed as likely to do harm to the claimants.PC’s Protection Policy, Gender Policy, Code of Conduct and Child Safeguarding Policy and guidance notes and tools including Core Competency Quality Assessment tools and the Protection Resource Pack all contribute to reinforcing and providing guidance on how to action the protection policy. PC’s Code of Conduct holds staff, partners and associates (e.g. contractors) accountable, while projecting a common set of standards to the beneficiaries being served. PSEA prevention and response training is part of the induction for all staff. At the community level, ICLA will support multi-sectoral, sustainable, and integrated programming with other humanitarian actors (e.g. through West Bank Protection Consortium), which will allow people to preserve or increase their resources and opportunities, thus making them more resilient, and more likely to find durable solutions. ICLA will ensure community engagement on different levels of project cycle management and echoing community feedback. ICLA will conduct needs assessments, service mapping and where relevant, refer beneficiaries to other PC core competencies or other partners providing specific services. PC ICLA will support OCHA in the assessment, dissemination of information and implement referral pathways in order to ensure coordinated prevention and response to emergency. ICLA will actively and regularly participate in the Protection Cluster, lead Legal TaskForce and collaborate with other Clusters and actors (e.g. UNRWA) on awareness raising, sensitisation and information dissemination activities to ensure that people have meaningful access to information and feedback mechanisms. PC Advocacy unit will seek to maintain the humanitarian situation in West Bank on the international agenda in an effort to shape priorities and address the impact of the GoI policies through an evidence-based approach with the consent of the individuals involved, while upholding the do no harm principle.

9.2 - Equal and impartial access to aid

ICLA programme is designed to ensure that the most vulnerable Palestinians in hard-to-reach areas have access to basic rights and humanitarian assistance, reduce their vulnerability and build resilience. ICLA activities will be complementary to other existing Protection activities in order to avoid duplication and ensure holistic protection interventions. Information dissemination and counselling: ICLA’s main priorities will be to empower women, men, girls and boys to have equal access to information and services. ICLA will develop information and communication products to support IDPs/refugees and host communities’ access to basic services, rights and entitlements. Accurate, timely and relevant legal information and awareness-raising messages will be disseminated through outreach activities, including face-to-face information and counselling by trained field staff. ICLA will also use a community-based and participatory approach to identify and train members of the communities to disseminate information, recognise protection concerns and refer cases to specialised service providers. Legal assistance including representation: ICLA will use judicial and administrative remedies to support access to justice for the most vulnerable including women, children and people with disabilities. Approaches and procedures will be selected on a case-by-case basis to allow for a customised response that meets the specific needs and interests of the beneficiary(s). In this respect, carefully selected and trained fe/male officers may provide legal information and advice, facilitate negotiations, and if relevant, provide legal representation. Legal assistance will be delivered through PC and partners’ legal aid clinics including ICLA mobile legal aid clinic to ensure widest possible outreach for people with limited mobility. Flexible and creative approaches will be used to address barriers to access to justice such as health, education, costs, distance, etc.Capacity Building: ICLA will also provide technical support (sensitisation, training, orientation, etc.) to local NGOs, youth and women leaders and community representatives, as well as humanitarian actors with a view to strengthening their capacity to ensure protection for IDPs/refugees and support host communities. Particular emphasis will be placed on IHL/IHRL, rules and regulations about the rights and issues of women and other vulnerable groups. Subject to needs assessment, ICLA may also provide financial and organisational support to implementing partner to strengthen their capacity and ensure sustainability of the intervention. Advocacy: Research and technical support on legal status, freedom of movement, access to rights and services and adequate standard of living, including housing will be used to inform the major protection actors, donor community, and other relevant stakeholders, with the respective analyses of the current protection situation, strengthen the networks and seek allies in order to influence policies and decision making in relation to the targeted population.
