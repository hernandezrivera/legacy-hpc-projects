Project Summary

This project will provide legal assistance to vulnerable people in Tripoli, Benghazi and Ajdabiya - both Libyans and non-Libyans. By targeting key thematic areas including housing, land and property issues; complex legal documentation cases; residency and status; and employment disputes, legal assistance will be targeted at reducing vulnerability and improving positive coping mechanisms, in support of durable solutions and resilience. A strong emphasis will be placed on negotiation and mediation, and engagement with community dispute resolution mechanisms in order to achieve positive outcomes for beneficiaris. The project impact will be achieved through the provision of information, counselling, legal assistance, training and through a Call Centre for refugees.

1 - Needs

In 2020, major shifts in the conflict dynamics in Libya and the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic have combined to exacerbate the humanitarian crisis. Measures adopted to combat the spread of COVID-19 included full lockdowns, closures of businesses and curfews, which limited the effective provision of services and created resultant socio-economic consequences. In June 2020, the collapse of the LNA offensive on Tripoli led to the retaking of Tarhouna and the subsequent displacement of approximately 28,000 people, bringing the total number of IDPs in Libya to 392,241. A key characteristic of the Libyan humanitarian context is the higher capacity of many Libyans to deal with initial shocks caused by conflict, due to the reliance on negative coping mechanisms in order to meet their basic needs. This, however, masks the true scale of the humanitarian impact of the conflict as it serves to privatize the problem. This is most evident in the shelter sector with the vast majority (85%) of displaced people housed in rented accommodation or with family and friends. Ultimately, this is unsustainable as evidenced by the fact that 61% of households have a ‘capacity gap’ (MSNA 2019), meaning they are unable to meet their basic needs and/or are using negative coping strategies to meet these needs. By utilizing negative coping strategies, conflict affected households are heightening their longer term risk profile, as without a significant change in their circumstances, they are likely to eventually be unable to meet any of their basic needs. Furthermore, negative coping mechanisms cannot withstand multiple shocks, and the conflict plus COVID-19 combined could lead to a complete breakdown in capacity. Long-term reliance on negative coping mechanisms increases vulnerability to risks such as eviction and homelessness. The time lag between the conflict-affected shock (displacement), and the collapse of coping mechanisms, can mean that evictions are often understood as socio-economic or chronic poverty concerns, rather than humanitarian needs associated with conflict displacement. The causal link to disaster is emphasized by the higher rate of eviction or threat of eviction reported by conflict-affected households – approx. 7.5% amongst IDP or returnee households – compared to non-displaced (4.2%). Housing affordability and adequate housing, according to OCHA, remains a serious challenge facing Libyan IDPs. This risk of eviction and homelessness is exacerbated for women who often do not have a good understanding of their HLP rights including inheritance laws that relate to real property. Culturally, it is common practice for a husband to own the family home in his own name with no rights in equity held by wives. The humanitarian needs in Libya are further complicated by the mixed migrant population made up of refugees, asylum seekers, migrants with legal residency and migrants who entered Libya illegally. This population totals 600,392 migrants and 46,247 refugees/asylum seekers. Many members of the mixed migrant population are regularly subjected to violations of international human rights law, international humanitarian law and are subjected to torture, sexual violence, forced labour and trafficking. During a series of FGDs conducted with refugees and asylum seekers in Tripoli, by the NRC ICLA team, common challenges reported by the community were a lack of information about pathways to legal residency, and not being paid for casual daily labour after the work has been completed. Although legal pathways to residency are limited, the misconceptions in the community about residency has led to many refugees and asylum seekers being exploited or preyed on with fake document schemes, including for residency permits. This can put them at further risk of detention and exploitation.

2 - Activities

In 2021, the ICLA team will focus on providing information, counselling and legal assistance to Libyans and non-Libyans on their HLP rights, civil documentation, residency and work rights, access to essential services and work rights. This will include the following activities; - Establishing an eviction tracking system in both Tripoli and Benghazi which identifies cases of eviction and responds accordingly; - Drafting standard form contracts to secure tenancy; - Providing women with information sessions on key HLP themes, and broader legal education topics to improve awareness of legal rights; - Collaborative dispute resolution for people who are at risk of eviction; - Providing specialised support for complex civil documentation cases which are affecting populations that are being discriminated against and for non-Libyans who are able to register births and other key life events; and - Operating a Call Centre for refugees and asylum seekers, in collaboration with UNHCR, that provides information, referrals and assistance to beneficiaries over the phone;- Establishing a Community Centre in Ajdabiya, which will be a focal point for legal assistance and where migrant community members will be able to access advice. - Small component of cash-for-rent or relocation grants where legal interventions to prevent eviction have failed, to be paid in cases of extreme vulnerability and only to prevent homelessness.
