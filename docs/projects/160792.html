Project Summary

The first objective of the 2019 Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP) is to “Save lives by providing timely and integrated multi-sector assistance and services to reduce acute needs among the most vulnerable women, men, girls and boys.’’ (UN OCHA Dec 2018). The cumulative effects of years of conflict have continued to drive displacements and disrupt economic activity and livelihoods, contributing to high levels of food insecurity across Uror, Aweil and Twic East. Tearfund, through the proposed intervention will achieve the following objectives in line with the sector objectives:
Cluster Objective 1: Provide food assistance to prevent famine and improve food consumption, dietary diversity and coping strategies for vulnerable populations (in IPC 5, 4 & some 3)
Cluster Objective 2: Enhance emergency food production through complementary vegetable and crop seeds and fishing and livestock support (in IPC 5, 4 and 3)
Cluster Objective 3: Reduce dependency on food and agricultural inputs to support and strengthen households’ ability to absorb shocks (in IPC 5, 4 and 3)
This will be done through the provision of lifesaving assistance to conflict affected communities by providing agricultural inputs and provide training vulnerable households to increase food production through good agricultural and fisheries practices to improve food security. Emergency livelihood kits distribution, and increasing access to local markets through seed fairs will be organised for local farmers and the project will provide direct distribution of food rations and cash to the most vulnerable communities.Tearfund will also look at ways of resilience-building that reduce recipient’s future risk and vulnerability to negative shocks.
The community and other stakeholders will be sensitised on the project and informed of the beneficiary selection criteria, which will be agreed by community members. During community sensitisation the importance of prioritising women and vulnerable groups will be emphasised in order to gain community acceptance of the selection criteria. Vulnerable HHs will be prioritised for assistance, including female, elderly and child headed HHs, those with malnourished children and people living with disabilities. Beneficiaries will be asked their preferences regarding seeds through FGDs, which will be held separately for men, women and specific vulnerable groups to ensure that vulnerable groups are able to fully participate in and contribute to the discussions. Community members, including representatives of vulnerable groups, will be involved in the selection and verification of beneficiaries.
The proposed project will be implemented in Uror, Twic East and Duk counties of Jonglei state as well as Aweil East county of Northern Bahr el Ghazal. The needs across all two states continue to escalate throughout 2019, with poor harvests, prolonged floods and conﬂict resulting in depleted stocks. Uror, Twic East, Aweil East and Aweil Centre are predicted to remain at Crisis levels (IPC 3) in January - April 2020, while Duk is predicted to maintain emergency level (IPC 4). The prolonged floods, continued armed conﬂict and insecurity has disrupted livelihoods activities, and severely limited market functionality.

1 - Needs

The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC)1 for acute food insecurity and acute malnutrition analysis was conducted in January 2019. In May-July 2019, the projection was 6.87 million people (60% of the population were facing acute food insecurity, with an emergency situation declared in 27 counties and 1.91 million people facing emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. According to the South Sudan Food Security and Nutrition Report (July 2019) “The food security situation continues to deteriorate due to conflict-driven displacement, low crop production, economic crisis, climate shocks and humanitarian access challenges.” Key findings from the survey include the fact that “Food insecurity in the country has reached record levels (74%) during the harvest season compared to the same time in previous years. This continues against a backdrop of deteriorating macro-economic conditions present before the signing of the peace agreement. The proportion of households facing severe food insecurity has almost doubled compared to December 2017 and overall it has reached its highest levels of 26% compared to the yearly trends during harvest season.” A significant proportion of the population has inadequate food consumption due to disrupted markets and declining availability of sources of food. Although households’ food consumption has improved by 3% compared to December 2017 the overall situation is not encouraging compared to the trends. The high level of food insecurity and shocks are also reflected in the severity of household coping mechanisms. Overall, 66 percent of households were either resorting to emergency coping strategies (30 percent) or crisis coping strategies (36%) while only 10% were practicing stress coping strategies. With extreme levels of food insecurity experiences throughout South Sudan, the worst affected areas include Twic East and Uror in Jonglei (77%). Household having at least one physically disable member (12%), The results also revealed a high proportion of female-headed households in conflict affected states Household having at least one mentally disable member (5 percent), Household having at least one chronically ill member (8 percent), Household having at least one injured member (4%). About 12 percent of the households had at least one disabled member with five percent of the households reporting at least one mentally disabled member in the family; 8% of the households had chronically ill members and another three percent reported individuals who were injured during recent conflictsFrom August 2019, the operating environment has been characterised by some incidents of fighting between South Sudan Defence Forces (SSDF) and South Sudan United Front (SSUF) in parts of Aweil East on the border with Sudan and in Aweil North and West. Flooding has also led to displacements, destroyed infrastructure like roads and as well as crops. Prices of basic items like food are still high as the people in the area are yet to harvest and this has further been compounded by the political unrest that was in the Sudan, which is the main source of commodities consumed in the Greater Northern Bahr el Ghazal. Limited access to farming inputs has affected the ability of communities to cope without further food assistance. The food insecurity particularly affects women as they are responsible for providing food for their families. Households mostly headed by women (84%), are using all their available energy, time and resources to meet their immediate food needs, often through gathering and selling firewood. While most of the population depends on farming for their income, women face barriers to access land such as traditional laws and inheritance customs (going through the male line). This points to the fact that there is still need for more awareness and sensitization on gender as well as involvement in decision making and empowerment of women if even if they now have 50% representation in community structures.

2 - Activities

In Aweil East, the project will build on current projects and will provide food assistance, vegetable seeds and tools as well as training on gardening and processing of vegetables as well as gender training and awareness. Duk is classified as IPC 4 and Duk, Twic East, Uror and Aweil East Classified as IPC 3.The project will undertake the following activities across the three locations:Seed Fairs Support using locally sourced seed (6400 households) as well as seed security & multiplication strengthening: (33,311 households) will be targeted for training on seed multiplication initiatives, using the lead farmer approach across Uror, Twic East, Duk and Aweil East.Distribution of seeds and tools (40,911 households).This activity will be conducted in Twic East, Duk, Uror and Aweil East complementing existing projectsEmergency food production - Fishing Kits, Vegetable gardens, farmer training targeting 44,045 households in Aweil East, Twic East, Duk and Uror. The targeted HHs will be trained on Crop and vegetable production, post-harvest practices, storage and value addition. Each HH will receive either a crop, vegetable and ﬁshing kits across Uror, Twic East and Aweil East. Unconditional in-kind food assistance – Across Aweil East, this activity will target 1,200 householdsConditional and Unconditional Cash Distribution with food security/ nutrition objectives (26,760 households) in three locations, Twic East, Uror and Aweil East. Distribution will target 18,100 households for conditional cash distribution and 8,660 for unconditional cash distribution As part of Tearfund’s continued risk mitigation approach, a comprehensive analysis will be conducted and periodically updated in all the project sites. Tearfund considers seasonal risks such as access during the rainy season and the security risks associated with the dry season. In order to mitigate this, items will be prepositioned where possible, and procurement will be done in the dry season. Appropriate measures will be taken to secure warehouses. Conversely, environmental risks such as flooding, disease outbreak/crop interventions have also been considered. Tearfund will work closely with FAO and other NGOs to ensure timely detection and reporting to the appropriate focal points. Additional emphasis will also be placed on diversifying the inputs provided in order to mitigate the risk of crop failure. Tearfund’s integrated approach to programming also adds a layer of security, as Tearfund in present in the operational areas, carrying out WASH and Nutrition interventions, as well as working through local partners. This has greatly enhanced the level of community acceptance.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

Yes it does we consult regularly community and community stakeholders and throughout the project they are involved in project design. At the beginning of each intervention, Tearfund provides the whole community with information about the project, including beneﬁciary targeting, locations, feedback mechanisms and expectations of Tearfund staff and volunteers. This is frequently re-iterated through community discussions and focus group discussions. Targeted communities are also made aware of the timing of key project activities and the locations. Feedback on these decisions is welcomed at the project design stage and throughout. The information is further disseminated by Tearfund's network of trained community volunteers, who are embedded within each village. Tearfund also employs Accountability staff who are solely responsible for community engagement.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

Yes, Tearfund involves community stakeholders and leaders, and the organisation has we internal systems that keep us accountable and impartial to both government and community systems

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

The project is linked to national objectives such as developing a realistic national plan for a sustainable economic development with emphasis on agricultural development, food security and disaster preparedness which includes building the capacity. This too is done in consultation and with the inclusion of local and national leaders and stakeholders. The project is also aligned to National Frameworks such as the Social Protection Framework as well as the National Refugee Return Framework in instances where Tearfund will work with IDP’s

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

While the environment is relatively safe, the proposed project takes into account the different social and cultural inequalities that exist within the project locations, particularly with regards to the roles and responsibilities of women as well as the risk associated with the implementation of the project. The project will prioritize women and other vulnerable groups, while also applying the Transforming Masculinities approach throughout project activities, aimed at changing the way society views women and girls as well as the disabled.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

To ensure sustainability Tearfund has considered integrating several elements within this project as follows:-Linkages to Lead farmers will be a coherent part of the exit and sustainability strategy. This project has been designed in a way that it rests on a bridge of Lead farmers- identified on-farm men and women with remarkable capacity and innovation to lead peer learning and transfer knowledge across the communities where the project activities are implemented. Evidence from our past projects show that this working model helps to facilitate relatively seamless and community-owned phase-out, and will be a part and parcel of all activities occurring related to agronomic training and support. Also, we will use an approach of inclusive training. Community members themselves will be trained on basic vegetable production and sustainable fisheries practices (such as disease identification and control). In this way, extension workers are only required for more severe or emergency contexts. And lastly, working with government extension workers at the Payam levels will be a strong component, aiming at facilitating enhanced responsibility over community service delivery, as well as putting into practice joint decision-making structures involving community members, to establish new norms of accountability and direct communication.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

This project fits within Tearfund’s Quality standard on accountability and is reflected as a cross cutting theme throughout the project cycle. The project will work with the existing Accountability to Affected Population committees to advocate for and conduct Complaints and Response Mechanism processes. Specifically, the AAPs will be trained on complaint, feedback and accountability processes, given an orientation on prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse and they will follow through to record any forms of abuse, the project will mainstream GBV activities including orientation of staff and volunteers on zero tolerance for exploitation and undertake safety audit of the distribution points, training venues, demonstration sites. During implementation phase, beneficiaries will be encouraged to participate in decision making, choice of beneficiaries’ selection criteria, and adjusting project strategies if required. Information about the project will be provided publicly in the local language and other vital information such as beneficiary selection criteria, entitlement and roles as well as a feedback mechanism for suggestions and complaints will also be presented in written formats and communicated verbally. The project will employ practical accountability measures like use of suggestion boxes and hotlines to report allegations. Tearfund will ensure that targeted communities are aware of all feedback and complaints mechanisms and the locations of feedback boxes during community sensitization meetings, and at the beginning of kits distribution. Tearfund staff are trained on how to handle feedback ensuring anonymity and confidentiality where appropriate, and pass on any concerns to relevant staff members. Any feedback regarding critical or sensitive protection issues will be referred directly to Tearfund Executive Team in Juba, investigated appropriately and responded to according to Tearfund’s Safeguarding Policy.
