Project Summary

Since the crisis escalation in late 2017, the North-West and South-West regions (NWSW)
have experienced a drastic deterioration of living conditions, putting children and women
at high risk of various forms of abuses, exploitation and violence.
Girls and boys are the primary victims of the current crisis. This translates into serious
violations of their fundamental rights and constant insecurity, causing both visible and
invisible trauma in the short and long-term, separation from their families and limited
access to basic social services such as education, for the past three years. Being direct or
indirect witnesses of violence linked to the conflict, their wellbeing, both physical and
psychological, is fundamentally at stake.
As of September 2020, the crisis in the NWSW has caused the displacement of an
estimated 200,000 children within those two regions, and an additional 155,794 children
are estimated to be displaced in West, Adamaoua, Littoral and Centre regions. Adolescent
boys and girls, children with specific needs and unaccompanied and separated children
(UASC) remain the primary categories at risk among all affected groups of IDPs, returnees
and affected host communities, especially in rural areas and hard-to-reach areas. Lack of
access to basic social services including child protection services remains a key risk
factor especially for children in rural areas, hard to reach areas and most remote localities
such as the ones along the Nigerian border where access and insecurity prevent protection
actors from implementing any program.
In order to protect children affected by armed conflict from violence, abuse and
exploitation, as a priority and main intervention, UNICEF will work on the offer of services
and provide psychosocial support to 340,000 children and caregivers. Psychosocial
support will be provided to caregivers as well when necessary and to the extent possible
so that they are better equipped to protect their children.
As a direct corollary, based on previous years trends, an estimated 10,200 unaccompanied

the project. All the 10,200 children will benefit from case management, appropriate
alternative care arrangements, support and individual follow-up while due to the difficult
access and insecurity UNICEF plans to reunify a minimum of 990 unaccompanied or
separated children with their family.
UNICEF with its implementing partners will run sensitization campaigns with the aim to
reach out to 169,000 persons, with specific attention to parents to pass on messages on
child protection in emergencies, trauma early warning signs for children affected by
armed conflict, effects of family separation, GBV in emergencies and other types of
violence children are at risk of at the community level so that parents and community
members can play an active role in child protection in emergencies.
UNICEF and its partners will also provide support to an estimated 1,200 conflict-affected
children who do not have a birth certificate, as they are particularly vulnerable to various
forms of discrimination and abuse because of the lack of birth certificates.
In urban and peri-urban areas, both in NWSW and outside where IDP children and families
from NW/SW are present, support will be provided to 2,700 families identified as at-risk to
protect children from child labour and exploitation. Support will be provided to children
already engaged in child labour (including WFCL, such as sexual exploitation and
recruitment by armed groups and armed forces) with case management and referral to
multisectoral services.
UNICEF as lead of the Child Protection Area of Responsibility will continue to consolidate
the coordination, build capacities of members and ensure alignment and complementarity
of interventions in the field of child protection.

1 - Besoins / Needs

The crisis in the North West and South West regions has causedthe displacement of an estimated 200,000 children within those two regions. Around 230,000 children are estimated to be displaced in West, Littoral and Centreregions (MSNA). Adolescent boys and girls, children with specific needs andunaccompanied and separated children (UASC) remain the primary categories at riskamong all affected groups of IDPs, returnees and affected host communities, especially inrural areas and hard-to-reach areas. Lack of access to basic social services including CPservices remains a key risk factors especially for children in rural areas and most remotelocalities such as the ones along the Nigerian border where access and insecurity preventprotection actors to implement any program. Physical violence and killing of children arereported. Most importantly, trauma and the psychological impact of the violence is directlyaffecting their emotional and cognitive development and remains one of the highestpriority needs in 2020. Specific data pertaining to children living with disability is notavailable, but they remain one of the most vulnerable categories at risk due to thedisruption of basic social services. Among displaced children, a growing number of UASChas been reported with 46,875 unaccompanied and separated children assisted as of October 2021and recorded against 22,056 UASC identified in 2020) with little alternative care solutions inplace. As a consequence some of these children end up being involved in some of theworst forms of child labor or survival sex to have access to food and shelter. Theprolonged closure of the vast majority of education facilities has had dramaticconsequences on children’s well-being, sometimes resulting in exploitation, drug abuse,physical abuse and, at times, reported use and recruitment of children by NSAGs.Lack/loss of birth certificates as a result of displacement and destruction of homes isanother key limiting factor for children to attend education facilities when securitysituation makes it possible and also constitutes an obstacle to benefitting from theprotection services they are entitled to as minors. Children leaving NSAGs receive nosupport for their reintegration, are stigmatized and risk being arbitrarily killed. Adolescentboys are also more likely to be victims of arbitrary arrests, forced recruitment andextrajudicial executions and are at greater risk of injury or death directly related to thecrisis. As a result, some families limit their sons' movements to eliminate the risk of theirchildren being killed or forcibly recruited. The phenomenon of recruitment and use ofchildren by NSAGs is difficult to measure but rather than forced recruitment, the reportedgrowing interest of young boys (and girls to a lower extent) in joining NSAGs as theycontinue to be deprived of learning opportunities, is worrisome. Children are exposed toSGBV as a direct consequence of the violence but also as a negative coping mechanismfrom families forcing their children (especially girls) into child marriage. The pre-existingdiscriminatory gender norms and practices disadvantaging girls; combined with theweakening of family and community support networks, leave girls and teenage mothers ina vulnerable situation. Girls living with disability are five times more exposed to sexualviolence in the ongoing crisis. Due to lack of documentation, fear of forced recruitment,harassment and arrest of boys and young men, parents often send girls to collecthumanitarian assistance or to fulfill their traditional roles by collecting basic items for thehousehold, exposing them to further risk of sexual violence as they are on the move.Absence of functioning health facilities in rural areas, lack of sexual and reproductivehealth programs and challenges in sharing efficiently information on existing services andreferral pathways are aggravating factors.
