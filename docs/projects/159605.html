Project Summary


According to the Somalia Food Security Cluster, there has been a significant scale up of emergency food assistance since May 2019. 1.2 to 1.4 million people were reached each month from May to June, 1.9 million people were reached in July and planned assistance is expected to continue at similar levels in August and September. The increased and sustained level of assistance is likely preventing worse food security outcomes in many areas. In the presence of assistance through September, most of rural Somalia is classified as Stressed (IPC Phase 2), while Bay-Bakool Low Potential Agropastoral, and parts of Hiraan are in Crisis (IPC Phase 3). This includes 1.2 million people in Crisis or worse (IPC Phase 3 or higher) and an additional 3.6 million that are Stressed (IPC Phase 2). An estimated 2.6 million people across Somalia remain internally displaced. Post-Gu assessment results indicate that most of the 14 major Internally Displaced Person (IDP) settlements are in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or Stressed (IPC Phase 2) in the presence of humanitarian assistance. In most urban areas, mostly stable food prices and employment opportunities have helped to sustain food security outcomes in Stressed (IPC Phase 2) or Minimal (IPC Phase 1). However, households in urban areas in Hiraan regions are facing food consumption gaps and are classified as Crisis (IPC Phase 3), driven by the high cost of living and limited income-earning opportunities. In the absence of assistance during the October- December period, food security is expected to deteriorate in most of northern and central Somalia and some parts of the South. Crisis (IPC Phase 3) outcomes are expected to be widespread in northern and central agro pastoral and pastoral livelihood zones, as distressed sales of livestock will outpace births and result in stagnant or declining livestock herd sizes. However, Emergency (IPC Phase 4) is most likely in Guban Pastoral livelihood zone, as births will not occur until December-January, and in Bay-Bakool Low Potential Agropastoral livelihood zone, given the severity of Gu food deficits. Without sustained humanitarian assistance, 2.1 million people will face Crisis or worse (IPC Phase 3 or higher) outcomes. An additional 4.2 million people are expected to be Stressed (IPC Phase 2), bringing the total number of people facing acute food insecurity to 6.3 million. Current levels of humanitarian assistance must be sustained through December in order to prevent anticipated food security Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or Emergency (IPC Phase 4) outcomes. Livelihoods support is also required for people that are Stressed or worse (IPC Phase 2 or higher)
HIDIG project will therefore, intends to provide immediate access to unconditional and conditional cash assistance to 7,200 most vulnerable women, girls, boys and men in IDPs and host communities, it will also provide capacity enhancement, farming tools as well as farm inputs to these target farmers in Beletweyne and Baidoa as well as agricultural seed inputs and tools to increase their farming production thus improving the nutrition of the target population in Bakol, Bay and Hiran region. The project will provide through cash for work will enable to provide immediate access to household income thus improving the overall well-being of the target communities. HIDIG will ensure to coordinates the foods security and livelihood activities closely with other humanitarian actors and concern ministries at regional and federal level to ensure better information sharing.

Mitigation Measures

This project targets areas with high concentration of IDPs including Baidoa and Beletweyne and pastoralist in Ceelbarde and Rabdhure therefore possible community tensions between IDPs and host communities is a risk for displaced populations who may face animosity from host communities who already feel overburdened and with limited services. With the IDPs living in camps, many of them not legalized, constructing permanent structure or own a land for food security and livelihood center may be a challenge exposing them to lack of comprehensive and timely care. To overcome these challenges, HIDIG will ensure IDPs have access to a comprehensive access to services within the IDP camps by establishing cordial relationship between them and with the host communities and local authorities. Based on the food security and livelihood Cluster Guideline on inclusion, both displaced and host communities will be consulted about needs and assess whether education interventions could cause tension, harassment or conﬂict between the IDPs and host communities.Lack of desegregated data impedes well planned interventions based on needs. HIDIG intends to overcome this by having a need based assessment and disaggregated data so that the special needs of IDPs beneﬁciaries and those with special gender needs are taken care of. Due to poor health and food security and livelihood infrastructure, many centers do not have proper planning so that those with disabilities can easily seek services. However, HIDIG will ensure services provided reach the most vulnerable IDPs and pastoralist in the far areas through identiﬁcation and prioritization so that discrimination and exclusion is avoided. Location, distance and access routes to the cash collection sites and services will be accessible to all members of the boys and girls, including the most vulnerable IDPs children. To support the inclusion of women HIDIG will support training of female members through employment and capacity enhancement on better livelihood. Building community structures including local temporary spaces will provided in consultation with community/member who owns the land to ensure tension is minimized. Staff will be trained on human rights based approach where all people should be treated equally, especially those who ran from conﬂict and violence. Communities ﬂeeing violence will be immediately supported through access to immediate food security and livelihood services without any discrimination.Where IDPs are involved, sites that support them will be built in consultation with them and the host communities. Marginalized groups will be prioritized so that they don’t feel excluded, which can otherwise aggravate their situation if they have run from violence
