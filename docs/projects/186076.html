Project Summary

Northeast Nigeria is facing a landmine crisis as a result of the ongoing conflict between
non-state armed groups and the Nigerian Army. MAG’s research shows that from January
2016 – December 2021 there were 992 confirmed incidents related to victim-activated
explosive devices, explosive remnants of war and other improvised explosive devices, and
1,441 casualties, with over 570 deaths. Over a third of victims were civilians. Nigeria has
one of the highest explosive ordnance casualty rates in the world. Through the provision
of Explosive Ordnance Risk Education sessions to internally displaced persons and host communities in Borno State, and the collection of information on the level of contamination, MAG proposes to continue its life saving mission started in June 2017. In addition to EORE, MAG will also conduct Explosive Ordnance Contamination Assessments through Non-Technical Surveys, Remote Contamination Baseline Assessments and data collection on incidents, accidents, and victims across northeast Nigeria.

1 - Needs

MAG has collated data on incidents involving explosive items from different sources, such as INSO and UNDSS, and analysed it to better understand the situation. Based on data covering the period from 1 January 2016 to 25 November 2021, the database includes 992 incident reports and 1,441 victims (both killed and injured). More than a third were civilian. According to MAG’s incident database, the five LGAs with the highest number of incidents, Bama, Gwoza, Konduga, Damboa and Ngala are all part of Borno State. However, also LGAs in Adamawa such as Numan, Hong, Madagali, Michika, Mubi North and Yola registered incidents while Gujba in Yola State registered a significant number of incidents as well. Surveys conducted in 2021 show men who farm, travel among contaminated territories and collect scrap metal, women and children who collect wood, shepherd and play around are most at risk. According to UNMAS assessments, surveys show more than 40% of the respondents would apply wrong actions when they witness an Explosive Ordnance incident, 12% said they would bring home or move Explosive Ordnance, and more than 70% believe common myths that can lead to unsafe behaviours, such as that burning a field makes all Explosive Ordnance explode and therefore the area is safe.

2 - Activities

Activity 1 - Procurement of Risk Education materials. These materials will be used during Risk Education sessions, including for children, and include banners, posters, and leaflets, in addition to child specific RE materials such as flipcharts, picture and colouring books. For the broader community, MAG will use permanent billboards. For humanitarian workers, materials used consist of MAG’s Explosive Hazard Awareness Aide Memoire. Activity 2 - RE sessions for conflict affected individuals. CL teams will be deployed to IDP camps, host communities, and schools across Borno State to hold RE sessions to reach 54,000 beneficiaries (50% children and 50% caregivers). Each CL team will facilitate at least two sessions a day on average. These individuals are accessed through schools, child friendly spaces and community focal points including LGA Chairmen, Wards Heads, IDP Camp Chairmen, Bulama (and other local leaders), and civil society organizations. Activity 3 - RE sessions for humanitarian staff. MAG will provide scheduled mine and ERW safety briefings for interested agencies at regular intervals throughout the project period. Using established coordination networks amongst humanitarian actors such as sector working groups, the HCT and INGO Forum and government bodies such as SEMA for NNGOs, MAG will effectively ensure that actors are aware of the available safety sessions for their staff. Activity 4 - CL monitoring plan. MAG's Community Liaison Supervisor will closely monitor the progress of the CL teams. Specifically, the monitoring plan will include weekly field visits and quarterly refresher training. Furthermore, MAG employs tools such as surveys to identify changes in RE beneficiary knowledge, immediately before and after RE activities. For MAG, it is vital that impact-monitoring activities remain gender sensitive, taking into account the different effects of contamination on women, girls, boys and men. Activity 5 - Non- Technical Survey (NTS). NTS is practically undertaken by the CL teams using a ‘Dangerous Area’ form. The information collected in these forms is later analyzed. MAG’s NTS activities support wider planning processes by identifying the location, scale and type of contamination that is present, providing data on the impact this contamination is having on local populations. NTS requires access to the location that is surveyed. MAG's ability to carry out this activity will be determined by the accessibility of potentially contaminated areas. Activity 6 - Remote Baseline Contamination Assessment (RCBA). In areas that are inaccessible, and therefore unsuitable to carry out NTS, MAG will gather information on contamination using focus group discussions among IDPs having fled from the same area and by triangulating information that they give on contamination they have encountered before or while fleeing from their former place of residence. This information will be recorded and used as a baseline for NTS once these areas become accessible. Activity 7 - Analysis of collected data and production of contamination assessments and maps. All the information gathered by CL teams will be analyzed by MAG and will be used to populate a global database, and create a contamination assessment per targeted LGA where MAG teams will be deployed.

9.5 - How will your project ensure feedback is collected from the most vulnerable community members?

MAG recognises the importance of facilitating multiple options for local communities to transmit feedback and raise concerns or complaints and offers several different mechanisms by which this can be done. CL staff provide beneficiaries with phone numbers to reach the Community Liaison Manager or Team Leader to report any issues and/or feedback from the community. MAG also provides contact information to Camp Managers so that if beneficiaries would like to raise a complaint but are not comfortable reaching out to MAG staff directly, they have the option of speaking to someone who may communicate on their behalf. MAG sets up this reporting mechanism in every camp location where activities are carried out. MAG also integrates feedback loops in training for Community Focal Points directly, which includes training CFPs on how to appropriately record, respond to and communicate information from the community back to MAG. Finally, MAG seeks to designate both male and female representatives tasked to communicate feedback to MAG, so as to guarantee that women, men, boys, and girls have equal access to effective feedback channels.
