Project Summary

The program’s overall objective is to strengthen resilience of communities in Northeast Syria. Mercy Corps emphasizes an integrated approach that builds households’ economic well-being as well as communities that are cohesive, inclusive and equitable.
Mercy Corps’ Making Markets Work in Crisis approach will drive interventions to increase economic opportunities. Through this approach, Mercy Corps will target market support to key value chains, market actors, producers and local institutions. The specific implementation approach will vary according to the different contexts in Syria, taking into account market robustness, and the constraints. While some of the activities can include simple financial support to key businesses, such as micro, small and medium enterprises (MSMEs) or smart subsidies using vouchers, other activities go deeper, analysing market systems and then providing support that enables both market actors and consumers to cope and recover through the local economy. These interventions may include better access to informal financial services and quality agricultural input supplies. For example, linking traders to bigger suppliers and providing them with better market information or short-run subsidies can reduce their operating risks. This improves traders’ ability to access key goods and sell these goods at affordable prices. Through this program Mercy Corps will address: 1) limited access to economic opportunities and financial services; and 2) the weak and insufficient, but slowly growing, body of private agricultural input suppliers in Syria.
Strengthening cohesive communities is vital to supporting the stability that is finally emerging in NES, and reinforcing post-conflict economic recovery. The combination of improving inter-communal relationships and reducing resource-based tensions will serve to prevent the escalation of some economic-based disputes into violence – for example between host and IDP communities over livelihood opportunities or social service provision – and reduce the ability of political actors to manipulate ethno-religious divisions and mobilise violence. To this end, and in close collaboration with local and neighborhood councils, Mercy Corps will implement community service projects to support local economic development and address resource-based tensions.

Needs

Livelihoods: High rates of unemployment (unemployment rates have raised to an estimated 53%, reaching 75% among youth, and are significantly higher among women), conflict-related loss of livelihood assets, combined with soaring prices, a devaluation of the Syrian pound and disrupted markets, have led to millions of Syrians being unable to earn a minimum income. Mercy Corps’ “The Wages of War” study found that nine out of ten Syrians live with moderate to severe fear of not being able to provide for their families and female-headed households consistently experience higher levels of fear. Two-thirds of Syrian households have lost one or more of their main income sources and two-thirds of households do not have secure access to food. The study also shows that households that have started new livelihoods are better off than “non-adapters”: they have better food security and higher expenditures (a proxy for income), despite similar circumstances before and during the conflict. Moreover, women and youth income earners overwhelmingly report increased self-esteem and self-relianceBefore the conflict, agriculture was a key pillar of the economy - providing work for the majority of the population as well as a regular supply of staple foods. The sector still accounts for an estimated 26% of Syria’s gross domestic product and represents a critical safety net for the 6.7 million Syrians – including those internally displaced - who still remain in rural areas. Due to the conflict, the value chain of many sectors has been interrupted or broken. Insecurity, multiple displacements, destroyed infrastructure, and dysfunctional markets have left women and men without access to land, quality agricultural inputs, and processing facilities, leading to overall reduced food production and loss of income generation opportunities. Even before the conflict, vulnerable people lacked access to financial services in Syria, and a culture of taking out loans from formal microfinance institutions has never existed. Mercy Corps’ Wages of War study showed that Syrians access informal microfinance opportunities through relatives, friends and traditional rotating saving groups (Jamyat). However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no organized program that comprehensively supports access to saving and lending groups. Social Cohesion: Deep divisions within and between communities across Syria are rooted in real and perceived differences across religions, ethnicities, and political positions. Despite relative stability in certain regions, grievances and violence related to the conflict are ongoing. The conflict has affected gender norms and dynamics in different ways across communities and groups; and put significant pressure on the infrastructure and basic systems that were already under stress pre-crisis. Communities in which there is a large concentration (over 30% of the population) of internally displaced persons (IDPs) and/or returnees, experience competition over diminished essential services and livelihood opportunities, and tensions over access to aid. The government has limited capacity and legitimacy to re-establish peaceful coexistence, but there is a critical opportunity for community groups and institutions to mitigate and de-escalate tensions. While it is expected that significant needs will remain for the next few years and that service delivery will not be fully comprehensive and equal to all populations, Mercy Corps believes there will be opportunities for markets and communities to recover.

Implementation of activities

1.1. Vocational and skills training provided1.2. Entrepreneurship supported 1.3. Support provided to rural enterprises and entrepreneurs- Mercy Corps (MC) will consult stakeholders, local market actors and technical bodies to identify potential sectors to be analysed, identify the barriers in market systems, and explore market interventions and potential income-generating opportunities. Based on the findings of the market analysis, MC will offer vocational and business skills training. Building on MC’s evidence-based business skills training curriculum and understanding of market systems, MC will link beneficiaries with business development firms and independent consultants in the local markets. In addition to direct delivery of business training, MC hopes to instead increasingly subsidize its beneficiaries to access the same training and services from the market through these firms. Beyond business skills training, business development services may range from career guidance to providing market information or business development or business growth advice to young Syrian entrepreneurs. MC will support further support young entrepreneurs with business grants.- Based on assessment results, MC will further use different entry points to engage and partner with the private sector to address barriers or to invest in some businesses that show high potential in the market. Mercy Corps will target key business sectors in specific sectors to create greater livelihood opportunities for vulnerable people. At the same time, Mercy Corps will work with vulnerable market actors and smallholder farmers to ensure that they are engaged in opportunities stemming from the interventions in the value chain, with business customers expected to indirectly benefit from investment in value chain businesses or MSMEs- MC will work on both the demand and supply sides of the agricultural inputs market. On the supply side, Mercy Corps will work with input suppliers, traders’ associations and local authorities to ensure supply in the markets is of sufficient scale and quality. In NES, MC will aim to work with the Agriculture Body pertaining to the Kurdish Self-Administration to improve the quality of inputs and establish a certification system. Interventions will be determined based on the needs of the market and consumers and will be adapted to the context. On the demand side of the agricultural input market, Mercy Corps will ensure producers are able to affordably and sustainably access inputs from the local markets. This will include organising trade fairs that link producers with relevant suppliers, providing market information to both producers and suppliers, and providing smart subsidies to producers to purchase higher quality inputs from local suppliers; thus build buying relationships. 2.1. Community initiatives supported-MC will support women and men to establish appropriate community-driven structures for accessing secure and appropriate financial services through culturally appropriate saving and lending methodologies. Saving and lending associations (SLAs) have been used globally in places with limited financial services to enable members to respond to economic opportunities and manage unforeseen shocks. In addition to the direct access to finance that members can access, SLAs provide opportunities for building better social capital, which is critical to income-generation opportunities. - In close collaboration with local and neighborhood councils, Mercy Corps will implement community service projects to support local economic development and address resource-based tensions. Examples of potential projects include: road, irrigation canal, or water network rehabilitation, repairing public infrastructure such as schools or markets, or providing schools with supplies (desks/chairs, education materials).

Protection Risk Analysis

Mercy Corps Syria has rolled out a protection risks analysis tool for staff and partners since 2018, as part of the protection mainstreaming strategies. Noting that the Mercy Corps submission is reflective of protection programming incorporated into several donor grants (often multi-sector), each project and key interventions will have a protection risk analysis. The principle of risk analysis and protection assessment is a corner stone to Mercy Corps protection mainstreaming. Mercy Corps staff and partners will be trained on key principles, staff will be gender balanced and complaints followed up on. The following illustrates the main protection risks associated with this program, and mitigating measures that will be taken by Mercy Corps. Mercy Corps acknowledges that there are a significant number of protection risks that individuals and communities can face as a result of poor programming or programming in a difficult environment. This does not capture them all.1. Risk of exploitation or abuse by staff members/service providers/partners: Mercy Corps monitoring and evaluation systems include accountability frameworks, and Mercy Corps has a beneficiary complaints mechanism. Mercy Corps is committed to the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse and Child Safeguarding through appropriate policies (Code of Conduct) which are enforced through on-boarding and training to all staff, suppliers and partners. Distribution are carefully planned to ensure they are done at times and in places that are safe and accessible to women and girls, as well as the elderly and people with disabilities, and that any barriers to access as a result of gender norms or physical ability are taken into account when planning for the distribution.2. Programming exacerbates community tensions due to perceived discrimination/favouritism: Mercy Corps upholds a conflict sensitive and DNH approach throughout programming. MC utilizes appropriate and transparent targeting techniques, prioritising the most vulnerable and those at risk, ensuring services will be delivered without discrimination, ensuring inclusion of minorities. Mercy Corps has invested heavily in the tools and capacity to understand the conflict and power dynamics of where we work through regular analysis at the of communities through to regional geopolitical politics to ensure all interventions are conflict sensitive and in line with a DNH approach. Our approach is grounded in analysis and, accountability (ensuring transparent and inclusive targeting, participatory design, and transparent feedback and complaints mechanisms. Mercy Corps staff and partners are representative of the demography in each area and have close community ties which facilitates community acceptance and aids accountability. Staff and partners are trained to ensure conflict sensitivity approaches are mainstreamed across interventions.3. Local authorities restrict activities of partners due to lack of support for protection interventions. Similarly, where programming is sensitive to authorities but a priority for local communities, Mercy Corps will endeavour to engage with local actors to increase opportunities for operating. 4. Persons without documents refused assistance: As part of a protection mainstreamed intervention, Mercy Corps does not require beneficiaries of protection or non-protection projects to possess national ID documents. In line with protection cluster guidelines, information can be triangulated using local verification processes. Protection interventions can increase stigma and risk to beneficiaries. Mercy Corps support project designs which mitigate risk to beneficiaries. Mercy Corps also is supporting projects targeting youth and adolescents and persons with disabilities who are historically excluded from target interventions.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Mercy Corps closely coordinates our contingency planning alongside the Humanitarian Access Team (HAT) which is hosted by Mercy Corps. Along with INSO, OCHA and cluster analysis, Mercy Corps operational planning strives to minimise risk to beneficiaries, partners and staff. 2019 is a challenging operational context in northern Syria.Administered largely by the Kurdish Self Administration (KSA), NES contains pockets under Islamic State in Iraq and ash-Sham (ISIS) control. The large coalition presence is signalling a reduction, with the potential to destabilize a delicate equilibrium between conflict actors which has been an enabler of humanitarian access since 2017. Tens of thousands of IDPs remain in camps and informal sites, unable to return to their homes. This has meant that there are questions of operational viability across northern Syria in 2019. Mercy Corps remains in close coordination with our donors and partners for appropriate contingency planning to enable and adaptable programming.
