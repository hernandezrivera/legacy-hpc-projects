Project Summary

Budget Education 2020: 830,000 USD; Cost per beneficiary: 30 USD; 831 beneficiaries with disabilities; The project is 100% funded by different donors. The project started before 2020 and will be extended beyond 2020 only for three schools (PSS training session and targeted events for parents and students will be conducted in 2021 to the same beneficiaries). In 2019, some preparatory activities, technical needs assessments and procurement were conducted. The project is part of a multi-sector integrated program. WASH and Health in schools is fostered. As consequence of more than 40% of Syrian schools being destroyed or damaged during the conflict, the lack of sustained access to basic education services continue to deteriorate with a limited budget available from the Ministry of Education for school rehabilitation and maintenance. Among the many consequences which derive from armed conflict are the psychological and mental disorders suffered from children (and teachers). Moreover, training the teachers to identify and refer children with symptoms of potential psychological, health and nutritional problems contributes to address underlying issues currently undermining the success of humanitarian interventions. The project is in line with the No Lost Generation (NLG) Initiative, the Education Cannot Wait (ECW) Investment for Syria and the Education sector. It follows the first 2 Education Sector’s HRP objectives. Indeed, AAH will provide protective, safe and dignified learning environments through small-scale rehabilitation, including gender-sensitive WASH facilities, combined with the provision of teaching/learning supplies, and student kits. AAH will integrate protection and gender-sensitive approaches to ensure the infrastructures are safe for women and girls, boys and men, and persons with disability. The above-mentioned activities will be complemented by ensuring conducive learning environments through training to the teachers, schools principals, parents and students in psycho-social support, hygiene and nutrition. Thus, teachers will support the psychosocial recovery of crisis affected children, and understand, identify and refer children with symptoms of various psychological and behavioural problems to trauma therapy. Whenever possible, AAH will promote the establishment of a referral pathway for effective management of advanced cases requiring mental health support or of acute malnutrition while providing essentials training for primary healthcare centres’ staff. Ad hoc recreational initiatives will be promoted. AAH will also design and distribute school-PSS kits. The project targets the most disadvantaged population groups in areas facing the highest severity of need, categorized as severe to catastrophic (severity areas 4-6). It gives priority to 26,800 crisis affected children (IDPs, host communities and returnees) and 900 teachers (720 women and 180 men) in 8 schools. The project contributes to the access to education and retention in schools for boys and girls. The project provides a safe place for children to learn and play, provides protection from further violence and exploitation such as forced labour, sexual exploitation and abuse, early child marriage, and recruitment and other life-threatening. AAH has protocols in place to include women, men, boys, girls and persons with disabilities to understand their needs. AAH ensures physical accessibility for children with disabilities, including commuting and moving around in the school environment as well as having safe access to water and sanitation facilities whilst at school. Training to the teachers, schools principals, parents and students in psycho-social support will also address attitudes towards children with disabilities, and how to prepare/support families for them to be encouraged to keep their children in school. Regular onsite monitoring will be carried out and complaints mechanisms are incorporated.

4 - Needs

Hundreds of schools and learning facilities have been damaged or destroyed and an estimated 40% of school infrastructures affected compared with the limited budget available from the Ministry of Education. Those still standing are often used as shelter by the displaced population. At the same time, schools in IDP/returnee receiving communities have limited absorption capacity for newly arriving students causing overcrowding. An estimate one third of children (2.1 million) have been forced out of school, with another estimated 1.3 million at risk of dropping out to engage in child labour, recruitment or for early marriage. More than 5.8 million school-aged children and 120,000 teachers are in need of education assistance, of whom 61% are in acute and immediate need. Overall, the institutional educational capacity has declined, while the psychological distress suffered by many children will likely have profound and long-lasting implications. Indeed, researches revealed the long-term neurological and psychological impacts of children’s repeated exposure to violence in their community, schools and homes. As result, the necessity to increase access to a protective, safe, and conducing learning environment among crisis-affected children and teachers, whereas teachers and schools can facilitate children’s recovery from psychological trauma, disability, and undernutrition among others, having a significant impact on their student’s well-being. Although it was assessed that almost 80% of teachers have not attended psychological support training courses, through a proper capacity building, teachers and school’s staff can identify and refer children with symptoms of various psychological and nutritional problems to proper care services. Moreover, training the teachers to identify and refer children with symptoms of potential psychological, health and nutritional problems contributes to address underlying issues currently undermining the success of humanitarian interventions.

5 - Implementation of activities

AAH will provide protective, safe and dignified learning environments in 8 schools through small-scale rehabilitation, including gender-sensitive WASH facilities, combined with the provision of teaching/learning supplies, and student kits (26,800 kits will be distributed). WASH facilities will also boost hygiene promotion and good practices among children. The above-mentioned activities will be complemented by ensuring conducive learning environments through training to the teachers (expected to be 900), schools principals, parents and students in psycho-social support, hygiene and nutrition. Thus, teachers will support the psychosocial recovery of crisis affected children, and understand, identify and refer children with symptoms of various psychological and behavioural problems to trauma therapy. Whenever possible, in the targeted schools, AAH will promote the establishment of a referral pathway while providing essentials training for primary healtstaff. Ad hoc recreational initiatives will be promoted. AAH will also design and distribute school-PSS kits to 500 teachers. The project gives priority to 26,800 crisis affected children (IDPs, host communities and returnees) and 900 teachers (720 women and 180 men). The project will be directly implemented by AAH. Regular onsite monitoring will be carried out. Complaints mechanisms are incorporated to enable beneficiaries to share their feedback on quality, timeliness and usefulness as well as propose additional suggestions to help improve assistance. AAH has significant capacity for its operations in Syria, with offices in Damascus, Hassakeh, and a presence in Aleppo. A new MoU has been signed in November 2018 with the Ministry of Education for the rehabilitation of schools, including gender-sensitive WASH facilities, and to build the capacity of educational-staff in phyco-social support for crisis-affected children.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

Protection risk: schools could be attacked. Likelihood: high. Impact: high. Mitigation: sector/cluster agreed minimum standards with members and local authorities; regular communication with local education authorities, community leaders, schools, etc.; all attacks will be reported to the Attacks on Education Reporting Mechanism. Protection risk: risk of insuﬃcient safety and health standards at schools, risk of not having enough safe WASH facilities for boys and girls. Likelihood: medium. Impact: medium. Mitigation: awareness raising on safety for students, school staff and organization; Do no harm approach and Code of Conduct. A new MoU has been signed in November 2018 with the Ministry of Education to ensure that WASH facilities rehabilitation segregated by sex and age and accessible to people with disabilities is part of intervention. Students, teachers and other education personnel will be provided with psychosocial support. Protection risk: Insufficient support from, or undue influence by, members of the community or local authorities. Likelihood: high. Impact: medium. Mitigation: AAH engages with community people (both men and women), to support in disseminating information in the communities. Trustful relationships. Feedback and complaint mechanism at community level. AAH staff is trained on conﬂict mitigation techniques. The selection process for rehabilitation of damaged schools follows sector guidelines ensuring all steps are followed in a transparent and compliant way. AAH has an already established country commission to identify and act in case a staff is identiﬁed as perpetrator of abuse and exploitation and act accordingly. Protection risk: increased risk of adding harm to children if knowledge is not applied appropriately. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: Teachers are trained properly with pre and post assessment for validation. Provision of teaching/learning supplies, and student kits. More risks are endorsed in the next section.

7 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Assumptions: security situation allows safe response; access to target areas and beneficiaries is granted; partners are willing to collaborate on timely manner; availability of suppliers and items in the market, prices remain stable; no major displacements occur. Risk: security situation is unstable and/or worsens, AAH has no access; there are additional administrative procedures imposed on D-INGOs, and restrictions on access to beneﬁciaries; lengthy processes to obtain approvals. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: AAH coordinates with local authorities, Ministries, and stakeholders to monitor the situation, to maintain access to target areas and beneﬁciaries, to develop relationships and avoid or mitigate restrictions. Security rules and plans are in place and regularly reviewed. Re-planning of activities to recover eventual delays. Advocacy and coordination with the humanitarian community. Risk: significant devaluation of EURO/ USD against SYP; International bank transfers are not ﬂuid and timely. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: signiﬁcant changes in the value of the Syrian currency will be monitored and, if materialized, communicated to donors and options discussed. Risk: aid diversion, financial fraud, and corruption. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: strict policies and procedures in place for procurement, financial management and risk management; internal controls set up. Monthly monitoring of financial spending. Risk: markets and services are not functional for planned deliveries; procurement of equipment/materials are delayed. Likelihood: low. Impact: medium. Mitigation: diversify procurement according to AAH guidelines. Launch framework agreements or tenders for medium time frames. Risk: any kind of abuse (sexual, power, etc.). Likelihood: low. Impact: medium. Mitigation: compulsory trainings on organizational ethical policies. AAH mission has a policy for PSEA and a reference person against harassment at country level.
