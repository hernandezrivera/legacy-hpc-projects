Project Summary

This project aims to increase access to basic services for the most vulnerable people and ensure responsible transition for prolonged humanitarian needs through durable solutions. Human Appeal will provide agricultural inputs or services to protect agricultural productive assets and help restore fragile livelihoods of returning or vulnerable families. This project will improve access to food assistance for 350 conflict affected IDPs, returnee and stayee families, and it will enable them to cope with the impact of crisis in the targeted locations to provide them with temporary livelihood opportunities through access to Cash for Work. Human Appeal will identify extremely vulnerable rural families in hard-to-reach areas in Hawija and provide them with opportunities for CFW. 250 beneficiaries will be engaged in CFW to rehabilitate agricultural infrastructure (irrigation canals and sub canals) for 40 days, while a further 100 female headed households will be engaged in CFW for kitchen gardens for 60 days.

Needs

The crisis in Iraq has left over 2.3 million IDPs and 3.5 million returnees in extremely vulnerable situations with very limited access to humanitarian assistance due to fragile security situation and access-related challenges in Hawija. Social tensions continue to rise, particularly in areas in which returnees and remainee communities face economic or social hardship. Displacement and its subsequent economic repercussions upon host communities directly contributes to conflict between returnee, IDP and remainee communities, according to a recent impact assessment conducted by Human Appeal in both regions. Debt is growing among displaced/returnee communities, as well as Iraqi society as a whole, creating greater reliance on a diminishing social protection floor. The ability of the protracted returnee population to find sustainable job opportunities continues to be severely limited, with income generation remaining among one of the top needs of those populations. Livelihoods needs often lead to social tension erupting in conflict. Assessments have shown that returnee, IDP and remainee community populations which are unable to address their needs or which struggle economically are significantly more likely to have poor relationships with other communities. This is confirmed by the fact that IDPs continue to face ethnic and socioeconomic discrimination. Where economic grievances may lead to social tensions, specific livelihoods interventions are needed to support vulnerable households. The conflict and civil insecurity have resulted in death, displacement and destruction and had serious impact on the food security of large number of people. Trans-boundary animal diseases and zoonosis are already a threat to the livestock population in Iraq and thus the danger to public health, especially the returnees, IDPs and remainees, is of great concern. Loss of assets and income opportunities together with disruptions of marketing activities, transport networks and farming have exposed large number of people to severe food insecurity; the significant number of returnee/IDPs is also putting high pressure on remainee communities. Immediate food assistance is urgently required to mitigate the impact of the crisis and avert further deterioration. As a result, due to the fragile security situation and access constraints; humanitarian response is reportedly very limited in all areas of Hawija, humanitarian aids provided after the district has been retaken by ISF is reportedly very limited to locations where the security enable safe aid delivery.HA has been monitoring the situation in Hawja from its well-established bases in Tikrit. HA Rapid Needs Assessment and Protection teams were deployed to pre-identify the urgent needs of IDPs, returnees and remainee communities in the targeted locations and collect the relevant data accordingly. Teams were composed of focal points for different sectors mainly protection, WASH, shelter, food security, education, access and security, in coordination with the relevant local authorities, ISF and PMF units. The assessment methodology included direct observation, key informant interviews and focused group discussion with targeted population group. The assessment missions have identified the following humanitarian needs: Around 70% of the markets in Hawija Markaz, Al-Riyadh and Al-Rashed are fully destroyed due to air strikes and heavy fighting took place near these markets. The survey shows widespread unemployment after displacement. More than 93% of all persons are jobless after the displacement, while 87% of them are currently without any source of income. Those with a job are often in low status and casual employment, where most of them have a daily work. The average income of the workers equals 4-10 USD/day. It is estimated that an average sized family could expect to spend around 53 USD per week to cover expenses of families’ main needs: Food items, Non-Food items, health care, transportation and buying water in priority. The majority of respondents reported their inability to find work and their reliance on savings and charity as their principle sources of spending. In fact, some FHHs reported total reliance on the charity of others in order to cover the expenses of their displacement. 87% of the respondents reported the lack of food commodities, i.e; wheat flour (81%) – rice (92%) – vegetable oil (68%) - Vegetables (82%), various beans (69%), and sugar (71%). It was also reported that very low number of local charities and actors are assisting at this level after MoMD and Sunni endowment assistance been delivered.

Protection Risk Analysis

1. SecurityThe security situation in Hawija district remains unstable. This poses a risk to both beneficiaries and NGO staff during transport to project locations and during project activities. 2. Mine ContaminationProject activities, particularly rehabilitation of irrigation canals, will be carried out in areas which have previously been contaminated with mines and therefore pose the risk of exposure to mines and UXOs. 3. Social CohesionCash transfer projects pose a risk to social cohesion due to grievances between or within communities, particularly from members of the communities who have not been selected for assistance.4. GBVProvision of small business grants or work opportunities may subject females to the risk of GBV, particularly by male members of their family5. Work-related InjuriesCFW activities, particularly those involving rehabilitation of irrigation canals, involve strenuous work with exposure to the elements, which pose a risk of injury or exhaustion6. Child ProtectionDue to vulnerability of older heads of households, project activities pose the risk of engaging members under the age of 18

How is your project accountable to affected population

The project team will ensure mainstreaming of Accountability to Affected Populations throughout the project cycle including (not exhaustive of) the following elements: 1. Consultation and participationDuring the initial phase of the project cycle, BBCM and FGDs will be conducted with affected communities. We will run separate training sessions for men and women. The different groups present in the community will be identified (eg by ethnicity, religion, age, gender, sexual orientation etc.) to ensure that the protection concerns are addressed. For example, key staff members will have both male and female representation. Where appropriate, separate groups can be established for women, while any women who are comfortable to attend the men’s group can act as a liaison. In the absence of this opportunity, the trainers and government representatives can act as liaisons. We will hire a driver to transport vulnerable people to and from the site of consultation meetings to ensure all sub-sections of the community are represented. Community sub-sections will be consulted in the establishment of mechanisms for CwC including information sharing and CFMs. This will ensure that mechanism are context appropriate and accessibly by all members of the affected population, particularly the most vulnerable. BBCMs will nominate PICs who will participate in project implementation to ensure the needs and concerned of the community are addressed. 2. Establishment of feedback mechanismsThe MEAL team has developed an effective feedback and complaints mechanism, and the team includes one dedicated accountability assistant to receive and communicate feedback and complaints through a toll-free number. In addition, Human Appeal will establish appropriate communication pathways and Complaints and Feedback Mechanisms (CFM) using FGDs, surveys, interviews, and other identified appropriate means of communication with communities. More than one pathway and CFM may be selected. This activity will be repeated regularly to ensure that communication channels remain appropriate, and that communities are aware of how to utilise them. Human Appeal will communicate and display information about the project, including information about the complaints and feedback system. The team will also establish complaints mechanisms at community centre or at community gatherings, in order to adhere to IASC’s Commitments on Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) and the Core Humanitarian Standard (CHS), particularly commitments 4 and 5, to ensure the project is based on communication, participation, and feedback, as well as to ensure that complaints are welcomed and addressed.
