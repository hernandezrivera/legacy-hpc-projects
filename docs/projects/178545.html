Project Summary

Shafak, in line with education cluster’s objectives and in coordination with involved authorities as well as targeted communities, intends to focus on achieving two main goals for a total of 21,856 school children (age, gender and disability disaggregated):
- scaling up access to formal and non-formal education for Syrian crisis-affected children
- enhancing the quality of formal and non-formal education within a protective environment
Access strategies will include small-scale rehabilitation of damaged schools and establishment of other safe temporary/alternative learning spaces through a gender-sensitive and disability-friendly approach. It is critical to improve the access to quality education services for IDPs in camps and within communities and ensure that these services are sufficiently linked to Child Protection interventions
Formal education activities in 28 schools targeting approximately 17248 students (8107 boys and 9141 girls) through classroom learning modality (with plan B to resort to distance learning modality, based on developments of COVID 19). This intervention will minimize the risk of students dropping out, where monthly incentives to teachers will be provided, as they don’t have any alternative fund in addition to provision of education materials for students and teachers and providing schools with necessary operational costs (including heating for winter and school safety equipment).
As education is one of the very basic children’s rights Shafak is planning to provide access to education for children with disabilities by providing transportation services and improve school environment by provide targeted assistance within the schools in order to encourage these children to continue their education and integrate them with their peers.
Non-formal education opportunities and pathways back to inclusive education at age appropriate levels are increasingly required to address the complex learning needs of children through classroom learning modality (with plan B to resort to distance learning modality, based on developments of COVID 19), making use of 4 BTL campaigns conducted for that end. Thus, non-formal education will target school girls and boys (aged 8-12 and gender disaggregated) to fill in existing needs, where 12 Non-Formal Education centers will be supported targeting approximately 4608 children (2166 boys; 2442 girls) to provide literacy and numeracy along with PSS sessions; the PSS activities will be selected from I-Deal curriculum; the Curriculum used for Non-Formal Activities will be Curriculum B, that combines 2 academic years in one or, the Self Learning Programme (SLP).
Children and youth accessing formal and non-formal education are not experiencing quality teaching, and there are growing concerns that the toll of the crisis in its many forms is preventing children from attaining foundational literacy and numeracy skills as well as those skills relevant to cognitive, social and economic empowerment. Hence, teachers will be equipped with the skills in non-formal education modalities, child-centred pedagogy, and positive classroom management to cope with overcrowded classrooms, address and accommodate host community, displaced and returnee children within their classes.
Stakeholders in the project implementation site, including children, adolescents and older persons, including PwDs and their opinions are taken into account while developing the project design, implementation of activities so that they have better access to humanitarian services and information in accordance to their specific situation.
The project will make sure that children engaging in child labour or recruited for military purposes, or living with older or disabled caregivers or adolescent girls at risk of early or forced marriage are given special attention and have their own space to participate in the project activities and are properly referred as per Protection cluster guidelines.

4 - Needs

In the northwest, 280,000 children have had their education cruelly snatched away. And an estimated 180 schools are out of operation — destroyed, damaged or being used for shelters. Another blow to children’s hopes and futures. The nine-year war has decimated public services, … three out of 10 schools, are non-functional, and one in three Syrian children is out of school. (UNICEF Executive Director brief to the Security Council on the situation of children in Syria, 2020)After ten years of conflict, it is critical that formal and non-formal education services are approached as a long-term investment by not only children and their families but by aid actors. COVID-19 and worsening economic conditions have exacerbated the consequences of systemic underinvestment in education service delivery. The consequences of this will continue in 2021. The reach of non-formal education will continue to be limited. Education capacity and effectiveness will continue to be constrained by insufficient numbers of paid teachers, lack of teaching and learning supplies and a lack of continuous professional development for teachers and other education personnel. (Syria HNO, March 2021) Priority gaps include in establishment and expansion of new learning centers and WASH facilities, as well as learning supplies. Where most schools are operating either fully or partially, partners are continuously prioritizing COVID-19 mitigation measures such as providing additional learning spaces, sterilization and awareness raising. (OCHA, Situation Report #. 26 - As of 26 March 2021).Shafak will be conducting very specific need assessments to reflect the estimated need as explained by these reports.

5 - Implementation of activities

The intervention is designed to provide quality education services to Syrian conflict-affected children who most vulnerable inside Syria, by proposing activities within areas with high density of IDPs and HCs. To do so, Shafak will make sure to involve stakeholders, including children, adolescents and older persons, including PwDs and their opinions in the implementation plan and that their voices are heard and taken into account while specific needs assessments are conducted and while implementing activities, so that they have better access to humanitarian services and information in accordance to their specific situation. These consultations include focus group discussions with participation of different target beneficiaries, including stakeholders such as teachers, school management, local authorities, and students themselves (with age and gender taken in consideration). Through these FGD, Shafak looks to identify particular needs as highlighted by the various groups and look at addressing these needs in the project design and implementation phases as possible. Other crosscutting issues such as GBV protection, child protection from violence, exploitation and abuse, and rights of vulnerable groups will be mainstreamed in the project. Shafak will start with validating the needs in the target schools through field surveys to reassess the needs. By these surveys, Shafak will identify the exact schools’ needs, children (girls and boys) and teachers (females and males) numbers in the proposed schools. This will offer an opportunity to know the number of schools, children and teachers available for contributing in the project.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

1. Risk: Existing traditional practices and community structures conflict with child protection principles (e.g. corporal punishment, child rights messaging is perceived as a threat, etc.). Mitigation: - thorough contextual analysis to be undertaken and ensure safe access as part of the project design; - teachers are trained on positive discipline techniques as part of their basic training and sign a code of conduct in the school; - teachers and classrooms are regularly monitored; a child safe-guarding focal point is identified in the school; - Parent-Teacher Committees are consulted on any reports from their homes of children being mistreated;2. Risk: Target areas are inaccessible or insecure because of advance of armed groups and/or aerial shelling, or that staff or beneficiaries may be injured, killed or kidnapped by armed groups during implementation of activities Mitigation: - Monitoring of the security situation; extensive acceptance measures to sustain good relations with communities for continuous understanding of local context and to facilitate unimpeded access to target areas; coordination for programmatic contingency (shifting resources/funds to other areas) - Minimal transfer of cash through staff to reduce exposure; 3. Risk: borders are temporarily closed, or humanitarian access restricted, between Syria and its neighbouring countries because of national regulations, of advances by GoS towards neighbouring countries' borders. Mitigation: - ongoing advocacy with relevant authorities for humanitarian access to vulnerable populations in Syria. Dividing stock of commodities between Syria and neighbouring countries in anticipation of risk; prioritizing local procurements (inside Syria).

Risk (1): Sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA), including harassment, against women and girls by distributors and male beneficiaries while queuing and before/after distributions Risk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure: - recruit male and female volunteers who are respected in the community and independent of armed groups. No children to be employed (under 18 years old) - make sure staff are already trained on Do No Harm, protection from SEA (PSEA), and child safeguarding; and sign and adhere to code of conduct that respects and uphold the rights of beneficiaries and PSEA - set up communication and complaints mechanisms or desks that are clearly marked; face interviews with beneficiaries, including women and children.

8 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risk1: Occurrence of COVID-19 cases in the targeted communities among children and education personnelMitigating Measure: daily communication and coordination with ED and other Edu and health organizations to know the latest updates about COVID-19. Suspend the schools immediately and prevent any kind of physical attendance at schools and shift into the adopted remote education modality. Risk2: Schools closure due to COVID-19MM: start the adopted distance education modality that Shafak has a good experience in, since the spread of COVID-19 disease all over the world, provide more training and feedback instruction by supervisors and S&S departments to teachers concerning the E-Learning skills to improve their performance with students.Risk3: Child safety incidents in school due to bullying and overburdened teachers MM: Training of partners and local teams on Child safety, development of schools’ specific child safety policies; establishment of complaint mechanisms in school; Security/safety training for organization, students and school staff.Risk4: Protection risks of mixing different age groups in the same centre/class MM: Create separate times of attendance for each age group.Risk 5: Some targeted teachers may receive salaries from a third party, or are forced to get part of their incentives deducted MM: Confirmations have been already obtained from all Education Directorates and education actors that no teacher is permitted to get paid gain from any other party. Moreover, Shafak MEAL team will be checking with all stakeholders in the area to see that none of the above happens, and in case there is such an act, the process of incentives provision is halted, and stakeholders are immediately informed.
