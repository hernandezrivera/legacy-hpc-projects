Project Summary

War Child Holland (War Child) and its consortium of Syrian implementing partners will provide an integrated education response for highly vulnerable children, caregivers and community members in communities in Northwest (Idlib and Aleppo governorates) and Northeast Syria (Deir Ez-zor and Raqqa) across a 10-month action.
Recognising the interconnection between protection and education, the action will increase access to non-formal education and strengthen the protection of vulnerable girls and boys who are out of school or at risk of dropping out, to build their resilience and provide a pathway back into a safe and protective environment in school and within their communities.
The action will include non-formal education interventions including Accelerated Learning Programme (ALP) for out of school children, remedial and homework support for children at risk of dropping out, and structured psychosocial support for adults and children. The action will adopt an integrated approach through developing the capacity of teachers on child-centred learning and enhancing the accessibility and safety of the school environment, while using protection as an entry point to strengthen the capacity of communities to identify, prevent and respond to concerns and ensure that children have adequate access to appropriate services.

Needs

Assessments within the Whole of Syria demonstrate that Idlib and Aleppo governorates in the Northwest, and Deir Ez-zor and Raqqa governorates in the Northeast, have significant areas with a severity rating of 6 for Protection and Education services. In Idlib and Aleppo, the Protection cluster estimates that there are 246 communities with severity ratings 4-6 not reached by any Child Protection actor (Northwest CP Sub-cluster Gap Analysis Overview, November 2018). MWL discussions with Idlib Education directorate have shown the weakness of education services in the selected area, as they quoted that: "The education system is overburdened and overstretched to the extent that schools and learning environments are unsafe, overcrowded and under-resourced. More than 20% of schools are out of service or need rehabilitation to increase capacity to receive the new IDPs children.” In the Northeast, since the October 2017 removal of IS control, 166,305 IDPS have returned to Raqqa city, and 244,655 to Deir Ez-zor governorate, with minimal CP actors on the ground (OCHA NES Situation Report 30). Despite thousands of children beginning to attend GoS schools in NES, a September decree from the Kurdish Self Administration (KSA) barred children from schools teaching the national curriculum. Almost half the 102,000 children enrolled in GoS schools have faced deliberate obstruction to attending (OCHA NES Situation Report 30, November 2018). The analysis confirmed a lack of access to basic education services and increasing protection risks for children and communities. Insecurity and attacks on education have led to unsafe and limited learning opportunities and low quality of education services, demonstrating a need for emergency measures, such as accelerated learning programmes and integrated PSS services. Displacement and its consequences are one of the main reasons children stop going to school. In their assessment, BDC found that 76% of informants noticed an increase in school drop-out between IDP children because of displacement. War Child found that the displaced children are more prone to dropping out of school than the children of the local communities due to such difficulties as integration into the new environment, bullying from children, and discrimination of teachers who cannot adapt to the psychological needs of the children. Barriers to education increased the longer the child was out of an educational environment, especially if there was no special programme for them. Whereas, after the ALP cycle, Kesh Malek found that 73% of the cohort reintegrated into formal school in September 2018. ALP programmes proved to be more accessible and easier for communities to accept. In Aleppo, 82% of parents were happy for their children to go to ALP classes, significantly higher than those prepared to engage with formal schooling. Parents worry about the children’s safety due to the long distance from their home to school. In answer to the question of what prevents children from joining school, 33% of respondents answered for economic reasons, 23% said for security related issues, and 13% attributed that to the distance to school. Among the other factors, the most frequently reported in the War Child Barriers to Education assessment were: ‘lack of transportation” (mentioned by 5% of respondents), and ‘cost of school materials’ (6% for parents and 5% for children).

Implementation of activities

War Child and partners will assist the large numbers of girls and boys who have been out of school for over a year by enrolling them in flexible learning classes – basic literacy and numeracy where necessary, and then most significantly as part of an accelerated learning programme. Children will be offered two cycles of ALP, reaching eight months in total. This will enable them to re-access the education system at the right level after a sustained absence. In the Northwest, the contextualised Curriculum B will be used. In the Northeast, an adapted version of the Kurdish-supported curriculum will be used in Arabic. Where possible, the ALP classes will take place with the formal school environment, to encourage reintegration and avoidance of a parallel system. Where the facilities are unsafe or not available, temporary learning centres will be established and equipped. To encourage attendance and address the security risks associated with travelling to the classes, transportation for those living at a distance will be provided (around 50% of children). The children will receive educational kits before the start of the ALP which include exercise books and stationery. To develop the capacity of the educational system to accommodate and properly support children affected by conflict, the action will include a coaching programme for educational professionals in the target area. In each of the implementation hubs, a network of coaches will be trained and supported to conduct classroom observations and assist teachers with their professional development through a training plan including classroom management, lesson planning, teaching a class of multiple levels, and positive discipline. Where necessary, light rehabilitation (painting, heating, window repair) and upgrading of WASH facilities will be conducted in formal schools. In Deir Ez-zor and Idlib, remedial and catch-up classes will be offered to students at risk of dropping out. To counter the psychological distress of displacement and trauma of conflict, structured PSS activities will be integrated into the non-formal education schedule. Teachers and facilitators will be trained on the War Child-developed DEAL methodologies, Structured Recreational Activity (SRA) tools and Caregiver Support Intervention (CSI). Children will be invited to participate in two sessions of PSS activities per week, appropriate to their age and gender, in a safe and protective environment. Resilience will be improved through an interconnected set of CP and PSS interventions that address both children and caregivers’ needs, and broader community. To support caregivers and teachers to understand and support their children, and address their own psychosocial distress, War Child will train partners to deliver Caregiver Support Interventions, with regular follow-up. Through the modules parents and caregivers can find positive coping mechanisms, identify and reduce the impact on their children, and strengthen social networks to find solutions together for challenges they face. To increase children’s protection against violence, exploitation, neglect and abuse, the schools and learning centres in the project will be assessed against a contextualised CFS checklist and enhanced to meet minimum safety standards. Within these safe spaces, teachers, facilitators and partner staff will be trained on War Child’s Child Safeguarding Policy (CSG), including ensuring each centre has an appointed focal point for children to safely report incidents or any harm caused by our staff. Field staff will also be trained on child rights and key principles of child protection, including information on referral pathways for more severe Child Protection risks.
