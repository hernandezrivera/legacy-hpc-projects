Project Summary

This project will strengthen non-specialist grassroots actors in hard-to-reach areas on GBV response, to extend effective service coverage and awareness in the four target Regions. This will be done through 1) extensive mapping in 4 sub-divisions and linking of grassroots first responders with GBV specialists, 2) Training and coaching on GBV SOPs 3) Grassroots service provision.
This project aims to assist women and girls in 4 conflict-affected Sub-Divisions of Anglophone Cameroon that currently have very little access to Gender-Based Violence and Sexual Reproductive Health services by creating meaningful access to these services. The project strategy targets local stakeholders currently providing non-specialized assistance to survivors of violence, local champions that are supporting survivors in conflict-affected communities, and just need enough knowledge, tools and resources to do it in a more dignified, more effective and safer manner.
Support will be given to these grassroots champions in the form of training and ongoing coaching on international Gender-Based Violence guidelines, psycho-social first aid, case-management and linkage with national and international Gender-based Violence and Protection stakeholders to reinforce the referral pathway.
The project comes from the realization that specialized services are lacking in rural conflict-affected areas, and it targets 4 of the most remote Sub-Divisions of the South West and North-West Region covering all rural Divisions of both Regions.
Outputs:
• At least 88 grassroots specialized and non-specialized first-responders to Gender-based violence are identified and documented in a database and referral map and effectively linked with national and international stakeholders to enhance the referral pathway.
• 88 community-based first responders based in 4 conflict-affected Sub-Divisions understand and appropriate international Standard Operating Procedures and Guiding principles on Gender-Based Violence, the local referral pathway, and Psychosocial First Aid.
• At least 1,313 boys, girls, men, and women surviving GBV receive psychosocial support from trained stakeholders.
• Grassroots Gender-Based Violence first-responders in 4 Sub-Divisions receive, understand and use referral vouchers to support 433 extremely vulnerable survivors of violence to access quality services.
• Support 160 survivors with economic empowerment cash grants and coaching

1 - Besoins / Needs

Cameroon's anglophone crisis was highlighted by the Norwegian Refugee Council as one of the most neglected humanitarian crises in 2019, 2020, and 2021.In the two Anglophone regions of Cameroon more than 400,000 persons are internally displaced, 58,000 are refugees in Nigeria and more than 300,000 are returnees following pendular displacement. At least 3,700 persons have been killed in this conflict, according to public data published by the Armed Conflict Location & Event Data project (ACLED). In September 2021, the Food Security Cluster marked 12 out of 13 Divisions in the South-West and North-West as being in “Crisis” Situation, and one as being “under pressure”. Cameroon has had a problem with Gender-Based Violence for decades: The country ranks 151/181 in the United Nations Development Programme Gender Inequality Index and in 2011 the Demographic Health Survey revealed that 63% of women aged 15 to 49 had been subject to physical or sexual violence.Moreover, women and girls displaced by the Anglophone Crisis in Cameroon have seen their vulnerability increase overnight. In a new environment, more vulnerable than ever to sexual abuse, and often separated from their partners (sometimes by death), taking care of separated children, and in most cases have lost their documentation. In August 2021, the GBV Sub-Cluster reported 1,205 cases of GBV reported only during that month, and 23% of those cases involved sexual violence. Access to specialized services has reduced critically with the shutdown and burning of health facilities, and the closure of some social services, such as delegations of the Ministry of Women Empowerment, Social Affairs, and local NGOs. The humanitarian response has severe access limitations due to intense scrutiny by both parties of the conflict, and the rural areas have very few identified actors that can support survivors of violence.In the search for safety, or forced by the conflict, women have found themselves in farms and forests connected by non-motorable roads and in communities without any basic service, increasing their isolation. Living with the constant fear of military raids, and under the control of Non-State Armed Groups, women and girls find themselves vulnerable to numerous forms of gender-based violence and most especially physical and sexual violence. Perpetrators are harder to prosecute, and the impact of the act is harder to mitigate. Today, a survivor of sexual violence in the North-West and South-West is significantly more likely to not seek or get access to clinical management of rape within 72 hours, than to obtain it, talk less of legal retribution or empowerment services. There are, however, non-specialist, non-conventional actors that could be empowered. Health practitioners who are displaced and operate in the forest communities, faith leaders and traditional leaders who still have access, small health facilities that defy the odds and operate in the middle of the conflict, Community Health Workers and women leaders who provide counsel to survivors. Reach Out has had success in engaging them to create sustainable change in the lives of women and girls, and they are ready to do much more if provided with the necessary resources and training.
