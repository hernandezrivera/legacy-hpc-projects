Project Summary

This project attempts to provide protection and support to vulnerable children and care-givers of remote conflict-affected areas of the South-West Region through case-management, psychosocial first-aid, referrals, and Cash transfers.
The project will focus on children in neglected rural areas where humanitarians lack sufficient effective presence, and will build the capacities of non-specialist, non-conventional actors to build community-based child protection mechanisms. These are health practitioners who are displaced and operate in the forest communities, faith leaders and traditional leaders who still operate in the hard-to-reach areas, Community Health Workers and women leaders. Combined, these grassroots child protection mechanisms will provide case management services to at least 1,184 vulnerable children of Koupe Manegouba, Manyu, Meme, and Ndian.
Children in rural areas of the South-West are being deprived of their basic health, food security, and education rights (90% of pxublic primary schools and 77% of secondary are closed). This project will attempt to contribute to the amelioration of this situation by:
1) Mapping, Identification and training of rural Child Protection Volunteers:
Community leaders will be mapped out during a series of field visits to remote areas. The final list produced will consider gender and ethnic balance. Protection will go through a training on Protection monitoring, vulnerability mapping, awareness-raising, psychosocial first-aid, case-management and the referral pathway. Training modules will be defined alongside the team of the Child Protection Area of Responsibility in Buea.
2) Awareness-raising campaign about child protection led by the protection volunteers. Communication will be tailored to the different age and gender groups of the community.
3) Set up community-based child protection mechanisms: The Child protection volunteers, alongside the project team, will work to set-up permanent structures to support child protection monitoring, awareness-raising about child protection, case management and program evaluation. These structures are gender and age balanced, they need to have 25% of their members from the elderly, 25% of their members from the youth, and 50% of their members need to be female.
4) Mapping of most vulnerable children per community with a scoring system defining critical vulnerability sector by sector (Health, Shelter, Nutrition, Food Security, GBV, early-marriages, Sexual Reproductive Health, Education, Family separation, Documentation, mental health, child labor).
5) Database of incidents and cases. Protection volunteers will present information to the project team on child abuse and trauma in the communities, which will be shared and analyzed with the Protection Cluster. Data Management and protection mechanisms will be respected.
6) A separate database for disabled children in the 4 target Divisions will be constituted and shared (with data-protection mechanisms in place) with the Protection cluster and other relevant partners.
7) Case management, referrals, and provision of health vouchers and multi-purpose cash grants to extremely vulnerable children and their care-takers. Beneficiary children have beneficiary satisfaction micro-surveys adapted to their level of understanding to give feedback about the project. Children 0-5, 6-11, 12-17 will all have different SOPs for the Protection Volunteers and the project team, in order to appropriately serve them.
8) Coordination Meetings. Protection volunteers will meet to discuss progress, sustainability strategy and way forward. International and National Protection partners will be invited and contacts between the grassroots activists and these organisations will be facilitated. The referral pathway at the local level will be analyzed, specific TORs will be drawn and gaps identified and shared with relevant partners.

Besoins / Needs

Violence against children has defined the Anglophone conflict since 2016. Children have been continuously exposed to life or death situations by both sides of the conflict. They have been massively displaced to forest self-settlements and to urban areas, often unaccompanied, many have lost one or both parents and/or are now staying with relatives or family friends. They present critical vulnerabilities in terms of health, nutrition, and education, among others. Young children of Ekondo-Titi, for example, have completely forgotten the use of English, written or oral, after three years, as reported by Reach Out´s Child Protection in the area. More than 209 cases of Global Acute Malnutrition were found in the recent measles vaccination campaign led by Reach Out in this Sub-Division, out of 2,969 children screened. Before the crisis malnutrition was so rare that the health system was not even tracking malnutrition rates. Moreover, this is directly related to other vulnerabilities, such as family separation, extreme poverty, lack of access to health, and food security. Serve as an example one case found in Ekondo-Titi: “These four [malnourished] children are from the same household. They are being taken care of by their grandmother who is unable to farm. These children can go several days without food. “ Living with constant fear of military raids, being apart from their core family members and sometimes being forced to leading households themselves, permanently or for days and weeks. In self-settled camps, children find themselves brutally exposed to the civil war and to several vulnerabilities, including sexual abuse and random killing.With limited access, only local stakeholders can provide comfort and response to the needs of rural children. As of now, most organizations cannot access the remote areas, and even those organizations that are accessing them do not have full safe access, but sporadic and under certain conditions. They cannot offer comprehensive proximity services, but only work at a distance that is safe for the organization but unsafe for the child. It is only local community based organizations, health practitioners, and leaders that can bring meaningful access to services for vulnerable children. Non-specialized service providers in hard-to-reach areas, however dedicated, also lack critical skills and could be harming children, or providing services oflimited effectiveness. To provide meaningful access and a beneficiary-centered approach in the target areas, the technical gaps of front-line service providers and activists need to be covered. The major ethnic groups of rural South-West all display male-dominated cultures. Reach Out has identified 45 cases of GBV in Ndian, and more than 70 in Fako in the year 2019, mostly physical violence. About 20% of the female adult population is illiterate, specially the elderly, as a result of the prioritization of male education in the past. Women marry early and have an average of 5 children. Unaccompanied children are often cared for by female-led households, including elderly women. Children of different ages and gender face different difficulties. Children 0-5 are extremely vulnerable to health hazards, food insecurity, and lack of hygiene and sanitation, their lives are directly threatened by these insufficiencies. Adolescents face the pressure to join Non-State Armed Groups, the lack of economic opportunites and/or education, and the exposure to early sexuality during the crisis. Different strategies need to be used with each age group, in order to meaningfully support them. All age groups will be involved in the project, as they will all take part in the local Child-Protection committees, and therefore they need to be approached and engaged in a gender-sensitive manner in order to meaningfully participate. Children 0-5, 6-11, 12-17 will all have different SOPs for Protection Volunteers and the project team, in order to appropriately serve them.
