Project Summary

The project will target Internally displaced children (IDP) living in and out of camps, their hosting communities as well as returnee children. The target age group will be the school-age population, between 3-17 years, meaning children at the basic level of education i.e. pre-primary, primary, and lower secondary. Support will be provided through both formal and non-formal education. Interventions will include continuity of learning for the three categories of children in formal schools and through the Accelerated Learning Programmes (ALP) in non-formal school for children who are out of school or have missed years of learning. The total beneficiaries will be 200,000 IDP children (104,000 boys and 96,000 girls) in camps and host communities. This target represents approximately 50 per cent of the Education Cluster caseload. Actions will include provision of prefabricated schools/classrooms to address shortages of schools and learning spaces for children, provision of teaching and learning materials, teacher orientation and training, the provision of psychosocial support and life skills education (LSE) as well as community mobilisation through ‘Back to School’ campaigns.

Needs

According to the Iraq Education Cluster, an estimated 2.5 million internally displaced, returnee and refugee children aged 3 -17 years old, including 1.2 million girls, will need emergency education services in Iraq in 2019. UNICEF has noted through internal monitoring and assessments that the needs of children still living in displacement, as well as those who have returned remain high. Estimates show that close to 30 per cent of children in camps and host communities have no access to formal education opportunities. Similarly, about 20 per cent of children in return areas require assistance to go to school. Most of these children have missed some years of schooling requiring support with Accelerated Learning Programme (ALP) and Catch Up Classes (CUC) for them to integrate into formal schooling. Furthermore, in areas such Anbar, Kirkuk, Ninewa and Salah al Din, many schools still require some form of clearing of unexploded devices and rehabilitation for them to be safe learning spaces fit for children. Additionally, issues relating to quality of education are a major concern for UNICEF. A recent Annual Status of Education Report (ASER) assessment conducted by partners on the literacy and numeracy skills of 1,731 children across five conflict affected governorates in Iraq established that less than half (48 per cent) of children aged 14 years old were able to read at second grade level, with literacy rates worsening by age group: 41 per cent of 13-year olds, 33 per cent of 12-year olds, 22 per cent of 11-year olds and less than 10 per cent of 10 year olds could not read at second grade level. To improve quality, UNICEF will continue supporting teacher training and provision of teaching and learning materials. A study by Save the Children also shows that many children are not in the right frame of mind to learn effectively. Many children suffer from the trauma of violence, displacement and loss and are in immediate need of psychosocial support. The issue of teacher incentives has proven a particular challenge in Iraq in recent years; while advocacy is required to lobby government to pay teacher incentives, there remains a critical need to provide interim support that keeps teachers teaching, and children in school. UNICEF may need to provide support until a nationally-led and more durable solution is found. Through the School based Management (SBM) model, UNICEF has observed that the participation of communities through Parents and Teacher Associations (PTAs) and School Management Committees (SMCs), has positive impact on improving learning environments which lead to better learning outcomes. However, according to findings from the Government of Iraq Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey-6 (MICS-6) (2018), only 43 per cent of parents participate in any form of school governing meetings and only 57 per cent had discussions with teachers regarding their child’s progress. UNICEF will continue to mobilise parents and communities to participate in school management through the SBM approach to improve learning outcomes for children. Lack of credible data, government-supported national learning assessments, and lack of capacity for decentralised education planning and budgeting are also major factors affecting delivery of quality education in Iraq including for children in humanitarian contexts.

Protection Risk Analysis

1. Some schools were used by the Army and Military groups as screening sites or warehouses, in this regard, UNICEF continues to work with the Ministry of Education, the relevant Directorates of Education and the local community to advocate for the use of schools for educational purposes only.2. Schools, particularly in the re-taken areas should be cleared from landmines and remnants of war, in this regard, UNICEF continues to work closely with the local authorities and other UN agencies to make sure that all schools are safe. 3. Unpredictable change of educational officials especially in Kirkuk and Ninewa4. Fluid security situation in the newly-retaken areas, UNICEF continues to monitor the security status in those areas by maintaining continuous link with local communities, the relevant Directorates of Education as well as education partners on the ground through periodic reports as well as conducting field visits when applicable.5. Environmental hazards including earthquakes, water crises and flooding, in this regard UNICEF continues to advocate and work closely with the Ministry of Education, the relevant Directorates of Education and UNICEF education partners to prepare and develop contingency plans in advance to provide immediate response in terms of conducting assessment and providing response accordingly prior to the occurrence of the crisis.6. Continuing national and regional level financial crisis affecting education for IDPs, refugees and resulting in limited allocation to education budget

How is your project accountable to affected population

UNICEF is accountable to women, boys, girls and men whose lives it aims to improve and places this responsibility at the core of its humanitarian policy. Dialogue between UNICEF staff and affected populations is facilitated to ensure effective engagement of beneficiaries and timely information provision. Participation by IDPs and returnee communities enables them to play a role in decision-making processes that affect them through the design of UNICEF education interventions and the review and evaluation of programmes. UNICEF interventions are informed by needs assessment of affected populations to determine the appropriate responses. In addition, UNICEF has a strong working relationship with the Ministry of Education and its Directorates at governorate level and ensures that these bodies remain the key duty bearers, alongside local non-government organisations (NGO) and civil society organisations (CSO), in the identification of services and supplies needed in the local area, and that these bodies remain accountable to the children and families they serve. Directly through its co-leadership of the Education Cluster at national and sub-national levels, coordination with Education cluster partners, and in-field presence of technical staff for periodic monitoring in coordination with government, NGO, CSO, and community-level partners, UNICEF maintains strong and regular lines of communication both with partners and with beneficiaries, ensuring timely feedback into operational and strategic levels of decision-making.
