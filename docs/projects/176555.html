Project Summary

The proposed project seeks to improve the protection of individuals and communities, as well as identify, prevent and address context-specific protection risks. NRDC looks to directly target 2,000 individuals from among the IDPs, Host communities and returnees (women, girls, men and boys) including the most vulnerable populations such as the elderly, people with disabilities, children, women, all being 2,000 (i.e. 1,500 for general protection and 500 for gender-based violence (GBV). In NRDC’s previous response, protection concerns were identified through the complaints and response mechanism and a number of protection gaps were identified including protection along distribution points, distance from households to the distribution collection points, issues around safety and accessibility to markets, Gender Based Violence, access to services, needs for legal aid and counseling thus including community protection interventions in the current project will help to protect internally displaced persons, returnees and host communities from violence and abuse and ensure an enabling safe environment through which the most vulnerable women/girls and men feel safe. The number of returnees in Manyo, Fashoda and Panyikang continue to increase owing to relative peace and stability, with the most vulnerable and affected virtually struggling to make ends meet, now exacerbated by the devastating effects of the COVID-19 pandemic that is precipitating protection concerns, more so gender based violence (GBV); this has exerted pressure on humanitarian actors to intervene in providing emergency Humanitarian assistance, through extending the intervention assistance and also increasing the number of target beneficiaries that will leverage our support among the most vulnerable, returnees, IDPs and Host communities in these Counties. NRDC will continue to strengthen its collaborations and partnerships with local authorities, stakeholders, community leaders, community structures..; these structures will be supported through capacity building actions, including workshops and training in protection, gender sensitivity and inclusion, peace-building, support ownership for sustainability and awareness-raising on protection during COVID-19... In the long term, we expect the communities to be resilient, self-sustaining and able to cope via the assistance of the local support structures established. NRDC will increase its field monitoring and support via regular interactions with community beneficiaries and ensure the response effectively reaches those in need, and local actors are strongly supported to work closely with communities, particularly existing community structures while promoting a holistic approach to integrating activities in line with priority needs of most vulnerable. This will increase efficiency and effectiveness of NRDC to improve programme delivery. NRDC will ensure protection concerns become captured in protection information products, such as assessment reports, monitoring reports, and protection snapshots, allowing for appropriate and relevant advocacy with the clusters, humanitarian partners, and other main stakeholders to mitigate risks arising from the consequences of conflict and the resultant displacement. It is important to note that NRDC is already active in these Counties and has been implementing a range of integrated projects including unconditional cash program, women empowerment through skills training, protection mainstreaming, market support, cash-for-work and provisioning of agricultural and fisheries inputs. NRDC protection programming will ensure evidence-based interventions, at the individual and community levels, ensuring appropriate action is taken to prevent and respond to protection risks while particularly focusing on existing community and government structures. NRDC shall remain vigilant for new protection threats brought about by the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic and will work with partners to address the same!

1 - Needs

OCHA has reported "Some 8.3 million - over two-thirds of the total population—are estimated to need humanitarian assistance in 2021." Food insecurity remains the main concern in the project areas. Needs remain much higher beyond the capacity of the humanitarian response , and the situation has now been compounded by the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic, that continues to destroy livelihoods and precipitate protection concerns, notably GBV. Worse still, OCHA paints a grim picture, that "Some 5.82 million people, almost half of the population, are likely to face Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worsening acute food insecurity in January, including 11,000 people in Catastrophe (IPC Phase 5) acute food insecurity category. Acute food insecurity is driven by conflict, low crop production, cattle raiding, presence of IDPs and influx of returnees, high food prices, restricted market access, poor market functionality and poor road conditions. The weakened community structures have over several years of instability, resulted in numerous protection concerns, which affect different demographics differently. REACH (2019 Fact Sheet) reports that in Panyikang the main protection concerns for boys below the age of 18 are related to conflict. Women and girls face sexual and gender-based violence as the main protection concern, as reported by 81-100% of assessed settlements in Panyikang, 61-80% in Fashoda and 21-40 % in Manyo. In Panyikang, 44% of assessed settlements report the presence of landmines or unexploded ordnance .Social cohesion has been weakened over the past years in the targeted communities, due to large scale and repeated displacement, as well as influx of displaced populations from elsewhere in the country. This has taken a toll on communities, which have traditionally provided protection and support. For example, in Fashoda, IDPs in 38% of assessed settlements report poor relationships with the host community. Competition over scarce resources has divided people, often along clan or tribal lines, but also between neighboring communities. They are less willing to share resources, such as water points, agricultural land, or grazing land and water points for livestock. The expected influx of returnees, as the peace agreement takes hold, has the potential to put more pressure on communities and may aggravate underlying tensions further. While a steady stream of returnees continues to return to their places of origin access to land and housing is still a challenge especially in areas of return, it is likely that majority are waiting to see evidence of full implementation of the peace agreement. Insecurity impedes access to services in many areas . In Panyikang, 61-80% of assessed settlements report safety fears when accessing their preferred water source, while 44% indicate that health services are not accessible due to insecurity and 11% fear for their safety when accessing education services. Even with these security concerns, 41 -60% of assessed settlements in Panyikang, and 0-20% in Fashoda and Manyo, report continued arrivals of new IDPs in the area. At the same time, 81-100% of assessed settlements in Panyikang, Fashoda and Manyo, all bordering Sudan, report the presence and continued influx of returnees. While in Fashoda the main concerns focused on military presence, water shortage, lack of infrastructure and limited access to services, respondents in Panyikang were much more concerned with inter-community and government-opposition conflicts but also with limited access to services. In all areas, early and forced marriage and sexual abuse are perceived as major threats to women and girls, while forced recruitment and lack of job and business opportunities are seen as major threats to men and boys. In all areas, people report not feeling safe at night especially when military personnel are present. Police and chiefs are seen to maintain law and order. Population displacement has been attributed to Sub-national violence and insecurity

2 - Project activities

NRDC's Protection activities, largely general protection (GP) and gender-based violence (GBV) focus on protection risk prevention and protection analysis, based upon identified and foreseen context-specific needs. The actions shall be protection-mainstreaming sensitive, taking into account affected people’s safety and dignity, avoidance of further harm, access to services, accountability, participation and empowerment. The proposed activities are: 1. Mobile and static protection assessments and protection monitoring. The protection data collected will be processed, analyzed, stored, and shared with relevant actors also for accountability reasons. Both data collection and monitoring will be done via community based protection networks (CBPN), monitoring visits, focus group discussions, key informant interviews, observation and population data gathering ensuring participation of affected persons. 2. Community based protection risk mitigation and activities to reduce vulnerabilities and enhance the resilience and coping mechanisms of communities facing protection risks and avoiding further harm. These activities include: community based protection networks and structures that will be trained to identify protection risks and supported to mitigate the risks. 3. Produce and share monthly static protection assessments and reports to inform protection and multi-cluster response and advocacy in the Counties. 4. Form Payam Peace Clubs among women and youth from the host community, IDPs and returnees for ensuring conflict-sensitivity and to support durable peace that will be critical for sustainability of the actions; this will include recreational activities for youths. 5. Establish/strengthen CPCs comprising men and women from the Payams identified; these committees will be elected through community meetings, representatives of the community as much as possible (general assemblies) in order to ensure accountability and credibility. The CPCs will be trained on Protection principles and they will identify the main threats they face, and the corresponding actions to mitigate them. 6.Produce energy-saving stoves through formation and training of women groups, youth groups in target Payams; this is a Protection-cum-livelihood empowerment activity and is part of the risk reduction community‐based protection actions; this will be an avenue to highlight and advocate for GBV concerns! The Project will support the implementation of fuel strategies to particularly reduce displaced girls and women’s exposure to violence, including GBV, and harm when they collect firewood; this is anticipated to be therapeutic and beneficial to their emotional/psycho-social well-being, this activity will provide an alternative income among the women engaged in production and sale of traditional alcohol, thus foster their safety! 7. Establish safe and friendly spaces for women and girls as an entry point for provision of other services including GBV services and referrals, psycho-social support. 8. Create legal awareness to the returnees, IDP’s and host communities on HLP to ensure they are aware of their rights and provide referrals for legal representation where necessary. 9. NRDC will Support legal empowerment for the displaced via provision of information, legal counseling, representation and mediation so that the full range of options for resolution of disputes can be understood by them. This will enable them to determine the process most appropriate for their situation. 10. Support legal assistance for displaced women to engage with all forms of dispute resolution mechanisms available to them at the local level. 11. Train local leaders, law enforcement agents local-level structures and support government and local authorities to ensure statutory law provisions promoting equal HLP rights are applied by customary and religious dispute resolution mechanisms. 12. Cash transfers to selected beneficiaries (conditional cash e.g CFW) and COVID-19 awareness-raising.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

The project has been designed in such a way as to stimulate and promote market functionality. We understand that markets contribute to both food security and livelihoods and are critical features for community’s social-economic survival and progress – even in humanitarian settings, yet they can also be platforms to perpetuate protection threats! Humanitarian actors now generally agree on the critical role of markets in humanitarian interventions! Planned activities such as ensuring market system actors become involved in emergency response, functionality of savings & loans groups, household-oriented market access interventions, cash-voucher transfers, training & capacity building including for on-farm production (farmer/agro-pastoral field schools, demonstration plots), income generation (including business skills), issuance of small grants for petty trade…are all geared towards market functionality! To the extent possible, the project encourages and supports both local hire of staff as well as local procurement (including for some of the farm inputs). NRDC shall remain vigilant about market dynamics and shall participate in planned partner assessments and market analysis – and where necessary, spearhead such exercise! The rationale is such that, to effectively assist the affected populations, a good understanding of critical market systems is important. Indeed, Practical Action (‘The Emergency Market Mapping and Analysis Toolkit’) has cautioned that, “lack of this market analysis in humanitarian programmes may be damaging the livelihoods, jobs and businesses upon which people’s long term security depends.” The design of the project is such that it stimulates market functionality by ensuring affected target groups access food, essential household items, fuel and other types of relief or services; in addition, beneficiaries may be able to access additional and urgently required agricultural inputs and services…..and afford them opportunities for wage labour, or link them to buyers for their produce. The project design is envisaged to result in better efficient use of humanitarian resources, curtail dependency on outside assistance (including for agricultural inputs) and stimulate transition to economic recovery, especially in the post COVID-19 pandemic dispensation. The project takes cognizance of the gender and power dynamics and addresses these in the selection of target groups; as we have alluded to elsewhere, NRDC shall continue to be part of local peace and security initiatives as stability and communal co-existence is critical for market functionality, growth and sustainability. Through other partner-supported, and as part of the market support options, NRDC shall carry out public works projects to clear debris and waste (also through WASH interventions!) using cash-for-work mechanism. Finally, NRDC shall be at the forefront in facilitating links between farmers on the one hand, and resources and service providers on the other (e.g. seed traders, agro-input dealers, transporters, veterinarians, business loan providers….). Last but not least, the various cadre of trainings shall also focus on capacity-strengthening for sustainable market functionality…

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

It is instructive to point out that over last couple of years, NRDC has gained significant insights and capacity in cash-voucher programming through strategic partnerships with established entities like Dan Church Aid (DCA)…... The tradition in humanitarian response, until in the recent past, has been through in-kind support. The use of cash or vouchers is a relatively new, but increasingly growing, phenomenon that is said to have numerous advantages, not least convenience, fostering protection and affording choice to would-be beneficiaries! The use of cash or vouchers in lieu of in-kind assistance now appears to be standard practice among humanitarian actors, and the practice is now popular in food assistance programming, and is increasingly becoming a popular choice in emergency shelter, education, and several humanitarian response options and initiatives! Proponents of Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA) argue that offering beneficiary recipients cash affords them an opportunity to use cash in a manner that they deem fit and is appropriate in meeting their range of needs, that the use of cash and or vouchers enables to stimulate markets (and access items like fuel that otherwise they may become exposed to risks...) and provides the beneficiaries with an assistance that is accessible via a local market system but restricts to a certain extent what recipients can buy or where they can make their purchases. One of the chief benefits of cash usage in the present COVID-19 dispensation is that it can be done through a telecommunications provider - i.e. some money transfer platform - and, therefore, not only help to overcome transport and supplies storage logistical constraints and be hastened/done in a timely manner, but also be compliant with COVID-19 protocols, as it can be cash-less! The usage of cash and or vouchers is particularly feasible and appropriate where markets can be stimulated and are functional. Cash transfer programming enables households to take care of their priority needs (including fuel), is cost-effective, affords beneficiaries their dignity, supports livelihoods, strengths local markets and promotes financial inclusion. However, there may also be some associated negative impacts such as intra-household conflict (for example, brought about by competing needs and priorities, can result in communal tensions and may undermine local markets, but all these can be mitigated through appropriate programme design (For example, ensuring that programming is based upon reasonable price forecasts, ideally using all-season time-series data, instead of prices on the day the calculation was done. Cash transfer-voucher usage can become more effective and impactful if deliberate action is taken to ensure security, guard against corruption/diversion, and mitigate potential delays in providing transfers and market failures. Cash and voucher usage can be done in a manner as to ensure equity and gender equality, ensuring female beneficiaries as well as all those with constraints related to gender, age and other factors that might affect access to assistance become effectively addressed.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

NRDC has the requisite PSEA Policy, complemented with a Gender Policy and a Staff Code of Conduct as a well as an established, fully fledged Protection Department. NRDC develops and or adapts context-specific PSEA outreach and communication materials targeting communities during project implementation, such as the following: Awareness-raising materials (including print materials) that explain core concepts, such as what can constitute “sexual exploitation and abuse’; the obligations of all actors to prevent and address such acts; protect witnesses, victims and survivors and clarify that protection, assistance and access to solutions are never conditional on sexual favours or relationships; Materials (including print, video or audio) that share information on where to safely report and seek help, in line with established complaints and feedback mechanisms (CFMs) and referral pathways; Community engagement tools, such as outlines and facilitators guides to lead community dialogues, role plays or focus group discussions that increase awareness on SEA risks, and the rights of communities and people affected by crises to receive protection and assistance free from sexual exploitation and abuse; and Other appropriate PSEA information, education and communication (IEC) materials, tools and approaches, as relevant to the local operational and cultural context. NRDC also ensures that activities are planned to ensure that the products and approaches developed are suitable for communicating with women, men, girls, and boys of diverse backgrounds, through means that are appropriate and accessible to all groups in a community. NRDC emphasizes some specific standards of behaviour - and at all times - amongst the organization’s staff, volunteers, community committee members (such as Project Committee Members..), consultants, service providers that NRDC deals with, interns…..; this includes both within working hours as well as outside of standard working hours. NRDC abhors sexually exploitative relationships on account of humanitarian assistance and vigorously disseminates information on the same! It is also the case that NRDC makes it clear to all and sundry (during meetings, staff dispatches/memos, public gatherings….) that any violation of the set standards constitutes gross misconduct. Failure to adhere to these standards or failure to report sexual exploitation or abuse is enough reasons for administrative disciplinary action! As standard practice, NRDC also ensures that PSEA materials developed under the various humanitarian responses become “open source” and publicly available for the benefit of other humanitarian actors and communities (for example, through depositing at various strategic locations like food distribution centres, stakeholder workshops and training venues..). We are also well alive to the fact that the project acknowledges that different groups (the disabled, IDPs, returnees, the chronically ill….) within the same community will have different communication needs and capacities. It is important to point out that for purposes of ensuring adherence, NRDC has established a PSEA Policy Steering Committee (under the stewardship of the Protection Program Manager) charged with monitoring and regularly reporting on progress to implement and observe the PSEA Policy; the report becomes available to NRDC’s Board of Directors and through the Executive Director! In summary, NRDC envisages fulfilling its commitment to protection from sexual exploitation and abuse through some strategic approaches as follows: Awareness, Prevention, Reporting, Response and Promoting a culture of openness, sensitivity and support.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

NRDC remains committed to accountability to affected populations and designs responses in a way as to ensure information provision to beneficiaries, as well as ensuring appropriate feedback/ complaint mechanisms. In so doing, we work closely with local structures, for example, fisher-folk and farmers groups, project Management committees (PMCs), women and youth associations and indeed, the local leadership, community volunteers…...NRDC deploys a number of approaches in ensuring community consultation, engagement and information provision, for example, i) Ensuring initial consultation with community members, that is, giving people a say, and getting them involved prior to the of planning a project. This happens in many ways including face-to-face conversations and engagement with their representatives;ii) On‐going engagement through-out the implementation process, indeed ensuring that beneficiaries also become actively involved in activity implementation;iii) Keeping people informed and up to date with activities and achievements through various means, including community meetings. NRDC has the programming practice of assembling project-specific feedback/complaints mechanisms and also deploys the following methods to consult and engage with target communities:-  Local media campaigns – especially local radio such as Miraya FM… Use of leaflets, flyers, posters….sometime with translated versions Customizing the organization website to make it an effective medium of information provision and community engagement; Use of Organization and project-specific Newsletters as well as email updates Deployment of surveys or questionnaires (now can use social media owing to COVID-19) Conducting competitions for project ideas / designs, soliciting and providing information at the same time Holding of Seminars / conferences / workshops (with the project-entry and closure Stakeholder Workshops become popular with beneficiaries… Public meetings/forums/ Space meetings… Use of Case Studies to disseminate vital project information, lessons and profiling key feedback /complaints encountered during implementation Use of beneficiary Comment Cards (e.g. ‘I wish this was’ ) deposited at vantage points like local authority offices, distribution points, training workshops…. Use of Focus Groups Discussion (FGDs) Forums/ User Panels  Scenario planning / community visioning (e.g. ‘Future Search’)… Community led planning often using community facilitators.NRDC also deploys SMS messages, mobile voice calls and individual one-to-one outreach with beneficiaries and also ensures consultations at the start of the Project, and making sure that we are open and genuinely consider people’s needs and priorities.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

NRDC very well understands and appreciates that Feedback mechanisms are valued by beneficiaries as avenues through which they can exercise voice, and indeed, influence the quality of responses! For this reason, therefore, and as standard practice for NRDC, we endeavor to the extent possible to consult beneficiaries at the start of the project design period…including through/during needs assessments, stakeholder workshops and consultative meetings with their representatives including local authorities. For this reason, therefore, and following from this exercise, we ensure the original project design as envisaged is adapted based on the initial feedback. Such consultations and feedback adaptation practices ensure that projects then address need that are relevant for the beneficiaries, for stakeholders and for the context! The Project Steering Committee (PSC), the feedback and complaints mechanisms, community volunteers…..shall all be important structures for collating beneficiaries’ feedback, documenting the same and ensuring it is shared with the Project Team for purposes of adapting project assistance. Through the regular reports filed during the implementation process (weekly, monthly, quarterly….), we shall discuss and agree on modalities for adaptation of beneficiaries feedback. NRDC also often uses Case Studies as part of the reporting process and will, in doing so, also track vital beneficiaries feedback that shall then be ‘ploughed back’ in the continuing implementation process as a means to improve on accountability and quality of the response. Owing to the COVID-19 pandemic, we shall endeavor to be a little creative and will deploy technology, including platforms like social media, to gather feedback and adapt the same for project assistance. NRDC shall continue to deploy established practices and standards, including for example, the SPHERE standards in ensuring beneficiaries’ feedback become adapted to project assistance. By capturing beneficiaries’ feedback through an ongoing process, NRDC shall promptly respond to emerging beneficiaries’ issues in real time and act upon it before the project ends. Due to the fragile and dynamic humanitarian context in which the response happens, there may be some instances that may require us to fine-tune or make bigger changes to the project mid-stream. Some examples of these instances include:-i) Changes in types of interventions for the context we operate in;ii) Changes in the quality of the services provided;iii) Changes related to the needs of beneficiaries that were not previously identified. It is important to mention that (also in line with counsel from the HNO 2021), “…the new reality of COVID- 19, risk communication and community engagement requires continuous efforts to ensure a two-way dialogue with refugee and host communities and inclusion of COVID-19 messaging through various platforms and feedback mechanisms, including rumour tracking mechanisms and addressing misinformation.”

8.11. Mitigation Measures

We shall mainstream peace across the project cycle and ensure local peace education and advocacy. The PMC is composed of men, women and youth and representatives from the local authorities.
