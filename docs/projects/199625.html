Project Summary

The project aims to provide community outreach activities to internally displaced persons (IDPs) and local communities in Maiduguri, Jere, Gwoza, Bama, Konduga, Monguno, and Ngala LGAs who are threatened by improvised landmines or other explosive remnants of war (ERW); security actors, humanitarian workers, and service providers.

1 - Needs

The intense fighting between Nigerian security forces and Boko Haram and other non-state armed groups has left widespread contamination of explosive ordnance (EO), most notably in Borno State – the epicentre of the crisis – compounding the devastating impact of protracted conflict and insecurity. The proliferation of landmines and EO plays a substantial role in the further destabilization of the region and presents a major threat to civilians, military personnel and humanitarian aid workers. Furthermore, due to the ongoing conflict, no coordinated mine clearance activities are being conducted in the region.The deteriorating security situation has prevented systematic survey of EO contamination, and therefore data remains limited and incomplete. To fill this need for mapping contamination profiles, MAG has been collecting data on incidents and accidents involving explosive ordnance across the Northeast Nigeria since January 2016. The data is staggering: from January 2016 to April 2021, MAG has recorded 1,316 casualties, including 816 injuries and 500 deaths, equating to an average of 20 people killed or injured every month. Even more shocking is the sharp increase in casualties reported so far in 2021, averaging nearly two people per day between January and March. Given the intensity of conflict and the confirmed new use of landmines, inadequate or absent medical facilities and the lack of reporting systems, it is certain that casualties from landmines and other EO are going unreported, likely in significant numbers. Nigeria is now one of the top five countries globally for landmine casualty rates. An UNMAS scoping mission of the BAY states in 2017 noted widespread use of victim-activated pressure-plate explosive devices along the main supply routes, which were configured to detonate from the weight of a person and therefore function as anti-personnel mines. The assessment found that Borno State was the most severely impacted, with civilians reporting the presence of victim-activated devices in 76% of Local Government Areas (LGAs) in Borno; 59% of LGAs in Yobe; and 52% of LGAs in Adamawa. This is consistent with MAG’s data, in which the majority of accidents were recorded in Borno State, where MAG has focussed its Explosive Ordnance Risk Education and survey activities.There is evidence of significant new use of locally manufactured landmines in the last few years. Landmines of an improvised nature, which are particularly used to target security forces, have been utilised extensively by Boko Haram and other armed groups, and they make up the majority of devices recorded by MAG through survey assessments and desk review. Contamination from conventional weapons (including unexploded rocket-propelled grenades, projectiles, mortars, grenades, and air-dropped weapons) have also been found, as a result of clashes between government forces and non-state armed groups as well as from air-raids.The current capacity to address the contamination is hindered by the absence of a national mine action authority, limited explosive ordnance disposal teams and the lack of technical capacity among the Nigerian army and police. That said, the Nigerian government has made important steps towards compliance with the Anti-Personnel Mine Ban Convention (APMBC), and MAG is currently assisting the Nigerian authorities in its efforts.
