Project Summary

NRC’s project will contribute to Education sector HRP objectives 1, 2 and. NRC will deliver this project through a combination of direct, partner-led and hybrid modalities. Partner names have not been included in this submission for reasons of confidentiality
The Education intervention forms part of NRC Syria’s integrated response. The Education team will continue working in close collaboration with the ICLA, Shelter/WASH, and Livelihoods and Food Security (LFS) programmes to enable conflict and displacement-affected populations to meet their basic needs, enjoy their rights, and benefit from pathways to durable solutions. We must ensure pathways back to learning and integration into formal education and acknowledgement of previous learning through supportive policies (accreditation, certification, examinations, and documentation). NRC will respond to the diverse problems and needs faced by vulnerable children and youth across Syria through the delivery of its three-pronged Child Education Pathways (CEP) and Youth Education Pathways (YEP) programmes. Each response modality (Emergency, Transition, and Recovery) is designed to ensure that conflict and displacement-affected children and youth are provided with age and context-specific educational pathways. All three modalities have been designed to provide equal access to and to welcome all out of school children and youth, prioritising those that have been displaced by the conflict and are returning home from both inside and outside Syria. All three modalities include capacity development opportunities for teachers, trainers, facilitators and coaches and will be implemented in cooperation with the ICLA, Shelter/ WASH and LFS programmes and partners.
The target group of the Education Programme in Syria is defined as conflict and displacement-affected children (aged 6-17) and youth (aged 15-18), including Syrian IDPs, IDP and refugee returnees, host community members, and Iraqi refugees. The exact target group definition will vary for the different response options:
• Emergency Response activities will target newly displaced and returnee children (6-14 years) and youth (15-18 years);
• Transitional Response will target out of school children and youth in contexts of long-term displacement and prolonged absence from school with activities tailored on their specific context (see more below);
• Recovery activities will target vulnerable populations living in post-conflict and/or stabilised non-camp settings (see more below).
Teachers, facilitators and trainers will also be targeted across the 3 response options, working in NRC programmes in formal and non-formal education settings. Whilst equal access will be provided to both male and female educators, considering the current scarcity of male educators, those present will be actively encouraged to participate, in an effort to provide positive male role models in all activities.
For all NRC Education activities, civil documentation will not be a pre-requisite to receive assistance.

Needs

Eight years into the Syrian crisis, 6 million school-aged children and education personnel are in need of support. 1.85 million school-aged children are internally displaced and 2.1 million children remain out of school and disconnected from learning and their peers (2019 HNO Draft). It is estimated that the 2.1 million out of school children and youth will cost Syria more than USD 11 billion in lost GDP. A cost that will be felt not only by individuals who are now known as ‘the lost generation’ and that will take decades for the Syrian economy to recover. 63% of school aged children in Syria are in GoS controlled areas. It is estimated that more than 987,000 people (mainly IDPs) have returned to their areas of origin between January and August 2018. Children, their families, and education personnel need access to PSS and other specialized protection services. Exposure to violence decreases attendance, leads to drop out, and affects learning outcomes by limiting concentration. IDP and returnee children are often not welcomed for the additional strain they place on educational resources already stretched and an education system no longer able to cope, resulting in an increasing number of children and youth disconnecting from learning. The Syrian crisis has had a devastating impact on both the education opportunities available and the quality of those opportunities; it has left a generation of children and youth unprotected, disconnected and unable to access their right to learn. Authorities, community members, parents, children and youth clearly identify that the most significant contributing factors that prevent access to safe and protected learning environments are security and poverty. These two factors result in family members choosing negative coping mechanisms that in normal circumstances would never be considered. This negatively impacts not only the ability of children and youth to access learning opportunities, but also, for those who manage to enrol and attend, to focus on their learning.With 1 in 3 schools destroyed or damaged, under attack and/or used for military purposes (2019 HNO Draft). Schools in IDP/returnee receiving communities have limited absorption capacity for newly arriving students, causing overcrowding. Shortage of qualified teachers also limits the quality and provision of education. Distances travelled to access learning are increasing. Many communities do not have functioning transportation services and most families do not have the resources to access the services that are available. Children face multiple risks whilst walking to school, including gender-based violence and recruitment. Furthermore, Eighty-two percent of communities surveyed by OCHA in 2017 identified that child labour, recruitment, begging and scavenging exist in their communities. Some families report that female children are often married early to reduce the financial burden and responsibility on families and ‘to keep them safe’. 310,610 education personnel are estimated to need assistance and 180,000 teachers are no longer in service, leaving schools in Syria both led and managed by unqualified teachers and understaffed. In a system that is stretched and under resourced, the most vulnerable children are those with disabilities. Previously available specialised education opportunities and personnel have all but disappeared, resulting in increased isolation and discrimination. Youth and adolescents (10-24 years of age) make up twenty eight percent of the population, with the highest percentage living in Damascus (40%), Homs (35%), Lattakia (30%) and Quineitra (29%). Whilst 24% of these youth and adolescents are receiving at least one humanitarian service, many Syrian youth’s needs remain unaddressed. Data regarding what those needs are is limited and often unavailable.

Implementation of activities

EMERGENCY RESPONSE: During the initial phases of an emergency it is essential that children and youth remain connected, protected and have the opportunity to learn. CEP-Emergency and YEP-Emergency: a 1 to 20-day programme designed to provide safe and welcoming spaces for children and youth when newly displaced. The programme includes life skills, psychosocial skills and recreational skills for pupils and educators and provides a space where children and youth can connect, make friends, discuss age-appropriate issues and concerns. TRANSITIONAL RESPONSE: It is important that all children and youth are provided with flexible pathways to encourage a return to learning and ultimately a return to school. These pathways provide age-appropriate, relevant, quality learning where the knowledge, skills and attitudes required to be successful in learning can be both acquired and practised. CEP-Transition: A non-formal learning opportunity delivered in community learning centres or through Mobile Teams for out-of-school children, preparing them to access education in formal settings, where available. The programme is implemented over 4 to 12 weeks and includes literacy, numeracy, life skills, and psychosocial and recreational skills. Self-Learning Programme (SLP): A non-formal programme that provides the opportunity for children who have been out of school for more than one year to catch up on lost learning (the completion of each level is expected to take 3 to 6 months), studying both at home and with the support of learning coaches available in community learning centres or through mobile teams. CEP in Camps: A non-formal programme targeting out of school children who do not have access to formal schools in camp communities. Where possible, the programme reflects the core competencies from the curriculum studied prior to displacement and aims to prepare children and youth to transition to formal schools when access and/or return become possible. YEP-Mobile Life Skills: a 5 to 10-day programme, designed to provide safe and welcoming spaces for youth. The programme includes life skills, psychosocial skills and recreational skills for youth. YEP-Mobile Vocational Skills: These 5 to 10-day competency-based training modules provide the opportunity for unemployed male and female youth who have not completed basic education to develop new or strengthen existing skills identified through market assessments as being required for the daily labour market. RECOVERY RESPONSE: The Education programme will contribute to NRC’s recovery response objective of “affected populations recover from the conflict and benefit from pathways to durable solutions” through the provision of opportunities for children and youth to make choices regarding how they will reclaim and reengage in their right to learn. Creating Welcoming Schools: The factors that impact on children and youth not returning to or opting out of school and learning are varied and include, among others, the number of years that they have been out of school, the safety of the learning spaces, the support of the community, and the capacity of educators to engage with the students. The intervention comprises of five main components: Rehabilitation of learning spaces, Teacher professional development, Small Grants for Student Councils and Parent Teacher Associations, Teacher and student kits. YEP 3+3: Through participation in a three-month competency-based training, followed by a three-month coaching and mentoring programme, unemployed male and female youth who have not completed basic education will develop the knowledge, skills and attitudes required for choosing and creating safe and sustainable livelihood options. NRC will aim for up to 10% of the participants in the programme being youth with disabilities. The YEP 3+3 consists of four components delivered in YEP centres and on site, within communities.

Protection Risk Analysis

1. Children, their families, and education personnel need access to PSS and other specialized protection services. Exposure to violence decreases attendance, leads to drop out, and affects learning outcomes by limiting concentration. IDP and returnee children are often not welcomed for the additional strain they place on educational resources already stretched and an education system no longer able to cope, resulting in an increasing number of children and youth disconnecting from learning. NRC incorporates Better Learning Programme in Education activities which introduces tools that facilitators and students can benefit from to reduce some of the impacts of the conflict had on them. Where NRC lacks technical capacity, it will refer to or invite specialised actors to complement with e.g. Psychological First Aid training, including specifically for children.2. Authorities, community members, parents, children and youth clearly identify that the most significant contributing factors that prevent access to safe and protected learning environments are security and poverty. These two factors result in family members choosing negative coping mechanisms that in normal circumstances would never be considered. This negatively impacts not only the ability of children and youth to access learning opportunities, but also, for those who manage to enrol and attend, to focus on their learning. Which is why NRC will continue to monitor the attendance of students to understand barriers that NRC can address, such as selection of centres and putting more effort into outreach to both encourage enrolment and retention of the most vulnerable, as per their suggestions. 3. Distances travelled to access learning are increasing. Many communities do not have functioning transportation services and most families do not have the resources to access the services that are available. Children face multiple risks whilst walking to school, including gender-based violence and recruitment. To mitigate this, NRC consults with stakeholders and conducts a Security Risk Assessment and Protection Risk Analysis to inform decisions of where centres or activities could take place in a safe manner that promotes meaningful access of different population groups.4. A lack of decent and safe livelihoods for family members often results in children being removed from learning and sent to work to support the daily needs of their families. Eighty-two percent of communities surveyed by OCHA in 2017 identified that child labour, recruitment, begging and scavenging exist in their communities. Some families report that female children are often married early to reduce the financial burden and responsibility on families and ‘to keep them safe’. NRC will aim to coordinate internally and externally with livelihoods and food security teams to establish referral mechanisms for children who are at risk of or have dropped out due to financial reasons, where possible and such services are available.5. In a system that is stretched and under resourced, the most vulnerable children to increased isolation and discrimination are those with disabilities. NRC will reach out to specialised actors to ensure staff are aware of and are considering inclusion in educational activities, this could be achieved through both strengthening physical accessibility and adapting activities to promote inclusion.6. IDPs and refugees often have even fewer educational choices, in circumstances where their freedom to move is restricted to a camp environment where limited or no educational services are provided. NRC will continue to expand its catchment areas to reach areas that are mostly neglected or vulnerable, through coordination with other actors and liaison with communities and other stakeholders. NRC will triangulate where the most pressing needs are and initiate plans to engage with stakeholders to gain principled access to deliver.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1. Escalation of violence and loss of safety and security for staff, partners or beneficiaries: NRC has dedicated Risk and Safety staff at different levels who monitor security change triggers and maintain communication with local and regional analysts. All offices maintain updated vulnerability assessments, risk tolerance statements and security plans for all operational locations. All NRC staff receive security and safety training2. Changes in territorial control. NRC will utilise available information to strengthen understanding of dynamics and nuances across different areas of operation to inform decision-making and maintain neutrality 3. Large-scale return of refugees and neighbouring states push for large-scale return of refugees: Collaboration and coordination with other NRC countries in the region. Development of contingency plans and adapting programmes as well as engaging in advocacy efforts4. Corruption: Enforcing NRC’s policies through the continuous developing and adherence to internal and donor regulations, supported by a dedicated Compliance units at various levels5. Inability or delays in acquiring permissions to operate. NRC will maintain regular and transparent communication with all stakeholders during all stages of the project to continue to build trust and create dialogue to facilitate access6. Interference by armed actors: NRC will continue to highlight the humanitarian nature of assistance and aim to strictly engage with civilian actors to coordinate and facilitate access. Attempts to interfere by armed actors will be reported and plans to engage will be discussed. NRC will utilise existing analyses to maintain a conflict and context sensitive approach and continue engaging with peers through coordination to share good practice and discuss challenges related to interference in humanitarian assistance 7. Lack of community acceptance: NRC will ensure that communities are consulted across different stages. Through recruitment and capacity building of local populations where appropriate and clear selection criteria. Maintaining transparency and opening feedback channels that contribute to effectiveness. 8. Conscription or kidnapping of NRC and LHPs staff. NRC will engage in advocacy efforts to protect humanitarian workers and put in place processes that strengthen security. NRC will coordinate closely with relevant actors to understand any contextual shifts that may amplify risks. Teams will be trained on personal safety and working in high risk environments. Decision making processes will be informed by an understanding of risks and feasible mitigation measures.9. Recruitment and retention of qualified staff in areas of operation: NRC will work to widely advertise available positions, vet and build the capacity of staff. A variety of introductory and technical training opportunities will be provided where available, in addition to promoting peer learning and exchanging of experiences.
