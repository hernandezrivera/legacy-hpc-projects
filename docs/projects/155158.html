Needs

Over one year into the response, food consumption and dietary diversity remains a critical gap for many of the 906,512 Rohingya refugees in Cox’s Bazar, Bangladesh. Limited access to fresh food items, as well as opportunities for income-generating activities are contributing factors. In the latest Needs and Population Monitoring Report, it was found that key informants did not cite access to food distribution as a critical gap. Instead, 40% of surveyed locations reported that households were consuming two or less meals a day. Findings from a market assessment conducted by the Cash Working Group in April 2018 indicate that 50% of Rohingya are selling a portion of their WFP rations to traders/intermediaries. Because food provided by WFP constitutes the only source of nutrition for many households, this implies that households are not meeting the minimum average intake of 2,100 kilocalories per person and per day, nor attaining the necessary nutritional requirements. This gap – between food access and food consumption – is an area of ongoing need. Many households continue to struggle with achieving an acceptable dietary diversity. On a scale from 0 to 11 (0 being no nutritional intake and 11 being high nutritional diversity), the average food diversity score for Rohingya was 4.8, meaning they consumed five or less food groups in the past day (RI April 2018 Multi-Sector Assessment). Less than half of the surveyed households (46%) reported vegetables as part of their diet, and an even smaller proportion consumed fruits (33%). With undocumented Rohingya unable to engage in economic activities and earn an income, more negative coping strategies to obtain cash and food heighten protection risks, increasing women and girls' vulnerability to sexual exploitation, trafficking, and child marriage (Oxfam, Rapid Protection, Food Security, and Market Assessment, November 2017). Consistent with the findings of the REVA, the majority of refugees interviewed by RI in April 2018 highlighted lack of cash and insufficient food within the top five constraints currently being faced. To cope with food insecurity, households turned to skipping meals (the average household ate less than 5 meals over the course of 3 days) and borrowing food from neighbors (an average of 4 times over 3 days). This food insecurity negatively affects mothers, who, on average, reduced their food intake for 1-2 meals a day, in order to provide more food for other household members. In terms of spending patterns, 75% of respondents listed food (especially fresh vegetables, when they could afford it) as their first priority in spending, followed by other NFIs (WASH products, clothes, etc.) These findings were further backed up by the Cash Working Group Assessment in April 2018 which also revealed that women cited food items as the most key commodities for basic needs, and that fresh vegetables, dried fish, and eggs were among the main food commodities sold to Rohingya by traders. Households are often exchanging humanitarian assistance for cash in order to purchase other food commodities. FGDs carried out in December 2017 cited food as the primary source of expenditure, with the majority of the recipients spending between 200-400 BDT per trip every week.

Activities or outputs

To meet these food needs gaps, RI will providing assistance through vouchers (e-vouchers) to 50,183 individuals (11,877 HH). Due to the large volume of households in need, additional direct implementation support is essential for rapid scale-up of e-voucher for remaining households. RI is well-positioned to continue supporting with vouchers in 2019. As part of its existing fresh food voucher (FFV) programming in camps 17 and 8W (funded by ECHO), RI has successfully secured approval from the District Commissioner to disburse e-vouchers. The e-vouchers will support WFP's strategy to transition from in-kind food assistance to e-vouchers by May 2019. The programme aims to reach 80% of the target population with an “acceptable” food consumption score (FCS). Coupled with the distribution will be a strong community engagement programme, which will include beneficiary complaints mechanisms, information desks, post-distribution monitoring, as well as community awareness sessions on e-voucher use, nutrition, and budget management. RI is planning to offer complementary services in health, GBV, and CP services in several of the target areas and will look to leverage its community presence in those sectors towards strengthening household engagement in food security activities.
