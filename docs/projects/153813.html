Needs

As of November 2018, there remain 1.87 million people who remain displaced across Iraq with over 93,000 households (HH) in formal camps (IOM DTM, November 2018). The returns survey (REACH, August 2018) reported that 50% anticipate remaining displaced over the next year with a further 29% not sure. They are unable to return to their places of origin due to continued insecurity, the presence of ERW, significant damage to buildings or services, lack of available services and lack of finance. Those HH remaining within the camps remain particularly vulnerable, and include those families who are perceived to be IS affiliated and either do not have the clearance or would not be welcome to return.There is also a high number of returnees who found their previous foundation of their lives fully or partially destroyed. Even 18 months after the liberation if certain areas across Northern Iraq their level of vulnerability has barely decreased and some areas remain unreached by international or governmental aid. Need for NFI in camp:IDP HH within camps have no assets, exhausted savings, high levels of debt and are reliant only on humanitarian assistance. This includes the provision of NFIs such as mattresses, blankets, kitchen sets, heaters and clothing. Whilst the majority of HH will have received a basic NFI kit on arrival, a significant number of the population have now been in camps for an extended period and items need replacing due to breakages, wear and tear and issues such as scabies. Winter NFIs are critical in the low temperatures experienced in Iraq during the cold season which runs from December to March. Winter clothing remains a particular gap as it is not often provided, clothes and shoes suffer a high level of wear and tear due to their regular use, with children outgrowing their few outfits. Clothing remains particular important from a protection perspective to enable dignity through decent, fitting and culturally appropriate clothes. The provision of winter clothing remains a significant gap within humanitarian assistance, with almost no organisation other than Tearfund (TF) and UNICEF carrying out activities. In 2018, UNICEF reported being significantly underfunded with funding currently only for less than an estimated 17% of children in camps as per November 2018; a situation likely to continue in 2019. Focus group discussion (FGDs) with IDPs held in Laylan 1 and 2 camps of Kirkuk (November 2018) by TF revealed urgent winter needs in the form of clothing, heaters and blankets. Many organisations are not willing to distribute clothing due to the extra complexities that arise from providing both the wide range of different items and the different sizes required across age and gender. Need for NFI: During and after the conflict in Iraq, population movements have been multi-directional; at the same time that hundreds of thousands of people have been fleeing their homes, hundreds of thousands have been returning. The pace and scale of displacement have made the Iraq crisis one of the largest and most volatile in the world. The nature of the conflict and the variety of different contexts in which vulnerable HHs find themselves necessitates a variety of different interventions and delivery modalities, each appropriate to meeting the different needs in the most effective way. Especially in the Ninewa plains around Mosul city, heavy fighting between all involved actors have left a lot of structural damage on many villages and single houses including a remaining risk through mines and other ROW. The lack of safe and adequate housing has been named repeatedly as one of the main reasons for people to not return to their pre-crisis living areas. A lot of the attention in 2018 has been on the wide destruction of Mosul city, whilst the same obstacles to are found in the smaller, peri-urban areas around the city like the sub districts of Hamdaniya, Hatra and Telkaif. The Iraq call center dashboard (IHPF Sept 2018) shows, the returns survey data highlights shelter / NFIs as the priority need for returnees and the IDP call center data for Ninewa indicates that poor shelter conditions are one of the top 5 reported needs. NFIs are frequently lacking as previously owned items have been destroyed, stolen and sold. Market analysis indicates that market functionality exits, and the cash modality the preference of most families. Currently Tearfund is working on cash modality for winerization in Wana sub district in collaboration with the UNHCR; and plans to expand NFI activities using the cash modality into other areas in Telkaif and into areas of need in Hamdaniya. In recent needs assessments in Wana sub district top items of need include clothing, kitchen set, cooker, fan and water jerry can.

Activities

Activities NFI:1). Selection of beneficiaries. The criteria will be determined by the context. The need for Winter NFIs (and clothing) tends to be widespread, including both children and adults, meaning that a blanket distribution may be most appropriate. However, with limited resources it may be necessary to prioritise dependent on the greatest need, which could for example be children under 15 or HH suffering from scabies referred from Camp Management. Tearfund (TF) has previously found that it is important to have very distinctive and easily understood criteria so as not to create conflict within the community.2). Determination of modality. TF has previously conducted a voucher based methodology for both NFIs and for clothing. In January 2018, TF conducted a voucher based NFI distribution, where HH could look at samples before ordering their selected items up to a particular value. This enabled the appropriate and effective use of resources, as it avoided duplication and ensured that people got what they actually needed. TF has run clothing markets for the past two years across 6 camps in Kirkuk, Dohuk and Nineveh governorates. TF works with a vendor to set up a ‘shop’ within camp rubhalls, where two representatives from each household can select clothing for their family up to the value amount. TF will also consider cash for clothing; however, even if there is access to markets and sufficient stock available, the protection concerns may mean that vouchers continues to be the most appropriate modality of assistance. In-kind will be used only where vouchers are not possible. 3). Provision of NFIs. TF will provide the range of NFIs and clothing according to cluster specifications and the stated needs of beneficiaries. Voucher amounts are based on the market prices to fulfil cluster specifications. For clothing, that has previously been $35 per individual. 4). Monitoring and evaluation. TF has a helpdesk at distributions, in addition to staff members verifying the quality of items and follow up visits to beneficiary tents. Post-distribution monitoring takes place around a month after distribution to confirm quality and usage of the NFIs.

Protection Risk Analysis

Protection risk analysis for NFI in Kirkuk: Cash continues to be the most critical item required by people living within the camps, with the highest need being for other food items not provided within the in-kind food rations. Regardless of the modality of distribution, it is anticipated that a proportion of HH will either sell some of the items (for a much lower cost), exchange for lower quality or spend on more critical needs such as food. TF will try to reduce the likelihood of this by ensuring that items are needed, to advocate with other actors to provide some of the food basket in cash, and to do messaging to encourage HH about the importance of clothing and NFIs. With perceived ISIS affiliated HH and Syrian refugees remaining in the camps who may have more restrictions on leaving the camps than others, TF will ensure that all HH have access to assistance. This also applies to disabled HH and illiterate HH, with pictures included on messaging (flyers and posters) and TF staff/assessors available to provide any additional supported needed. Ensuring that women have equal participation and decision-making is also a critical concern. TF specifies on the invitations that of the two household representatives attending distributions, at least one of them must be female. Female assessors and female vendor staff are also present, and gender-disaggregated focus groups held to ensure that items include priorities for both genders. Protection is mainstreamed in this project through the selection of a modality that optimises choice, and ensures dignity, clothes that fit and cultural appropriateness. Protection risk analysis for NFIs in Ninewa: Risk of social exclusion and/or conflict: TF is aware of protection concerns relating to HH that are perceived to be affiliated to IS. There have been complaints raised to community and governmental authorities that assistance has been going to these HH; there has then been pressure on TF to share beneficiary lists. All civilians are impartially entitled to receive humanitarian assistance. There are concerns that this will continue to be an issue, and that community leaders will be under pressure to exclude HH. Previously-displaced persons may be more vulnerable in terms of needs, but those that did not flee may face increased vulnerable situation as a result of perceived connection with the suspected perpetrators. These issues can influence project activities when selecting beneficiaries for projects. In addition, vulnerable groups may not have social integration and acceptance in the community to apply for the project at all, e.g. IDPs in host communities. As previously mentioned, the project target locations are very diverse with different ethnic and religious groups, balance among these communities in proportion and size may have shifted significantly due to displacement or/and impact of the conflict. Negative drivers of the conflict, favouritism and stigmatization are expected issues that can as well interfere with the willingness of beneficiaries to attend. Gender and age considerations: Female-headed HH are being recognized as highly vulnerable and between 10-25% of the HH in Ninewa Plains are assessed to be female headed (REACH, MCNA, 2018). There are cases of female headed HH who would struggle to attend an assessment or distribution due to the perceived cultural appropriateness of being present in a public place. Access for the elderly particularly those who have no support from family can be challenging, and often the elderly risk being missed during the assessment and distribution process.

Mitigation Measures

Protection Mainstreaming: Following the key criteria 1. Prioritize safety & dignity, and avoid causing harm; 2. Meaningful access; 3. Accountability; 4. Confidentiality and 5. Compliance with humanitarian principles. TF ensures that all staff and casuals complete e-learning modules covering safeguarding (children and adults), fraud awareness, whistleblowing, corruption and bribery; which give staff clear guidelines on how to conduct themselves. TF develops community acceptance through regular meetings with community leaders, establishing community committees, and listening to the community concerns through key informants. Solutions including access for women and the elderly are discussed, and solutions for the safe delivery of cash modalities resolved. In line with best practice and the recommendations of the Shelter Cluster, TF mainstreams protection throughout its project. Protection needs are considered in the selection of beneficiaries. Vulnerability criteria include: individuals missing documents (which can affect their ability to receive government assistance and return to their area of origin); child headed HHs; widowed or divorced women; and families demonstrating one or more negative coping mechanisms. TF has internal data protection guidelines and trains staff on them. TF is committed to ensuring robust data sharing agreements are in place with actors when coordination requires data sharing. Gender and age participation: Proportionate participation by men and women will be guaranteed during staff recruitment, FDGs, beneficiary selection and creation of community female feedback groups to advise how to increase female participation and ensure gender sensitive approach trainings. Community committees will include representation from both men and women. TF trains staff on gender-sensitive service delivery. At distributions a beneficiary accountability desk is composed of female and male staff ensuring feedback and issues can be discussed with men and women separately so each gender is able to speak freely. TF has experience in ensuring gender sensitivity in this sector. Given that it often tends to be the men who attend distributions, TF insists that at least one of the two people attending a voucher distribution needs to be a woman, so they are included in the selection of items for their HH. TF has mixed gender teams at assessment, implementation, monitoring and beneficiary accountability level, ensuring that issues can be discussed with men and women separately so each gender is able to speak freely. Assessments have shown that men and women have often had differing priorities and preferences of modality of distribution. During distributions TF works with community leadership, community committees, Mokhtar's and camp management to ensure that individuals with specialised needs are identified and steps taken to ensure that they can still access distributions and have equal access to the assistance provided. This is particularly relevant for elderly individuals or those with disabilities.

How is your project accountable to affected population

Understanding the local context: In the initial assessment and analysis of community’s needs, FGDs are carried out with women, youth and different ethnic/religious groups to get a better understanding of their vulnerabilities and abilities. Key Informant Interviews are used to better understand the impact of the crisis on their activities and their ability to meet basic needs for their families and to identify bottlenecks for successful project activities. Beneficiary accountability officer: A dedicated beneficiary accountability officer will carry out spot checks and provide input to the project teams to ensure inclusion of beneficiary feedback into project design details and decisions. Beneficiary communities and individuals can contact the free hotline number to report issues and complaints related to the project activities; with each call reviewed and responded with recommendations to improve the project systems or to indicate any action to be taken. Monitoring Evaluation Accountability and Learning (MEAL): As part of the project cycle the teams will be supported by a system of monitoring, evaluation, accountability and learning through the MEAL team who operates independently to the project team. The MEAL team will take responsibility to manage beneficiary database to cross-check transparency and accountability of vulnerability assessments and registration process. The MEAL team will facilitate a midterm lessons learned workshop will be conducted with both Partner’s project to reflect and learn from the first project cycle. Relevant findings, learnings and/or success cases of will be shared with communities. Additionally, an external evaluation will be conducted for the project to assess the impact of the project. Whistleblowing: TF as an organization has whistleblowing mechanisms that can be used by staff to report any identified behaviours or actions by other staff members that compromise both the values of the organizations and put beneficiaries, host communities and key project relationships in a vulnerable position or at risk. All staff complete online or other training to understand the Whistleblowing structure and process for their organization. Exit strategy:Developed with the beneficiaries and service providers (e.g. veterinarians) for ongoing support if required post distribution/project period. Community meeting for closure of project targets and feedback; hand over to those community networks for ongoing monitoring and support. Referral information provided to beneficiaries about accessing service providers as needed on issues they may face in the future. Ongoing access to the TF beneficiary accountability hotline
