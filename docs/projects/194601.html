Project Summary

To protect livelihoods of vulnerable rural and urban populations and improve food
security through the provision of livelihood support during the main planting and dry
season (crop and vegetable seeds, tools, fishing kits, livestock vaccines and drugs),
support food security information, analysis and coordination and strengthen absorptive
capacity and build resilience at household and community level.

1 - Needs

Eleven years after gaining independence, South Sudan continues to face ongoing and violent sub-national and local insecurity, compounding climate-related issues such as flooding (2022 marks the fourth year in a row South Sudan has seen severe flooding) and drought. The insecurity, which has greatly increased since 2020, has driven families from their homes, effectively eliminating their housing, food, and financial security, leaving them highly vulnerable to shocks and stresses. The most recent Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) analysis, released November 2022, reports that there are currently 6.6 million (54% of the population) South Sudanese people experiencing acute food insecurity, or classified as IPC level 3 and above. 61,000 people are experiencing the worst levels of food insecurity, IPC 5 (Catastrophic) in Fangak, Canal/Pigi, and Akobo of Jonglei State, and Pibor County in the Greater Pibor Administrative Area. Jonglei (67%) Unity (66%), and Upper Nile (58%) States are among the most food insecure states in South Sudan, with over 50% of their population facing IPC 3 (crisis) or above. As of October 2022, inter-agency assessments, led by OCHA, have assessed 924,259 people that have been affected by flooding, with nearly 182,200 people displaced: 84,400 in Northern Bahr el Ghazal State, 65,300 in Unity State, 30,000 in Western Equatoria State, and 2,500 in Eastern Equatoria State (ACAPS, 2022). Floodwater extent has more than doubled between early August and early October, from 22,000 sq km to 48,000 sq km (UNOSAT, 2022) with floodwaters expected to remain until early 2023, given past precedent. Flooding has effectively eliminated hundreds of thousands of people's livelihoods and sources of household income, with an estimated 16.5 kha of cropland potentially affected by flooding (UNOSAT, 2022). The 2022 IPC also revealed that displaced people are more likely to be severely food insecure (IPC 3 or worse) with 61.1% of total IDPs being severely food insecure. This is particularly significant in Western Bahr el Ghazal (69.9% of the total IDPs), Central Equatoria (67.3%), and Unity (61.2%) States. In Eastern Equatoria, returnees are relatively (80% of the total number of returnees) more food secure compared to others residential group. The residents tend to be the most food insecure among residential groups in Eastern Equatoria, Jonglei, Northern Bahr el Ghazal and Upper Nile States.Furthermore, food prices have risen significantly in 2022, with the price of Sorghum increasing 153% since October 2021 (JMMI, 2022). These high market prices have been attributed to the decrease in road and river access to restock markets, due to insecurity and flooding, especially in Unity, northern Jonglei, and Upper Nile where all markets have 'reduced functionality' (JIMMI, 2022). With the continued protracted crisis in South Sudan, livelihood opportunities have decreased, forcing many families to resort to negative coping mechanisms, including transactional marriages, sometimes with children and child labour.Looking ahead to 2023, the IPC analysis predicts continued widespread acute food insecurity, with only one county out of 79 total counties falling under IPC 3; Nzara County in Western Equatoria State. Furthermore, levels of malnutrition will persist with an estimated 1.4 million children under the age of five in South Sudan will suffer from acute malnutrition (IPC, 2022). Support to livelihoods and food security is vital to South Sudan’s future, and FAO will continue to work closely with humanitarian and development partners to assess shifting needs across the country, throughout 2023.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

The Joint Market Monitoring Initiative (JMMI) created by the Cash Working Group in south Sudan conducts markets assessments across the country on prices and market indicators to guide cash and voucher programming. The FAO’s Crop and Livestock Market Information System (CLiMIS) provides real time analyzed data and information on agricultural and livestock products to aid understanding of market performance and planning of relevant interventions. FAO also carries out rapid market assessments in intervention locations prior to guide implementation. Additionally, given the nature of FAO cash interventions, (with the objective of supporting increased/improved production/productivity), these market assessments are complemented with broader assessments including the CFSAM and SSSA which do have a specifically relationship to the agricultural markets, ensuring a holistic process of analyzing market functionality.

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

Cash and voucher is preferred because they offer multiple benefits to the local economy and the recipients of the assistance. Cash or voucher are a dignified form of assistance (where the conditions are favourable). They place the decision making power in the hands of the recipients to identify and meet their own priority needs. The recipients of the assistance have choice over type and quality; therefore, their decisions are valued and influence the agrodealers/input providers to endeavor to deliver quality at competitive prices, in a bid to attract more buyers. The beneficiary has control, which is lost with in-kind support. Moreover, resources are directly injected into the local communities, enabling them to access goods/services from the local markets, therefore creating demand, which in turn stimulates supply. These boost the small and local traders and markets, improving their functionality therefore contributing to boosting the local economy.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

FAO has a toll free number (515) for general grievances and 882 for SEA complaints, AAP assistants based in all field offices to whom the vulnerable members can share feedback in local languages.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

FAO is formally signed-up and committed to the IASC Commitments on Accountability to Affected People (AAP). These aim to produce quality humanitarian and development programs by maximizing the dignified and active involvement of communities in all aspects of programs that affect their lives mostly on how power imbalance can be reduced. FAOSS has functional feedback mechanisms including suggestion boxes, feedback desks, toll free hotline numbers (515 for general grievances and 882 for SEA complaints), AAP focal points that are trained and employed by FAO in each field office. The various options for feedback and complaints are expected to facilitate access to the system to ensure the most vulnerable feel safe to speak up. FAO’s project designs and implementation makes use of community committees whose membership comprises of people with disabilities, women and youth representatives, among others, to participate and engage in the project. FAO is a member of the UN PSEA Task force which supports Community Based Complaints Mechanism (CBCM), which also comprises of national and international NGOs.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

All Implementing partners (IP) are regulated under a Letter of agreement with FAO that include information on PSEA and training on PSEA and corruption specifically. FAO also supports PSEA and anticorruption training/awareness at cluster level. FAO AAP assistants and M&E staff monitor such aspects closely.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

FAO is committed to providing a high quality, accountable and dignified response, which takes account of the needs and capacities of women, men and vulnerable groups. Together with the international aid and humanitarian community, FAO has adopted the seven (7) Commitments on Accountability to Affected Populations, which means that FAO meaningfully engages affected people at every key stage of the project cycle (assessment, design, implementation and monitoring). These commitments are: * Supporting staff to acquire the understanding, knowledge and competencies that will enable them to meet FAO’s commitments. * Communities receiving assistance should receive information about the services and support available in local languages. Means of communication are appropriate and tailored to local circumstances including communicating the targeting criteria and the available feedback mechanism. Ensure information on inputs during the seed fair and distribution is provided to beneficiaries in local languages. *Ensure the Channels are able to capture project based information and offer response to close the feedback loop * Ensure there is a feedback desk at every distribution point and feedback is recorded clearly on the template.

Yes

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

With a continuous reduction in land for cultivation and grazing due to flooding, displacement is in part driven by a loss of peoples' abilities to maintain and/or develop their livelihoods. Livelihood support to populations that are being inundated by floodwaters is vital so they are able to provide life-saving nutrients to their family and community. Additionally, FAO uses in-house GIS expertise to conduct weather and climate forecasting, mapping, and resulting disaster risk-reduction. This information is routinely disseminated across South Sudan through radio programming.

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

Community resilience, as well as the elimination of hunger, cannot be achieved without closing the gap between women and men in agriculture and food production, as well as ensuring the protection of vulnerable women. In recognition of this and women’s essential role in improving household food security and nutrition, FAO has designed its programme to respond to their needs. The ELRP seeks to help women as they help their families manage the consequences of crisis and food insecurity, providing opportunities for income generation and increasing access to nutritious food sources. In this regard, FAO activities ensure 60 percent of its beneficiary caseload is women-headed households. Moreover, protection is mainstreamed throughout FAO’s programme, ensuring at-risk groups’ specific needs are taken into consideration and risks mitigated where and when possible. Those at risk include not only women and girls, but also men and boys, elderly, unaccompanied children, disabled, minorities and displaced persons. FAO contributes to the protection of individuals from gender-based violence and sexual exploitation and abuse as part of its efforts to restore and promote sustainable livelihoods, such as mitigating gender discrimination in participation, decision-making and access to productive resources. Feedback mechanisms are shared with beneficiaries to enable channels for raising issues.During the community sensitization and mobilization stage of all FAO projects, an AAP committee is formed and a Safety Audit is conducted, not just for assessing safety around distribution sites, but also the routes taken by beneficiaries, and planting areas. GBV risk is one of the primary considerations when selecting distribution times, locations, and input use, and this risk is continuously assessed by FAO and IP staff during activities and monitoring throughout project duration and beyond.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

The objective of the project is to save lives and to enhance households’livelihoods and own food production and reduce their annual food gap.Further, the project aims to decrease the degree and frequency with which households depend on external food assistance and strengthen their capacity to absorb shocks. As such, the Programme sets the foundation for enhancing the long-term adaptive capacity of communities and strengthens the synergies among FAO’s short- and long-term strategies to build resilience.
