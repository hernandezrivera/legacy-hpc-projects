Project Summary

PURPOSE: To survey and clear priority cluster munition strikes as well as UXO spot tasks reported by the community in Juba, Magwi, and Torit counties to provide safe access and land in areas with high rates of returns; building resilience by releasing land back to the communities than can be used for agriculture, grazing, and settlement. The project will also educate displaced persons, returnees, and host community members about the dangers of and safe behaviors to adopt when encountering explosive hazards, with an emphasis on reaching men and boys who are most at risk of death or injury. In addition, the project will train community focal points who can provide safety briefings to returnees, while also increasing the number of women in leadership positions in the community.
TARGET: The multi-task team (MTT) will survey and clear 420,000 square meters of high priority cluster munition strikes and EOD spot tasks, which equates to survey and clearance of 50 hazardous areas, inclusive of spot tasks. The community liaison team (CLT) will provide explosive ordnance risk education (EORE) to 15,000 people (W: 3,959, M; 4,176, G 3,492 B:3,375); and train 100 community focal points (W: 50, M: 50).
SUMMARY OF ACTIVITIES: MAG will deploy a multi-task team (MTT) and community liaison team (CLT) to Juba, Magwi, and Torit counties during 2020. When appropriate for the nature of the terrain the MTT will be supported by a Bozena with Mulcher attachment. MAG is the only mine action NGO utilizing such equipment for clearance in South Sudan, and as such MAG can deliver clearance of large cluster munition strikes faster and more cost efficiently than manual teams. The CLT will deploy to areas with large numbers of displaced persons, prioritizing people who are likely to return to their home villages to provide explosive ordnance risk education (EORE).
IMPACT: Clearance increases a community’s resiliency by clearing land that can then be used for settlement, agriculture, and grazing of animals; making communities will be less dependent on food aid. Clearance also provides access to public services and aid. Therefore, mine action activities can improve outcomes across all sectors. EORE provides community members with the means to keep themselves and their families safe and to report potentially hazardous items to keep their communities safe. Training community focal points gives the communities the means to continue to spread safe behavior messages even after the project is over. Through this integrated approach of clearance, EORE, and training community focal points, the project can sustainably prevent and mitigate protection risks.
MAG’S EXPERIENCE: MAG has worked extensively through Central and Eastern Equatoria, throughout its history in South Sudan. While the crisis of 2016 prevented access for clearance teams focusing on area clearance for a period, MAG returned to Magwi county in September 2018 and has been working in Amika boma—the town centre area—since then. In addition, beginning in July 2019, MAG has deployed six teams (four on a temporary or short term basis) to the outer areas of Magwi county close to the border with Juba county and along the important Juba-Nimule highway, where they have been conducting battle area clearance. In addition, the multi-task team proposed for this project has been working in Juba county along the Juba-Nimule highway—initially in Aru Junction—since December 2018. In addition, MAG’s MineWolf team has been operating in Juba county since October 2017 and is now operating in Torit county. Three of the MAG teams operating in Magwi county are expected to relocate back to their normal area of operations in Terekeka county in the dry season, and therefore this project will continue to provide capacity for BAC clearance in Magwi as well as Juba and Torit counties. This project will allow MAG to continue the high priority clearance working taking place along the important Juba-Nimule and Juba-Torit roads.

1 - Needs

The people of South Sudan are still living in danger from the decades long conflict between the SPLA and the SAF, which left behind landmines and cluster bombs, as well as other explosive remnants of war (ERW) such as air-dropped bombs, motars, and small arms amunitionThese bombs and explosive hazards, left behind from conflict, continue to kill, maim, and disrupt lives and livelihoods long after the conflict has ended. In the first 9 months of 2019 alone, there have been 7 reported deaths from mines and ERW, as well as 27 casualties (IMSMA monthly report, September 2019 sent via email from UNMAS), although this number is likely underreported due to the lack of transportation, medical and communications infrastructure in the country. In total, there have been a recorded 5,071 injuries and deaths from mines and ERW, according to IMSMA, as of September 2019. Because of this threat to physical safety, explosive hazards also reduce the ability of community members to access land, services such as schools and clinics, and natural resources such as natural water sources, wood for charcoal, and land for grazing. According to data from IMSMA, between them, the counties of Juba, Magwi, and Torit, have a total of 196 known hazards areas, including minefields, cluster bomb strikes, known UXO spot tasks, and confrontation areas; amounting to an estimated area of over 6 million square metres of contaminated land (Data provided by UNMAS, dated 30 June 2019). Magwi, Juba, and Torit counties are the first, second, and fourth most cluster munition contaminated counties in South Sudan according to IMSMA data, with Terekeka county, where MAG also has operations funded by other donors, being the third most contaminated county with cluster munitions. The most sever contamination is primarily along the border counties, areas where people displaced either internally or externally are expected to return to. Of the known hazardous areas in Magwi, Juba, and Torit, 71 are cluster bomb strikes and 66 are minefields (IMSMA data as of 30 June 2019). These explosive hazards can block communities from safely accessing basic services. For example, in Lobonok, Juba county, MAG cleared a large minefield that was blocking access to the local school and health clinic. In Amika boma, Magwi county, MAG has cleared a school yard as well as farm land that was littered with PM1 cluster munition bomblets. By clearing areas such as these, mine action interventions can improve outcomes across all sectors including education, health, and food security. Minefields are most efficiently cleared with large mechanical assets, such as the MineWolf—During the first half of 2020, MAG already has funding committed to operate two of these machines. Given that MAG’s minefield capacity has already been funded until the end of the dry season in 2020, this project will focus on clearance of cluster bomb strikes. Explosive hazards affect all members of the community: women, girls, boys, and men. However, men and boys are at an elevated risk of being harmed by mines and explosive remnants of war (ERW), making up 74% of known accidents in the IMSMA database between the year 2000 and June 2019 (data provided by UNMAS; casualties nation-wide: 74% male, 16% female, 10% not specified or unknown). In addition, men often are more difficult to reach with EORE: in many communities, young men may be away from their villages with their cattle, in other communities they may be focused on farming activities and may be reluctant to take time away from their farming activities to attend risk education sessions. Community liaison teams, upon deployment to the community, conduct focus group discussions with community leaders, youth, and women (and possibly other groups, e.g. cattle keepers, if relevant to the local context) to determine what types of contamination different members of the community encounter.

2 - Activities

MAG will deploy one MTT to the tier 1 counties of Juba, Magwi, and Torit (sequentially) to conduct survey and clearance of explosive devices, specifically through battle area clearance (BAC) of cluster bomb strikes as well as the clearance of EOD spot tasks. When appropriate for the terrain, the MTT will be supported by a Bozena with mulcher attachment, (part resourced by project) which speeds the rate of clearance by efficiently cutting through vegetation to allow the use of metal detectors. The MTT is expected to clear and release 420,000 square meters of land back to the community. MAG expects to survey and clear 50 hazardous areas —the majority of these HAs will be EOD spot tasks. However, the focus of this project will be on area clearance of priority cluster munition strikes, which take significantly longer to clear and often fade out beyond the initial HA. MAG will also deploy a community liaison team (CLT) to conduct explosive ordnance risk education (EORE), with particular attention paid to targeting recent returnees and currently displaced persons who intend to return to their homes, by targeting areas with high concentrations of IDPs. MAG aims to provide EORE to 15,000 people: 9,000 displaced persons in POCs and/or other areas with high densities of displaced persons and 6,000 host community members and existing returnees in Magwi, Torit, and Juba counties. MAG will reach all members of the community with EORE, however, MAG will give special attention to identifying the best way to reach men, boys, returnees and displaced persons likely to return, in order to make sure that they are aware of the nature of the contamination and understand the mitigation measures that they can use to reduce the physical threats to themselves. In addition, MAG will train 100 community focal point who are able to reinforce EORE messages and provide safety briefings to returnees and newcomers to the community. Because being a community focal point is a position of leadership in the community, and to ensure that women, girls, and members of female-headed households—who may otherwise be overlooked by community leadership—are supported by community focal points, MAG aims for at least 50% of the CFPs trained to be women. Although in the past MAG has found it difficult to reach equal numbers of men and women for CFP training, beginning in 2019, MAG set targets for women CFPs and has put greater emphasis in encouraging community leaders to nominate women to be CFPs and encouraging women to take such roles. This experience has been largely successful, and teams that have a specific target for women CFPs have trained on average 46% women during 2019; compared to 33% women for teams without gender specific targets. Mixed-gender community liaison teams lead on engagement with the communities. Upon deployment to the communities, they conduct community mapping and focus groups to identify where contamination may be and to understand how contamination affects the lives of the people living nearby. In addition to such data gather activities, they also share information about MAG’s clearance activities, ensuring that community members understand the specific safe behaviours they need to adopt during clearance (such as not moving pickets or rocks identifying cleared an uncleared areas, respecting safety cordons during clearance and demolitions). Community members are also notified prior to any demolitions, and community liaison teams and the clearance team work with the community after clearance to make sure that they understand that the land is safe to use. Clearance of BAC tasks—in which bomblets that are under vegetation and often under the surface of the ground—mitigates the risk to the population of such hazards and prevents accidents. EORE further mitigates the risks by helping people to identify dangerous items and understand safe behaviours upon encountering dangerous items.

6.6 - Will beneficiaries' rights be legally recognised?

MAG participates in the housing land and property technical working group. Once HLP legislation is finalized, MAG will ensure that community leaders understand the law and community members understand their rights in regards to housing, land and property.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

Clearance makes more land available for safe use, including for housing, agriculture, grazing, and social services. In particular, clearance can free land that can be used for resettlement of returnees and/or displaced persons, or that can be used for cultivation by returnees and displaced persons. The project will also specifically target displacement affected people with the provision of explosive ordnance risk education. Displaced persons are at greater risk as they are unfamiliar with the contamination history in their place of resettlement. EORE can allow displaced people to be empowered with the knowledge of safe behaviours and the ability to identify hazardous items.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

Community liaison teams meet with members of the local communities--including groups of women, youth, and community leaders, and conduct mapping exercises during which the communities identify blockages and priorities for clearance. Once clearance is completed, land is available to be used long after MAG has left the area. It can be allocated by the community leaders to community members in a manner that makes the most sense for the community, and can provide additional land to allow for the settlement of returnees and/or displaced persons.

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

The national development plan identifies mine and ERW contamination as a key challenge in achieving the aims of the countries reconstruction efforts.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

Land clearance involves teams living outside of towns in unfenced camps. Although MAG recongizes that the risks faced by community members are different from the risks faced by aid workers, protection risks to community members would also be seen as a threat to MAG's operations and MAG would not deploy to an area where beneficiaries would significant face protection risks.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

Once land is cleared, it is handed over to the local community. Community members are allocated land for use by local community leaders in line with the law.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

MAG has a well-established suggestions and complaints mechanism, with various options for feedback provision. We have easily seen and accessible locked boxes in each location, where both staff and local community members can put all their feedback anonymously. We also train our community liaison teams to be gender-inclusive focal points for the feedback in case community members wish to raise any concerns. We also advertise contact details and easily understood (in local languages and pictures) reporting chain in case anyone would want to raise concerns via phone or internet. All submitted concerns are always responded to within a defined delay, following MAG policies on safeguarding.
