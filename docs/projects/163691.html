Project Summary

Mental Health is a chronically neglected field in the country with many longstanding problems. The World Health Organization’s review of the mental health program in Libya revealed significant gaps in the availability of and accessibility to mental health and psychosocial support services including underdeveloped community and specialized services at primary and secondary level, shortage of qualified workforce from psychiatrists, psychologist and trained nurses, lack of facilities, providing mental health and psychosocial services, social stigma towards people with mental illness and funding. In addition, the current and long-lasting violence in the country is believed to further increase the proportion of the population in need of mental health and psychosocial support, requiring a combination of immediate and longer-term interventions.
The target of this project:
 To increase the number of health facilities providing the mental health and the psychosocial support to the community at primary, secondary level.
 To provide mental health care services at community-based settings in Libya , with focus on areas with high demand of services.
 To empower community members to cope with emerging psychosocial needs

1 - Needs

The armed conflict in Libya has caused deaths and injuries to civilians, significant displacement and disruption of social and health services and damage to infrastructure. The World Health Organization’s review of the mental health program in Libya revealed significant gaps in the availability of and accessibility to mental health and psychosocial support services (including mental health professionals) to adequately address the needs of the affected population (2019). Armed conflicts and civil unrest can negatively impact the mental health of the populations in the short as well as the long term through: Worsening of the Pre-existing problems (e.g. severe mental disorder; depression, alcohol abuse); Causing mental health problems (e.g. grief, non-pathological distress; alcohol abuse; depression and anxiety disorders, including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD); and Distress experienced by a significant proportion of the exposed population most of whom will recover over tie but a small proportion of whom will go on to develop mental health problems. It is estimated that now at least 1,003,840 Libyans all around Libya would need mental health care for conditions such as depression, bipolar, PTSD, anxiety, and schizophrenia (181,060 of those are among population in need of humanitarian assistance), this represents 1 in 7 of whole population of Libya. (calculation based on WHO projected estimates for mental health needs in conflicts published in Lancet 2019, HRP 2020 for Libya and GBD 2017 data). Although the number of specialists and cities providing services has doubled, there is only one specialized mental health professional for every 300 000 people in Libya (neighboring Tunisia has one mental health professional for every 100 000 people). Only five cities in Libya – fewer than 12% of the country’s cities – are offering mental health services. Given that it will require many years to establish nationwide, community-based mental health services, the activities described in this project proposal have been designed to fill urgent gaps in the most affected areas. Moreover, the acute shortage of specialized psychiatric nurses when expat nurses have left the country due to the unrest situation, have affected drastically on the MHPSS service delivery at all levels including the tertiary level.WHO is supporting the MOH recent request to strengthen the core capacities of the MOH in service delivery for mental health and psychosocial support “MHPSS” particularly in capacity building of health workforce at different health care levels including trained general physicians, psychologists and specialized psychiatric nurses and MH tutors in nursing faculties on the emergency context for MH Nurses for continuous professional development. The project aims to target at different geographical regions in Libya, as WHO will use standard tools such as the WHO MhGAP, and WHO-AIMS tool to restore and maintain mental health system in emergency and developing settings.

• Lack of awareness of needs of MHPSS at different healthcare levels and in community.• Many national stakeholders representing MHPSS unit. • There are continuous shifting lines of conflict. The situation (political, security) is fluid. There are de-factor two governance structures (GNA and LNA). There is a large degree of uncertainty. • No information is available to population about functioning and supported Mental health health services. • There are limitations related to the absence of civil society, community based approach in health sector planning and response.
