Project Summary

NRC Cameroon’s Shelter interventions include the construction of permanent shelters for returnees settling for an extended period of time in a formal site set up by local authorities or by communities themselves. NRC also provides emergency shelter kits and NFI kits for new IDPs and refugees. NRC ensures the participation of beneficiaries so that shelter designs are culturally appropriate and meet the special needs of women and girls, i.e., ensure the placement of door locks and sufficient lighting. NRC will integrate safe programming principles for people with disabilities and the elderly to ensure meaningful access and equal participation of people with disabilities and special needs. The same households receiving emergency shelter assistance will also receive NFI kits in order to provide a more holistic service to beneficiaries.
For displaced communities, NRC conducts assessments to document emerging shelter gaps. Then, through participatory approaches in which beneficiaries contributes and oversees a substantial part of the shelter and infrastructure developments, NRC identifies beneficiaries, provides shelter/NFI kits and basic construction tools for emergency shelters.
NRC’s Shelter team works closely with local authorities to foster environmental protection in the projects and had adopted essential environmental standards and employs tools like the NEAT+ to ensure environmental mitigation is considered in project implementation as well as liaise with environment authorities on advocacy aimed at minimising the destruction of forests through harvesting of local shelter construction materials by the local communities.
Interventions respond to different infrastructure needs in project locations such as new construction of temporary shelters, rehabilitation and repair of existing homes aimed at promoting access to basic housing, thus improving privacy. Supporting permanent shelters works best when displaced populations return to their places of origin or integrate into host communities. This is currently happening with many returnees in the Far North Region.
Target group
The NRC programme will target IDPs, returnees and vulnerable host communities’ households who need shelter assistance to improve their living conditions. Particular attention will be given to households that meet the following vulnerability criteria:
• IDPs living in makeshift shelters exposing them to bad weather;
• IDPs living in host communities where they scramble for the very limited space available and share sleeping spaces;
• IDPs in rental accommodation who are at risks of eviction as a result of inability to pay rents,
• Returnees (former IDPs), where houses have been vandalised, destroyed or dilapidated due to the crisis;
• Schools in conflict affected areas with increase in number of students as a result of influx of IDPs and those that are used as collective centres by IDPs.
The identification of beneficiaries will be based on the findings of needs assessment conducted by NRC and guided by gaps on the current 5W matrix at cluster level and guided by the Emergency tracking tool updated and shared by IOM every month. The final selection of beneficiaries will be done during focus group discussions with the community in a beneficiary validation process involving NRC’s CFM staff.
NRC already has Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) systems in place that ensures disaggregated data collection from the beneficiaries.

1 - Besoins / Needs

The Far North is one of the poorest regions of Cameroon and has been hard hit by the armed conflict inflicted by Boko Haram (BH) group because of its proximity with northern Nigeria. IOM Dashboard No. 24, dated 17th February 2022, indicated that the number of displaced people has increased. The situation in Cameroon has 377877 IDPs, 130690 Returnees (former IDPs), and 49564 Refugees out of camp. The proportion was 49% men versus 51% women. Minors account for 62% of the population. The average household size in the Far North is 8 people. All NRC interventions will be concentrated in these Divisions, Logone-et-Chari, Mayo Sava, Diamaré and Mayo Tsanaga. The crisis currently affecting the Lake Chad Basin states results from a complex combination of factors, including conflict with Non-State Armed Groups, extreme poverty, underdevelopment and a changing climate, which together have triggered significant displacement of populations. As of 24th September 2020, Cameroon, Chad, Niger and Nigeria were hosting an estimated 5,034,347 affected individuals made up of Internally Displaced People (IDPs), Refugees (both in- and out-of-camp), Returnees (Former IDPs and Returnees from abroad) and Third Country Nationals (TCNs). 76 per cent of the affected population (representing 3,833,232 individuals) were located in Nigeria, while 11 per cent resided in Cameroon (559,699 individuals), 8 per cent in Chad (377,898 individuals) and 5 per cent in Niger (263,518 individuals). Social services and infrastructure in the Far North region are insufficient to meet the needs of the displaced populations (IDPs, refugees and returnees) due to either having been destroyed during conflict or poorly maintained. The attacks and lootings by BH in the region have also led to the destruction of houses and property worsening the suffering of the most vulnerable populations. Due to insecurity, populations, whose main source of income is agriculture and livestock, have left their homes seeking refuge, living in informal settings with makeshift undignified shelters, and traveling long distances in search of water, increasing the risk of exploitation and no longer having access to land and livelihood opportunities. Local host communities have little capacity to support refugees and IDPs. According to Central Bureau of the Census and Population Studies (Nov 2005 report), the Far North is Cameroon’s poorest and most fragile region, with 74% of the population living below the national poverty line. Host communities have also seen their livelihoods and access to basic services severely impacted by the current crisis. Displaced populations have also either lost their identification documents during displacement or members of BH seized and destroyed them, leading to movement restrictions out of fear of police controls. Since mid-August 2022, the Far North region has been experiencing flooding. This phenomenon is the result of heavy rainfall with overflowing rivers and/or breached dikes. As of September 20, 2022, more than 37,000 people were affected, including about 27,000 in Mayo-Danay, 9,000 in Logone et Chari, and 1,300 in Mayo-Tsanaga (source: OCHO OCHA Floods_en_v8_20220926) The locations where the IDPs have fled for safety include areas where they are living with relatives and friends in homes whose space is insufficient to provide the needed separation and privacy between the different ages and sex. In some situations, it is still common to find a home hosting almost 22 persons as depicted by NRC’s profiling data. The situation is reported to be worse in the bush where the IDPs sleep on the bare ground with no privacy and are exposed to harsh weather conditions and protection issues. In addition to limited access to livelihoods, IDPs, and their host/surrounding communities face high unemployment rates and low income. As a result, most IDP households cannot afford appropriate shelter materials or rent.
