Project Summary

The IRC will deliver life-saving and life-sustaining primary healthcare services for 252,524 conflict-affected populations in northeast Syria (Ar Raqqa, Al-Hasakeh and Deir ez-Zor governorates). The IRC’s PHC services are in line with the Essential Health Service Package for northern Syria and WHO guidelines for Syria and include prevention and management of communicable and non-communicable diseases, integrated management of childhood illnesses, reproductive health, pharmacological and non-pharmacological MHPSS, health education and behavioral change communication. The IRC will contribute to disease surveillance through systematic collection, analysis and reporting of health data and weekly reporting through Early Warning and Alert and Reporting System (EWARS) to inform preparedness and response planning for potential outbreaks in the target locations. The IRC proposes to continue implementing services in Ar Raqqa (Ar Raqqa city, Al Hisha/Jdida, Hazima, Mahmoudly, Turkman, Koromaza); Deir ez-Zor (Abu Khashab); and Al-Hasakeh (Al Hol camp, Areesha camp, Abu Rasin, Amuda, Qahtaniya PHCC, Roj camp, Newroz camp, Derek, Jawadia and Washokani). Services will be delivered through 12 static health facilities and 10 mobile medical units - the latter will allow the IRC to rapidly shift programming to meet emerging needs.
In addition, the IRC will deliver pediatric in-patient care services through a 24/7 unit in Al Hol camp. The IRC estimates to reach 279,162 individuals in 2020.

4 - Needs

Despite an increase in the number of available health facilities across NES, which includes public healthcare services, large proportions of households still struggle to access treatment. Healthcare suffers from insufficient personnel, a lack of specialized services such as dialysis treatment, and a lack of vital medical supplies such as prosthetics. Main healthcare needs include emergency care, provision of assistive devices and limb reconstruction surgery, and treatment for chronic diseases. There is also an increased risk of communicable diseases.According to Syria HNO 2020, an estimated of 11.06 million people are in need of humanitarian assistance during 2020, including 4.6 million people in acute need. In all conflict-affected areas, limited access to basic health services is a persistent challenge due to unavailability or unaffordability of services. The high needs for health services in frontline areas in Raqqa and Hasakeh have also increased as these areas experienced receipt of highest influx of displacements. Other population groups with critical needs are host communities in rural Ar-Raqqa and Deir-ez-Zor governorates. Reduced services and lack of certainty around continuation/resumption of services have also added to the stress faced by people in need.Shortages of medical supplies and pharmaceuticals have been widely reported. MHPSS needs are estimated at 15% prevalence, with reported shortage of treatment, under-staffing and prevalent stigma around MHPSS issues. Epidemiological data showed that influenza-like illness, acute diarrhea, cutaneous leishmaniasis, and suspected hepatitis are the most common communicable diseases. These challenges are exacerbated by damaged or non-existent health facilities, burdening the local health system, as well as lack of referral mechanisms and specialized health services and interruption of medical supply chains.

5 - Implementation of activities

PRIMARY HEALTHCARE (PHC): IRC will deliver life-saving and life-sustaining PHC services for conflict-affected populations in line with the EHSP for northern Syria and WHO guidelines.- Curative consultations provided by GPs and specialized doctors (pediatricians, gynecologists, psychiatrist).- Outpatient specialized health services include pediatric (2 facilities), obstetric and gynecological (3 facilities); psychiatric (all).- RH and newborn care services, including ANC, PNC, institutional deliveries, prevention and management of STIs, family planning, CCSAS, infection prevention and control measures, and waste disposal. The IRC will support 2 24-hour BEmONC facilities.- MHPSS services (pharmacological and non-) in line with WHO mhGAP in all IRC facilities and MMUs. - Health education: 125 community mobilizers will carry out integrated health, MHPSS, RH, Protection, Nutrition, Early Child Development messages, conduct screening/active case finding and referrals. - Strengthening the disease surveillance system and improved epidemic preparedness and response capacity through EWARS system and HIS. - Referral pathways - inter-sectoral and between community outreach, primary and secondary health care services.SECONDARY HEALTHCARE: Pediatric in-patient care services will be delivered a 24/7 unit in Al Hol camp, addressing integrated management of childhood illnesses, intensive and critical care to stabilize critically sick children under 5, before referral to secondary health care outside Al Hol.PARTNERSHIP: IRC implements directly and in partnership with local actors. MoUs detail the responsibilities of each party (including monitoring and oversight measures) and scope of work to ensure that implementation is aligned with humanitarian principles.COORDINATION: IRC coordinates with HWG, NES Forum, HAO, civil councils and community leaders to ensure coverage, avoid duplication and build community acceptance.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk: Community and family stigmatization and discrimination of and violence against children, women and persons with specific needs receiving specialized services provision such as MHPSS, CCSASImpact: High Mitigation: Community acceptance strategy, including recruitment of community-based health educators. Ensure specialized services are properly messaged to the community. Embed specialized services in broader service-provision to de-stigmatization, using confidential and discrete spaces.Risk: Barrier to beneficiaries’ access of health services due to physical impediments (such as the selection of their location) as well as normative restrictions to access Likelihood: Medium Impact: High Mitigation: Conduct inclusion analysis, deploy MMUs to reach vulnerable populations; deploy community outreach teams to support with referrals of vulnerable individuals and integrate health service delivery across other sector responses.Risk: Targeting of IRC health facilities/MMUs Likelihood: Medium Impact: High Mitigation: IRC and partner staff trained on safety SOPs and incident reporting; community acceptance strategy; low-profile approach to branding; advocacy through Health WG, NES Forum.Risk: Inadvertent harm to GBV survivors. Likelihood: Low Impact: High Mitigation: Healthcare providers trained on CCSAS; dissemination of health educational messages; integration with IRC protection; housing of GBV services in dedicated spaces within IRC health facilities for confidentiality, privacy and discretion.Monitoring measures: IRC conducts weekly field visits to monitor all sites. MEAL staff conduct bi-monthly client satisfaction surveys at all facilities to assess satisfaction with facilities, quality of care and IRC personnel and impact of services. IRC operates a multi-channel feedback, accountability and response mechanism (FARM) to receive and address feedback. Potential breaches of IRC Code of Conduct are investigated immediately by the Ethics and Compliance Unit.

7 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

- Data protection: Confidentiality is maintained using the health information system (HIS), which is accessible only by key health staff. A designated data clerk at each facility is responsible for data entry and weekly submission to the HIS Officer. For MHPSS, identity is anonymized using codes known by the responsible doctor and key health staff only.- Community acceptance, especially when working with sensitive topics such as MH and RH: IRC build community trust through close community engagement and community mobilizers from the target locations.- KRI border closure and interruption of lifesaving health services will be mitigated through pharmaceutical contingency stock. IRC participates in NES Forum contingency preparedness and response planning.- Access/security: IRC regularly reviews and updates contingency plans for its operations inside Syria to mitigate against changes in context including multiple changes in territorial control and interruption of access. Access staff conduct negotiations with local actors, work closely with program leads to identify and manage risks and engage with local councils to avoid any interference or diversion of aid. IRC has zero tolerance for interference and\or diversion of aid to any parties to the conflict. IRC has a Security Management Plan and Standard Operating Procedures to guide IRC operations and staff to provide lifesaving assistance using the safest possible methods.
