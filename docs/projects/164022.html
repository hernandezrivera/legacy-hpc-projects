Project Summary

DRC will respond to the endemic and acute protection needs of up to 35,000 IDPs, returnees and vulnerable host community members in locations with 3 or above on the severity scale. The focus will be on at-risk women and girls, boys, men of recruitable age, the elderly and people will disabilities. The overall objective is to provide people of concern with protection and humanitarian assistance while being supported to identify durable solutions. This will be achieved through the delivery of four key outcomes: to increase the psychosocial wellbeing and reinforce coping capacities of displacement- affected individuals; to ensure displacement-affected individuals have access to rights and safe, inclusive, participatory humanitarian assistance through protection mainstreamed services; to mitigate the impact of protection threats affecting displacement-affected individuals, and to strengthen self-protective capacities and empowerment by building and supporting community-based protection mechanisms.
Structured courses that address key PSS needs will be delivered to improve psychosocial and general wellbeing, enhancing capacities to cope with the shocks and stressors of protracted displacement and acute emergencies. This will be supported by one-to-one counseling for more complex psychosocial cases requiring and other group-based activities. Safe locations will be provided where people can meet and socialize with semi-structured activities. Facilitators will be present to provide space where people can take the lead in developing social networks which can eventually extend beyond DRC operational spaces. Activities will be delivered to increase awareness of basic protection and humanitarian principles across the response through protection mainstreaming and tailored protection capacity building. Further efforts will be committed to the analysis of trends in the changing protection environment in Syria, with a view to better informing both programming and advocacy.
DRC will address the concerns of children and vulnerable men and women through the delivery of case management and individual protection assistance (IPA).
DRC will implement community-based protection involving the participation of communities to identify risks and propose solutions, offer peer support and inform programming and advocacy. This includes supporting community-based structures such as protection committees. DRC will also address the threats posed by the presence of Unexploded Ordinance in populated areas, for 50,000 IDPs, returnees and vulnerable host community members, to improve the physical safety for Syrians living in, moving through, returning to or settling in contaminated areas through life-saving Humanitarian Mine Action response

4 - Needs

13.6M men, women, boys, and girls are in need of protection in Syria (HNO, 2020). Violence, protracted displacement, chronic insecurity and widespread destruction to civilian infrastructure has left many without access to basic rights and services. Set against a backdrop of increasing economic hardship and widespread unemployment, individuals and households are at risk of resorting to harmful coping strategies. Children are at a particular risk of early marriage, child labor, and sexual violence and other forms of exploitation. Furthermore, the lack of responsive and remedial services, and a deteriorating protection environment, present signiﬁcant obstacles to achieving positive outcomes for at-risk individuals. Research shows that many women have developed their own self-protective capacity and resilience (The Syria crisis, displacement, and protection, FMR 47). The risks associated with the return of people into an altered household and community structure together with ﬁnancial hardship increases the risk of violence, abuse, neglect, and exploitation. The serious threat of explosive hazards in Syria remains largely unaddressed and represents a major protection concern that compounds civilian vulnerability. Severe explosive hazard contamination endangers or limits access to basic service infrastructures such as schools and hospitals. Approximately one-third of populated communities in Syria is estimated to be contaminated with explosive hazards (HNO 2019).These accidents can result in serious injury and death, leading to long-term physical and psychological trauma.IDPs and returnees are commonly unaware of explosive contamination risks and patterns in areas left behind, The lack of surveys on suspected hazardous areas attitudes and practices of affected populations towards explosive hazards has hampered prevention and mitigation measures.Addressing the issue of it is vital to ensuring the conditions for a safe return of Syrian IDPs and refugees are met.

5 - Implementation of activities

Qualiﬁed and trained staff in DRC community centers and other safe spaces will support persons of concern to become active agents in their own protection. The project will establish community protection structures (e.g. protection committees) to facilitate community-based initiatives to raise awareness of and identify protection risks and their mitigation. This will include individual protection assistance for people with speciﬁc protection needs in the form of in-kind support to alleviate acute concerns, as well as awareness sessions on key protection issues as identiﬁed and led by committee members. These activities will build on existing community structures possible with a view to longer-term sustainability after project cessation. DRC will enhance the protective reach of humanitarian interventions in Syria through tailored capacity building activities including training and peer support, with a focus on improving child protection services in line with minimum standards, and mainstreaming gender and protection across all sectors through the establishment of structures to monitor and evaluate achievement against mainstreaming checklists. Sustained and structured PSS will be supported by unstructured PSS to provide safe spaces for social interaction; focused on child and youth curricula and activities, positive parenting and engaging boys and men in accountable practices. Trained and dedicated staff will identify at-risk individuals and families for case management, delivered in a child-friendly space, with referral made for cases exceeding DRC technical capacity to equipped sector members. Mine action activities will include broad-based risk education targeting both communities and those who serve them, while surveys suspected hazardous areas will be undertaken to inform marking and clearance of located items capacity in Aleppo; adapting the organizational mine action model to the Syrian reality and creating synergies with DRC’s existing other sector activities.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

1.Persons of concern exposed to further harm through perceived facilitation of community mobilization (protection committees, community-based protection), Likelihood: Medium, Impact: High, Mitigation Measure: Consultation with field staff on existing structures; ensuring participants fully informed of benefits and risks; meeting in safe space identified by participants. 2. Loss of sensitive data and other personal information, Likelihood: Low, Impact: High, Mitigation Measure: Data protection policies updated with all staff trained on protocols of safe data storage and utilization adhering to international standards. SOPs on consent for data collection, storage, and utilization in place. 3. Exacerbation of existing stressors/re-traumatization, Likelihood: Low, Impact: High, Mitigation Measure: Recruitment of fully qualified staff; continual training and supervision; revision/cessation of activities in response to feedback. 4. Insufficient referral pathways exposing individuals to further harm Likelihood: Medium, Impact: High, Mitigation: Routine updating of service mapping; informal referral and follow-up mechanisms in place if no formal pathway is known/established; adherence to SOPs; clear communication to ensure expectations are managed. 5. Sexual Exploitation and Abuse. Likelihood: Low, Impact: High, Mitigation: Training (including refresher) on Code of Conduct, Child Safeguarding and PSEA policies; transparent response to SEA complaints; GBV mainstreaming in operational spaces/activities; Feedback and Complaints Reporting Mechanism. 6. safe and timely response to contamination is complicated by lack of reliable data, recent and existing contamination, and scarce availability of services, Likelihood: Medium, Impact: High Mitigation Measure: Coordination with GOS and National Security; conducting quality surveys pre-empting clearance; program and support staff trained in and dedicated to international and national minimum standards for mine action.

7 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risk: Targeting of most vulnerable not possible due to externally-imposed restrictions (e.g. access)Mitigation: Adherence to clear vulnerability criteria; geographical targeting of areas of high vulnerability (e.g. active hostilities, returnee communities, overburdened host communities); dialogue with authorities on adherence to humanitarian needs-based targeting. Assumption: Qualified, well-trained staff are available in areas of intervention Mitigation: Remuneration and non-cash benefits (e.g training) sufficient to facilitate the recruitment of staff in new areas; continual training and supervision ensured to retain and maintain quality. Risk: Humanitarian space is further squeezed with increased restrictions on access.Mitigation: Dialogue with authorities on the importance of humanitarian access and protection space; coordinated advocacy with DINGOs, Risk: significant changes in lines of control creates an influx of IDPs into DRC areas of intervention, exceeding the capacity to respond. Mitigation: Endorsed Emergency Preparedness and Response Plan in place; an emergency roster of capacitated staff available. Risk: DRC fails to obtain relevant national and local permissions or authorizations from GOS including national security to conduct survey, mapping or clearance activities Mitigation: Clear agreement on activities including any restrictions to DRC’s mine action activities in the form of an MoU and additional formal agreements; Ongoing, regular liaison with GOS at different administrative levels to maintain transparent and positive relationship; Establish clear and expansive reporting frameworks between DRC, GOS on ongoing activities; Engage UNMAS in data analysis to benefit GOS’s broader mine action strategy and activities in-country.
