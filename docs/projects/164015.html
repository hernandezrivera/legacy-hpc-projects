Project Summary

Close to eight years of conflict have severely stretched the capacity and resilience of people living in Syria to cope with shocks and stresses brought about by the crisis. The loss of jobs and livelihoods, the depletion of productive assets and savings, increasing debt, the destruction of civilian infrastructure, large-scale displacement and the disruption of social services have all contributed to increased economic hardship that have eroded people’s ability to cope. The deterioration of basic services continues to undermine the viability of productive sectors, feeding a vicious cycle of unemployment, diminishing resources and resulting in increased levels of poverty. Deprived of the productive tools required to meet their basic needs and in the absence of state-supported basic services, many households are resorting to improvised solutions to address their needs. The Early Recovery and Livelihoods (ERL) sector estimates that 13 million people in Syria are in need of humanitarian assistance of whom 9.8 million are in acute need. This compares to a 2017 figure of 13.8 million, of whom 10 million were in acute need.
The main trigger of food insecurity is uncertainty, depleted livelihood opportunities, poor access to food, decreased crop production, and livestock decline. Areas with a higher concentration of IDPs have higher rates of food insecurity, reaching up to 54% of the population. Approximately 50% of the families have reduced the number of meals they consume daily. More than 30% of the reduced food consumption by adults so that children can eat.
The humanitarian organizations continue to appeal to all parties in control of displacement areas and of A’zaz district to adhere to their obligations under IHL and IHRL, and to allow internally displaced persons to either return voluntarily, in safety and with dignity, to their homes or places of habitual residence or to resettle voluntarily in another part of the country. The frequently reported lack of official civil documentation, due to loss or destruction, remains a key concern, and the fact that partners specialized in civil documentation has not yet been able to reach several areas of displacement makes it challenging to address this assistance gap.
A’zaz is a district of Aleppo Governorate in northern Syria. centuries the city of A’zaz the district had a population of 251,769 as host communities and 167,463 IDPs. The district lies between Afrin District to the west, Mount Simeon District to the south and al-Bab District to the east
Coming to IDPs into A’zaz, OCHA’s reports “CCCM Cluster “indicate that displaced people from East Ghouta, southern Damascus, and other locations, where local agreements resulted in the evacuation of large numbers of people, moved to A’zaz district as it hosted about “167,463” INDs after arrival in northern Syria.

4 - Needs

Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO ) reported in December 2018 that: An estimated 14.5 million people in Syria need humanitarian assistance. A deep economic recession, fluctuating national currency, soaring food prices, disrupted markets and food insecurity have contributed to Syrians’ extreme vulnerability. More than 15 million people – over half of Syria’s population – have fled their homes, including 6.6 million who are internally displaced and 4.8 million who have sought refuge in neighboring countries as of December 2016.Food production in Syria has hit a record low. Approximately 7 million people are projected to be food insecure in 2018 and a further 2 million are at risk of food insecurity. Agriculture-based livelihoods face severe constraints across the value chain – from production to market. These include reduced availability and increased cost of farming inputs (seeds, fertilizers, animal feed, veterinary supplies, etc.); damage to farming equipment and infrastructure (including irrigation, storage, and seed processing facilities), and limited veterinary supplies and services; internal population displacement and restricted access to land. Crop production has been adversely affected by the scarcity and high prices of agricultural inputs and fuel, low-quality inputs, damaged irrigation infrastructure, shortages and high cost of farm labor, and loss of or damage to standing crops. The livestock sector has suffered substantially since 2011 with reductions in terms of herd and flock numbers of over 50 percent for cattle and over 45 percent for sheep and goats, while poultry, the main and most affordable source of protein of animal origin, has shrunk by 60 percent mostly due to unavailability of poultry feed at affordable cost.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

Although all Syrian population has been heavily affected by the current crisis, due to traditional local norms, women are exposed to additional protection related risks. All types of violence are cited as occurring in opposition areas of Syria, with domestic violence, sexual exploitation and child marriage as the top three. While sexual exploitation is somehow new, child marriage and domestic violence are often normalized and ingrained in the culture. Women and girls are exposed to structural and societal risks as the lack of safe spaces and public transportation, limited freedom of movement, poverty, unemployment, displacement, the stress associated with war, gender role change, lack of documents. All these poses enormous pressure, men feeling they failed in protecting and securing a good life for their families. With men perceiving women as a threat to their control due to gender roles change, domestic violence has increased in Syria. Whereas men's actions are explained as a result of stress, women are often blamed for provoking the violence or allowing it to happen. Parental violence against children has also been reported as increasing. Displacement and living in camps are noted as being particularly unsafe environments for women/girls due to the lack of privacy (in tents or in latrines), overcrowding and mixing of people, poverty, financial desperation, and lawlessness. Women and girls occupy a central role in Al-Ameen for Humanitarian Support (AHS)’s strategy for 2018-2023. Part of the development approach, Al-Ameen seeks to empower women to become more resilient in the conflict setting while securing their safety at all levels. When designing the project, Al-Ameen has applied all its security protocols to ensure that interviewed GBV survivors and leaders of communities are not exposed to any risk for their involvement. For this purpose, Al-Ameen and its partners have a set of policies and procedures to be respected when working with GBV cases.

7 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks:1- Deterioration of the security situation or escalation of conflict/natural disaster preventing access to the project site.2- The threat of warring parties 3- Corruption and diversion of assistance.4- Drought and other climate-related risks.5-Conflict around access to project support. Mitigation measures:1- In case the situation requires temporary suspension or adjustment or permanent suspension of activities in a given area, the consortium, through the PMU will immediately inform the NMFA and discuss options for re-programming. 2- Al-Ameen Security department coordinates and constantly monitor the security situation.3- Al-Ameen staff is trained on accountability, and, wherever possible, feedback and complaint mechanisms are established. 4- Large areas of Syria have been affected by recurrent drought with a severe negative impact on agricultural livelihoods and food security, worsened by lack of availability/affordability.Al-Ameen mitigates this risk by ensuring that agricultural inputs are locally adapted (e.g. seeds), and by promoting adapted production techniques.Community infrastructure rehabilitation/construction projects (implemented through CFW) could include water infrastructure, including irrigation systems.Farmers and agricultural laborers immediately affected by the impact of the drought will be supported to develop coping strategies that help them better adapt to recurrent droughts.5-Discussions and awareness-raising sessions are organized for local stakeholders, and community members to identify and address perceptions around resource allocation and related risks.
