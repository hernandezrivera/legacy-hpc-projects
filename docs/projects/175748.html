Project Summary

This intervention is in line with the 2020 Food Security and Livelihood cluster Strategic Objectives (SOs); (1) Physical and mental wellbeing, (2) Living standards and (3) Coping mechanisms, resilience & recovery as well as the FSL Cluster Objectives (COs) two on complementary emergency cropping, livelihood & livestock support (cropping/ veg/ fish/ livestock) and CO three on ability to recover and absorb shocks (resilience) through training in improved agronomic practices and vaccination & treatment of livestock .
Rural Community Action for Peace and Development targets 432,503 individuals (72,084 HHs) of whom 50% are female, 54% children and 15% will be persons living with different disabilities.
The food insecurity levels is high and will be aggravated by depleted stocks, extremely high food prices, and a reduction in the availability of livestock products as cattle move away from homesteads in search of water and pasture; few livestock returning since there was extensive losses to floods. Additionally, the impacts of regional and national COVID-19 control measures is distressing market functionality and the economic crisis goes on as well as year on year inflation. Insecurity as well as disruption of infrastructure by rains e.g. bad road and airstrip conditions affecting access to humanitarian assistance as well as market access and functionality. There will also be early depletion of cereal stocks even before the onset of the lean season because of conflict and climatic shocks that led to low crop production and lack of compensatory effects of livestock products resulting from the serious animal losses and flood caused deaths. Finally unavailability of wild foods and fish. (IPC Dec 2020, ECHO daily flash 11th Dec 2020, 27th Jan, 2021).
In the period of Dec 2020 to March 2021, Upper Nile State, 58.8% of the population will be in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse. Maiwut will be (IPC Phase 4). In April to July 2021 projection, 70.8% of the population will be at Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse; with Baliet, Panyikang, Maiwut) falling at Emergency (IPC Phase 4) and Malakal which will stand Crisis (IPC Phase 3). In CE, (Morobo and Lainya), Dec 2020 to March 2021 projection estimates 66,000 people at Emergency (IPC Phase 4). In April to July 2021, an estimated 778,000 people (51.6% of the population) will be at Crisis (IPC Phase 3) with 227,000 at Emergency (IPC Phase 4). Morobo will be at Emergency (IPC Phase 4) and Lainya will be at Crisis (IPC Phase 3). In Jonglei (Canal/ Pigi), the period of Dec 2020 to March 2021, an estimated 1.55 M people (78.3% of the population) will be at Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse whereby 719,000 will be at an Emergency (IPC Phase 4) and 11,000 people will likely be in 10 Catastrophe (IPC Phase 5). April to July 2021 projection will be worse with 1.68 M people (84.7% of the population) at Crisis (IPC Phase 3) of whom 792,000 at Emergency (IPC Phase 4. During this period, the entire State will be classified in Emergency (IPC Phase 4).
The organization seeks to contribute to addressing some of these drivers of food insecurity through provision of agriculture, fisheries and livestock livelihood inputs and training.

1 - Needs

In the periods October to November 2020, December2020 to March 2021 and April to July 2021, an estimated 6.35, 5.82 and 7.24 million people respectively are projected to be facing acute food insecurity (IPC, Dec 2020). The HNO 2021 estimates 7.7 M People to be in need, a sharp rise (compared to between 2017-2021) with 8% in stress, 33% severe and 59% at extreme level in the coming months with significant increase of people facing catastrophic or famine-likely conditions of whom 50% are Female, 54% are children and 15% are persons with different disabilities. In 2021, there was a rise in people in need by 108,000 people, double of 2020 people in need; with the -2021 lean season projected to be more severe. The severity levels (Crisis, emergency and humanitarian catastrophe) projected for April- July 2021 for the three proposed States stands at: Upper Nile 70.8%, Jonglei 84.7%, and Central Equitoria 51.6%.The deteriorating food insecurity situation in 2021 is because of compounded shocks experienced by South Sudan driven by insecurity, multiple population displacements, declined crop production, diseases and pests, the economic crisis, the effects of COVID-19, limited access to basic services, and the cumulative effects of prolonged years of asset depletion and loss of livelihoods as well as triple shock of intensified conflict and sub-national violence, climatic shocks- a second consecutive year of major flooding which has caused destruction of property, infrastructure, crops and displaced people and livestock. In addition, there is already existing high levels of different vulnerabilities caused by complete asset depletion and impoverishment experienced since the start of the protracted crisis – such as the continued and protracted macro-economic crisis, accompanied by currency depreciation. This has also resulted in high food prices, eroded household purchasing power and made food unaffordable for a significant proportion of the population. Constrain of humanitarian access experienced in 2020 which saw 9 humanitarian aid workers killed has continued into 2021 with by 14th February 2021, 4 humanitarian workers have already been killed! (IPC Dec 2020, HNO/HRP2021/ CFSAM May 2020/ UN OCHA/ DG ECHO).On the same note, South Sudan is the 4th highest globally on the 2021 INFORM Index for Risk Management. The top five factors – (which are also the main drivers of food insecurity) that make South Sudan a high risk country are: low HDI and poverty indices at position 185 ( considering SDG2, SDG 3, SDG 4.3 and SDG 4.6, high intensity of national and sub-national violent conflict, as well as high economic dependency on aid and humanitarian assistance (HNO 2021/ 2020 HDI Report office).Of another high concern, is the nutrition status of the Greater Upper Nile and Jonglei where RuCAPD plans to intervene. According to the IPC AMN scale, 53 of the 78 counties are in IPC AMN Phase 3 (Serious) or higher. Of which 29 stand at IPC AMN Phase 4 (Critical) and 24 at IPC AMN Phase 3 (Serious). An estimate of 80% of the counties in IPC AMN Phase 4 (Critical) are in the Greater Upper Nile. The increase in counties in IPC AMN Phase 3 (Serious) or higher above and the overall severity with 9 more counties classified as IPC AMN Phase 4 (Critical) in 2020. Out of these, 5 counties are in Upper Nile State while 4 are in Jonglei State (1) (IPC Dec 2020/ FSMNS 26).To ensure that we leave o one behind, RuCAPD envisions a zero hunger South Sudan. The proposed interventions aims at contributing to this.

2 - Project activities

 Emergency food production: main season agriculture seeds & tool distribution; fishing, vegetable & crop kits;  Nutrition vouchers (plus poultry & shoat distribution) Emergency food & livelihood support: fishing kits/ vegetable kits; rapid response kits (fish kit/ cow pea & three variety of fast maturing vegetable seed) Livestock treatment and vaccination; livestock disease surveillance, Community Animal Health Workers Training and inputs support, cold chain establishment and maintenance, prevention & treatment Livelihood support to returnee households Provision of timely food security information Support to seed fairs using locally sourced seed; seed security & multiplication strengthening/ Support market functionality through assisting traders to re-supply markets/ local procurement for FAO ITFs & aggregation of produce for WFP assistance;  Support value chain addition through agriculture, fishery and livestock products/ by-products preservation & marketing Training & capacity building: both adaptive and absorptive on-farm production (farmer/agro-pastoral field schools, demonstration plots), income generation (business skills), vocational training etc.  Multi sectoral models: livelihoods, nutrition sensitive food security, hygiene, sanitation Reducing pest and crop disease including FAW through surveillance, detection, prevention & eradication Reduction in postharvest losses and introduction of appropriate preservation technologies through training and provision of hermetic storage bags. Diversification & restoration of livelihoods; small grants for petty trade; village saving & loan schemes & micro finance etc. Use of local biomass briquettes & fuel-efficient stoves  Training HH on proper safe management of food supply chain Trainings and interventions to promote market access Training natural resource management Enable conflict related meetings to strengthen local peace structures

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

This aspect is well embedded in our community communication and engagement strategy. RuCAPD ensures that the affected population knows and upholds their rights and dignity throughout our interventions. No favors – whatsoever-are to be exchanged for the services offered. It is totally based on the standard FAO (and agreed upon during beneficiary selection stage) criteria.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

This project takes into consideration all the feedback from people ( men, women, boys and girls) with different vulnerabilities including the elderly, persons with disability. And using available referral pathways to close the loop on all feedback and complaints registered by the the different categories and vulnerability of the people we serve.Rural Community Action for Peace and Development clearly recognizes that more substantive efforts toward inclusion of persons with disabilities will need to continue concurrently in relation to how disability issues are not only considered but holistically included in all our projects / intervention designs, monitored and evaluated. This involves recognizing the need for a wider capacity building effort focused on disability inclusion not only throughout all our interventions. One of the selection criteria for registration as a vulnerable household is having or headed by someone living with disability and elderly persons and therefore will be eligible to receive inputs during the distribution.Both formal and informal feedback/ complaint collection strategy will be used by RuCAPD. Since our staff are within the community, the field visits and home visits to the beneficiaries is one of the avenues we use to obtain feedback.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

RuCAPD aims to work with all the stakeholders especially the communities we serve to shape all the decisions and actions in relation to challenges, problems, opportunities, outcomes as well as solutions. It is about having the communities actively participate and be consulted throughout the project cycles.During assessment and project initiation and indeed throughout the project cycle, it will be clearly communicated that their feedback or complaints are highly welcome. The setting up of feedback/complaint desks at the different distribution sites is also key. Throughout our intervention, RuCAPD is guided by the following community engagement principles: • Integrity: RuCAPD upholds integrity in our engagement with the community. We are transparent and clear in scope and purpose of all our interventions. • Inclusiveness: Community engagement is accessible and balanced therefore capturing a full range of values and perspectives in the community. • Dialogue: in our Community engagement, we promote dialogue as well as open genuine discussions supported by timely and accurate information; which we use to weigh the options we have, and develop common understanding on way forward.• Influence: Community engagement is always reflected in outcomes. The community is able to see and clearly understand the impact of their involvement in the intervention.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Feedback, (positive and negative) from the population we serve is invaluable. It is one of the AAP pillars too. RuCAPD, has mechanisms in place to help with this. We have community communication agents at all the project sites. Their main function is to collect all feedback from the community and channel it to the concerned personnel for further communication and action as well as receiving feedback on the community concerns and closing the loop by reporting back to the community on the way forward/ action taken. Use of local language, chief’s barazas to deal with matters arising as well as use the public address systems to announce issues concerning the interventions. Currently, we have suggestion boxes at our distribution sites (managed by two officers -one male one female) to help people communicate their concerns. Both formal and informal feedback and complaints are duly registered and dealt with to ensure closing the loop. In case of issues beyond our intervention for example health matters, we refer to the relevant partners/institution and ensure necessary action has been taken.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

RuCAPD interventions are geared towards inclusion of all gender, age and diversity- disabled and the chronically ill members of the community. This is key in helping avoid exclusion of women and girls in consultations and decision making. RuCAPD has a working PSEA policy which is upheld at all levels and the principles set forth in this policy apply: To all RuCAPD staff, whether expatriates or national; At all times, i.e. during and outside of office hours and during periods of leave. To all people or entities or stakeholders who have signed a contract with RuCAPD: consultants, day laborers, suppliers as well as partner organisations.On the same note, RuCAPD is part of the founding and steering committee of Nuswan Giddam which is a movement that seeks to change the narrative about the woman and girl of South Sudan. That South Sudanese women and girls are born to lead. Lack of access to/awareness of referral services for survivors of protection incidents (GBV, child protection, for example) - RuCAPD is part of the Protection cluster, child protection sub cluster as well as the GBV sub cluster. and will also work hand in hand with the other protection partners in our areas of operation like Nile Hope, DRC, RI,UNHCR,AHC, HDC, SSUDA among others to ensure that matters protection are amicably and ably dealt with. Beneficiary registration is done after the criteria has been clearly explained during mobilization and project awareness. This is based on the FAO selection criteria. After clearly agreeing and disagreeing with the different stakeholders including the local authorities, representatives of the different vulnerable groups (women, the chronically ill (e.g. HIV/AIDs) youth, people living with disability, the elderly) as well as religious leaders. Therefore all the selected beneficiaries will be registered accordingly.Contamination of targeted project areas with explosive hazards- Bearing in mind that some of our areas of intervention have faced the brunt of the protracted crisis, the risk of explosive hazards is real. We will continue working together with UNMASS and G4s and other partners in this field to ensure the safety of our staff as well as the community whom we serve. Where land is required for purposes such as group farms and demonstration plots, written consent from the owners if private or local authorities if communal will be sought before use of such lands. Beneficiaries risk to violence during registration and distribution will be minimized by having teams assisting women and men on separate desks. The distribution will also be restricted to between 8am and 5pm to allow the beneficiaries to reach home in time- for the women to perform their family roles and avoid GBV caused by delays at the distribution sites; the timing also helps in safeguarding against abduction ( of boys and girls)as these perpetrators fear daylight. The number of Distribution points will also be increased to make sure beneficiaries do not travel over long distances to access our services. This will also be helpful in this era of covid-19 as it goes a long way into making work flow faster.

12.1.1. Decreased prevalence of global acute malnutrition among children under age 5 in severity levels 3 and 4

To reduce the increase in counties in IPC AMN Phase 3 (Serious) or higher above and the overall severity RuCAPD will not only integrate our intervention with setting up gardens at the nutrition centres but also ensure all pregnant and lactating women with children under 1000 days are considered to benefit. This is because to acute malnutrition is caused by high prevalence of diseases (36%) and poor or lack of quality and diversity of food (where the MAD is 7%, MDD is 15%). The eminent food insecurity (IPC AFI Phase 3 and higher)in these states/counties contribute to acute malnutrition. According to UNICEF/WHO/World Bank Joint Child Malnutrition Estimates, 2020 edition, nearly half of all deaths in children under 5 are attributable to malnutrition; malnutrition puts children at greater risk of dying from common infections, increases the frequency and severity of such infections, and delays recovery. Measures of child malnutrition are used to track development progress. In the Post-2015 Development Era, estimates of child malnutrition will help determine whether South Sudan as a country is on track to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals – particularly, goal 2 to “end hunger, achieve food security and improved nutrition, and promote sustainable agriculture”. The onset of COVID-19 in 2020 further makes it harder to achieve these goals especially as the nutritional status of the most vulnerable population groups is likely to deteriorate further due to the health and socio-economic impacts of the pandemic. Overcoming hunger and malnutrition in all its forms, including under nutrition, micronutrient deficiencies and wasting, is about more than securing enough food to survive: what people – and particularly children – eat must also be nutritious. A healthy diet costs far more than US$ 1.90/day, the international poverty threshold. Yet 80% of the population in South Sudan is classified as poor- therefore cannot afford a healthy diet.
