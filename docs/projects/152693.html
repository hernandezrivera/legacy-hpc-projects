Project Summary

Gender based violence (GBV) is a pervasive, protection and human rights issue that is often exacerbated in emergency contexts. As stated at the IASC guidelines for integrating Gender Based Violence Interventions in Humanitarian Action, while the existence of GBV may be undocumented in any given emergency, research has shown that incidences of GBV are underreported and occur regardless of the presence or absence of evidence. While humanitarian actors acknowledge the importance of systematic prevention, mitigation and response to GBV, there are still many gaps in our system that prevent this from happening.
As a leading INGO in the fight against hunger, AAH will contribute to addressing these main barriers by targeting global and national humanitarian nutrition coordination mechanisms. AAH is a member of the Global Nutrition Cluster, and is also an active member of national nutrition coordination mechanisms across 45 countries, reaching a total of 14.9 million direct beneficiaries from 2010 to 2015. AAH is thus a legitimate and powerful actor that can make an impact within its level of influence by addressing some of the gaps identified in outcome number 2 of the GBV Call to Action.
More specifically, this project aims to establish a solid foundation to strengthen GBV coordination mechanisms within the nutrition cluster and national nutrition coordination mechanisms by strengthening accountability and providing effective leadership through example. Thus, the project will contribute to Indicator 2a of the call to action “85% of global level GBV Area of Responsibility members satisfied with leadership, performance, and accountability of the global humanitarian GBV coordination mechanism by 2020”.

This project will also proactively promote effective local leadership of and robust engagement in assessment, planning, implementation and monitoring of GBV and gender equality interventions; one of the key actions identified at outcome 2 of the GBV call to action. In order to do so, 3 countries of operation will be targeted within the AAH international network, one in East Africa, one in West Africa and one in Asia. The criteria used to select pilot countries will include composition of beneficiaries (50% of beneficiaries being refugees), vulnerability of affected population, access to beneficiaries and the interest of the country office and nutrition organizations nationally in hosting the pilot experience.

Needs

Gender based violence (GBV) is a pervasive, protection and human rights issue that is often exacerbated in emergency contexts. As stated at the IASC guidelines for integrating Gender Based Violence Interventions in Humanitarian Action, while the existence of GBV may be undocumented in any given emergency, research has shown that incidences of GBV are underreported and occur regardless of the presence or absence of evidence. While humanitarian actors acknowledge the importance of systematic prevention, mitigation and response to GBV, there are still many gaps in our system that prevent this from happening. 34.4% of respondents indicated that GBV occurred and the most common form of GBV was domestic violence at 25.2% closely followed child marriages at 21.02%, rape and attempted rape at 13.7%. The category of people most vulnerable to GBV were middle-aged women at 28%, young female adults at 27% and female children at 15.2%. The existing mechanisms for addressing GBV are largely based on local customs, the most prevalent being negotiation by elders at 44.8%. A number of GBV cases were adjudicated in a court of law, and the perpetrator was jailed (15.1%) and there were situations where nothing was done (16.1%). The system of traditional courts is quite strong at community level and this explains why they handle most of the GBV cases. Nonetheless, it has been questioned whether customary law addresses the rights of the victim. These courts commonly rule very lightly against the perpetrators of GBV offences: they either pay a fine or have the option of marrying the survivor (who is then forced into marriage). Which confirms a matter of concern that a large proportion of respondents (15.1%) indicated that there are instances that nothing was done to address the violation. This can be associated with fears faced by the victim of retaliation and the stigma attached to having been a victim of GBV acts such as rape. ACF USA has established a solid foundation for the prevention and mitigation of GBV within its organizational priorities through pushing forward an organizational gender agenda. ACF USA has taken many steps forward, including the institutionalization of a gender policy and capacity building of all staff to create an organizational culture that promotes gender equality at all levels. With this momentum, ACF USA is well positioned to explore GBV and gender challenges within our scope of programmatic interventions.

Activities

In order to consolidate the advances reached so far and to move a GBV agenda forward with respect to the institutional commitments outlined in the GBV Call to Action, the following needs have been identified: - Large scale roll out of Gender Minimum Standards and gender analysis methodologies across 45 countries (for ACF USA and national partner organizations)- Adapt key technical and institutional tools and methodologies to ensure GBV and gender considerations are mainstreamed across the organization- Build capacity of ACF USA and national partner organization staff to ensure methodologies and tools are correctly used and implemented- Pilot the use of adapted tools and share results and best practices internally and externally through humanitarian coordination mechanisms The analysis conducted by ACF USA on GBV within the humanitarian sector identified the following major barriers to reaching the targets set out in the call to action on protection from GBV in emergencies:- Key GBV prevention and mitigation principles followed by humanitarian agencies are articulated as tips and recommendations (such as the IASC GBV guidelines). There is a subsequent gap between the GBV tips and recommendations and its translation into field methodologies. - GBV is not considered a life saving issue, and thus a priority, within certain cluster such as the nutrition cluster. - GBV initiatives are mainly coordinated at the Global Protection Cluster, the GBV Area of Responsibility and the Sub-working group on Gender and Humanitarian Action. - Other clusters and working groups are less involved in GBV prevention, mitigation and response initiatives which results in upholding less accountability towards it. While other technical clusters are aware of responsibilities towards GBV in emergencies they are often either lacking adequate resources and/or have multiple mainstreaming initiatives to manage with their membership (ie. GBV, gender, AAP etc.) As a leading INGO in the fight against hunger, ACF USA will contribute to addressing these main barriers by targeting global and national humanitarian nutrition coordination mechanisms. Does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

Yes

How does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

The project staff engages with beneficiaries of the OTP/TSFP sites and the catchment communities through focus group discussions and individual consultations with gender/age diverse groups and individuals. For other organisations that benefit from this project, it provides information through direct consultations and workshops on the integration of accountability for GBV in nutrition. Internally, ACF USA provides information to its staff through circulars, workshops, meetings and publications.

Are messages context-specific or/and tailored?

Yes

Briefly describe how

The messages are specific to the context of South Sudan and provision of nutrition services and its relation to the community and gender dynamics within the population. Each group receives a tailored message (language, description, mode of delivery) depending on whether we are talking to women at the nutrition site / community, men and boys in the community, partner organisations or internally within ACF USA.

This is a GBV prevention and response project. In addition, Action Against Hunger implements it PSEA policy through a robust internal system and ensures reporting on any identify cases.

Nature of the consultations

Yes

Briefly describe the nature of the consultations

The project staff has spoken with the stakeholders at the grassroots level, as well as national actors (clusters, MoH / Nutrition department), partner organisations and internationally with the Global Nutrition Cluster. The project has takin into account their concerns and priorities. Participants from a wide range of communities and gender and age groups participate in mass sensitization and awareness campaigns conducted by the organization. For example, during such campaigns, women and men are involved in the selection of safe distribution points and nutrition sites to reduce associated risks (distance, location, facility).

Protection Risk Analysis?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Action Against Hunger in South Sudan conducts Safety Audits and Gender Analyses in order to assess protection risks associated with its nutrition program. For example, a recent safety audit identified waiting time at nutrition sites as protection risks for women caregivers and children accessing those services. In 2019, Action Against Hunger will assess and pilot different tools and methods in order to mitigate this waiting time, such as looking at the flow of service delivery within the actual site operations.Action Against Hunger mainstreams this analysis in its work with partner organisations and stakeholders, so that findings and good practices can be integrated across the interventions of agencies delivering nutrition and humanitarian programs.

Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

The project’s main purpose is to identify and addresses barriers to access that different population groups could face in relation to the project, especially in relation to women and children. Safety audit at the site level, consultations within communities through the focus groups, as well as consultations with partner organisations benefiting from the training and commonly identified tools, all ensure that all partners identify and address access barriers in their programs.

Does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Part of this project is the conducting of the Safety Audit, which specifically measures access of beneficiaries to nutrition and WaSH services of other programs based and barriers which are related to Gender-Based Violence and other forms of physical insecurity. The project’s Gender Analysis component measures the access of beneficiaries based on prescribed gender roles and responsibilities.

Does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Action Against Hunger in South Sudan conducts Safety Audits and Gender Analyses in order to assess protection risks associated with its nutrition program. Safety risks related to GBV and physical insecurity are identified and measured in all areas of intervention. The Safety Audit also proposes solutions for such safety risks, such as the training of staff on GBV, improvement of flow at nutrition sites to mitigate waiting time, and increased awareness raising among both gender groups to enable safe mobility of women and children to nutrition sites.

Does the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

Yes

How the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

Through its survey mechanisms, the project looks at beneficiaries’ quality of life and experience of violence related to gender and conflict. As dignity holds a highly personal definition, beneficiaries are consulted on an individual bases and in through focus group discussions to capture their specific experiences and level of satisfaction. Therefore, the overall level of dignity maintained by beneficiaries can be deduced from such information. Additionally, a major component of this project is the engagement with national organization and actors, whose perceptions are those of the population of South Sudan. Therefore, their point of views are highly weighted in the overall design of the project’s outputs.
