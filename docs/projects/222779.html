Project Summary

"The IRC strengthens the protective factors and reduces child protection risks through its integrated and holistic program which anchors on socio-ecological approach. Activities to enhance the protection of and response to neglect, abuse, violence and exploitation will be conducted in camps and host communities in Cox’s Bazar, Bangladesh. It will focus on critical interventions and services tailored to the needs of children of varying ages, genders, and diverse groups with the primary targets being Unaccompanied and Separated Children (UASC), child-headed households, survivors of child marriage, domestic violence, and child labor. IRC's CP program encompases:- partnership with DSS contributing to establish community-based child protection mechanisms, strengthen capacities of social workers and deliver case management. IRC will support DSS with more social workers for case management and on government leadership within the Child Protection sub-sector for better coordination in the response. The project is well aligned to CPSS objectives. Reaching 89552 children—42336 Rohingya (20769 girls, 21586 boys) across 5 camps and 47217 host community children (22493 girls, 24232 boys).
In addition, provide CP case management to child survivors and those at-risk of violence and use CPIMS+, psychosocial support, community-driven child protection, life skills education for adolescents, positive parenting, and capacity-building initiatives. Using IRC’s Safe@home, and ""Now I am Stronger"" modules, psychosocial support strengthens children’s resilience and coping skills. The Safe Healing and Learning Space (SHLS) offers inclusive, structured environments where children, including those with disabilities, can engage in recreational and supportive activities. To ensure inclusive CP program, IRC will conduct surveys using Washington Set questionnaires to identify children with disabilities, for inclusion as project participants. Community-Based Child Protection Mechanisms (CBCPM) empower community members to recognize and address child protection risks, supported by technical and operational guidance. Adolescents benefit from life skills education that builds leadership and social cohesion, helping counter child marriage and child labor risks. Caregivers participate in positive parenting sessions based on the Family Makes the Difference module, promoting nurturing, non-violent practices, while Early Childhood Development (ECD) programs aid young children’s social and learning skills. A community outreach initiative will also raise awareness of child protection issues. IRC will support the development of a robust Child Protection Situation Monitoring system, working closely with the CPSS and using KIIs and FGD.
The IRC embraces its role as SAG member in CPSS, a member of case management technical working group and collaborating with local government and child protection agencies. IRC aims to strengthen a cohesive, sector-wide response. integrating community feedback, IRC ensures that program design and monitoring reflect children’s, caregivers’, and community members’ voices, promoting transparency, accountability, and alignment with community priorities. This project will establish safe, inclusive spaces where boys and girls can participate in life-skills training and psychosocial support, overcoming gender-specific barriers such as child marriage and hazardous labor. Engaging caregivers and community leaders will promote positive attitudes towards the children and gender-equitable norms, supporting girls’ participation in community-based child protection activities. With continuous gender analysis and adaptive programming, the project will meet evolving needs, creating secure environments that protect children, advocate for their rights, and challenge harmful gender norms in Rohingya and host communities."

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

The Rohingya, the world's largest stateless population, have faced decades of severe persecution, systematic discrimination, and targeted violence by successive Myanmar governments, leading to their mass displacement to Bangladesh, where over 1 million (including new arrival) now reside. The 2024 Joint Inter Sector Needs Assessment reveals critical child protection gaps, as 66.5% of Rohingya children remain excluded from community-based child protection programs, lacking vital support networks that increase their vulnerability to various risks.According to the 2024 Child Protection Sub-Sector Situation Analysis, child labor significantly restricts educational access, with 65.57% of respondents citing it as a barrier, particularly hazardous for boys. Child marriage also persists, with 33.95% acknowledging its prevalence. The 2024 CPIMS+ analysis underscores child protection issues, highlighting neglect (31.86%), psychological distress (11.57%), physical abuse (10.48%), emotional abuse (7.84%), and child labor (7.58%) as critical concerns. Cases of child marriage (4.53%) and unaccompanied children (5.18%) further stress the need for targeted interventions. Reports of children associated with armed groups, though rare (0.09%), underscore the urgency of protective measures.Child Protection Situation Monitoring (CPSM) data similarly reveal pressing concerns for both Rohingya and host children. Over half (56.25%) of respondents acknowledge children's psychological problems, with 38.84% rating them as severe. Child labor is widely reported (62.5%), further emphasizing the prevalence of hazardous labor. Additionally, child marriage affects 25% of the communities surveyed, while 61.61% observe caregiver neglect, and 52.23% note instances of child violence. The Q1 2024 Joint Protection Monitoring Report reinforces these findings, with 74% of respondents indicating children’s psychological problems (53% severe), 74% reporting child labor involvement, 40% aware of child marriage cases, and 23% citing child neglect.These findings illustrate pervasive child protection concerns and child rights violations affecting Rohingya and host communities. Decades of displacement have left Rohingya children vulnerable, as evidenced by 66.5% lacking access to community-based child protection programming. Child labor, especially among boys, severely limits educational opportunities, while child marriage continues to undermine girls’ well-being and prospects. CPSM and other assessments indicate severe psychological distress, neglect, and violence, with 74% of respondents noting psychological concerns and high incidences of hazardous labor and child marriage.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

"The IRC will prioritize protection mainstreaming by embedding core principles such as safety and dignity, prevention of harm, meaningful access, accountability, participation, and empowerment within its interventions. By enhancing the capacity of partners in protection mainstreaming, IRC aims to uphold clients' rights, ensure diverse populations have easy access to services, and establish effective participation and accountability mechanisms. This strategy fosters staff sensitivity to the ""do no harm"" principle and empowers communities to take control of their lives. IRC will mainstream protection at other programme internally and externally by ensuring meaningfull access, dignity of the beneficiaries, accountability and participation. Each activity will be reviewed to identify and mitigate child protection risks, promoting high-quality humanitarian programming sensitive to age, gender, disability, and cultural contexts. The project will integrate protection sectors—Child Protection, Gender-Based Violence, and Gender Programming—with other IRC sectors and partners, focusing on safeguarding mechanisms for various protection concerns. Continuous assessments will be conducted through a protection mainstreaming lens to identify program limitations and develop a Protection Mainstreaming Action Plan (PMAP). Regular reviews of the PMAP will facilitate tracking progress and identifying new opportunities, guiding actions to minimize negative impacts and maximize positive outcomes. IRC will collaborate with protection mainstreaming focal points to ensure the harmonized implementation of mainstreaming programming across project locations.

2.3 - Age & Disability

The IRC will integrate age and disability considerations throughout the project cycle, ensuring the active involvement of diverse individuals, including people with disabilities. With a strong presence in the Age and Disability network within the ISCG. IRC maintains robust relationships with other specialized organization CDD as technical partner that will be provided technical in this regard. The project will facilitate service referrals in the sector, working group, partnership, and camp levels. All IRC tools and formats will be reviewed through a gender-diverse lens to ensure that diversity and inclusion considerations are adequately integrated, and staff will receive training on Gender, Equality, Diversity, and Inclusion (GEDI). Protection mainstreaming will help identify risks faced by diverse populations and guide delivery service tailored to their needs. The integrated programming approach will enhance cross-referrals and allow for individualized assistance to beneficiaries.IRC will assess the potential risks and needs of clients concerning age and disability, developing a risk matrix with strategies to prevent, minimize, or mitigate these risks. Services will be designed to address specific needs, supporting individuals over 18 with multiple diagnoses through comprehensive assessments, providing high sensitivity care for children aged 0 to 5, and offering culturally sensitive content and services for adolescent boys and girls. Interventions for children and adolescents will be contextualized and customized to reflect the varying age groups. The project will also ensure that the activities consider children and adults with disabilities, and diverse groups will be prioritized in staff recruitment and the selection of project staff and volunteers

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

The IRC will implement a transparent beneficiary selection process that actively involves affected populations in setting priorities and will maintain regular communication to clearly explain selection criteria. A comprehensive community participation plan will guide evaluation activities, with results shared in accessible formats, and annual consultations will inform ongoing improvements. The project will address cross-sectoral needs by providing community-friendly access to response plans and validating JRP priorities through consultations with diverse groups reflecting age, gender, and vulnerability. Through a coordinated strategy, clear information on completed and upcoming assistance, as well as actions taken on community feedback, will be widely disseminated. Community perceptions on quality, timeliness, and inclusivity will be regularly monitored through Complaint and Feedback Response Mechanisms (CFRMs). Communities will remain engaged in all project stages, receiving timely updates on any service changes through accessible channels such as leaflets and loudspeakers. Inclusive IEC materials will be available in English, Bengali, Burmese, and Rohingya-English transliteration in both audio and visual formats. Ensuring inclusion by age, gender, and disability, the IRC will address the specific needs of women, older adults, persons with disabilities, and youth at every AAP stage, fostering meaningful participation throughout the project. At all level of the project, information to be shared with the beneficiaries contineously.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

"The IRC is committed to mainstreaming Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) within this project, guided by IRC’s global policy framework, the Core Humanitarian Standards, the PSEAH Index, and the Standard Operating Procedures for SEA Complaint Referral in the Rohingya Refugee Response in Bangladesh. Dedicated PSEA Focal Points will work closely with the network to strengthen the project’s safeguarding mechanisms. Frontline workers and relevant staff will receive thorough training on confidentiality, the “do no harm” approach, and PSEA protocols, ensuring robust confidentiality standards.IRC, in partnership with other organizations, will conduct communication and awareness activities to promote clear understanding of safeguarding and PSEA policies among all staff, incentive workers, volunteers, and beneficiaries. Awareness materials, including key PSEA messages and information on accessible complaint feedback mechanisms, will be prominently displayed for beneficiaries. Child safeguarding is a top priority and falls under mandatory reporting protocols, with both preventive and responsive mechanisms in place. This includes regular training sessions for all staff and incentive workers who have direct client contact, a clear reporting flowchart, and anonymous reporting options at all IRC-operated facilities. Immediate support and referral pathways for survivors are operational to ensure swift response.Child safeguarding risks are continuously assessed, with mitigation strategies embedded throughout the project management cycle. IRC prioritizes child safety and security, ensuring all information and reporting channels are safe, accessible, and inclusive of all beneficiaries and staff, regardless of gender, age, or other protected characteristics. All investigations adhere to the highest standards and core principles, underscoring IRC's commitment to PSEA and child protection as central elements of this project."

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

This project will integrate Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) anticipatory actions, environmental safeguards, and climate change adaptation and mitigation to enhance resilience among vulnerable children and their communities in Cox’s Bazar. Anticipatory actions will focus on early warning systems and preparedness training for community-based child protection members and volunteers, including refreshers on climate-adaptive child protection practices. Simulations and community drills for cyclones, flooding, and landslides will help community members develop responsive strategies to anticipate climate impacts.The project will implement community-led disaster preparedness and response plans, designed through participatory risk assessments that account for seasonal climate patterns. These plans will designate focal points for child protection services like temporary care, psychosocial support, and rapid reunification to address risks associated with forced displacement and family separation during extreme weather events. Awareness-raising activities will incorporate climate change education, promoting community-led neighborhood watch programs to mitigate risks of trafficking and abduction, which are often exacerbated during disaster scenarios.To support environmental sustainability, the project will prioritize eco-friendly practices in resource distribution, favor biodegradable materials, and discouraging practices that harm local ecosystems..Facility risk assessments will incorporate climate risk evaluations to preemptively adapt structures against potential climate hazards. Additionally, the project will collaborate with internal DRR unit to identify suitable adaptation and mitigation practices, such as rainwater harvesting, green infrastructure, and ecosystem-based approaches, thereby fostering a holistic, climate-sensitive child protection framework that addresses both immediate risks and long-term environmental resilience.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

This project integrates Emergency Preparedness and Response measures in line with ISCG guidelines, embedding preparedness protocols at every level of project activity. Community-based child protection members, volunteers, and staff are trained regularly in child protection in emergencies, conducting rapid assessments, and implementing immediate, crisis-tailored response actions in collaboration with EPRWG and ISCG.Clear roles and responsibilities are defined for both agency staff and community members, enabling a swift and coordinated response that prioritizes child safety and protection. This includes provision for activating psychosocial support, establishing temporary alternative care, and initiating family reunification as needed.A dedicated contingency fund is available to quickly mobilize essential resources, including emergency shelter supplies and medical care, reducing response delays. This fund enables the activation of pre-positioned supplies and the deployment of additional resources in high-risk areas, especially during peak climate risk periods. The project also includes regular risk assessments and simulation exercises to test and improve EPR measures, ensuring that both community members and response teams are resilient, responsive, and well-prepared to protect children during and following disaster events.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

Yes, IRC will promote youth engagement in alignment with the JRP 2025, establishing an age limit of 15 to 18 years and above to adhere to donor priorities and inter-agency protocols. Youth engagement activities will include forming adolescent clubs, providing opportunities for adolescents to join the Community-Based Child Protection Committee (CBCPC), and facilitating youth-led initiatives, such as disseminating messages within peer groups and the wider community. Additionally, the project will mobilize youth-led initiatives to observe important events and train young individuals to assume responsibilities during emergencies. For above 18 years old youths IRC would engaged them to its awareness and outreach activities to sensitize them on child protection issues.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

Yes, The project provides structured and sustained psychosocial support (PSS) for children through IRC’s signature curriculum, Now I Am Stronger that alleviate children's strees and building resilience. This program is designed to alleviate psychological distress and enhance emotional well-being in children who are struggling emotionally and need PSS to navigate their challenges. The primary goal of the program is to build resilience in children, reducing emotional and psychological barriers and empowering them to overcome adversity. Additionally, the project includes activities for caregivers based on another IRC curriculum, Family Makes the Difference, which supports caregivers in managing their own stress and strengthening their resilience and capacity to raise children in a safe, violence-free environment. The project also incorporates social cohesion activities that teach children to live together harmoniously and foster a cohesive community.

4.2 Do project activities in the host community link to development initiatives, and/or align with development priorities in the Cox’s Bazar district? If yes, briefly describe.If yes, briefly describe.

Yes, The Child Protection (CP) project collaborates in an integrated approach with other sectors, such as Gender-Based Violence (GBV), Emergency Preparedness and Response (EPR), and Economic Recovery and Development (ERD). The CP activities focus on system strengthening, working alongside the Department of Social Services to enhance its capacity in case management, supporting the functionality of the Child Welfare Board, and establishing Community-Based Child Protection Committees, across other Upazilas. In addition to the Joint Response Plan areas, these activities also extend to Chakaria and Ramu Upazila in collaboration with ministry of Disaster Management and Relief and MoWCA.
