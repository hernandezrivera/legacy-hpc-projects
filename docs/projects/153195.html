Project Summary

The project is designed to meet the basic requirements of the cluster objectives and Iraq HRP 2019 Strategic Objectives, because the project aims primarily to support marginalized and affected groups of displaced persons and returnees particularly in Ninewa (Baaj and Tal-Afar), and SAD (Balad and Shirqat).
In order to achieve the main goals of the project, Justice center will closely work with its Partner, Triangle Generation Humanitarian (TGH), INGO specialized in Child Protection and Education in Emergency. Through mutual referrals of children's cases in need of legal support (detention cases, adoption/guardianship, documentation) -from TGH to Justice Center-, and of children supported by Justice center in need of case management, PSS or support to access education - from Justice center to TGH, children will benefit from a comprehensive response adapted to their needs.
The following objectives have been formulated:
1. Providing legal protection and legal support through four directions:
a. Providing legal services of legal representation by the lawyers through mobile and static teams who work to obtain official documents (civil IDs, death certificates, divorce documents, birth certificates, ration cards, and residence cards).
b. Providing legal services to represent and issue the power of attorney to the detainees of IDPs and returnees including the juvenile.
c. Conducting legal consultation to marginalized groups, as most of the population lacks adequate legal awareness.
d. Holding legal awareness sessions and workshops in IDPs camps and returnees' areas.
2. Working to receive compensation cases for returnees whose property has been damaged as a result of military operations;
a. Providing legal representation for compensation Claims.
b. Assistance through legal consultation to populations affected by recent conflicts to raise their legal awareness.
c. Performing legal awareness sessions and activities regarding HLP rights.
3. Raising awareness of IDPs and returnees regarding the legal, fundamental and formal procedures to facilitate returning to their original areas.

Needs

Due to the HPN (Humanitarian Practice Network), stated that the lack of documentation limits freedom of movement and heightens the risk of detention. Male IDPs are more likely to be detained by the police or prevented from moving from one location to another. The heightened risk of detention at checkpoints over lack of documentation is exacerbated by sectarian tensions, as the situation can be exploited to deny access to safe territory for specific groups or to make arrests under Article 4 of Iraq’s Anti-Terrorism Law. Obtaining personal status documentation in Iraq is difficult at the best of times. The system remains largely paper-based and location-specific. This means that, in order to obtain a new nationality certificate or Civil ID, people are expected to present themselves at the office in their governorate of origin. They are also subjected to complicated procedures, fees, administrative backlogs and a high burden of proof. In the current climate, these barriers are further complicated by constantly changing rules, which are often created to discourage movement and force return. Inadequate staff capacity to deal with the volume of applications causes significant delays; instructions change frequently, as do the locations for issuance of documentation. In some cases power of attorney is not accepted, forcing IDPs to appear in person, despite significant risks to their safety and security.Regarding HLP issues and needs and due to an assessment conducted by Multi-Cluster Needs Assessment in coordination with REACH field staff across Iraq. Analysis was guided by the Multi-Cluster Initial Rapid Assessment (MIRA), indicated that 33% of IDP households cited shelter damage, secondary occupation, or unresolved HLP ownership issues as a top reason for not intending to return (MCNA, 2018).In particular, there are needs to legal assistance and HLP issues in Balad where Justice Center is the first actor to work in this district. Justice Center had voluntary advocated and has worked on 67 documentations and 20 cases of compensation claim. Justice Center has excellent experience in dealing with government institutions in Balad specifically and in SAD and Mosul generally. According to the information obtained from the compensation judge in Mosul, there are more than 76 thousand compensation cases of houses, cars or victims of military operations, are pending.As Justice Center present in Mosul and conducting awareness workshops in this city for the purpose of raising the people’s understanding of the procedures of compensation claims and their rights regarding HLP. Justice Center has understood the need of the people to be fully aware of their rights, and the procedures of compensation claims’ requirements. Justice Center has professional lawyers they have experienced in HLP, civil documentation and detention. Justice Center has 32 lawyers working in SAD and Ninewa who specialize in HLP, civil documentations and detentions. According to an assessment conducted by Justice Center in the areas of operation of Justice Center, concerning the documentation, 70% of the people need civil IDS and 20% need national IDs. Regarding the challenges and impediments of obtaining civil documentations. Mostly, because PoC cannot afford Excessive costs, the other issues are that PoC don not have copies of missing IDs or the other identifiers such as (marriage contract or birth certificate) or security clearance. Some other reasons were confirmed that PoC don’t have sufficient knowledge of the procedures required. In addition, legal assistance for children sensitive cases, such as detention, adoption and guardianship is necessary to ensure children’s needs are being addressed within a comprehensive approach. Justice Center has supported more than 1600 children documentation cases (mainly in Ninewa, but also in SAD) as well as 50 detention cases, and will work in partnership with the INGO TGH (Triangle Generation humanitarian), specialized in Child protection and Education.

Activities

1. Provide legal assistance or counselling (on civil registration or documentation, family law matters, and access to social welfare benefits [ MoLSA ], PDS rations. Regarding children’s cases, Justice center will collaborate with Triangle Generation Humanitarian (TGH), through mutual referrals and in order to provide a comprehensive response as legal issues impact children’s access to their rights. 2. Assign mobile teams of lawyers to provide the power of attorney to the detainees of IDPs and returnees including the juvenile, and follow up their cases in concerned detention centers, courts and others concerned directorates. 3. Deliver awareness raising sessions on rights, entitlements and access to assistance (dissemination brochures of mass information materials during awareness raising sessions). 4. Conduct assessments (either specifically on HLP, or incorporating HLP components) in priority areas in order to identify HLP needs and rights violations. 5. Provide legal assistance to establish lease agreements or resolve lease disputes, restore ownership documentation, or process property restitution or compensation claims. 6. Conduct awareness raising sessions on HLP rights, services and redress mechanisms, including dissemination of information products. 7. Conduct trainings on HLP rights, legal/administrative mechanisms to address HLP issues, or collaborative dispute resolution skills.

Protection Risk Analysis

There are several potential risks that are associated with implementation of the project activities and the major ones include the following; 1. Risks related to the detention cases of ISIS affiliates, the lawyers involved in such cases are at risks of being intimidated by these security forces. 2. Field survey is often risk in areas where contaminated by explosive devices, mines and explosive remnants. “If persons of concern cannot obtain affordable housing or do not have a secure legal status, they may be forced to seek shelter in informal settlements that lack security of tenure or services (including civil registration, healthcare, education, police, security and justice). They are at risk of forced eviction, causing them to become landless or homeless, or exposure to predatory landlords or agents. They may also be excluded from participation in the political and cultural life of the regions in which they live.The construction of critical infrastructure for humanitarian purposes may do unintended harm (for example, by causing forced evictions or relocations), if site selection and use are not transparent, consensual and in accordance with applicable laws, customs and standards.Large-scale displacement, whether to camps or urban settings, can put pressure on scarce local resources, generating tensions or disputes between communities.Some (statutory or customary) tenure systems discriminate against women, and emergencies often exacerbate pre-existing inequalities. Discrimination may take various forms: women may have restricted access to rental housing during displacement; widows may be forcibly evicted under inheritance laws that are discriminatory, unclear or not applied uniformly, etc.Stateless persons may experience discrimination, if laws do not permit the sale, rent or inheritance of property to those without a nationality. It may also be impossible for persons of concern, especially if they are stateless, to obtain access to competent dispute resolution and justice systems in cases of dispute.Even where non-nationals have the right to own or lease land, they may lack identity documents or be unable to open a bank account. This can prevent stateless persons, as well as refugees, returnees and IDPs in some contexts, from completing administrative and practical procedures that are necessary to exercise their HLP rights.HLP that persons of concern left behind when they fled may be illegally sold or occupied by displaced people, other vulnerable groups, or criminal elements. Failure for humanitarian reasons to prevent fraudulent sales, reverse illegal occupation, or regularize occupation often complicates the restitution of abandoned land, houses and property and the return of persons of concern.In many cases, persons of concern lose during displacement, or never possessed, documents that prove their claim to land, housing or property. Pastoralists, share-croppers and other communities may hold their HLP assets in customary tenure, which may not be documented or may not be legally recognized in other courts. To recover abandoned HLP, it may be necessary to furnish evidence of ownership, occupation or usage. Persons of concern may have difficulties proving their claims, especially if witnesses cannot be found or reforms at the end of an emergency formally or informally revise the administration and management of HLP”. (UNHCR, Emergency Handbook).
