Project Summary

Millions of conflict-affected children and women in northeast Nigeria require urgent support to recover from the impact of the ongoing armed conflict. According to HRP 2022 Gender-based violence (GBV) continues to be a major protection concern facing IDP women and girls in north-east Nigeria. The protracted nature of the conflict continues to displace millions of people, the majority of whom are women and girls. Many of them find themselves trapped in a vicious cycle of violence including sexual or gender-based violence (SGBV), exacerbated by pre-existing gender inequalities. Incidences of GBV, especially intimate-partner violence including sexual violence, continue to be reported across the BAY states. The vast majority affected are women or girls, though a significant number of boys are also victims of sexual violence.
There are limited prevention mechanisms and response services to address GBV and sexual and reproductive health for women and girls, including mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS). Analysis of MSNA data indicates that a large percentage (78%) of households with GBV survivors lack access to psychosocial support services. There is also a huge need for legal services for women, girls, boys, and men affected by GBV (of whom 65% reported needing such services, per the HNO) and a similar percentage (61%) of women and girls who reported needing safe spaces within their community
An estimated 1.3 million individuals need GBV prevention and response services across the BAY states in 2022 (of whom 46% are IDPs, 23% are returnees, and 31% are host communities; 82% are women or girls). The majority (63%) of those in need are in Borno State, 22% in Adamawa, and 15% in Yobe.
The proposed action seeks to assist women, girls, boys, and men in Borno state across Maiduguri, Jere, Dikwa, Gwoza, Bama, Ngala, Konduga, and Damboa LGAs where high numbers of IDPs are currently arriving. The need to provide Improved access to quality life-saving and well-coordinated GBV response services for survivors and individuals at risk, enhance well-being among survivors and vulnerable individuals through survivor-centered service provision GBV risk-mitigation in humanitarian response efforts and strengthen community resilience and systems or institutions that prevent and respond to GBV, including harmful practices, is critical and urgent. CHAD will work in close coordination with state and local government agencies, especially the Ministry of Women Affairs and Social Development and the Ministry of Local Government and Emirates Affairs to further strengthen the required critical GBV services and advocacy efforts that will be implemented.

1 - Needs

Although violence against women, girls, boys and men has been reported in many of the camps and host communities, it is believed that, as in any other humanitarian emergencies, GBV incidents are under-reported. Few cases have been reported due in large part to the absence of safe and confidential response services. The fact that information is coming forward, even in the absence of services, is quite alarming and indicates that the issue is pervasive. There are limited health and psychosocial support services in the Northeast, particularly clinical management of rape services and specialized GBV case management and mental health psychosocial support. There are almost no services in place allowing access to formal justice for rape survivors. Community leaders often mediate cases involving rape or sexual assault, with little or no training to support survivor-centered processes to prevent further harm. Forced marriage or payments are often used to resolve such cases. The minimal response services available in most target LGAs provide general support to address emotional needs (but often do not include targeted and specialized GBV case management and counselling), while clinical management of rape for adults and children as part of the health response in these areas is limited due to the limited number of health care providers with technically trained staff on clinical management of rape and care for survivors of sexual assault. There is a huge gap in case management for child survivors of GBV in general due to the limited knowledge and technical expertise on GBV. The need to provide Improve access to quality life-saving and well-coordinated GBV response services for survivors and individuals at risk, enhance well-being among survivors and vulnerable individuals through survivor-centred service provision and GBV risk-mitigation in humanitarian response efforts and strengthen community resilience and systems or institutions that prevent and respond to GBV, including harmful practices, is critical and urgent. An estimated 1.3 million individuals need GBV prevention and response services across the BAY states in 2022 (of whom 46% are IDPs, 23% returnees, and 31% host communities; 82% are women or girls). The majority (63%) of those in need are in Borno State, especially across Maiduguri, Jere, Dikwa, Gwoza, Bama, Ngala, Konduga and Damboa LGAs where high numbers of IDPs are currently arriving.

2 - Activities

The physical safety and psychosocial well-being of an estimated 1.3 million individuals who need GBV prevention and response services in north-east Nigeria remains greatly compromised due to the protracted exposure to extensive protection threats and brutal violence. Family separation, heightened abuses, severe psycho-social distress, sexual violence and other grave child rights violations are among the major concerns requiring immediate intervention. Within the transit/reception centers and IDPs camps, overcrowding, communal living, lack of livelihood opportunities and increased stress and tensions are also contributing to an increase in sexual exploitation and abuse, forced and early marriages, intimate partner and domestic violence. Additionally, the inability of many families to access livelihoods is creating an environment in which girls are at a particular risk of being sexually exploited and abused, forced into marriages at younger and younger ages (sometimes as young as 12 or 14 years old) and forced into prostitution or transactional sex to earn a living or support their families. Families are forcibly marrying daughters as young as 12 years old members of armed groups and other well-off members in the camps and communities, citing poverty as the main reason. Similarly, there are limited prevention mechanisms and response services to address GBV and sexual and reproductive health for women and girls, including mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS). Analysis of MSNA data indicates that a large percentage (78%) of households with GBV survivors lack access to psychosocial support services. There is also a huge need for legal services for women, girls, boys and men affected by GBV (of whom 65% reported needing such services, per the HNO) and a similar percentage (61%) of women and girls who reported needing safe spaces within their community. Within the transit/reception centers and IDPs camps, overcrowding, communal living, lack of livelihood opportunities and increased stress and tensions are also contributing to an increase in sexual exploitation and abuse, forced and early marriages, intimate partner and domestic violence. Additionally, the inability of many families to access livelihoods is creating an environment in which girls are at a particular risk of being sexually exploited and abused, forced into marriages at younger and younger ages (sometimes as young as 12 or 14) and forced into prostitution or transactional sex to earn a living or support their families. Families are forcibly marrying daughters as young as 12 years old members of armed groups and other well-off members in the camps and communities, citing poverty as the main reason. Girls and boys in particular continue to be targeted by sexual and other forms of GBV, including child marriage, sexual exploitation, female genital mutilation and the worst forms of child labour. Under the 2022 HRP, the GBV sub-sector uses the Protection Sector severity rankings and the severity analysis for the GBV AoR to calculate the number of people targeted in each state and LGA. Based on these analyses, the GBV sub-sector and its partners plans to target 1,030,000 people (46% IDPs, 23% returnees, 31% host communities; overall 82% are women or girls) with GBV prevention and response interventions in 51 LGAs (25 in Borno, 15 in Yobe and 12 in Adamawa) with the severest needs, as informed by the JIAF and other analyses from the GBV Information Management System. The majority (63%) of those to be targeted are in Borno State, 22% are in Adamawa and 15% in Yobe. As highlighted by the GBV Information Management System data, GBV continues to be a major protection concern for women and girls in north-east Nigeria and thus the reason why they remain the main target. A survivor-centered approach will guide all interventions. All activities will be based on dignified support, and strengthening the resilience and coping mechanisms of individuals and communities.

8.6 - Please describe briefly the modalities your project will put in place to ensure protection mainstreaming (particularly referring to the above questions).

By integrating every strategy with the protection principles, The Center for Community Health and Development International (CHAD INT’L) will mainstream protection (GBV & PSEA) throughout the execution of this project and advance meaningful access, safety, and dignity of both beneficiaries and of employers and volunteers. Safety, decency, and avoiding any kind of harm to both beneficiaries and employees are priorities for CHAD INT’L. Additionally, CHAD INT’L will also make sure that the target population has equitable access to the service, free from impediments and prejudice, in accordance with their needs.CHAD INT’L will pay attention to people and groups who could be especially vulnerable or have a hard time obtaining help and resources, including those with disabilities and GBV survivors who have been identified. CHAD INT’L will integrate GBV Risk Mitigation efforts to reduce or decrease GBV risk to beneficiaries.CHAD INT’L will work consciously in compliance with the Sphere Humanitarian Core Standards and Imperatives. All approaches will align with the principles of protection as we remain committed to ensuring that this project does not expose beneficiaries to more risk in compliance with the DO NO HARM Protection Principle.Also, CHAD INT’L will be intentional about maintaining an environment where there is zero Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (SEA). All staff, volunteers, contractors, and vendors engaged directly or indirectly to implement this project will be given PSEA orientation. They will be familiarized with CHAD INT’L 's policy on PSEA, and they MUST express their unwavering commitment by signing CHAD INT’L 's Zero Tolerance to SEA Code of Conduct. Also, CHAD INT’L will also lay down reporting protocols for the reporting of PSEA cases if unfortunately, they occur. CHAD INT’L will also ensure that the identity of the reporter(s) is kept very confidential. When convenient, CHAD INT’L will encourage reports to be made on anonymous grounds.Also, being guided by the Inter-Agency mechanism for reporting SEA, CHAD INT’L will ensure a systematized engagement with affected populations including confidential complaints and investigations procedures at both the system and individual agency levels. As recommended by IASC Task Force on PSEA which identified inter-agency cooperation in the creation and maintenance of community-based complaints mechanisms (CBCMs) as a key component in the prevention and response to SEA.CHAD INT’L will continue to raise awareness to the target population that humanitarian assistance is free and never conditioned on sexual favors as well as to be informed on how to access the appropriate complaints mechanisms, and support to set up in the community if SEA occurs. Furthermore, CHAD INT’L will ensure that established CBCMs in target locations are effective and that there is inter-agency coordination to ensure consistent messaging and that access to the mechanism is as broad and straightforward as possible for potentialcomplainants.CHAD INT’L will also be intentional about the environment and safety of the communities where we implement by ensuring that our approaches and tools do not contribute to the degradation of the environment. In addition, CHAD INT’L will also integrate climate change and provide routine sensitization of the project beneficiaries on climate change prevention.

9.5 - How will your project ensure feedback is collected from the most vulnerable community members?

CHAD INT’L will be consolidating its established community-based project accountability mechanism through this project to improvecommunity stakeholders and project beneficiary participation both in the project decision-making and implementation. This project wasdesigned in consultation with the community stakeholders and children based on lessons learned from our previous implementation in the target LGAs.CHAD INT’L uses a people-centered feedback mechanism to gather opinions, and complaints of the community members and beneficiaries on its project to inform decision-making for quality project implementation. However, lessons learned from the approach includes, forming an LGA-level project committee that will include all community groups' representative and stakeholders to improve participation of the affected community in the project implementation process. CHAD INT’L has also made provision for anonymous feedbacks complaint/feedback boxes, SMSs, and direct calls to the Compliance and Accountability team.CHAD INT’L project team will conduct an LGA inception workshop with the established community-based project committee to discuss the project deliverables and the community-level needs and how this project will contribute to resolving the needs. Also, the LGA project committee will work with the project team to define project beneficiaries’ vulnerability based on the context of the LGA, agree on vulnerability criteria, and provide a recommended approach on how the project can be implemented to better reach and serve the project targeted groups.Also, the project team will conduct quarterly review meetings with the community-based project committee at the LGA level to inform how each quarter can consolidate with the previous quarter's achievement to improve the quality of services rendered. CHAD INT’L will also use a feedback mechanism through focus group discussions to gather perceptions of the project beneficiaries and community members.
