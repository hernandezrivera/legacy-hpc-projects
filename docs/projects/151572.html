Project Summary

The project aims to promote, protect and support the well-being of migrants, IDPs, returnees and host communities in at-risk situations with activities that reduce their vulnerabilities and create enhanced protection environment. This intervention will be made through the reinforcement of tailored case management including referrals to available protection services.

Needs

An estimated 800,000 people are in need of humanitarian assistance in Libya as a result of political instability, conflict and insecurity, a deteriorating public sector and a dysfunctional economy. While the impacts of the crisis on people in all affected groups have been severe, there are specific needs according to the varied contexts of different populations in Libya. The 2019 HNO reports that Protection needs are amongst the most commonly reported needs in Libya for all people affected by the crisis, whether Libyan or foreign nationals. Protection needs are primarily driven by exposure to conflict and violence, human rights abuses, contamination from explosive hazards in urban centres, and major challenges related to impediments to access critical services and essential goods and commodities. Migrants face many of the same protection challenges as Libyans primarily due to direct exposure to conflict and increased criminality throughout the country. Due to their irregular status, in Libya, migrants often face heightened risks of discrimination, arbitrary arrest and prolonged detention, human trafficking, labour or sexual exploitation, and ill treatment. The risks of abduction, kidnapping, extortion, robbery, sexual exploitation and abuse, forced labour, slavery, arbitrary detention and human trafficking are very high. Over 90 per cent of migrants transiting through Libya have reportedly been held against their will during their journey. Due to their irregular status, in Libya, migrants often face heightened risk of discrimination, arbitrary arrest and prolonged detention, human trafficking, labour or sexual exploitation, and ill treatment. Gender-based violence is widespread in Libya, takes various forms and has been exacerbated by the conflict in the country. Approximately 40 per cent of respondents to a 2017 assessment survey in Libya indicated that gender based violence in the country is either very common or common. Among the migrants, refugees and asylum seekers, smugglers, traffickers, criminal gangs, armed groups, and State actors and individuals are implicated as perpetrators of rape and other forms of sexual violence. This includes women in detention centres who are often held without female guards and are at risk of sexual violence, torture, abuse and other forms of ill-treatment. The overall prevalence of violence against children in Libya is a major concern. UNICEF assessments indicate widespread child protection issues including physical, sexual and emotional violence, both in schools and at home. Children from poorer backgrounds are significantly more likely to experience violence. Being a child from an ethnic minority background and/or having a physical disability increases the likelihood of experiencing violence. Refugee and migrant children transiting through and living in Libya are especially vulnerable and at high risk of exposure to sexual violence, physical abuse and abduction. An estimated 35 % per cent of migrant children in Libya are unaccompanied, and are at particularly high risk of exploitation or abuse. Duty bearers’ limited capacity and lack of awareness on protection and human rights are significant challenges in assisting the most vulnerable individuals in Libya. This requires continued engagement of international actors and capacity building of national actors. IOM will utilize its DTM as a rapid assessment tool to conduct location assessments as well as to identify the sector-specific needs of the affected population in order to better plan and tailor its response. According to the MHPSS.net 2017 mapping, mMigrants, refugees, men, caregivers and elderly were less targetedable than other community groups living on Libyan territories to access MHPSS services. In addition, 53% of the services provided are under layer II from the IASC intervention pyramid while only 6% cover layer I which is social considerations for basic needs.

Activities

The planned activities include 1) Identify persons in need of protection assistance amongst Libyan and non-Libyan populations including IDPs, returnees and migrants through protection monitoring visits and needs assessments within urban communities, at disembarkation points and in governmental run detention centers 2 ) Provision of specialized case management (including protection services and referrals) to vulnerable and conflict-affected Libyans and non-Libyans, in particular for those who are at increased risks such as adolescent girls, unaccompanied children, victims of trafficking or torture and persons with mental health conditions. 3) Promote adherence to international protection norms, humanitarian and human rights law and facilitate community-based approaches to protection by capacity building of key actors .4) awareness raising and information provision on the dangers of irregular migration and available services along the migration routes. In total under this project, 20 protection case workers and 5 MHPSS case workers will be hired on CTG contracts in order to be operational throughout the country (with no security restrictions which would be the case if they are hired on IOM contracts), in addition to this 10 staff will be hired for the counter trafficking awareness campaign (also through CTG). With regards to specialized MHPSS activities, those will be provided through an implementing partner. IOM will also make sure that appropriate referrals are being made to other service providers that can meet the particular needs identified by the CTG case-workers. This will include referrals to other UN-agencies, INGOs, NGOs and public services offered by the Libyan authorities. Double reporting will be avoided through the use of a case management online platform (mimosa), where every migrant accessing services provided by IOM (protection, health, MHPSS, direct assistance etc) will be registered to ensure the implementation and monitoring of the services provided. Therefore, migrants benefitting from multiple services/elements of the project will be accurately registered and retraceable and will not be double-counted. Additionally, the number of beneficiaries set for the year has been counted including not only direct protection services, but also migrants assisted through the Voluntary Humanitarian return (VHR), as they benefit from access to a variety of services that don’t only include the return. Migrants returning through VHR are previously assessed by protection caseworkers and referred to specialized services (ranging from health, to a variety of protection services, MHPSS, reintegration etc) both in Libya and in country of origin and benefit therefore of access to specialized services.For this reason, the target numbers set may seem high, but in fact include both direct services provided by the Protection Unit, as well as migrants assisted through VHR. In 2017, IOM assisted over 19,000 migrants to return via its VHR programme. All the activities in this project will be coordinated with all relevant protection actors including Protection working group partners, GBV-WG and CP-WG and mixed migration platform partners.

Access, safety and dignity of beneficiaries

- Through the strengthening of specifically designed feedback mechanisms (hotline)- Through the consultations including focus group discussions with the beneficiaries during the whole project cycle - Through outreach activities including MRRM Children and adults that are part of IOM’s project will be actively consulted throughout all the phases of the project. All beneficiaries are interviewed by dedicated protection staff trained on interviewing techniques, protection principles and PFA for both children and adults. During this participative process, migrants (both children and adults) can express their fears, stories and problems both faced in Libya and that they might face in country of origin upon return and as well as express their needs. After the initial interview (and often follow up interviews) children and adults are referred to the adequate specialized services, based on their needs, issues and expectations. Services are therefore tailored to the needs of each migrant encountered. For children, content and interviewing techniques are adapted considering the location (detention vs urban area) and the services will be tailored also taking into consideration their developmental stage, maturity and age. With regards to projects targeting the Libyan population, focus groups are held in the initial phase of the project, to tailor the assistance and activities proposed on the real needs identified during the focus groups (and individual interviews when necessary). For children, the process will be adapted in terms of messages and interviewing techniques, taking into consideration age and development stage of each child.
