Project Summary

Over the past five years, the number of people in need in the BAY states has steadily declined, dropping from 8.7 million in 2021 to 7.8 million this year. This represents a 10 per cent reduction over the five years. This year’s figure represents a slight reduction of 100,000 people (1.3 per cent) compared to last year’s estimate of 7.9 million. For instance, while persistent conflict remains a critical driver of vulnerability, improved humanitarian interventions, targeted programming, and localised stabilisation efforts may have reduced the overall number of people in need. The humanitarian community continues to struggle to respond to urgent humanitarian needs with the already overstretched available capacity.
These overlapping crises have left 7.8 million people in need. Of these, 5.1 million are projected to be acutely food-insecure during the 2025 lean season, and 4.8 million are malnourished. Displacement has worsened the crisis. A total of 2.1 million people are displaced. Additionally, flooding has displaced 500,000 individuals, with 50,000 still unable to return home. IDPs and returnees make up 28 per cent and 19 per cent of the affected population, respectively. They face severe challenges accessing shelter, food, and essential services. Fragile healthcare systems are overwhelmed by disease outbreaks, especially in flood-hit areas and camps. Women, children, the elderly, and people with disabilities are at higher risk of gender-based violence, neglect, and limited access to clean water and medical care.
This situation will likely worsen the overcrowding situation and have a direct impact on the health situation of the population, especially in light of the ongoing measles and cholera outbreaks in the region. The onset of the rainy season coupled with a weak sanitation infrastructure further increases the risk of a cholera outbreak further exacerbating the situation, especially in IDP camps.
The Aftermath of the flooding in the year 2024 in Borno, Adamawa and Yobe states will tell a lot in the coming months which has its effects on food security. In addition, Lack of shelter and food has a direct impact on people’s health especially children, pregnant women, the elderly, and other vulnerable groups exposed to harsh weather conditions.
The health sector, in collaboration with development partners including the State MoHs and Ministries/Committees of Reconstruction, Rehabilitation, and Resettlement (MoRRR), will support a comprehensive recovery strategy for the reconstruction and rehabilitation of health facilities with the overall objective of health system restoration and with the underlying principle of “building back better.” Moving away from geographically and thematically fragmented interventions. The program is designed with the flexibility to respond and adjust to situational changes and to address the health needs of each community. The provision of well-measured training and incentive packages to health professionals to offer essential uninterrupted healthcare services in communities is an additional priority of the health sector. Training of state M&E and partners to improve data collection and inform better decisions also needs to be prioritized.

1 - Needs

According to the Sectoral Needs and Risk Analysis[1] (SNRA), the conflict has affected 7.5 per cent of the BAY states’ population. Borno carries the largest share (65 per cent), followed by Yobe (18 per cent) and Adamawa (17 per cent). Floods, the most significant climate-related disaster, have impacted 28 per cent of the three states’ population. Yobe accounts for 66 per cent of flood-related shocks. Disease outbreaks, though affecting only 1.2 per cent of the population, hit returnees (33 per cent) and IDPs (29 per cent) hardest.These overlapping crises have left 7.8 million people in need. Of these, 5.1 million are projected to be acutely food-insecure during the 2025 lean season, and 4.8 million are malnourished. Displacement has worsened the crisis. A total of 2.1 million people are displaced. Additionally, flooding has displaced 500,000 individuals, with 50,000 still unable to return home. IDPs and returnees make up 28 per cent and 19 per cent of the affected population, respectively. They face severe challenges accessing shelter, food, and essential services. Fragile healthcare systems are overwhelmed by disease outbreaks, especially in flood-hit areas and camps. Women, children, the elderly, and people with disabilities are at higher risk of gender-based violence, neglect, and limited access to clean water and medical care. The ongoing crisis disproportionately impacts women, children, people with disabilities (PwDs), and rural households, exacerbating pre-existing inequalities and vulnerabilities. The 2024 sectoral needs and risk Assessment (SNRA) indicates that women and girls face acute challenges, including heightened risks of gender-based violence, child marriage, and unemployment. Women, especially those in female-headed households, are 34 per cent more likely to face severe hunger and 16 per cent more likely to have poor food consumption compared to male-headed households. These women are also significantly more prone to borrow and accumulate debt to meet their basic needs (36 per cent) and unemployment (23 per cent). At the same time, their children face a 41 per cent higher likelihood of dropping out of school to supplement family income through begging. Children, particularly girls, experience limited access to education and high rates of malnutrition, particularly in rural areas where access to health services and nutrition programs is constrained. Households with disabled members are also heavily affected, with a 20 per cent higher chance of resorting to begging for food and a 31 per cent higher likelihood of child marriage as a survival strategy. Rural communities are 76 per cent more likely to live over three hours away from healthcare services and struggle with access to essential services, further deepening the crisis in remote areas.Health needs also significantly contribute to the overall PiN, with a share of 19.1 per cent, reflecting the strain on healthcare services due to increased disease outbreaks and limited access to healthcare facilities.The BAY states are at high risk of disease outbreaks due to seasonal flooding, displacement, overcrowded camps, and the common practice of open defecation in rural areas. Flooding often contaminates water sources, leading to cholera outbreaks and outbreaks of other types of waterborne diseases. In these states, the cholera fatality rate is 3.5 per cent—more than double the national average of 1.5 per cent—mainly because of poor health infrastructure and inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene facilities. In 2024, flooding and poor WASH infrastructure resulted in over 6,400 suspected cholera cases and 50 deaths, with Borno being the most affected. On average, between 6,000 and 15,000 cholera cases are recorded each year.

2 - Activities

1: Improve access to health care through continuous support for existing health care delivery in all priority locations and IDP camps and the expansion of health services in hard-to-reach areas and underserved communities. 2: Strengthen or expand disease surveillance, outbreak prevention, preparedness, and response capacities for key communicable diseases. 3: Support secondary health services – streamline and strengthen the referral system from primary to secondary facilities. 4: Provide treatment and care to GBV survivors and offer first-line support and appropriate referrals as a way of improving holistic care for survivors. 5: Address gaps in sexual and reproductive health and support mother and child health care. 6: Mental health and psychosocial support—treatment and referral support 7: Health system recovery and strengthening of the humanitarian and development nexus. 8: Continuous strengthening of LGA-level coordination, advocacy, community engagement, and accountability to affected population structures. 9: Capacity building for the M&E staff of both states and partners. 10: PRSEAH (Preventing and Responding to Sexual Exploitation, Abuse and Harassment)
