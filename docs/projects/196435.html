Project Summary

Through the Humanitarian Response Plan Project (HRP) 2023, the World Health Organization aims to contribute towards the reduction of prevalence of acute malnutrition in South Sudan by the end of 2023. South Sudan continues to experience the effects of prolonged conflict, and ongoing subnational violence in most parts of the country, coupled with climatic shocks, these have contributed to increased level of acute food insecurity and malnutrition. About 6.6 Million people are currently food insecure, and the situation is expected to worsen in the lean season (April-July 2023) where an estimated 7.76 Million people will be food insecure with 1.4 Million (345,893 of the children under 5) are expected to suffer from severe acute malnutrition by June 2023. South Sudan suffers from a general deficiency in service level availability across all sectors due to poor performance of the economy characterized by low production and limited access to essential goods and services, hyperinflation, and erratic market prices for essential goods and services contributing to high protection risk and low quality of life (HDI of 0.433) (UNDP 2020) across the country. As a result, Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) rates remains above the emergency threshold of 15 percent (very high acute malnutrition level- WHO classification) at 16.0% (15.4 -16.7) in the lean season. GAM levels have been on the rise since 2019, when the GAM prevalence was 16.2% (13.8-18.5) in lean season. The main drivers of malnutrition in the country are linked to worsening food insecurity, suboptimal infant and young child feeding practices, inadequate availability of safe water and high prevalence of childhood diseases commonly diarrhea, fever, and cough. To address this situation, the WHO strategy will directly target 10% of the children under five who suffer from severe acute malnutrition with medical complications in priority locations with a high burden of acute malnutrition and food insecurity. WHO will ensure that enhanced quality lifesaving medical treatment service is provided for SAM/MC children to reduce the burden of and prevent unnecessary death resulting from medical complications because of acute malnutrition.
WHO will support (a) capacity building of medical doctors, nurses and other health workers responsible for case management of severely malnourished children through trainings and on job mentorship. At least 200 health care workers will be trained on inpatient management of SAM/MC using the SAM/MC package designed by WHO and integrated in the National Protocol (b) treatment of medical complications in children with severe acute malnutrition in stabilization centers through provision of WHO PED-SAM kits 2020 which is especially designed to provide medicines, renewables and equipment suitable for treatment of severe acute malnutrition with medical complications (c) reporting system in stabilization centers through provision of stabilization center reporting tools. d) conduct supportive supervisions to stabilization centers to ensure quality lifesaving service delivery
In order to maximize the output from the fund WHO will work together with health and nutrition partners to reinforce and promote access to integrated nutrition, health, WASH services provision in the project target areas, through enhanced close coordination and integrated programming with other Clusters (Health, WASH). This project aims to enhance nutrition-sensitive interventions that will ensure safe, equitable and dignified access to critical cross-sectoral basic services; to further decrease the prevalence of acute malnutrition, and ultimately, to contribute to the prevention and reduction of morbidity and mortality associated to malnutrition in South Sudan.

1 - Needs

In South Sudan, acute food insecurity, famine, and malnutrition are driven by climatic shocks (floods, dry spells, and droughts), insecurity (caused by sub-national and localized violence), population displacements, persistent annual cereal deficits, diseases and pests, economic crisis along with cyclic hyperinflation that drives prices of food and other essential goods higher that the purchasing power of most households. South Sudan reported couple of emergencies in 2021 and 2022 including the complex humanitarian crisis, flooding, food insecurity and several diseases outbreaks (cholera, hepatitis E, meningitis, anthrax, and measles in 14 counties, with COVID-19, and malaria) and is now faced with Ebola Virus disease threats. The cumulative effect of these emergencies has left Some 8.9 million people in need of humanitarian and protection assistance in 2022 as opposed to 8.3 million people in 2021 (HNO 2022). An estimated 2,175,546 people (1,469,919 in the host community, 315,503 returnees, and 390,125 internally displaced people -IDPs), will suffer from acute malnutrition (AM) in SS in 2023. These include a total of 1,437,735 million children under five years (733,245 girls, 704,490 boys) of which 353,606 will have SAM (180,339 girls and 173,267 boys), 1,084,129 (552,906 girls and 531,223 boys) will have MAM, and 737,812 pregnant and lactating women (PLW) will have acute malnutrition (AM-PLW)- FSNMS Round 28 and the 2022 SMART survey . The above shocks are in addition to the already very poor performance of the country's economy. South Sudan remains among the poorest countries in the world with 85% of its working population surviving on non-wage agriculture and livestock; four out of five people subsist below the poverty line ($1.90) a day. The government's ability to provide basic services is low and inconsistent across the country due to years of under-investment, conflict, and disasters resulting in high dependency on humanitarian aid. There is a general deficiency in service level availability across all sectors due to poor performance of the economy characterized by low production and limited access to essential goods and services, hyperinflation, and erratic market prices for essential goods and services contributing to high protection risk and low quality of life (HDI of 0.433) (UNDP 2020) across the country. The weak and inconsistent capacity of the government to provide services is reflected in the poor performance indicators across sectors e.g the very low health worker density reported at 3.5 (per 10,000 population), and only 44% of the population staying within a 5 km radius of a health facility (HSSRP 2020/22). There is poor access to safe water as only 39 percent of the country’s population had access to enough water to meet their household needs, meanwhile, 80% of households lacked latrines (FSNMS, 2021) increasing the risk of water-borne diseases. South Sudan suffers from acute malnutrition and the causes are linked to poor maternal and young child nutrition (MAD 9% and dietary diversity 26%), worsening food insecurity and inadequate availability of safe water, and high prevalence of diseases among children 56%; most common are Diarrhea 16.9%, Fever 44.2%, and Cough 28.0%) (HNO 2022). In 2023, a total of 31 counties in four states (Jonglei, Unity, Warrap, and Upper Nile) will have Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) prevalence above the emergency threshold of 15 percent (very high acute malnutrition level- WHO classification). The most affected counties include Rubkona, Koch, Leer, Mayendit, Mayom, Guit, Panyijiar, Uror, Duk, and Akobo, with GAM levels of 20% or above. From the 2022 Integrated Phase Classification for Acute Malnutrition analysis (IPC- AM), 15 counties are classified in phase 3(Serious) and 30 counties are classified in phase 4 (critical). Projection for 2023 lean season indicates a worsening situation with a total of 59 counties projected to be in phase 4.

2 - Project activities

• Training of health care workers from stabilization centers on Inpatient Management of Severe Acute Malnutrition, Maternal Infant and Young Child Nutrition (MIYCN)• Provision of training materials and national guidelines on inpatient management of severe acute malnutrition to support training of health care workers • Provision of Paed/SAM kits to support treatment of medical complications including malaria in children with SAM admitted in the stabilization centers. • Provision of stabilization centers reporting tools to improve reporting systems in SC• Integration of child stimulation and early childhood development in the provision of care for children with SAM (stabilization centers) through the provision of psycho-social simulation tools. • Conduct supportive supervision and on job training for health care workers to ensure quality service delivery by the front-line workers at stabilization centers. • Improve water quality for safe preparation of F75 and F100 in the stabilization centers through provision of water candle filters.Distribute long lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs) to SAM children admitted for prevention.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

The most vulnerable people in WHOs nutrition programming context are the PWDs , the elderly, illiterate women and girls, child head of households, and child mothers who have been disadvantaged by years of conflict, flooding, and food insecurity. WHO will use its nutrition materials and treatment protocols which are translated into drawings and local languages to address issues of illiteracy among its beneficiaries People with disability will be reached through disability organizations, community leaders and caretakers where there is acute impairment. Meanwhile, women and girls in other categories will be reached through age structured focused group discussions, in the context of the nutrition project, the stabilization centers will provide good opportunities to speak to mothers on admission as well as display information about the project.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Beneficiaries to the WHO nutrition project will be boys and girls 0-59 months, mothers of children affected with severe acute malnutrition with medical complications admitted for care at stabilization centers, health care workers who will benefit from different types of training under the project, women groups community, and opinion leaders. To ensure a strong feedback mechanism is in place, health care workers who will be trained to support the treatment of severe acute malnutrition at different levels will participate in the pre-and post-test training evaluation to help WHO assess their level of skills and knowledge, they will also be engaged in follow up supportive supervision visits to the centers to assess the level of practice and lessons learned. Mothers on admission will be engaged in FGDs and exit interviews at the sites to get their feedback on treatment and care services provided to their children, meanwhile children will also be engaged during their ECD sessions on how they are coping with the treatment, especially for those children in the category who can speak. Feedback from children and mothers will inform WHO about the quality of care and what capacity or service gaps will need to be closed. Feedback from the health care workers will be used to design further training and advocate with nutrition cluster actors for more tailored support. Women groups and community leaders will be engaged during nutrition program monitoring through key informant interviews where their feedback will be obtained, and relevant lessons used to inform project actions.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

At the national level, WHO has mainstreamed PSEA messaging into all media briefs conducted by the senior leadership for partners and communities to appreciate WHO’s role in safeguarding, WHO has recently engaged a full time PSEAH focal point based in Juba but with regular field missions aimed at raising awareness, risk assessment and working with States offices to ensure adequate mechanisms are in place. As such, radio talk shows, media briefs will be used as a platform to sensitize communities about WHO’s PSEA policies. At the community level, the state’s offices have PSEA policies posted in offices, vehicles, and public boards with clear steps for reporting. Staff, contractors, and volunteers do mandatory PSEA courses and participate in regular townhalls on PSEA and they have also signed PSEA policies as part of their contractual agreements. Through these channels, WHO informs its staff on the immediate and confidential mechanisms to report abuses including for WHO staff members and WHO collaborators or any other person who may have been a victim of PSEA or may have witnessed or otherwise been informed of a case of PSEA involving WHO, as such, all staff members at all levels organization-wide, collaborators and beneficiaries who have reason to believe a SEA situation may have occurred, by a WHO staff or collaborator, or another UN international civil servant, must report either by contacting the PSEA focal points, the ethicsoffice@who.int. or through the Integrity Hotline, where they will receive confidential advice. The Stabilization centers where most of the project will be implemented are established with gender and protection lens to ensure ease of access by at-risk populations especially women and children, for instance, distance and average walking time to the Stabilization centers. Through this project, WHO will further strengthen PSEA mainstreaming using available Information boards, and community meetings with mothers/caregivers on admission with children with SAM/MC at the facilities. PSEA awareness materials and leaflets will be provided to stabilization centers where they don’t exist.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Feedback on WHOs nutrition interventions will come from established formal and informal complaints and feedback mechanisms. There will be personal interviews and Key Informant Interviews from health workers, stabilization center workers, and community leaders, meanwhile, mothers and children will provide feedback through FGDs and exit interviews. WHO nutrition technical officer and M&E officer will analyze different feedback from segments of beneficiaries and provide onsite feedback where necessary. Where an immediate response is not available or the services requested are not offered, WHO will refer beneficiaries to relevant cluster partners for assistance. The project will take remedial actions in line with needs expressed by the beneficiaries including the provision of information in case gaps in information existed and increase participation of affected people. In addition, feedback from monitoring and support supervision by WHO and local authorities will be used to shape the project towards its intended goals, where lessons do not apply to immediate needs, such feedback will be used to design new interventions, meanwhile, others will also be used to advocate for more resources for the nutrition cluster.
