Project Summary

Access to basic healthcare is the priority concern addressed by this project. Due to the protracted conflict, ongoing in Libya since 2011, the capability of providing healthcare services of several public hospitals and medical centers has significantly decreased due to their proximity to the conflict zone. Considering the subsequent relocation of health centers, local community members and IDPs lack appropriate information of the available services. On the other hand, migrants and refugees, devoid of Libyan documentation, are denied access to the public healthcare system, and are thus forced to rely on the expensive private system. Therefore, medical conditions are often neglected or improperly treated with homemade solutions. This project, in particular, focuses on addressing the health sector’s gaps and it is dedicated to alleviating the suffering of vulnerable refugees, IDPs, migrants and host communities in Tripoli. As both vulnerable Libyan and non-Libyan children and unaccompanied and separated children are the most disproportionately affected group, particular attention is paid to them through the delivery of primary health care services via a mobile unit approach. In light of the same rationale, community engagement and awareness raising activities on health, nutrition, hygiene and sanitation will be conducted in migrants’ schools and INTERSOS’ Baity Center in Tripoli.

1 - Needs

Libya remains one of the most vulnerable countries in the region due to the ever-present threat of large-scale hostilities, the presence of foreign armed groups, the trafficking of drugs and migrants, uncontrolled borders, organized crime and corruption. The dire humanitarian situation has been exacerbated by the continuing spread of COVID-19 in Libya. Health assessments conducted in 2020 showed the top challenges were fragmented health sector institutions, poor governance and a lack of accountability and transparency regarding the allocation of funds and distribution of medicines, equipment and supplies from national to municipal levels. The situation was compounded by acute shortages of health care facilities, staff, medicines and supplies across the country. The pandemic has furthermore deepened the vulnerabilities of migrants and refugees, many of whom no longer have access to the informal work opportunities they once had. These groups, by virtue of their displacement or their legal status, have limited access to health care services. Migrants who try to return home find they can no longer do so because their sources of income have disappeared and travel is extremely difficult. The absence of an approved COVID-19 national preparedness and response plan has resulted in a fragmented, uncoordinated response in an already poor health system. There are acute shortages of health care facilities and health workers across the country. Health staff are being paid only sporadically, and most of them have had to wait for months to receive back-pay. Many health care staff are refusing to report for duty because they have no PPE or because they have not been paid. Hospitals and other health care facilities are attacked regularly, and many of them have been forced to close or suspend their services. General and specialized hospitals across the country are regularly forced to close due to high rates of COVID-19 infection among staff and patients. In 2020, more people were forced to seek private health care or buy their own personal protection equipment, diverting resources from already overstretched family budgets. Reliance on life-saving and life-sustaining health care services supported by the humanitarian response will continue across the country in 2021. While IDPs, refugees and migrants have the most severe needs, returnees and non-displaced Libyans in the worst-affected areas also need humanitarian assistance. Other vulnerable groups include children and adolescents, the elderly, patients with chronic health conditions and families facing economic hardship. The availability of essential medicines is a major challenge (70% of functioning PHC facilities do not have any of the top 20 essential medicines). Sexual and reproductive health care is scarce and of poor quality. Obstetric services are available mainly in the private sector. Services for the survivors of gender-based violence are inadequate and difficult to access (especially for female refugees).
