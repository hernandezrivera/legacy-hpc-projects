Project Summary

The food security and livelihood sub-component of the program is aimed at improving the overall food security and livelihoods of 32,100 affected households including 3,367 IDPs, thus improving resilience. It is expected that by improving HH agriculture production and fishing techniques, HHs will rely less on humanitarian assistance and will be able to sufficiently meet their own food needs. Furthermore, local economies will be strengthened and communities will become more resilient against current and future shocks. This is achieved through layering and sequencing of interventions across the two interlinked and mutually supportive sectors, i.e. agriculture/food security and economic recovery and market strengthening. This integrated approach ensures that beneficiaries not only receive the resources they need to thrive, but also the training and knowledge they need to sustain their own livelihoods.

1 - Needs

In 2020, some of the gains were reversed in Abiemnhom, Mayom,and Mayendit. Food insecurity across the three counties was exacerbated by the combination of shocks, including two years of flooding that has destroyed crops and livestock; conflict, including the cyclical cause and effect of conflict and livestock loss; the macroeconomic crisis; and the effects of COVID-19. Due to these factors, Maiwut, Abiemnhom, and Mayendit Counties were projected to face emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity during the April –July 2021 timeframe, while Mayom is expected to continue to see crisis (IPC Phase 3) levels of acute food insecurity. Samaritan’sPurse HH surveys found that, in Mayendit, only 9% of the population had 11 or 12 months of food self-sufficiency. Following this, 26% of HHs in Mayom, 30% in Maiwut, and 61% in Abiemnhom had the same number of months of self-sufficiency. Across the four counties, SP is seeking long-term solutions that will lead to greater food security and resiliency. Some of the challenges farmers face across the country include pests, such as the desert locust, and the fall armyworm, which is known as one of the most damaging crops pests. While SP HH surveys showed success in current programming areas (Abiemnhom, Mayom, and Mayendit), in Maiwut, only 17% of HHs utilize appropriate crop protection procedures. In Maiwut, only 7% of HHs had sufficient seeds to plant in the regular planting season. In areas of current programming, 64% of HHs had access to sufficient seeds to plant, while still evidencing the need for additional distribution. This requires sustainable solutions including SP’s plan to select additional community seed multipliers for the coming year in Abiemnhom and Mayom. There is also a need for additional data to inform these longer-term objectives, for which SP is seeking to conduct a seed security assessment. The SP HH surveys found that between 28% -31% of HHs are involved in fishing in Abiemnhom, Mayom, and Mayendit. In Mayendit, 46 kg of fish wereharvested per HH, while Mayom and Abiemnhom together reported an average of six kg per HH. Samaritan’s Purse sees an investment in this sector as an opportunity to further address food security and livelihoods needs in these areas.

2 - Project activities

The agriculture/food security’s sector’s aim is to improve HH agricultural production through promotion of improved agricultural production techniques (including pests and disease management and adoption of improved post-harvest handling techniques), small-scale irrigation, seed system strengthening, as well as adoption of sustainable fishing practices. The project seeks to strengthen agriculture production through support vegetable and fishing practices (CLFSCO2). It is expected that by improving HH agriculture production and enhancing community knowledge surrounding sustainable agriculture and fishing practices, the HHs will be more resilient. This aligns with cluster object CLFSCO3 by seeking to reduce HH dependency on humanitarian assistance. To improve HH agricultural production, SP plans to train farming HHs in improved crop, management and post-harvest handling techniques in Abiemnhom/Mayom and Mayendit. The trained HHs will be also provided with agricultural inputs, both tools (malodas, matoks, picks, knives, etc.), and assorted staple crop seeds. In addition, HHs will be supported to engage in profitable vegetable production through the provision of vegetable seeds, training in vegetable production and nutrition practices. To achieve this, agricultural training groups will be formed in Abiemnhom/Mayom and Mayendit. All groups will be trained in organic pest management practices. Meanwhile, the HHs will be supported to access micro-irrigation equipment (watering cans and treadle pumps) to water their vegetables during dry seasons. Beneficiaries will also be trained to repair and maintain irrigation equipment. Finally, to encourage adoption of sustainable fishing practices, HHs will be provided with fishing kits (twins and hooks) with aim of improving fish harvesting and consumption. These HHs will be trained in sustainable fish harvesting, processing and marketing. The activities planned under Economic Recovery sector are designed to improve the social economic status of the host community and IDPs living in Abiemnhom, Mayom and Mayendit counties. This is based on the understanding that improving economic statuses will directly contribute to the improvement in overall food security and living standards of the affected HHs. To achieve this, SP will carry out carefully planned interventions across the three mutually supportive sub-sectors of market system strengthening, financial services/inclusion, and new livelihoods development. Market system strengthening activities are aimed at enhancing opportunities for income generation among IDP, returnees, and vulnerable resident populations through strengthened engagement with local markets. To this end, a series of activities have been planned which include (1) conducting a market assessment to help understand the dynamics and entry points for vulnerable HHs, (2) creation of business groups, (3) establishment of savings and lending groups, and (4) improving post-harvest handling and processing in order to broaden the benefits arising from agricultural production. The financial services have the main aim of increasing access by vulnerable/non-bankable HHs to financial services through the village, savings and loan association (VSLA) model. Specific activity targets Agriculture:- Staple crop production: 64.25 MT of seeds and 15,300 pieces of farming tools distributed to 5,100 HHs.- Kitchen Gardening: 204 vegetable groups formed (5100 HHs)- Training in Agricultural Production: 204 Lead Farmers to lead Agriculture Training Group of 25 members each- Establish Youth Block Farm Groups: 20 members per group- Post-harvest handling training: 80 Lead Farmers trained- Seed system security assessment- 200 local seed producers trained- Training on Integrated Pest Management (IPM)- Provide fishing kits to 3,200 HHs- Training on sustainable fish harvestingERMS:- Establish and train 100 VSLA groups (30 members each)

5 - Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA)
5.1. Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA)?

Yes

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

In 2020, SP piloted seed vouchers in Abiemnhom County after the feedback from program participants indicated that beneficiaries preferred to plant local seeds rather than imported seeds that are not adapted to the local condition and needs. The provision of seeds through seed vouchers was then proposed to enable farmers to access seed varieties they needed, specifically the staple crop (sorghum). The seed voucher also strengthens the local market system given that most HHs buy seeds from within Abiemnhom.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

Samaritan’s Purse agronomists, in close collaboration with the state level Ministry of Agriculture technical staff, conducted seed quality and viability tests on seeds from potential suppliers from Abiemnhom market. In addition, the project team conducted a market assessment to ascertain market functionality and availability of seed in the area. The study also confirmed that the proposed voucher approach will not disrupt access to seeds by other local farmers who are not direct beneficiaries of this project. Building on this experience, additional project beneficiaries will be provided with seed vouchers and will be able to access 5kgs of sorghum and 3 kgs sesame seed per HH.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The feedback submitted in boxes will be regularly collected on and combined with the feedback received by the committees during distributions. Non-sensitive feedback will be summarized and shared with beneficiaries and staff at the field level. The information generated via feedback and complaints system will be used to continually improve program implementation. In particular, sector managers will meet at least quarterly to discuss general program feedback received and to agree on action plans to address any concerns raised. Program teams will then provide feedback to beneficiaries about what will be done to address their concerns, and will then carry out the agreed action steps. Program staff will provide feedback to beneficiaries at the start of program activities to inform them about steps taken to address their concerns (e.g. through pre-distribution messaging for distribution activities, at the start of trainings for training activities, etc.). Where applicable, sector leaders will also inform the relevant local authorities about plans to address general concerns beneficiaries have raised. Non-sensitive complaints that do not involve general program feedback, but which are specific to an individual or individuals, will be reviewed by the Complaints Manager, presented to the Area Coordinator, and shared with relevant sector leaders as part of the Program Manager’s monthly reporting. Where such feedback necessitates a response, Program Managers, in consultation with the Area Coordinator, will agree on action steps and the individual(s) who raised the complaint will be notified about what will be done to address their concerns. Feedback involving critical or sensitive protection issues, however, will be reported to senior management at the country office level. The country office will also notify SP headquarters. In such cases, an action plan will then be created to address the issues raised and a team unconnected with the complaint will investigate.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

As stated above, SP has put in place a number of mechanisms through which feedback and complaints from all project participants, including vulnerable members, can be collected. These include suggestion boxes installed at project meeting venues/distribution points, complaints desk manned by a trained officer at each input distribution sites, and community project committees, through which beneficiaries will be able to submit their complaints or feedback about the services provided by SP. Vulnerable members such as persons with disabilities, those who are not literate, women, and girls will be educated on how best they can share feedback using any of the above channels without fear of their information being disclosed.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

Before program activities commence, beneficiaries are informed of their right to file complaints, including reports of SEA and sexual harassment, through established reporting mechanisms such as the SP hotline. Samaritan’s Purse highly safeguards safety, confidentiality, and dignity of the reporting beneficiary and handles the matter with utmost respect and in a non-discriminatory manner. Program-specific complaints are directed to the PSEA focal point in the country office. Any report of SEA or sexual harassment is reported immediately to the Country Director who will communicate with SP’s international headquarters, per the Whistleblower Policy, for an appropriate response and/or investigation. Per the Whistleblower Policy, the International Compliance Officer will enlist support from various individuals to ensure a prompt, diligent, and confidential investigation appropriate to the complaint. For allegations of SEA, the International Compliance Officer will involve the Director of Global Safeguarding to coordinate the investigation efforts with appropriate personnel in the country office, including the focal point(s).

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Samaritan’s Purse will seek feedback from affected populations in order to address any problems that may arise during program implementation and to improve how activities are designed and carried out. Beneficiaries will be informed about how they can register complaints or provide feedback in a variety of ways. For example, during distributions, a helpdesk will be set up where beneficiaries can provide feedback or register complaints with a trained staff member. Beneficiaries are then notified about availability of such services. The staff member at the help desk will be a member of the monitoring and evaluation (M&E) team, rather than the distribution team, in order to ensure adequate separation of duties. The helpdesk staff member will have a standard form for receiving and recording feedback/complaints. At each of the distribution sites, there will also be a complaint box where community members can submit written complaints if they prefer. In addition, project steering committees will be established in each community to receive and submit to SP any complaints/feedback community members bring them. Beneficiaries will be informed about the availability of these complaint and feedback channels on a regular basis (i.e. during community meetings, training sessions, before and during distribution exercises) so that they are well informed.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

8.1) To minimize the risk of exposure to violence during registration and distribution as a result of long distances travelled by beneficiaries to access humanitarian assistance, efforts will be taken to ensure that distribution sites/locations will be identified in close consultation with community leaders as well as beneficiary representatives (women, girls, and older persons). Project staff will ensure that such sites are located in areas that are accessible and safe for women and girls. Registration of beneficiaries will be conducted by SP staff in close partnership with members of the project steering committee. This reduces the risk of people moving to far places for registration. 8.2) Samaritan’s Purse upholds a code of conduct that is consistent with the six core principles of the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) Task Force on PSEA in Humanitarian Crises. Beneficiaries will be informed of their right to file complaints, including reports of SEA and sexual harassment, through established reporting mechanisms such as the SP hotline. Samaritan’s Purse highly safeguards safety, confidentiality and dignity of the reporting beneficiary and handles the matter with utmost respect and in a non-discriminatory manner. Program-specific complaints are directed to the PSEA focal point in the country office. Any report of SEA or sexual harassment is reported immediately to the Country Director who will communicate with SP headquarters, per the whistleblower policy, for an appropriate response and/or investigation. In this way, the risk of women, girls and boys without or with disabilities being exposed to violence, including sexual exploitation and abuse, will be minimized.8.8) There is a likelihood that external stakeholders may try to influence the criteria developed to guide the selection of beneficiaries. To avert this, SP will continue to collaborate with all agencies and government institutions involved in the project to solicit their input in the selection criteria. Any disagreements arising from selection will then be addressed before commencing the selection and registration of beneficiaries.8.9) There is a likelihood of increased tension and conflict between refugee, returnees, and resident communities during the process of allocating land for demonstration purposes. To avert this, SP will continue to collaborate with all agencies and government institutions involved in the promotion of peaceful coexistence between refugee and resident communities. Any issues arising will then be handled before they escalate into larger problems.

12 - Contribution to Collective Outcomes
12.1. Does your project contribute to any of the three Collective Outcomes, i.e. Improved Food security; Enhanced support for prevention and response to needs of survivors of Gender-Based Violence; and Flood displacement?

Yes

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

The program will provide emergency assistance to beneficiaries with a particular focus on those in IPC 3, 5 and 5 living in unity state (Abiemnhom, Mayom and Mayendit). The assistance will ensure that critical inputs are available, accessible, and utilized in a manner that reduces the suffering and vulnerability of those affected by conflict and natural flooding. They will be provided with emergency food production assistance including main season agriculture seeds & tool distribution and fishing, vegetable, and crop kits. With this type of support, affected HHs will be empowered to produce sufficient food to last them through the lean months when food is normally scarce. Dry season response kits, fishing kits and vegetable kits, which will be provided just before the onset of the dry season, will supplement this. Women and youth will be targeted as they have a key role in ensuring food security in the household.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

The project adopts an integrated multisectoral approach with interventions occurring in other sectors (WASH, Health, agriculture/food security, economic recovery, etc.) that directly contribute towards the reduction of global acute malnutrition (GAM) rates among targeted HHs. For example, by encouraging beneficiaries (women) to join VSLAs, the project will offer a platform for additional group-based activities and information sharing on topics such as nutrition and health. Field supervisors and village agents will be trained to layer these topics into VSLA group meetings. Samaritan’s Purse plans to conduct cooking demonstrations with a focus on the vegetables promoted by the project. The purpose of conducting the cooking demonstrations will be to enable the mothers/caregivers to gain new food preparation skills, develop their confidence in preparing new dishes, and create awareness of underutilized locally available vegetable/staple crops. All HHs participating in dry season vegetable production activities will be instructed on how to prepare nutritious meals in addition to other topics (e.g. washing or cutting vegetables, food hygiene, meal frequency, etc.). In addition, through the provision of fishing kits and training on fish harvesting and fish processing, the targeted beneficiaries will further be provided with another layer of support through which they will be able to improve household food security and nutrition.
