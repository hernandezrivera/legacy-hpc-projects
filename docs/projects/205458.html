Project Summary

The IRC Child Protection (CP) program will provide life-saving immediate support to the extremely vulnerable and conflict-affected children of returnees, internally displaced individuals (IDPs), and host communities in Unity, Northern Bahar el Gazhal (NBeG), and Central Equatoria States in South Sudan. The IRC CP program will reach 40,525 individuals (13,275 B, 13,650 G, 6,010 M, & 7,610 W) individuals through an integrated static and mobile response in the priority locations of Rubkona, Guit, Panyijiar, Koch, Aweil East, Juba, and Pariang Counties. The proposed CP responses are linked with two strategic objectives (SO) of the 2024 HRP such as (SO) 1: vulnerable crisis-affected people have reduced morbidity and mortality through equitable and dignified access to life-saving assistance to meet their needs and (SO) 3: Vulnerable people’s capacity to withstand the impact of shock is increased, and nexus opportunities are optimized. The IRC will continue responding to Sudan Crisis in Rubkona, Guit, and Aweil East while continuing the humanitarian response in Koch, Panyijiar, Pariang, and Juba (IDP Camps). The IRC will provide comprehensive case management services to children with protection concerns, family tracing and reunification (FTR) services, alternative care arrangements for unaccompanied and separated children (UASC), and cash assistance to meet the basic needs of children. The Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) service will be provided for children and caregivers with psychological distress using a combination of approaches such as Social and Emotional Learning through Safe Healing and Learning Space (SHLS), Families Make the Difference for parenting skills of caregivers, Supporting Adolescents and their Families in Emergencies (SAFE) for life skills of adolescents, and IRC’s Brighter Futures Model for malnourished children with protection concerns. Finally, the community-based child protection (CBCP) structures at the lower community level will be established and strengthened, a series of community awareness sessions on CP risks and prevention messages will be done, and capacity building of the frontline staff and community volunteers will be done. In addition, child-friendly feedback and complaint reporting mechanisms will be established.
The IRC CP program has ongoing funding from various donors, such as BHA, SIDA, PRM, SSHF, GFFO, UNICEF and GiZ, during the proposed HRP period. The CP response will be complemented by the existing IRC’s programs, including GBV, general protection, education, nutrition, health, and livelihood programs, to ensure comprehensive care.

1. Needs

The South Sudanese government has made significant strides in developing a legal and policy framework to address child protection needs. However, a critical gap persists, with no consistent, functional, and sustainable government-led national child protection system to prevent and respond to violence against children. Child protection initiatives rely heavily on resources from international humanitarian agencies. Unfortunately, humanitarian crises are intensifying, and funding for responses is decreasing, resulting in a growing need for humanitarian assistance.According to the Protection Cluster's 2024 projections, 5.53 million people require protection, with 3.15 million being children and adolescents, including caregivers. The persistent violations of children's rights pose a grave concern, exposing children to various risks such as abduction, recruitment into armed groups, family separation, abuse, neglect, exploitation, and violence.The intensification of humanitarian crises is attributed to factors including conflict, climate shocks, food insecurity, displacement, disease outbreaks, and economic decline. These pressures lead parents to resort to negative coping strategies, such as of engaging children in child labor such as fishing, and canoe rowing, as well as increased child marriages in Rubkona, Koch and Panyijiar and, jeopardizing children's well-being and education. The IRC documented the case of 333 (169 B, 164G) children with various protection concerns, including unaccompanied and separated minors in alternative care arrangements, child neglect, child labor, domestic violence, and sexual exploitation in Rubkona, Koch, Panyijiar, Aweil East and Parinag counties that need continued support in line with their respective case plans (SS CPIMS + October 31, 2022).Additionally, an influx of people from Sudan, escaping ongoing conflicts since April 15, 2023, has exacerbated the pre-existing challenges in most of the locations. According to the UNHCR -IOM dashboard (4th of Nov 2023), a total of 357,947 individuals have arrived in South Sudan. This influx has taken a toll on the psychosocial well-being and mental health of both children and caregivers, leading to daily stressors. This further exacerbated the humanitarian situation in a country where 9.4 million need humanitarian assistance in 2023 and put additional strain on already limited resources and available services, heavily impacting children and adolescents who represents 50% of the displaced population entering South Sudan.Upper Nile, Unity, and Northern Bahar el Ghazal states are hosting a significant number of returnees, causing overcrowding in some areas and increasing food insecurity. Returnee children have faced harrowing experiences, including violence and abduction during their journey, leaving them traumatized and in need of support.The IRC has been responding to child protection concerns in seven targeted locations, focusing on case management, Social-Emotional Learning, and capacity-building. However, due to the vast child protection needs, not all issues can be addressed. The IRC has established community child protection structures but acknowledges the need for capacity improvement.The IRC aims to target 40,525 individuals (13,275 B, 13,650 G, 6,010 M, & 7,610 W) vulnerable children in the seven counties in 2024 through a comprehensive and integrated program response. This response will prioritize flood and conflict-affected children, including those from displaced communities, unaccompanied and separated children, children associated with armed forces and groups, children in detention, malnourished children, returnees, and those in host communities, addressing their specific needs and vulnerabilities.

2. Project activities

The IRC proposes life-saving CP interventions for this HRP period, focusing on both protective and preventive modalities through static and mobile service, totaling 40,525 individuals (13,275 B, 13,650 G, 6,010 M, & 7,610 W). The targets population includes conflict and crisis affected UASC, GBV survivors, CAAFAG, and children with disabilities from the returnees, IDP, refugees, and host communities. The IRC will continue responding to Sudan Crisis in Rubkona, Guit, and Aweil East while continuing the humanitarian response in Koch, Panyijiar, Pariang, and Juba (IDP Camps). The proposed CP responses align with the strategic objectives 1 and 3 of the HRP and the first two priority locations outlined in the HRP inter-cluster prioritization for 2024. The CP intervention will focus on three program priorities of the CPAoR. The IRC aims to create a safe and protective environment for children, providing quality protection services while empowering vulnerable girls and boys. Concurrently, efforts will be made to strengthen local government and community-level prevention mechanisms in alignment with established standards, including the Minimum Standards for Child Protection in Humanitarian Action, IASC Guidelines for MHPSS in Emergencies, and Core Humanitarian Standards. Additionally, the IRC will utilize standards and guides developed and adapted by the Child Protection Area of Responsibility (CP AoR).The proposed CP activities include:(A) Comprehensive Case Management: The IRC will continue to provide case management services including FTR to the most vulnerable children. The IRC will use inter-agency case management forms and Child Protection Information Management System (CPIMS+) to manage individual cases. The IRC caseworkers and supervisors will be trained in the revised inter-agency Level-1 and 2 Child Protection Case Management Training Package for Caseworkers in Humanitarian Settings. Besides, the existing service mapping and referral pathways will be updated in all the operational areas in collaboration with the State CPAoRs. To ensure dignified and safe service delivery, IRC will prioritize cash assistance for children identified for case management support as per their child case plan and the cash and voucher for protection SOP. (B) MHPSS: The IRC will also provide PSS/SEL services through a variety of methods, including Safe Healing and Learning Space (SHLS), PSS clubs in schools, a child protection help-desk, SAFE, and parenting skills training. The rehabilitation of existing centers and the construction of new SHLS will create a safe environment for children to attend PSS/SEL activities. Teachers, PTAs, and SHLS facilitators will be trained in UNICEF’s PSS manual and IRC’s SEL and SHLS management standards. Furthermore, schools will be supported with the establishment of CP help desks, PSS training for the teachers, and playing materials provision for clubs as part of the PSS activities. (C) CBCP: The CBCPC will be better equipped to prevent and respond to child abuse, neglect, exploitation, and violence. The CBCPC members will be trained in CPiE, case identification, and referrals to the CP actors. Community awareness will continue on CP risk and prevention messages using different platforms in the community. (D) Child friendly feedback, reporting /complaint mechanisms: The IRC will establish complain mechanism to enhance accountability to children and their caregivers. The CP response will be complementing the existing IRC’s nutrition, health, livelihood and GBV programs to ensure comprehensive care.
