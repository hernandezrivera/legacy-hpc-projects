1 - Needs

In 2022 HNO report, an estimated 2 million people in South Sudan will be at risk of acute malnutrition, including 302,078 children (148,018 boys and 154,060 girls) with SAM, 1,039,214 children (509,215 boys and 529,999 girls) with MAM, and 675,548 pregnant and lactating women with acute malnutrition (AM-PLW). In addition, 50,638 (15.4 per cent) out of a total of 328,610 refugees in South Sudan will need treatment for acute malnutrition, including 8,388 SAM children, 26,092 MAM children and 16,158 AM-PLW. There isa slight overall increase of 100,000 people (5.2 per cent) in 2022 compared to 1.9 million people who needed treatment for acute malnutrition in 2021. FSNMS+ Round 27 and SMART Surveys results were used to project the 2022 people in need where extrapolation of GAM/SAM was used since no primary data collection was conducted due to COVID-19 restrictions. According to the FSNMS+ round 27 results, 26 counties in six states (Jonglei, Unity, Warrap, Upper Nile, Northern Bahr el Ghazal, and Eastern Equatoria) have Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) prevalence above the emergency threshold of 15 percent (very high acute malnutrition levelWHO classification). The most affected counties are Pibor, Aweil South, Baliet with GAM of 21.6 per cent, 23.1 per cent Likewise, 4 counties that were previously IPC AMN Phase 2 (Alert) during the last lean season are projected to be in IPC AMN Phase 5 (Serious) for the 2021 projection period. Out of all the counties in IPC AMN Phase 4 (Critical), nearly 70% are in Greater Upper Nile followed by Greater Bahr el Ghazal (18%).Counties in Upper Nile State (29%), Jonglei State (24%), Unity State (16%), Eastern Equatoria State (13%), Warrap State (8%), Northern Bahr el Ghazal State (5%) and Lakes State (5%) are in IPC AMN Phase 4 (Critical).Available evidence on nutrition indicates extremely critical levels of acute malnutrition in the 4 payams (namely Gumuruk, Verteth, Lekuangole and Pibor) in the Western part of the Pibor county, which account for an estimated 80% of the total population in the county. Country level FSNMS results, which only covered the Western part of the Pibor county, show a GAM based on MUAC prevalence of 17% with the following breakdown of prevalence by payams: Gumuruk 29%; Verteth 26%; Lekuangole 12%; and Pibor 11%. According to a MUAC screening conducted as part of an IRNA rapid assessment in three payams, the prevalence of GAM by MUAC is Gumuruk=37%; Lekuangole=33%; and Verteth=8%. The MUAC screening was conducted by trained enumerators and was exhaustive in Verteth town, The major factors contributing to acute malnutrition include high prevalence of diseases (up to 36%), poor quality and diversity of food (Minimum Acceptable Diet: 7%, Minimum dietary diversity: 15%). 7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Data for assessment, baseline and monitoring will be collected through - institutions and HH surveys, - focus group discussions with representatives of various groups (teachers, parents, students disaggregated by gender and age), people with disabilities, - interviews with key informants, authorities and other NGOs present in the area. Each time CRESA and/or enumerators will inform about the scope and aim of data collection. Collected data will be a base for designing activities, adjusting to the needs or external circumstances. CRESA will give special attention to women and girls' voices to ensure the team's protection issues are well-known. As the GBV cases are often unreported, Community Resilience Emergency and Support Aid (CRESA) officers will engage the community's caseworkers. They will be present in the community as often as possible to build trust and encourage adequate psychosocial support. TOR for all activities will be presented to the authorities; plans will be discussed with the community. People are engaged as community-based caseworkers in GBV referrals, translators, enumerators. CRESA will organize regular meetings with the community representatives during intervention implementation to present plans of the activities and receive feedback on the implementation process and suggestions for improvement. At all phases of the intervention, Community Resilience Emergency and Support Aid (CRESA) staff will always consult and engage with the beneficiaries and local authorities to ensure that the decisions are made jointly with their prior knowledge. To ensure adequate records of feedback/complaints from the beneficiaries for follow-up, Community Resilience Emergency and Support Aid (CRESA) team will establish accessible CRFMs (complaint response feedback mechanisms) such as complaint forms, complaint desks, hotlines, complaint boxes and complaint committees based on the area of applicability. Complaints and feedback will be registered and followed

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

For the people with special needs, whose access to feedback and complaint mechanism is limited, Community Resilience Emergency and Support Aid (CRESA) will try to adjust mechanism paths. In the case of children with disabilities, with limited mobility, CRESA officers will visit them directly to ensure that their ideas are heard and considered during an assessment at the time of intervention. For the illiterate parents, complaint committee and enumerators will be encouraged to document the complaints and feedbacks since they are selected from within the community and are familiar with the local language. Beneficiaries will be made aware of the complaints and feedback mechanism and the opportunity to provide feedback directly to Community Resilience Emergency and Support Aid (CRESA) program staff or monitoring team. People can also share feedback and complaints through local chiefs, FGDs, hygiene promoters, pump mechanics, water user committee enumerators who participate in all implementation activities. Depending on what people feel comfortable with, they can share the feedback personally or via trusted persons.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

The PSEA information is part of information campaign conducted at the beginning of the project implementation. The messages will be given by Community Resilience Emergency and Support Aid Community Resilience Emergency and Support Aid (CRESA) officers (including women) to groups of beneficiaries during FGD baseline studies, KII, education campaigns. PSEA will be important part of training of community members engaged in the project – Parents, Teachers, authorities, health club members and volunteers

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Community Resilience Emergency and Support Aid (CRESA) to ensure adequate records of feedback/complaints from the beneficiaries for follow-up, CRESA team will establish accessible CRFMs (complaint response feedback mechanisms) such as complaint forms, complaint desks, hotlines, complaint boxes and complaint committees based on the area of applicability. Complaints and feedback will be registered and followed up with the communities on ongoing bases and used to adjust project activities. The records of complaints and feedback are kept and regularly analyzed by MEAL team in Juba, whose officers track each case and advise/support the programme team. CRESA will target all vulnerable groups with attention to their needs and capacities. Complaint and feedback mechanisms are always in place to find out about any gaps and address them. Separate focus group discussions will be carried out specifically for women and girls by a female staff or protection officer to open up. Community Resilience Emergency and Support Aid (CRESA) works closely with local authorities, institutions and NGOs to clearly understand the cultural norms, inter-communal dynamics and potential risks. 12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

Due to the flooding, in Greater Upper Nile dependence on traditional coping strategies and income generating activities have reportedly been severely affected. The loss of traditional livelihoods along with reportedly minimal access humanitarian services is increasing food insecurity in the county.Flooding has reportedly led to displacement in the county with the majority of communities moving to the highlands along the Nile and Sobat rivers, as well as along the Jonglei canal. This displacement has led to the emergence of entirely new settlements and has substantially increased the size of other settlements in these areas. Displacement has further added pressure of an increasing internally displaced peoples population on host communities resources.CRESA and other Humanitarian agency providers service have reported that response remains challenged by the floods with disruptions and delays in services.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

Between April to July 2023, coinciding with the lean season, over 1 million people in South Sudan experienced high levels of acute food insecurity, classified in Crisis or worse (IPC Phase 3 or above), due to flooding, economic decline and the effect of the COVID-19 pandemic. These include about 107,000 people (1%) classified in Emergency (IPC Phase 4), 1,507,000 (13%) in Crisis (IPC Phase 3), and 5,030,000 (43%) in Stressed (IPC Phase 2). With the upcoming harvest of the major cropping season (B), from June to September 2022, the population in high levels of acute food insecurity (Crisis and Emergency phases) is likely to fall from 1.61 million (14% of the total population) to 1.04 million (9%), a decrease of 35% between the two periods.
