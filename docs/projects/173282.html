Project Summary

BRAC will support the Rohingya and host communities with access to correct information to counter the adverse impacts of COVID-19 and tackle social concerns through joint engagement (e.g. school campaigns) of the two communities. BRAC will develop print and e-materials to support relevant stakeholders in disseminating information on COVID-19 and ensuring evidence-based communication with special consideration given to social inclusion, protection, environment, emergency preparedness and culture. Materials (also including video shows and radio podcasts) will be developed (in a community led message development workshop) in local Bangla dialect and Myanmar language. BRAC and partner staff along with volunteers (youth, imams and hafezas) from both communities will be trained in peace building and social cohesion through inter-community events. These volunteers will motivate the communities to hold joint events on COVID-19 prevention, ills of drug use, anti-trafficking and other social ills. Information dissemination will also include public health messaging e.g. for immunization; services and service points (e.g. union parishads, health facilities) for health, protection, disaster management and other services.
In line with AAP, information and services accessed and available with respect to needs of vulnerable women, girls, boys and men, disabled and elderly will be introduced and monitored. BRAC will scale-up its own and that of its partners, the accountability and feedback-complaint mechanism by installing service desks in both communities through engaging community volunteers who will report back to BRAC and partners to arrange for resolution of complaints and community issues. BRAC will contribute to sector discussions on high priority concerns in information dissemination (e.g. COVID-19 rumours and panic; trafficking; host-Rohingya conflict, vulnerability to natural disasters etc.). BRAC will emphasize on coordination, advocacy and effective communication strategies with upward partners among ISCG, RRRC and DC while doing the same for partners, host and Rohingya communities.

1 - Identified Needs

Currently Cox’s Bazar, one of the poorest districts in Bangladesh hosts over 870,000 Rohingyas across 34 camps. When the largest influx of Rohingya fleeing Myanmar violence came Cox’s Bazar in 2017, the Bangladeshi host community were the first responders to support them, however the rapid influx created a high level of poverty and environmental vulnerabilities putting a strain on infrastructure and services of the host population. Conflict and social tension has since then been rising, within the camps among Rohingyas as well as between Rohingya and host community. The COVID 19 lockdown exacerbated these creating fear and panic among both communities. The COVID 19 pandemic resulted in a country wide lockdown which meant all interventions except critical services in health, WASH, protection in the camps were suspended and host communities too were likewise impacted. It set back improvements in the basic indicators across the sectors (Food Security, Livelihoods, WASH etc ) as reported by the recent Joint Multi-Sector Needs Assessment (JSMNA) in October 2020. The reduction in services and humanitarian programming was sometimes communicated negatively leaving the Rohingya community confused (IOM ACAPS 04/2020) creating panic and fear overall. As part of the COVID-19 response, there was wide message dissemination, which was not always adequately relayed in sync with the rapidly changing information needs of the community. The messaging had not always incorporated traditional and general understandings of health, illness, and how religious beliefs interact with people’s understanding of health. Since the general information was not perceived clearly, the mass prevention messaging on hand-washing and cleanliness instead increased the social tension and stigma towards the Rohingya since the host communities considered them unclean. According to the Rapid Gender Analysis conducted in June 2020, community leaders, volunteers and religious leaders are the most trusted information sources for the Rohingya (UN Women, CARE, Oxfam 07/2020). People with disabilities (PWD) and marginalised populations such as women, elderly and adolescent girls, were not able to fully access information among both host community and Rohingya. Since access to camps was restricted for humanitarian staff, the Rohingya could not communicate back to humanitarian actors their limited understanding of the messages. Restricted network access made it difficult to inform the population of changes in services and developments regarding COVID-19 and furthermore, only 9% of Rohingya households and 25% of host community households were found to own ‘extended assets’ such as a mobile phone (REVA 3, 12/2019). Women in particular have limited access to mobile phones. Pre-recorded messages delivered were in the Chittagonian dialect, considered the nearest to Rohingya language but it was found that the lesser educated Rohingya found it hard to understand. In terms of community feedback mechanisms, the Rohingya mostly mention preference of the Majhis as point of contact for complaints, however the majhi system does not meet the minimum standards of representation or impartiality (UNHCR/REACH, 2019) since majhis often abuse their position. Rohingyas also the report of a lack of open communication between themselves and humanitarian agencies and government authorities. They stressed their desire to play an active part in decision-making in issues affecting their daily lives. Considering all the problems in proper message development and dissemination, the message development should be community led and channelled through sources culturally accepted and in their own language. There should also be more engagement of Rohingya with humanitarian actors, government and host community to ease tensions and create better understanding and cohesion.
