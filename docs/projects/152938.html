Project Summary

This project aims to enhance protection monitoring and support community-based protection initiatives to improve the physical safety and access to services for IDPs and (2) Improve access to legal assistance and legal remedies for highly vulnerable IDPs. Using the methodology of Unarmed Civilian Protection (UCP), the project maintains a focus on IDPs with specific protection needs such as persons with perceived IS affiliation and persons at risk of SGBV, and will contribute to the identification, prevention and mitigation of protection risks.

Needs

The Jed’ah and Hamam Al Alil (HAA) internally displaced persons (IDPs) camps house approximately 100,000 IDPs. UNHCR predicts that 35% of all IDPs nationwide lack a durable solution for return [Bilateral Meeting with UNHCR, April 2018] and although more than 590,000 IDPs nationally have returned home in 2018, this is well below the predicted number and the pace of return is slowing down as the remaining IDPs face significant obstacles to return. With the Governorate Returns Committees (GRCs) established in April 2018 to oversee camp closure in different areas of Iraq and consolidation resulting in relocation of IDPs in Ninewa camps, along with diminishing funding for camps-based humanitarian interventions, IDPs are facing significantly more challenging conditions in settings of displacement. As humanitarian presence decreases, this will compromise the civilian character of camps, with increased presence of security actors and targeting of IDPs. This deteriorating security environment, strain on resources and decreasing assistance is likely to result in a further reduced sense of safety and security, increased use of negative coping mechanisms, social upheaval, and increased inter-communal tension. Amongst those lacking a durable solution are numerous families that have been targeted for collective punishment through denial of return and abuse within the camps because they have relatives with alleged IS affiliation. NP's engagement with communities in the camps suggests that a disproportionately high percentage of female headed households (FFHs) are perceived as IS affiliated. Interviews with security actors, including Ninewa Police and National Security, have seen Jed'ah 5 Camp referenced as the "Daesh widows’ camp" and the "Daesh camp." In the HAA Camps, as noted in the January 2018 CCCM Cluster and REACH IDP Camp Profiling Report, 29% of the displaced population live in FFHs, leaving them particularly exposed to risk. In effect these camps act as quasi-detention centres for this highly vulnerable group with their movement restricted by the threats of violence and unlawful detention, the lack of necessary security clearances or lack of civil documentation. As documented by Human Rights Watch, organizations and lawyers that have sought to procure documentation for such IDPs have faced threats and abuse from security actors. This has dramatically limited their ability to represent those with the greatest needs. Thus, lack of documentation remains a significant obstacle to accessing services, freedom of movement and safe and dignified returns. NP conducted baseline assessments in HAA Camp 1 in December 2017 and in Jed'ah 5 Camp in May 2018. These assessments were conducted to measure IDPs' perceptions of safety and security, access to services, community representation, and means for resolving disputes. The data was primarily collected through attitudinal surveys, in which randomly selected IDPs were asked to rate their responses to statements on a scale between one and five regarding levels of agreement. In HAA 1, NP surveyed a total of 223 household representatives out of approximately 3,800 families or the officially registered 19,956 individuals in the camp. As such the survey statistics are recorded with 95% confidence and a 6.5% margin of error. In Jed'ah 5 NP collected 202 surveys for a population of 19,200 individuals. This gave the baseline survey a 95% confidence level and a 6.85% margin of error. This quantitative data was supplemented by qualitative data from interviews with IDPs, security actors, and partners as well as focus group discussions with IDPs. During the Jed'ah 5 baseline assessment, the team documented 37 incidents of abuse perpetrated by armed actors in the camp. This was reflected in the responses of 35% of IDPs surveyed who said that they often heard stories of violence and harassment within the camp. This number increased to 42% when the respondents were from female headed households. In HAA1 40% of women reported feeling unsafe in the camp, as did 27% of the total surveyed. Key informant interviews with women from Hawija reported threats made by the PMU there that should the women ever return to their home areas they will be killed along with their children. This demographic also reported the highest rates of insecurity within the camp. Young men also expressed a significant fear of arrest for alleged IS affiliation. Interviews with IDP women and SGBV partners also indicates that survival sex is widespread in the Jed'ah Camps, which is further exacerbated by the decreasing services.

Activities

1. Protection monitoring: NP will identify protection concerns and needs through conducting household level protection monitoring in the camps. NP staff will provide daytime and nighttime presence to respond to identified specific protection concerns and, where necessary, will carry out accompaniments for identified and/or referred cases where civilians are under threat or at risk of physical violence. NP will continue to provide data into Incident Matrix which is shared with the protection cluster. 2. Service Referrals: NP will refer vulnerable individuals with specific needs to specialized services and assistance. NP’s close engagement with the community means that is trusted to engage on sensitive issues such as targeting of persons with perceived IS affiliation, SGBV and child protection. NP will contribute to identification, prevention and mitigation of such cases using the methodology of UCP to prevent violations in the first place and to facilitate the protection of survivors who are at continued risk. As NP is often the first point of contact with survivors of SGBV, NP will provide referrals to health, mental health/psychosocial support, case management, and legal services according to standard practices and referral modalities. 3. Community-based protection: 3.1. Camp Protection Forums: NP will provide continued support to the camp protection forums that have been developed in the HAA and Jed’ah Camps. These three forums give an opportunity for men, women, and youth respectively to discuss their protection and security concerns and raise them with community leaders, camp management, and the respective security duty bearers. Considering that IDPs find themselves in communities they are unfamiliar with, these forums work to strengthen peaceful existence within the IDP community and with external security actors, giving them the confidence and knowledge necessary to independently access police, military and government officials when future threats and/or protection risks arise. 3.2. Build the capacity of vulnerable civilians to protect themselves: The participants of the camp protection forums, as well as other targeted groups, will be supported to mitigate protection risks through training on protection issues and approaches peaceful co-existence activities. This practical training in conflict prevention and resolution skills will help reduce their vulnerability to violence by giving them tools for peaceful dispute resolution. Furthermore, it aims to give the members the confidence to develop their own initiatives and mechanisms to prevent violence and reduce the impact of violence. 4. Capacity building support to relevant duty bearers to ensure they play their role to protect civilians effectively: NP will aim to enhance the responsiveness and efficacy of local authorities and duty bearers and key stakeholders to protect civilians by engaging in capacity building on international humanitarian law, UCP methodologies, protection norms and applicable legal standards for example, the Principled Returns Framework. Capacity building will be achieved during the camp protection forums and through targeted trainings and local-level advocacy. 5. CwC: NP is working closely with all service and assistance providers in the camp to set up effective CwC channels, and, improve community-feedback mechanisms. As part of this, NP will deliver awareness raising sessions on rights, entitlements and access to assistance including specific information campaigns, for example, KBYG. 6. Monitor & support distributions: NP will continue to carrying out distribution monitoring in response to multiple complaints from the displaced population in camps to ensure that distributions taking place are fair and equal, and in line with humanitarian principles and camp rules. 7. Provision of legal assistance: For highly vulnerable IDPs e.g. FHH, IDPs who have not been able to access services through other partners, IDPs who have specific fear of engaging with the authorities, or have received threats when seeking civil documentation, NP will provide accompaniments to IDPs who request support to navigate the legal processes. NP assistance will include the following: (i) facilitate transport, (ii) provide international presence throughout the process including for IDPs and the legal team, (iii) provide legal representation through NP’s legal team and follow up with government departments for issuance of civil documents and (iv) document the process and obstacles the IDPs face. The focus on complex high-risk cases, coupled with international accompaniment for lawyers, will complement the work being conducted by other legal partners by ensuring equal access for IDPs with different needs and/or high-risk cases.

Protection Risk Analysis

The primary protection risk to both the beneficiaries and humanitarians is in regard to perceived IS affiliation. Humanitarians, particularly lawyers representing IS affiliated families seeking documentation have been detained, beaten, harassed and threatened by security actors for providing this service. Concurrently, in respect to Do No Harm principles, engagement and support to these families can draw undue attention to them, increasing the risk of retaliation by security actors or exclusion from their communities. Organizationally, by focusing on those protection cases with the greatest need, this often mean prioritizing families with perceived IS affiliation. This has led to accusations that humanitarians are supporting IS. This can have a grave impact on national staff who are hired from the local community, irrespective of the roles they play within the organization. A second critical risk is the potential for honour related violence against women who report SGBV perpetrated against them by family members, other IDPs or security actors. Women reporting such violence have repeatedly spoken of their fear of being attacked by their relatives should the information become public and they are accused of bringing shame to their families. Alongside this risk is the possibility of retaliation against men accused of perpetrating SGBV against women outside of their families, which can escalate the conflict to a communal level. In the event that conflicts have escalated this has resulted in violence and the expulsion of families from the camps. The deteriorating security situation in Qayara and the growing IS insurgency, epitomized by the recent VBIED attack in the marketplace and the attack on a civilian bus, presents growing security risks to both humanitarians and beneficiaries. If this trend continues it will likely result in decreased humanitarian presence, decreased assistance and greater challenges to the civilian character of the camps. Engagement with armed actors on issues of International Humanitarian Law (IHL) and principles of durable solutions, particularly in respect to incursions into humanitarian space and allegations of abuse against IDPs risks developing an adversarial relationship with armed actors. For instance, in the aftermath of the recent Qayara VBIED several high ranking security actors accused INGOs of being responsible for the attack and for harboring IS terrorists, thus re-emphasizing the integral importance of maintaining humanitarian neutrality. NP considers that sensitive issues may be raised against security actors in the camp protection forums, that could result in retaliation against civilians. Security actors may also respond to cases in a manner that exacerbates the protection concerns of those involved. In addition, combative forums may further alienate civilians from the security duty bearers. Following consistent and regular international and national protection actor presence in the camps, any reduction or withdrawal of presence could result in: (a) a reduced sense of safety and security in the camps, (b) create opportunity for an increase in prevalence of human rights violations, (c) a reduction in reporting of violations, and (d) an increase in presence of security actors. IDPs may feel less secure in accessing services and therefore a reduction in access and freedom of movement may be observed.

Mitigation Measures

To mitigate the risks facing IS affiliated families NP has worked closely with camp management to move families within and between camps in the event that they face heightened threats. Coupled with this, so as not to draw undue attention to them, meetings with them are traditionally held in the NP office, or in safe spaces provided by SGBV partners. All information regarding case management is confidentially maintained in line with cluster guidelines with no hard copies of paperwork kept in the field and all soft copies encrypted. In respect to SGBV survivors NP has worked closely with SGBV providers to ensure that they receive the appropriate services and with camp management to move families away from the perpetrators of violence, or to move the perpetrators themselves, without highlighting the reason. This has been the primary means of mitigating the circumstances when women are too fearful of honour-related violence to openly report the issue. NP’s operational model is designed to mitigate threats against national staff by pairing them with International Protection Officers (IPOs). This model has proven effective in the Iraqi context, allowing national staff to engage on high-risk cases, while mitigating the risks of retaliation towards them or their families. In respect to the deteriorating security environment NP has demonstrated its ability to operate in high-risk areas by maintaining strong local networks with community and religious leaders in and out of the camps that provide real time security information. NP hires staff with considerable experience operating in insecure environments. As NP's work is inherently connected to security and risk mitigation: with an exclusive concentration in improving the security of civilians, field teams are constantly analyzing and mitigating security risks. In regards to engagement with armed actors NP has a proven track record of engaging with a wide range of armed actors in Iraq including PMU units that have been traditionally hostile to western organizations. By placing relationship building at the center of its work NP commits considerable resources to maintaining working relationships with a variety of actors. This allows NP to engage on sensitive issues from a position of mutual trust. In addition, in Qayara and HAA NP has collaborated with organizations such as ICRC and NRC to present to the security forces a unified voice amongst actors engaging on these issues. These relationships have also helped NP to mitigate the perception that is supportive of IS by outlining the humanitarian principles and demonstrating NP’s non-partisanship. To mitigate risks associated with the camp protection forums, NP will carry out consultations with community member participants of the forums prior to inviting security actors to attend to ensure that sensitive issues are expressed in a constructive and appropriate manner. NP will provide a tailored protection response to any community member who perceives specific, targeted and immediate risk of harassment or retaliation from security actors. NPs community-based protection activities (camp protection forums and training on protection issues and approaches peaceful co-existence activities) aim to provide the IDP community with practical tools to continue to carry out protection activities and mitigate protection risks using UCP methodologies if the presence of protection actors in the camps is reduced or withdrawn. Throughout these activities, NP will work to develop the confidence of members to apply their skills by supporting initiatives and mechanisms proposed by the community themselves. Parallel to this, capacity building with relevant security duty bearers will aim to promote understanding and application of international humanitarian law, protection norms and applicable legal standards with an aim to gradually improving their responsiveness to protection of civilians.

How is your project accountable to affected population

NP implements a methodology known as Unarmed Civilian Protection (UCP). UCP seeks "to create a safer environment for civilians to address their own needs, solve their own conflicts, and protect vulnerable individuals and populations in their midst.” One of the core principles of UCP is the primacy of the local actor, which means that all of NP’s UCP programming is designed to prioritize the needs of local actors. Accountability to affected populations is at the core of this project design. Beneficiaries were involved in the design of this project, through NP’s baseline survey, focus group discussions and individual interviews carried out in Hamam al Alil and Jed'ah camps over the last year. Because NP's methodology and the specific activities entailed in this action require constant engagement and dialogue with beneficiaries across all age groups and genders, they are primary decision-makers in project design and implementation throughout every activity. NP staff engage with each beneficiary individually to inquire about their needs and together decide on the response for those facing threats of violence or barriers to accessing services. After activities are conducted feedback is sought from the beneficiaries as to what steps NP can take to improve services. In addition, coordination with authorities is inherent in the project design, and NP will be in constant contact with authorities, particularly security duty bearers. This is another key point for accountability to affected populations, since ultimately, the state holds the primary responsibility for protecting civilians; NP's role is not to take over this responsibility; instead, NP seeks to augment existing civilian protection measures, identify gaps, and encourage or facilitate responses from authorities to better respond to civilian protection needs. Relationship building is one of the key methods within UCP. NP builds relationships with community leaders, local authorities, relevant armed actors, and diverse representations of civilians - both within and external to existing leadership structures. It is through these community relationships that NP solicits feedback and to make programmatic decisions. In Iraq, NP encourages the use of the IDP Call Center, where IDPs are able to call and provide feedback for any humanitarian agency responding in areas with IDPs. This service is publicized widely and is used frequently. NP also keeps complaint boxes in both HAA1 and Jed'ah 5 to allow IDPs to make complaints, comments, and suggestions about NP activities taking place in the camp and about the activities of other humanitarian partners. NP ensures national and international staff bring the required skills and experience to their roles, but also are diverse enough to most effectively engage with the affected population. Staffing diversity in terms of gender, nationality, ethnicity, religion, language skills, and other areas is a crucial way to enable community engagement. International humanitarian agencies work in vulnerable communities, making the power disparity between staff and beneficiary communities extreme. Ensuring that all activities do not cause harm to civilians is of the highest priority for NP, and a core piece of AAP. NP has a set of policies and procedures designed to safeguard community members, and in particular vulnerable groups within those communities, including NP's Code of Conduct, Child Safeguarding Policy, and Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) Policy.
