Project Summary

The IRC has developed this project in alignment with its global vision, the Bangladesh Strategic Action Plan 2025, and the Joint Response Plan (JRP) 2025 objectives. Activity prioritization and beneficiary targeting are informed by the Joint Multi-Sectoral Needs Assessment (J-MSNA), REVA 7, market analysis, client feedback, insights from IRC field teams, and a gap analysis, which confirm the livelihood potential, vulnerability, and interest in skill-building, market demand, and income generation among the population. In response, IRC and its partners (YPSA, Pulse Bangladesh, Shushilan, and CNRS) will provide skills training and livelihood support to targeted groups in both refugee camps and host communities. The project will operate in Refugee Camps 1E, 1W, 22, and 25, and selected host communities in the Ukhiya and Teknaf sub-districts, aiming to enhance income-generating readiness by equipping participants with market-aligned skills that open employment pathways, reduce protection risks, and increase women’s empowerment.
Following the guidelines of the Livelihood and Skill Development Sector (LSDS) and the Refugee Skills Development Framework, a 360-hour competency-based training (CBT) curriculum will be provided to 100 refugees (50 females and 50 males). The training will cover Community Health Work, Small Engine Mechanics, Electrician, and Plumbing, with 80% practical demonstrations, structured class sessions for 4 hours, and home learning for 1 hour daily. Graduates will be connected to employment pathways as skilled or semi-skilled volunteers in the health sector, including IRC health centers, and also in other sectors to further develop their employment opportunities. Additionally, 200 refugees interested in Non-Formal Technical Training (NFTT) will receive 120 hours of training in trades such as Weaving, Knitting, Household Repair, Tailoring, Gas Stove and Solar Panel Repair, and Carpentry, along with equipment for practice and self-employment. A stipend of BDT 150/day will be provided per trainee in accordance with LSDS guidelines, and competency levels will be assessed by an independent assessor upon completion, coordinated by the LSD Sector.
For the host community, the project will provide diversified vocational skills training to 80 youth and women in market-relevant occupations through private and government institutions under the National Technical and Vocational Qualification (NTVQ) framework, along with toolkits and employment support. Additionally, 480 youth and caregivers will participate in IRC’s signature "Learn to Earn" (L2E) business skills module, designed to support business ideation and ensure safe income generation, particularly for women. Graduates of L2E who develop a business plan will receive technical training, business grants, and personalized business coaching. Upon completing vocational training and business plans, 560 participants will receive inputs and cash grants to support procurement of necessary tools and equipment. The funds will be transferred as e-money through IRC's Safer Cash approach, ensuring protection and safety for all recipients. The project will also facilitate an apprenticeship program for both male and female youth in collaboration with the chamber of commerce and the private sector, creating employment opportunities. To enhance financial inclusion for vulnerable women, the project will help establish 50 digital Village Savings and Loans Associations (VSLAs) in partnership with DreamStart Lab, a US-based fintech company. Graduated entrepreneurs will be connected with government and market actors to improve access to capital, inputs, and market linkages for sustainable business growth. Lessons learned during implementation will be shared with the LSDS sector to inform ongoing review and development.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

Following a huge influx of refugees in 2017, Cox’s Bazar is facing a protracted refugee crisis. Currently 994,124 stateless Rohingya refugees residing across the camps within Cox’s Bazar (GoB–UNHCR Population Factsheet, Aug' 24). An additional 345,972 host community members across the Teknaf and Ukhiya sub-districts are also experiencing the effects of this growing crisis (JRP 2024). This demographic shift has severely strained local resources, services, and systems and increased competition for employment for both refugee and host communities. The refugee crisis has also increased environmental degradation and the depletion of natural resources. Combined the crisis has increased poverty levels and reliance on external aid, as well as exacerbated tensions between refugees and host communities. Due to legal restrictions, Rohingya refugees have minimal access to formal employment opportunities, leaving many reliant on humanitarian aid. This lack of sustainable income generation limits their ability to meet basic needs and plan for the future. 97% of Rohingya households are highly vulnerable, with 85% of female-headed and 87% of male-headed households affected. In the host community, 42% of female and 27% of male-headed households are high vulnerable. These disparities highlight the need for targeted, gender-sensitive interventions. (REVA-7). Due to limited livelihoods opportunities, the situation has also deteriorated in the surrounding communities, where the poverty rates in Teknaf (30%) and Ukhyia (40.2%) position them as poorest sub-districts in the country (HEIS 2016). Over the course of seven REVA, Bangladeshi households reported a gradual decline across all indicators, (food consumption score, reduced coping strategy index, livelihood coping strategy index and multi-dimensional deprivation index) with highly vulnerable households increasing from 13% in 2017, to 30% in 2024. Around two-thirds of households in the camps (67%) and half in the host community (50%) rely on less preferred or less expensive food as their most frequently used coping strategy. Refugee households reported a median monthly expense of $69, significantly exceeding the humanitarian assistance of $40 they receive. As a result, they are compelled to seek alternative sources of income or cash inflows to cover their basic needs. Over half (53%) of refugee households report not having any livelihoods opportunities nearby, limiting their ability to support themselves, and their families (J-MSNA’23). Both refugees and host communities often lack the necessary vocational skills to participate in market-driven employment opportunities. This gap reduces their chances of securing jobs or starting viable businesses such as agriculture, handicrafts, and services. Women in both refugee and host communities face additional barriers to employment due to cultural norms, limited mobility, and lack of access to resources. Without targeted support, women’s economic participation will remain low, affecting overall household income and resilience. Limited access to credit, seed funding, and business services also hampers the ability of refugees and host community members to start or expand income-generating activities. The Market Assessment (Aprl’23) by IRC and BBC Media Action explored livelihoods and skills development opportunities for Rohingya and host communities, aiming to inform sustainable project outcomes. For long-term viability of income-generating activities, the study recommended aligning individuals' skills with local private sector demands through close collaboration with government and community stakeholders. It also highlighted the need for foundational training for refugee women to facilitate self-employment and skills training for refugee men to transition to safer employment options. Furthermore, access to capital and skill development opportunities were identified as essential for enhancing the livelihoods and resilience of host community members.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

Women and men face significant barriers in accessing livelihood and skill development opportunities due to entrenched socio-cultural norms. These norms limit their ability to participate in vocational training, start business, engage with market actors, or manage resources, resulting in restricted participation in livelihoods activities and hindering effective asset management. Furthermore, market actors often overlook gender-specific needs, limiting women’s access to services and excluding them from fully benefiting from livelihood opportunities. To address these challenges, the project will adopt a gender-sensitive approach to empower women and ensures their safety and dignity. Central to this approach is the Do No Harm principle, the activities will implement targeting women and vulnerable clients, including vocational training, life skills, cash or input distribution, and market linkage engagements. In addition, the approach will protect women from violence, exploitation, and discrimination throughout their participation. Safeguards will be implemented to ensure activities take place in safe, confidential, and respectful environments, protecting participants from potential harm. The project will focus on enhancing women’s business skills, providing market-relevant information, and equipping them with the technical skills necessary to respond to livelihood opportunities. By ensuring that women are not only participants but also decision-makers in business and marketing activities, the project will foster a more inclusive and equitable market system. Integrating gender-sensitive strategies and upholding the Do No Harm principle will contribute to a safer and more sustainable market, benefiting both women and their communities. This approach will enhance women’s livelihoods, safeguard their well-being, and drive positive social change, promoting long-term resilience and development for both the Rohingya refugees and the host communities.

2.3 - Age & Disability

The IRC will target adolescents and youth aged 18-24, along with vulnerable women up to 60 years, for livelihood and skills development programs, while also engaging women and men as caregivers to support youth and adolescents. The disability inclusion strategy prioritizes accessibility, tailored support, and the meaningful participation of persons with disabilities (PWDs), aiming to ensure inclusive access to project services with an estimated 12% reach. Using the Washington Group questionnaire, IRC will screen and identify PWDs, followed by baseline assessments to understand their specific needs. Program staff and volunteers will receive training in collaboration with the sector to assist PWD clients, and delivery mechanisms will be adapted with accessible equipment to support skills development and business activities. Activities will include vocational and life skills training, provision of assistive devices, targeted grants, and employment facilitation through partnerships with inclusive private and public sector employers. A coordinated network and service mapping will enable referrals for clients to other service providers as needed. Caregivers will also participate in activities to address challenges faced by PWDs. Monitoring will incorporate disability-sensitive indicators to track participation, access to opportunities, and outcomes, ensuring adaptive programming and sustainable impact. Dedicated funding will increase the reach and effectiveness of services for all clients.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

IRC and all implementing partners have robust Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) Frameworks in place. The project's AAP approach aligns with the IASC’s commitments on AAP, emphasizing the integration of AAP principles and practices into all aspects of humanitarian programming. Consult with the women, men and youth participants, design feedback and complaints mechanisms, and ensure AAP-related questions are incorporated into templates. IRC's primary goal is to comprehensively understand the level of satisfaction with services, identify community needs, concerns, hopes, and preferences, and incorporate these insights into program design to enhance intervention quality. The participation of affected populations, including marginalized groups, is an essential and ongoing component of the program, involving in-depth participatory research and frequent community consultations. An effective and accessible Client Feedback and Response Mechanism (CFRM) is already established, allowing communities to provide feedback, raise complaints, improve referral mechanisms, and facilitate two-way communication between IRC/implementing partners and the communities. Prioritize strengthening the CFRM for protection from sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA). Maintain PSEA Network membership and provide PSEA training to all staff, volunteers, and contractors. The project will regularly collect community voices to support advocacy, allocate budgets for AAP activities, assess SEA risks, and ensure affected groups can register complaints and provide feedback during implementation. Information on accessing services will be widely disseminated, referral pathway will be developed and shared within PSEA networks and program teams and conduct orientation on AAP-related actions and principles, including PSEA for all.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

The IRC is dedicated to integrating Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) into the Rohingya Refugee Response in Bangladesh through a robust framework that includes IRC’s safeguarding policy, Core Humanitarian Standards, and the Minimum Operating Standards for PSEA (MOS-PSEA). IRC will ensure all staff and partners are informed of and adhere to codes of conduct and safeguarding policies, with rigorous hiring processes that screen for SEA history, including self-disclosures. Policy implementation is championed by the Senior Management Team (SMT), promoting a zero-tolerance culture through ongoing PSEA training. Specialized Safeguarding staff and PSEA Focal Points will coordinate with project teams and the PSEA Network to maintain alignment. Tailored PSEA training for staff and partners, especially frontline workers, will emphasize a “do no harm” approach and confidentiality. Beneficiaries from both refugee and host communities will be informed about PSEA policies and available protections during training sessions, enhancing awareness across communities. Community engagement is central: IRC will create accessible, safe complaint and feedback mechanisms for beneficiaries. A survivor-centered approach ensures survivors’ safety, confidentiality, and support access without mandatory reporting. Feedback channels—such as in-person, hotline, and complaint boxes—will accommodate all genders and ages, with mandatory reporting for minors. IRC commits to notifying donors and stakeholders of SEA concerns, conducting high-quality investigations to support survivors, hold perpetrators accountable, and reinforce prevention. Program quality will be enhanced through regular field visits, interviews, and focus groups, fostering continuous improvement and best practices.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

The project will integrate disaster risk management and climate resilience through community-based approaches, empowering communities to enhance their preparedness and response capacities. By building knowledge and skills in multi-hazard preparedness, the project will involve community members in risk identification, mitigation, and preparedness to reduce vulnerabilities.The focus will be on strengthening disaster preparedness, anticipatory action, and forecast-based early response. IRC will train youth and community groups on disaster risk management and equip them to support their communities. Special training will be provided to vulnerable households in landslide- and flood-prone areas, particularly targeting women and youth, enabling them to lead resilience-building efforts within their communities.To promote sustainable practices, the project will include green skills training and raise clients’ awareness of climate-smart practices, empowering them to adopt environmentally responsible methods. Youth climate activists will be trained to support their communities in adopting climate-smart practices and DRR activities aimed at minimizing the impact of climate-induced disasters. Additionally, IRC will establish information-sharing mechanisms to strengthen the local disaster risk management system and build community resilience.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

The project incorporates Emergency Preparedness and Response (EPR) measures to minimize disruption during natural disasters or emergencies. It focuses on raising disaster preparedness awareness among clients to help protect their businesses from damage and loss, providing early information through project staff and connecting clients with CPP (Cyclone Preparedness Programme) volunteers. IRC will also establish Emergency Response Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs), ensuring all team members understand their roles and responsibilities during a crisis for a rapid and coordinated response. While the project does not include a specific contingency fund, it will be supported by IRC's dedicated disaster risk reduction team, which will mobilize and allocate funds during and after a disaster to assist affected clients and ensure continued operations. These measures are designed to ensure the project's resilience and smooth continuation even in the face of unforeseen events.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

Yes, IRC will establish pathways to engage youth aged 18-24 as central to project activities, focusing on skill-building programs, mentorship, and active participation. Using vulnerability and skill assessment tools, IRC will identify youth needs based on age, skills, and potential opportunities, providing vocational training, entrepreneurship support, life skills, and development of green skills. Diversified livelihood interventions, grant support, and partnerships with private and public institutions will create income-generating and employment opportunities. In the Refugee Camp, the youth will be engaged as volunteers in collaboration with development agencies and sectors to generate income opportunities depending upon their skills. IRC will collaborate with private sector entities like hotel owners' associations, the Chamber of Commerce, and the tourism industry, to co-design training curricula and facilitate apprenticeships or internships, enhancing employability. Additionally, youth will be involved in civic engagement and disaster risk reduction activities, raising awareness, supporting preparedness, and disseminating early warning information. Employment pathways will target sectors such as hospitality, tailoring, mechanical engineering, and retail. In the host community, IRC will coordinate and collaborate with the department of Youth to engage youth for vocational training and access finance for creating employment opportunities. This engagement will foster youth leadership, resilience, and inclusion in community decision-making while tracking progress through adaptable, data-driven mechanisms for sustainable impact.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

Yes, the project will promote self-reliance and resilience by equipping refugees with technical skills tailored to camp and future reintegration needs, alongside essential life skills. Through practical, competency-based training aligned with camp job opportunities, the project will enable refugees to contribute productively and reduce dependency on humanitarian aid. Trades like tailoring, handicrafts, and carpentry will empower clients to pursue income-generation activities feasible in the camp. The provision of tools and home-based learning will further strengthen capacity to apply skills and meet household needs. The project also emphasizes community support networks linking refugees to camp-based marketplaces, volunteer roles, fostering a cohesive economic structure within the camp. By establishing pathways for skill application and earnings, it will enable refugees to create a foundation for self-reliance both within the camp and in preparation for potential future transitions.

4.2 Do project activities in the host community link to development initiatives, and/or align with development priorities in the Cox’s Bazar district? If yes, briefly describe.If yes, briefly describe.

Yes, the project activities in the host community align with the development priorities in Cox’s Bazar, specifically with the District Development and Growth Priorities (Dec 2022). The project's focus on enhancing workforce development through vocational skills training, entrepreneurship development, financial access, and market linkages for women and youth directly contributes to the district's goals of improving employment opportunities, economic growth, and social cohesion. By providing technical skills, business development support, and connecting communities to the private sector, the project also supports Cox’s Bazar's broader development objectives of fostering sustainable livelihoods, increasing women's economic empowerment, and strengthening local economic resilience. These activities are in line with the district’s priorities to promote youth employment and enhance the economic capacity of host communities.
