Project Summary

In 2024, DRC will continue integrating both its global strategy and country strategy into this joint response plan, aiming to enhance coordination with the food security sector at the grassroots level. Through these initiatives, DRC aims to increase socio-economic access of both Rohingya and host community men and women, elderly people and people with disability (PWD) by placing a strong emphasis on food security programming. A total of 7000 households and individuals will be reached out through this project where 2500 and 4500 Rohingya and host community, respectively those are at risk towards the effects of climatic change, environmental degradation, and natural disasters through overall protection services. The services include skill and knowledge development on green agriculture for increasing vegetable & crop production, waste management, climate sensitive innovative interventions, eco based adaptations, nature-based solutions, natural resource management, and some cash-based interventions (both conditional, unconditional, and multipurpose cash grants) for Rohingya men and women under the proposed food security project. In skill transfer initiatives, DRC will focus on improving the skills and awareness of Rohingya and affected host communities to adapt climate smart green agriculture, livestock, and fisheries production, eco system-based adaptation practices, bamboo made handicrafts production, land stabilization through plantation, soil conservation, construction, and reconstruction of community livelihood assets through mobilizing cash for works. Under green agriculture a good number of Rohingya women and host community women will increase their skill and knowledge on vertical gardening, compost preparation, biochar production from bamboo waste and using this compost fertilizer in their garden. Improving the skill and awareness on food security and livelihoods more emphasis will have invested to strengthening the institutional capacity of the community-based organizations/community groups on market systems approach- business plan development, entrepreneurship development, women friendly market space identification and improvement through renovation works. DRC will also initiate the technical capacity building of the community volunteers, CBO representatives, implementing partners and relevant field officials of government technical agencies like department of agriculture extension, Department of livestock’s services, department of fisheries, development of cooperatives, department of women and children affairs on conservation green technologies and CBOs sustainability approach for better services. Apart from Rohingya, the most vulnerable host community households will also receive multipurpose cash assistance support in compliance with the Cash Working Group guidance to cope with the recent price hike shocks that establishing the social cohesion among the communities. The food security team will continue to support the most vulnerable and marginalized individuals such as transgender and survivors of GBV, referred by the protection teams, through cash and or in-kind inputs such as cattle, poultry, sewing machine, vegetable seeds, nursery plants, etc. Finally, establishing strong linkage and coordination with government and other actors through increasing presence in camps and host and capacity development of local volunteers and community-based organizations will also be prioritized. By this piece of the project, all segments of the vulnerable Rohingya and host households - most vulnerable men and women, people with disabilities of all aged groups, households having with elder people, under two children, pregnant and lactating mothers -under targeted areas will be benefited for ensuring food and nutrition security at households’ level.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

"The assessment (REVA -6 Report, June 2023) findings showed that, despite the current level of humanitarian assistance, 38 percent of Rohingya households cannot afford the minimum expenditure basket (MEB) where economic vulnerability has decreased among Rohingya (by 13 percent) and Bangladeshi communities (1 percent) compared to 2021. However, if the assistance is removed, 93 percent of Rohingya would not be able to afford the MEB, showing significant dependence on humanitarian assistance for survival. The report is also showing that vulnerability like inadequate food consumption (poor and borderline-44%), are remains higher, where showed deteriorated condition for Bangladeshi (34%). This highlights the continued challenges Bangladeshi communities have faced in meeting their food consumption needs since the COVID 19 pandemic began and their slow recovery from job and livelihood losses. Both Rohingya and Bangladeshi households are struggling to put food on the table and are frequently resorting to negative coping strategies to meet their food needs. About two-thirds of households in the camps (65 percent) and half of the households in the Bangladeshi community (55 percent) relied on less preferred or less expensive food as the most frequently used coping strategy for both populations. The protracted Rohingya crisis in Bangladesh is becoming more and more dire with the increasing funding crisis and inflation due to the global economic crisis. In 2023, the situation will become increasing more dire for the Rohingya as significant cuts to the monthly ration loom on the horizon, potentially wiping out any slight improvements evidenced compared to 2021, especially with the dearth of income-generating opportunities available for the population (REVA-6 report).The price hikes of community’s consequences of the Covid pandemic also excavated by the Russia Ukraine crisis (the financial express, August 2022). Moreover, Cox’s Bazar and its surrounding districts of Bangladesh are among the lowest-performing districts in terms of education and skills training (World Bank, 2019). The district is historically lagging both in the economic performance as well as the human development index. There is significant discrepancy in terms of gender parity in labour market and educational attainment. The above findings, however, has clearly indicated to take for needs-based targeting of Rohingya households and prioritization of assistance, updating and revise the MEB, increase self-reliance programming and scale up existing programmes that promote the consumptions of nutrient dense foods as effective ways to improve nutrition among Rohingya households. As therefore, DRC aims to intended to increase food production and income generating opportunity for both Rohingya camps and host communities. DRC’s interventions mainly are vertical vegetable gardening, high value crop cultivation, nutrition rich gardening, livestock and fisheries production, go green approach-biochar production, composting/vermicompost preparation, etc. However, self-reliance and income-generating activities are also like waste management, land stabilization through plantation, DRR interventions through cash for works ought to rely on even more rigorous targeting criteria in the Rohingya and surrounding community. A scaled-up self-reliance programme in the camps and incomegenerating opportunities will contribute towards reducing the high vulnerability among the Rohingya community. These activities will strengthen the economic interlinkages with the Bangladeshi community and Rohingya population that effects to minimizing social tension of both communities which is essential. Considering the above challenges, the DRC’s proposed project will contribute to enhance the self-reliance and food security situation and building resilience among the communities through implementing the mentioned delivery and associated assistances."

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

In line with the rationalization Principle #3 for promoting integrated approach, DRC will aim to fill the gaps in food security services both in Rohingya and host targeted beneficiaries including PWD, ethnic minorities, and H2R communities. DRC has a strong presence in 7 camps (camp5, 8E, 8W,10,11 and12) where food security (FS) component only in two camps (Camp5 and 8E). From 2020, DRC attending regular meetings with FSS, submit 5W reports, adopting guidance, partaking field visits and adopt best practices. DRC has experienced team on FS component (agriculture, livestock, food security and DRR experts) and stay always connected with relevant sector, UN agencies (FAO, WFP) for better programme delivery and technical solutions with guidance/modules, targeting, avoiding duplication. DRC will also seek to improve the inter-sectoral camp-based coordination in a coordinated manner through SCCCM and integrating appropriate service delivery to refugees and host communities with the CiCs and other sector agencies. Considering Principle #4 for ensuring predictability and continuity, DRC will ensure its continuity and stability of the programme together with other donor funded projects by DANIDA, ECHO, SIDA, UN and other donors. DRC has a FS and DRR components by which will ensure integration with protection and continue integration of the interventions in both camp and host community to improve relations with refugees, the government, and other partners. In terms of sustainability strategy, DRC has adopted national partners-ESDO for delivering interventions. DRC has already secured 90% funds on FS for 2024 and planned to work with additional camps. Furthermore, DRC has ECHO and DANIDA-funded projects, which will ensure the activities continuation. All coordination will be maintained through camp management and Upazila administration at camps and host, respectively. Coordination and guidance will also be followed from ISCG’s FSS and Environment and Energy Network.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

As DRC is a protection focused organization, all protection policies and compliances will be followed in its food security programming in accordance with the guidance and coordination of the protection sector. As current practices general protection, protection monitoring, case management support will be sought from the DRC’s internal protection sector and the capacity of the Food security project staff will be developed by DRC's protection sector. However, four Protection Mainstreaming principles – a) safety, dignity, no harm, b) meaningful access, c) accountability and d) participation & empowerment will be promoted throughout the project implementation; while the Complaints and Feedback Mechanism (hotline for complaints and feedback) will be constantly upgraded in line with the DRC CFM Policy, procedures, and the DRC CFM Handbook. DRC has also engaged the PSEA Network to train camp volunteers during inductions, and DRC has a designated PSEA focal point for reports concerning DRC staff as part of needs assessment and risk analysis, project design, implementation, monitoring, and evaluations.

3.5.1 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

In the food security sector, the integration of Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) measures is of utmost importance, especially in refugee camps and host communities where displaced populations are vulnerable. By prioritizing the implementation of comprehensive PSEA strategies, organizations can uphold the dignity and well-being of beneficiaries during crises. This involves establishing clear policies, fostering a culture of accountability, and having zero tolerance for sexual exploitation and abuse. By incorporating PSEA into the core principles and practices of their operations, organizations create safe environments where women and children can access vital resources without fear of exploitation. Training and capacity-building initiatives are also fundamental in equipping staff and community members with the knowledge and skills to identify and respond to instances of sexual exploitation and abuse. Outreach programs within refugee camps and host communities play a pivotal role in raising awareness about PSEA, fostering community engagement, and encouraging the reporting of incidents without fear of reprisal. Efforts to mainstream PSEA not only safeguard the immediate well-being of vulnerable populations but also contribute to the overall resilience and stability of communities affected by displacement. By aligning humanitarian actions with the principles of protection, organizations can foster an environment that respects the rights and dignity of all individuals, laying the foundation for sustainable development and recovery in the face of adversity.
