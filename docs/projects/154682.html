Project Summary

As the humanitarian crisis in Iraq has entered a new phase and hundreds of thousands of displaced people are willing to return to their communities, major efforts are undertaken to clear return areas of explosive hazards and rubble and restore vital infrastructure and socio-economic facilities. At the same time there is increased pressure on kick starting local economies and encouraging education, livelihoods and economic opportunities, so that the returnees can access credit, gain incomes and provide for their families, therefore being self-sustaining.
In line with the 2019 Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP) and the Protection Cluster (PC) strategic objectives and based on the Mine Action Sub-Cluster (MASC) strategic interventions, activities and geographical focus, this project seeks to deliver much needed mine action programming to some of the most affected communities in Ninewa, Kirkuk and Salah al-Din in the post-conﬂict phase.
More speciﬁcally the project will seek to address HRP SO1 “increase access to basic services for the most vulnerable conﬂict-affected people and ensure responsible transition to durable solutions for those with prolonged humanitarian needs” and PC O1 “provide specialized protection assistance, and community-based support, to vulnerable conﬂict- affected individuals, thereby enabling them to secure their rights, access humanitarian assistance and public services, and overcome barriers to achievement of durable solutions.”
With more camps planning to close in 2019, survey and clearance activities in areas of origin and return and risk education activities in both camp and out of camp settings as well as informal settlements remain at the core of the humanitarian response.
By implementing non-technical (NTS) and technical survey (TS), manual and mechanical clearance as well as risk education (RE) and community liaison (CL) activities, DDG ‘ efforts will encourage future returns of population as well as help relieve the pressure put on communities that host large numbers of IDPs. By facilitating humanitarian access as well as allowing some of the most vulnerable people to access

services and vital infrastructure, DDG will facilitate socio-economic development and contribute to the transition towards durable solutions in the post-conﬂict environment across some of the most affected areas in Iraq. DDG will also continue to support the efforts made by the Government of Iraq and subsequently the Directorate of Mine Action (DMA), in achieving their priorities with regards to the mine action response while mitigating the threats posed by explosive hazards.

Needs

According to data collected by the end of October 2018 through IOM’s Displacement Tracking Matrix, the number of returnees across eight of the most affected governorates totaled 4.1 million, with returns to Ninewa, Anbar and Salah-al Din representing 84% of the total number of returns. Approximately 1.8 million people are still displaced across Iraq, with 1,483,068 people displaced only in Ninewaa, Salah-al Din and Kirkuk governorates. Ninewa remains the epicentre of the crisis, with 39% of all returns or approximately 1.6 million people. Out of these, 944,718 returns were recorded in Mosul alone, meaning that Mosul remains one of the most affected areas in Iraq, with over half of the entire number of returnees to Ninewa concentrated in East and West Mosul. 1,121,820 people are still displaced across Ninewa. In Salah-al Din, the total number of returnees is 575,736 or 14% of the total number of returnees across Iraq. Out of these, 171,336 returns have been recorded in Tikrit alone, or 30% of the total number of returns in Salah-al Din. Al-Shirqat and Baiji districts follow closely with 123,234 returns in Al-Shirwat and 77,034 in Baiji. 233,580 people still remain displaced in Salah-al-Din with a majority living in camp and out of camp settings as well as informal settlements. They are at high risk of having to forcefully return to their areas of origin in the last months of 2018 or early 2019, as authorities threaten to close the camps and complexes where they currently reside. In Kirkuk governorate, a total of 313,764 returnees have been recorded by the end of October 2018, with 152,952 people returning to Kirkuk city. Approximately 127,668 people still remain displaced across the governorate. In addition to the lack of basic services and vital infrastructure in Ninewa, Kirkuk and Salah-al Din, the threat of explosive hazards remains the main struggle for returnees, IDPs and host communities, putting displaced families at risk and impeding safe returns. Moreover, without the prioritization of clearance activities, upon return, people in these vulnerable communities are at risk of increased poverty levels. Families are concerned about their safety due to signiﬁcant destruction of houses and massive contamination of explosive hazards. Upon return, many families are staying in partially destroyed houses that may collapse at any time while a high number of men and young boys are putting themselves at risk trying to remove rubble where explosive hazards can be found. Although there is no accurate information on the total number of casualties, the majority of victims are young and adult males who get injured or killed while grazing their kettle or trying to remove the rubble, while children mainly get injured or killed while playing with explosive hazards in contaminated areas. As the main income for vulnerable families comes from agricultural activities and the agricultural lands are littered with explosive hazards, livelihood opportunities and the ability to access income are seriously hampered. Once the areas are cleared, assistance should be provided to support and restore services including distribution of clean delivery kits and the provision of food and water.

Activities

Ninewa Activities: Non-Technical Survey/Community Liaison/Clearance In Ninewa DDG plans to conduct much needed Non-Technical Survey (NTS), Community Liaison (CL) and clearance in West Mosul and Hamdaniya. The multi-trained NTS/CL/clearance teams will deploy in parallel, at ﬁrst collecting information and building a clearer picture of the location, size and type of contamination and if any explosive items are found deploying clearance assets on the spot. This method does not only allow DDG to remove a large number of explosive hazards but to also cancel vast numbers of square meters through NTS, thus potentially returning areas to the communities to be used for socio- economic purposes. All clearance prioritization will be done in close conjunction and cooperation with RMAC-N and UNMAS. Apart from survey and clearance methods, DDG will also deploy RE teams to continue conducting awareness sessions about the risks posed by explosive hazards and how to adopt safe behavior when encountering such items. Kirkuk Activities: Technical Survey/Community Liaison/ Mechanical Clearance In Kirkuk DDG will continue its survey and clearance operations in both Kirkuk and Daquq sub-district by deploying multi-trained technical survey assets in parallel with mechanical assets, thus allowing the teams to clear and release a larger number of sqm to the local communities. The mechanical asset will be used in a dual role for both the technical survey, clearance of base lanes and to remove improvised landmines (ILM). This will enable DDG to safely remove ILMs and greatly speed up the removal process. It is expected that the machine will clear around 5,000m² per hour, this number is based on achievements produced by other mine action partners using the same equipment. DDG will also deploy RE to conduct sessions for IDPs, returnees and host communities. Salah al Din Activities: Non-Technical Survey/Clearance/Risk Education/Community Liaison In Salah-al Din, multi-trained RE/NTS/CL teams will be deployed in Tikrit and will target mainly IDPs residing in Al Alam Camp (where approx. 2,982 people/ 497 families live), Qadissya Complex (with approx.9,300 people living in 1060 households) and Dream City (approx. 4,656 people or 776 families living here). The teams will also seek to conduct RE sessions targeting other IDPs or people seeking to return to villages in Baiji sub-district. The DDG RE sessions will be tailored based on gender and age considerations to reach both males and females as well as different age and gender groups and all subsequent data will be disaggregated. Moreover, all RE staff will be trained in CL techniques. Appropriate and context relevant materials such as posters, leaﬂets and note-books with explosive hazards awareness messages will be distributed among targeted population. Baiji town was completely destroyed during the conﬂict with Daesh. A large number of explosive hazards have been left behind by militants while thousands of booby traps were left in abandoned houses. Some of the explosive devices have been defused by the ISF, while a large number of explosive hazards are still not cleared. In 2017, when displaced people were allowed to go home, there were several mine/IED related casualties reported, involving children and adults, and explosive hazards still claim new victims. The clearance team will target highly contaminated areas in Baiji based on information received from the survey teams.

Mitigation Measures

1. DDG will maintain continuous communication with the security and humanitarian actors in the areas of operation to assess safety. 2. If is not possible to access project areas for a prolonged period, the project timeline as well as area of implementation will be adjusted as a result of guidance provided. DDG has recruited and will continue to recruit and train all staff from local population and communities to mitigate from an early stage any potential future issues. 3. By following DRC/DDG internal policies as well as the Standard Operating Procedures, all operational activities are planned and implemented ensuring all risk assessments are carried out and full safety briefs are given on the start of a task as well as daily and on completion. DDG carries out regular internal quality assurance checks to monitor the team’s performances to ensure all tasks are carried out safely and in line with DDG SOP’s, regular feedback is given to team leaders to continue development. Close and consistent communications are carried out with national security forces to ensure all items located are removed safely and then disposed of accordingly. Regular training is done to, both practical and theoretical to maintain the highest of standards at all times, teams and medics are regularly tested with casualty evacuation drills and full debriefs are given post training.4. DDG tries to conduct prior assessments regarding existing House Land and Property (HLP) issues in all areas where it plans to implement activities. DDG integrates conflict sensitivity into programming and tries to gather information through community liaison efforts. DDG is also in constant communication with local authorities, other implementing partners and organizations dealing specifically with conflict resolution on HLP issues. The DDG survey teams are trained in lines of questioning that will help them identify and flag any potential HLP issues, for referral to NGOs specialising in this area in both Mosul and Salah al-Din.

How is your project accountable to affected population

DDG is committed to ensure transparency, accountability and informed program adaptation and development through lessons learned, so that projects have a positive and durable impact on the communities we support. The principles of participation, accountability and "Do no harm" are integral to DDG's sustainability strategy. By consulting closely with target populations on their needs, constraints and vulnerabilities throughout the entire project cycle as well as with local authorities and mukhtars, DDG ensures that projects are tailored and address real community needs. During survey and risk education activities, the teams collect vital information through interviews directly from community members about the location and size of contamination and receive special training on how to flag any pre-existing factors that could exacerbate the conflict, especially those related to HLP issues. DDG teams reach out to members of the communities of all genders and ages to try and capture reliable data, while their answers and the general data used for creating baseline and end line assessments, as well as for monitoring and evaluation purposes is recoded and disseminated based on gender, age and diversity considerations. DDG ensures high levels of accountability through the Core Humanitarian Standard (CHS) certiﬁcation, which focuses highly on beneﬁciary information and involvement in all phases of project implementation. DRC/DDG proactively seeks on- going input from beneﬁciaries through community engagement and discussions and based on the constantly changing situation on the ground adapts its programming and theories of change. Moreover, DRC/DDG has established a complaints/feedback mechanism which beneﬁciaries can access to report issues relating to unfair treatment during service provision, violations of the DRC/DDG code of conduct by staff members, as well as pose queries or provide feedback on service provision.
