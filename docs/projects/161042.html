Project Summary

War Child Holland (WCH) has been implementing child protection, psychosocial support, case management and cash based programming projects in Eastern Equatoria, Upper Nile and Jonglei States targeting communities affected by conflict and displacement including IDPs, host communities and returnees. Backed with its substantial experience in protection sector, War Child is proposing community base child protection, PSS, case management and cash based programming with focus on child protection risk prevention, mitigation and response in line with child protection sub cluster strategic objectives (S1 , S2 and S3) and protection cluster strategic objectives (SO 1, SO 2 and SO3) . Extremely vulnerable children with protection concern and their caregivers will be the primary targets of the project including children affected by separation, recruitment, psychological distress and different forms of violence and exploitation.
The purpose of interventions is to contribute to strategic objectives of child protection sub cluster and protection cluster through interventions designed to enhance access to case management services for vulnerable children as well as age and gender appropriate PSS for vulnerable girls, boys and their caregivers. The project also prioritize community based child protection and psychosocial support capacity strengthening of local community structures and actors to prevent and respond to violence through effective coordination and evidence based programming. The project envisages to reach 21,400 children and caregivers through different interventions based on their specific needs in 4 project locations. WCH is member of CPSC and relevant TWCs both at national and state levels. Hence, the interventions are aligned with cluster priorities and minimum standards. Summary of specific interventions planned in the proposed sites included but not limited to:
• Conduct participatory child protection risk assessment in project locations in collaboration with other partners to identify issues affecting children in the community
• Provide capacity building training on child protection for 80 local government social workers, teachers and representatives (45 M, 35 F)
• Train 120 CBCPN members and leaders (50 M, 70 F) to enhance ownership and leadership on child protection and psychosocial support actions at community level in 4 project locations.
• Support operation of 14 child friendly spaces (CFS) in project locations to provide psychosocial support to 7,500 children through semi-structured, structured and recreational activities in 4 locations.
• Identify and support 500 extremely vulnerable children (280 B, 320 G) with protection concern including separated children, children living in female headed households, children experienced sexual violence and child labor and children without parental care.
• Provide un-restricted/emergency cash assistance to 100 vulnerable children (40 B, 60 G) in project locations on the basis of need identified.
• Facilitate parenting and psychosocial support sessions to 1,100 caregivers (450 M, 650 F) who are experiencing psychological distress and facing difficulty to provide adequate support to their children
• Organize community level awareness raising and advocacy mass events to reach 14, 500 children (6200 B, 8300 G) and 6,500 adults (2700 M, 3500 F) with prevention message focusing on separation, recruitment, child marriage, sexual violence, child labor, corporal punishment, and neglect .This includes the use of local radio, promotional materials such as t-shirt, banner, posters etc.
• Train and support in-school and out-of-school based child right and protection clubs to reach 2,500 children (1100 B, 1400 G) through life skills activities to effectively address psychosocial needs of children and build their resilience.
• Strengthen referral pathway in 4 project locations to ensure effective collaboration and multi-sectoral response to needy children

1 - Needs

Some 2.5 million children and adults in South Sudan were identified as in need of protection support including IDPs, separated and unaccompanied children (UASC), GBV survivors, children associated with armed groups and forces (CAAFAG) and communities living in Counties severely impacted by the protracted crisis (HNO 2020). The proposed 5 locations (Malakal, Fashoda, Magwi , Bor South and Pigi Canal) are categorized as Tier 1 showing severity level of protection needs. The project catchment area hosts significant size of children and caregivers in need of protection response including people in PoC, IDP settlements, returnees and host communities. Due to the recent instability in Sudan and anticipation of positive outcome from the ongoing peace process at national level, number of returnee population in project catchment area has increased in 2019 both from neighboring countries and surrounding IDP locations. According to 2019 HNO, there are more than 1.4 million IDPs across South Sudan including 29,000 in Malakal PoC and 8500 in Aburoch/Fashoda IDP camp. As a result of adverse consequences of conflict and worsening humanitarian situation, community members in general and children and women in particular has suffered from psychological distress and violation of protection rights. Conflict affected children in South Sudan will continue to experience psychosocial and physiological distress as the current protection environment is likely to remain the same specially for children who are displaced from their places of origin (HNO 2020). Impacts of population displacement and deterioration of traditional support mechanisms affected the capacity of caregivers to provide adequate care for children and other vulnerable community members who are often dependent on social support. There are more than 19,000 children affected by recruitment, 1 out of 2 women or girls experienced GBV, 15,000 children were separated from their primary caregivers (HNO 2019). MSF (2019) also reported that there are limited MHPSS services to community members compared to alarming needs of the people especially children and women. Specific child protection and psychosocial support issues affecting children in proposed Counties include: - Increased risk of girls and boys to recruitment, sexual violence, school dropout, neglect, child marriage, child labor and mine risks.- Existence of large number of children affected by separation and neglect due to population movement and stretched caring capacity of families. - Psychological distress and self-harm behaviors among children and caregivers due to exposure to violence, displacements and livelihood disruption - Gaps in child protection and psychosocial support interventions in the proposed locations- Deterioration of traditional support mechanisms for children and other vulnerable community members due to the impacts of conflict and displacement. - Exposure of vulnerable children to risky activities including recruitment, exploitation, robbery and street life - Significant technical capacity gaps of child protection and welfare focused ministries and community based structures and institutions in planning, coordination, implementation, monitoring and evaluation of child protection and psychosocial support interventions in the proposed locations War Child Holland has presence and operation in Malakal, Fashoda and Magwi, and Bor with established working relationship with key stakeholders in the proposed states and locations. Hence, this will help to effectively implement additional protection response within the proposed Counties.

6.6 - Will beneficiaries' rights be legally recognised?

The proposed project locations have basic functional government system in place. Hence, project activities will be coordinated with relevant local and state level authorities to ensure legal rights of the beneficiaries to access services including access to housing and land.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

The proposed project locations such as Malakal, Fashoda/Aburoch, Magwi and Pigi Canal hosts significant number of IDPs and returnees. Hence, most of the project beneficiaries will be IDPs and returnees who are in need of child protection and psychosocial support and services.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

War Child uses community led approach to child protection to ensure the sustainability of interventions and promote community ownership. The project activities include capacity strengthening of child focused government offices and community structures (CBCPNs) which are responsible for child protection.These capacity building activities and community participation enables to enhance local capacity and to effectively deal with child protection issues in the future. Community structures and local government will be involved in assessment, planning and implementation of project interventions.

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

The proposed project is aligned with national priorities as relate to support to IDPs, returnees and host communities. There is ongoing discussion and preparation in project locations among protection partners and state authorities including contingency planning in reference to IDPs and returnees. Hence, the project activities considered both government and humanitarian plans and guidelines.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

The proposed project activities will build on existing community structures and capacity with the view of sustaining impact and foster community ownership. Hence, community structures including child protection committees, schools, faith based organizations and CFS will receive capacity strengthening support to enable them to continue implementation of basic child protection and psychosocial support activities in a longer run with minimum support.The interventions will also help to ensure smooth recovery and strengthen resilience of both children and caregivers to effectively cope with the new environment. With anticipated positive peace agreement implementation process, the locations are expected to receive large number of returnees both from IDP and refugee camps on top of the existing IDPs and returnees. Hence, proposed activities contribute to assess protection risk , provide necessary information about availability of services in the project catchment areas and assist smooth integration of returnees.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

As part of exit strategy, the project focuses on empowering community structures through engaging them on all phases of project cycle including on assessment, planning, implementation and monitoring of activities. Hence, project activities and structures will be handed over to community leaders and local authorities for continued implementation of activities and use of project structures.

8.18 - Please indicate how the risks selected above will be mitigated.

1) Despite women and girls constitute significant proportion of the population and disproportionately affected by the impacts of conflict and violence, their participation in project design and management is very limited. The influence of local tradition and increasing domestic work on women and girls can be cited as some of reasons preventing or limiting their participation. Cognizant of this challenges, conscious effort will be made to encourage women and girls involvement in project activities. To this end, consultations will be conducted with community leaders to include women in leadership structure and increase their participation in project activities. 2) Anticipated influx of returnees to the project locations pause risk of land and property dispute as IDPs in some locations such as Malakal are occupying the plots of expected returnees. Hence, WCH will closely work with other protection cluster members and local authorities so that preparations are made beforehand to avoid potential risk. Local authorities are expected to play a key role to address outstanding HLP issues. 3) As part of commitment to ensure accountability to affected population, WCH will put in place community feedback and complaint mechanism in place. To this end, WCH closely works with community leaders and beneficiaries and actively seek feedback using different channels depending on the preference of beneficiary including organizing monthly meeting with adults, children and women groups; use of hotline number and placing feedback registration book and suggestion boxes in project sites.
