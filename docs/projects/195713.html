Project Summary

PUI’s FOCOS strategy programming continues to be undertaken multi-year strategically, with 2022 and 2023 representing the eighth year of implementation of PUI’s integrated-area-based approach (IABA) in Syria. Through this approach, PUI makes sure to create synergies between the different interventions implemented in areas in need in order to respond to the needs of the Population of Syria as a whole and to help them to go back to their pre-crisis standards of living to enhance self-sufficiency and resilience in the long run and to promote social cohesion.
In line with PUI’s I4SC approach (initial standards, self-sufficiency and social cohesion)and the Early Recovery and Livelihoods sector’s objectives, PUI aims to support vulnerable conflict-affected populations toward self-sufficiency. The project will be focused on developing and enhancing income-generating capacities through:
1- Support to 500 families through the provision of Start-up Small Business Grants (SSBG) at the admin four level. Aleppo Governorate (Aleppo 90, Dayr Hafir 30), Dara Governorate (Dara 60, Busra Esh-Sham 20, Sheikh Miskine 15, Tafas 25), Deir-ez-Zor Governorate (Al Mayadin 30, Deir-ez-Zor 100, Ayyash 25, Hawayej 25, Kharita 25 and Salhiyeh 15) and 40 SSBG in Rural Damascus (Dhameer).
2- Rehabilitation of 3 Vocational Training High Schools in Palmyra, Quaryatain and Qussair in Homs governorate;
3- Distribution of Livestock to 75 families in Dara governorate Agricultural activities (Kits-Inputs);
4- Support to the community in the rehabilitation of the Irrigation System in one in Aleppo (Dayr Hafir), one in Dara, 3 in Deir-ez-Zor (Al Mayadin, Deir-ez-Zor and Salhia) and 3 irrigation channel rehabilitation in Lattakia (Al-Haffa, Rabee'a and Salanfa);
5- PUI will also support the rehabilitation of 2 Bakeries, one each in Aleppo (Hanano) and Dara (Kafr Shams)
Through the proposed livelihood interventions, PUI is planning to reach a total of 97,975 individuals in the targeted Governorates while all the interventions are planned at the admin four level. PUI estimated 2,635,000 USD to support the proposed interventions with 27 USD per beneficiary cost.

3 - Needs

Based on the 2022 HNO, 14.6 million people need humanitarian assistance, an increase of 1.2 million from 2021. Similarly, 13.9 million Syrians require early recovery and livelihood assistance. Over 10 years of crisis have inflicted immense suffering on the civilian population. Throughout 2021, the macroeconomic context continued to deteriorate sharply and is accelerating into 2022. The combined effects of currency depreciation, soaring prices, reduced fiscal revenue and increasing domestic debt, widespread job loss, and unilateral coercive measures have plunged additional segments of the population into humanitarian need, most notably in areas historically less affected by hostilities and displacement. People’s ability to meet basic needs has decreased compared to 2021, with a disproportionate impact on female-headed households, older persons without family support, persons with disabilities and children. An increasing number of women and adolescent girls are forced to work to provide for their families, either as sole breadwinners or contributing to the family income. Across Syria, households are now reverting to negative coping mechanisms more frequently than before. This includes child labor and child marriage, and the sale of productive assets. On average, less than two third of those surveyed indicated they had a job in the last three months. Nonetheless, the top need for 71 percent of households remains food baskets/bread, immediately followed by livelihoods (56).

4 - Implementation of activities

Regarding the distribution of tools, materials and SSBG activities, PUI has standard SoPs for implementing those activities as follows:1- Assessment Phase: PUI will conduct community consultations with the local communities and representatives from local authorities to discuss needs and how to best respond to each need.2- Announcement and Registration phase: PUI will announce its intention to implement the agreed-upon activity, launch beneficiaries' registration, and conduct verification visits. 3- Selection of beneficiaries based on pre-established selection criteria.4- Training the selected beneficiaries on relevant topics based on the activity (agriculture best practices for ATK, business skills for PTK, etc.)5- Provision of needed materials, goods or equipment, PUI will launch a procurement process to supply the beneficiaries with the needed materials.6- Distribution of the materials on the selected beneficiaries.7- Conducting monitoring visits to provide needed guidance and assistance for the beneficiaries.8- Conduct PDM for the implemented activities to measure beneficiaries' satisfaction and impact. While for the irrigation networks and bakery rehabilitation, PUI will consult with the local communities and contract a contractor to implement the agreed-upon works. For the MPCA, PUI will register the names of beneficiaries based on selection criteria and provide the selected beneficiaries with multiple installments.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

PUI identified the key protection risks before the launch of the intervention to ensure mitigation measures are in place to reduce the likelihood and impact of these risks on the project. Below are the risks identified, along with related mitigation measures:-Risk: The security situation deteriorates, and humanitarian support (whether directly from PUI or contracted vendors) cannot reach the target area. (Likelihood: High – Impact: High)Miti: 1. Assess the situation in targeted areas before the intervention and constantly during the implementation; 2. PUI and the donor retain certain levels of flexibility linked to the selected areas of intervention in case it requires shifting due to the lack of access.Risk: Selection of beneficiaries and lack of community ownership can create community tensions. (Likelihood: Medium- Impact: High)Miti measures: 1. Perform conflict analysis before planning the intervention; 2. Ensure as much as possible, considering the accessibility constraint, to provide equal services in areas where tensions exist; 3. Ensure local community participation in the design, implementation, monitoring and evaluation of the project to ensure good acceptance and avoid the creation of any tensions at the community level due to the intervention. Risk: Incidents of child labor, youth marginalization and GBV (including Sexual exploitation by humanitarian workers). Likelihood: Medium- Impact: High) Mitigation measures: 1. Cooperation with other stakeholders.


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

Première Urgence Internationale has a zero-tolerance policy towards sexualexploitation and abuse (SEA). As such, PUI is committed to implementing the six principles adopted in 2012 by the Inter-Agency Standing Committee Task Force on preventing and responding to sexual exploitation and abuse. Risk: Vulnerablebeneficiaries are exposed to sexual exploitation and abuse by PUI staff or other related entities; (Likelihood: low. Impact: High)Mitigation measures: Raise the awareness of existing service providers where victims can seek help; Enforce policies with/through all partners to prevent beneficiaries’ exposure to these risks. Establish a confidential complaint mechanism; Train Staff and other involved personnel on positive discipline; Train staff on code of conduct; address any SEA complaints.


9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Assumptions:1. No curfew or lockdown imposed by the government due to security or pandemic;2. Acceptance of the local population for PUI´s interventions remain high;3. Humanitarian access to the proposed locations continues;4. Approvals for the proposed activities are obtained;5. The security situation remains stable;6. No mass movements of populations into/out of intervention areas;7. No major fluctuation in exchange rates Risks: 1. Lack of accountability creates high unmet expectations;2. Aids is diverted to armed groups;3. Limited facilitation by partners contributes to a delay in implementation;4. Variations in exchange rates negatively impact local procurement;5. Independent access to beneficiaries is restricted during monitoring stages;6. Beneficiaries awarded for the SBPs are unable to obtain necessary authorizations to establish businesses;7. Productive assets procured through the project are sold on the local market by beneficiaries
