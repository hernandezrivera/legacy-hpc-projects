Project Summary

This project seeks to ensure food availability in the Mayo Tsanaga division for at least 600 households through the establishment of and strengthening the sustainability of cereal banks and to support increased agricultural production in at least 150 beneficiary households. Cereal banks as a strategy to reduce food insecurity have been successfully tested by RELUFA for about 15 years now and have even been hailed by many, including the UN Special Rapporteur Oliver De Schutter while visiting Cameroon in 2012. He said that cereal banks as an approach to reduce food insecurity need to be encouraged and supported. This mechanism allow communities to borrow or buy food in the village at low cost by community members during the lean season to be reimbursed and restocked during the harvest period. It facilitate positive impacts for communities’ reduction of household hunger, reduction of village outward migration and promotion of family cohesion, extension of cultivated areas etc.
The direct beneficiaries will be vulnerable households that derive most of their income from agriculture but have difficulty accessing cereals during the lean season. Local traditional authorities, agents of the local councils and community leaders will be involved in the project for proper selection and ensuring the sustainability of the action to be implemented and through capacity buildings. Capacity building for beneficiary communities that integrates all aspects related to the technical and financial management of cereal banks, monitoring and price analysis for a better decision making, to nutrition education are key for the sustainability and ownership by the beneficiaries. All the associated activities offered in the creation of a cereal bank, including initial cereal allocation to the community, capacity building on storage and conservation and building of storage facilities, promotes better food availability, physical and economic access to food and improved nutritional status.
At least 50% of the participants are expected to be women as the formation of women's groups will be encouraged. From our past experiences, women have proven to be the best managers of cereal banks that we have operated so far. In the region, women are always sidelined, but having women led groups is a strategy for engaging and involving them in decision making.
Local councils will be targeted to include cereal banks in their communal development plans and extending them to other communities in the long term thus increasing the number of beneficiaries.
In order to increase agricultural production in beneficiary households, we will have to go through capacity building on techniques of adaptation to climate change and fertilization of degraded soils, including agro-ecological, by financial support for the implementation of these techniques and by the provision of agricultural inputs such as improved seeds and irrigation equipment for communities close to riverbanks.
Community members have participated in the design of the project by providing information on the actions that can be taken to improve the food insecurity concerns. Beneficiaries will contribute during the implementation by devoting their time to the management of the cereal banks. They will also provide space, locally available material for construction of the storage facility (sand, gravel) and also some local labour. The initial training is clear that they hold the keys not only of granaries, but also of the decisions they want to make for their communities according to their needs and their context.
The communities have the possibility to denounce any interference or attempted corruption or diversion of their stocks by any person of power in the region such as elites or animators or to formulate any other complaint through the supervisory committee which will be set up. This confidence in their "powers" therefore allows them to take ownership of the project.

1 - Besoins / Needs

Food production in some divisions of the Far North region is very regularly in deficit due to climatic hazards and other anthropogenic factors. For many households, the annual coverage of food needs is often only 5 to 6 months out of the 12 months of the year with a lean period from July to September characterized by low food availability in most villages. Since 2013, the Boko Haram sect’s insurgency has made the food insecurity situation even worst due to the influx of refugees from Nigeria and over 342.416 internally displaced persons fleeing from border villages.The diet of the population is essentially cereals-based (millet, sorghum, maize). They are often confronted with various difficulties particularly during the lean period (July to September) which makes it difficult to meet their food and nutrition needs. Poverty estimated at 7.4.3% in this region limits their capacity to buy food and other basic needs. Most households are forced to adopt survival strategies such as reducing the number of meals (from three to two or one) per day or qualitatively and quantitatively reducing the food consumed within households. This has an impact on the nutritional status of households and particularly on the most vulnerable members such as children and breastfeeding women. In many communities, as a way of coping, men have had to leave the village in search of money to buy food for their families.Physical access to markets for supplies for those who can buy is made difficult by poor nature of the roads especially during the rainy season when food reserves are exhausted. This is why it is important to support the creation of local food storage facilities in the villages as a strategy to combat hunger.More than 30% of the region's soils are infertile and this rate is increasing from year to year. Even if some initiatives are undertaken by various organizations to promote the techniques of fertilization of degraded soils, it’s still not enough. People still need capacity building and financial support to cope with the climate change effects. People also need support for irrigation in terms of capacity building and equipment. One response from RELUFA in concert with these communities is to create cereal banks to ensure the availability of cereals in the villages during the lean season so that certain households would have access to food. All the associated activities offered in the creation of a cereal bank, including initial cereal allocation to the community, capacity building on storage and conservation and building of storage facilities, promotes better food availability, physical and economic access to food and improved nutritional status. Capacity building for beneficiary communities that integrates all aspects related to the technical and financial management of cereal banks, monitoring and price analysis for a better decision making, to nutrition education are key for the sustainability and ownership by the beneficiaries. At least 50% of the participants are expected to be women as the formation of women's groups will be encouraged. In the region, women are always sidelined, but having women led groups is a strategy for engaging and involving them in decision making. Ensuring the sustainability of the cereal banks would involve the promotion of good practices within a network of cereal banks and anchoring in institutions with a permanent presence in the field, such as the local councils.In order to increase agricultural production in beneficiary households, we will have to go through capacity building on techniques of adaptation to climate change and fertilization of degraded soils, including agro-ecological, by financial support for the implementation of these techniques and by the provision of agricultural inputs such as improved seeds and irrigation equipment for communities close to riverbanks.
