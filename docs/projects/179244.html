Project Summary

The overall objective of the Emergency Food Assistance for Syrian Households (EFASH) project is to improve the overall food security among vulnerable Syrian households. This will be achieved through the provision of food vouchers to vulnerable communities of men, women, boys, girls and people with disability by providing an approximate of 70 percent of their monthly food requirements.
Priority locations where ADRA has operational presence and ongoing programs include Rural Damascus, Homs, Aleppo and Deir Al Zour are also in alignment with the Food Security and Agriculture Sector. Exact locations will be selected in close coordination with local partners such as the Syrian Arab Red Crescent (SARC), WFP, FAO and the FSA sector. Prioritization will be given to areas with high food security severity scales such as Northern Rural Homs, Aleppo and Rural Damascus (Duma), presence of IDPs/returnees and areas with limited previous interventions (such as newly accessible areas).
Duma is located in East Ghouta, Rural Damascus and was heavily involved in the conflict. It was besieged from 2013 until it came under Government of Syria (GoS) control in 2018. The area is still heavily controlled with access restrictions for those wishing to enter and leave. According to ADRA assessments in Duma city and surrounds, levels of food insecurity remain high, especially with the most recent economic hardships faced during 2020. From those assessed, 57% had an unacceptable food consumption score, 73% reported that they had not enough food to meet their family needs in the past 12 months and an average dietary diversity of 8 . The major issue faced by much of the population is access to food commodities, rather than availability, with the average monthly household income found to be only $40 .
Northern rural Homs, encompassing Al Rastan, Telbisa and Teldu was also under siege until 2018 when it became accessible to humanitarian intervention. The area was heavily damaged, including to basic infrastructure, housing and farming land. The population have been slowly returning since that time. Despite being an agricultural area, the levels of food insecurity remain high, due mostly due to limited livelihood opportunities and widespread poverty (average monthly income of $31 with an average of $94 of debt ) with high levels of disability. 72% reported that there were months they did not have enough food to meet their family needs, with 59% having an unacceptable food consumption score .
The city of Aleppo (Jabal Saman) was spilt between opposing forces until 2016 that extensively damaged local infrastructure and resulted in mass displacement. What was once the most populous city in Syria, Aleppo became home to the highest number of individuals classified as food insecure. As a highly populated urban area, the market is fully functional, although the most vulnerable lack sufficient funds to access sufficient food commodities.
A total of 10,000 food insecure households will be targeted to receive eight months’ worth of food assistance of the twelve-month program through a food voucher directly given to beneficiaries equivalent to the amount of $70, to redeemed from local contracted venders, the voucher will be specific to a range of nutritious food item list endorsed by the FSA sector. All activities of the project will be aligned with the FSA sector SO1 response package and will be aligned with WFP through bilateral discussions.
Distribution of the food vouchers will contribute to positive outcomes in livelihoods of the beneficiaries allowing them to spend their income on their other needs such as health and education.

4 - Needs

The number of People in Need (PiN) and who are food insecure have risen to 12.4 million, of which 1.3 million people are considered severely food insecure. Significant need exists in the targeted areas of Homs and Rural Damascus as well as Aleppo and Idleb with many communities classified with a CARI score between 4 and 6. Food security has further deteriorated with many individuals being moderately to severely food insecure: 59.1% Aleppo governorate, 40.6% of Rural Damascus, 51.5% in Homs and 86% in Idleb (FSA Factsheet 2020). It is also reported that 50% of Syrian HH spend 75% of their income on food, while 28% are adopting crisis or emergency livelihood coping strategies (HNO 2021). Food prices in Syria have increased dramatically in 2020 and WFP’s national standard reference food basket price in November 2020 was 251% higher when compared to November 2019 (2021 Needs and response Summary). The reference food basket, which is a basic food basket, meant to support the food needs for a five-member household, is now higher than the highest-paid official government monthly salary of SYP 80,240 and the prices continue to soar in 2021. Based on ADRA’s observation, most IDPs and other vulnerable crisis-affected people in Syria remain dependent on humanitarian assistance to meet their basic needs, including food, education, health care, shelter and water, sanitation and hygiene services. The national assessment showed that 52.8% of IDPs were food insecure, indicating a higher level of vulnerability than residents (49.7% ) while returnees were found to be the most food insecure at 60.3% (HNO 2021)

5 - Implementation of activities

The potential beneficiaries will be identified through SARC and other local partners and will undergo the verification process. The selection criteria will be set in line with the FSA recommendations including but not limited to HH demographic indicators (FHH, PLW, PWD, family size), access to income and accommodation status. Each household will receive a monthly voucher based on the FSA sector and CWG guidelines estimated to cover a minimum of 1,500 Kcal per person per day of family consumption (approximately 70% of daily requirements) for a period of 8 monthly cycles allowing the targeted HHs an opportunity to return to their previous income generating activities, improve their nutritious status and to support local markets. A list of items will be identified including the basic food parcel and nutritious commodities such as fresh fruits and vegetables, dairy, canned fish and other culturally acceptable commodities ensuring the nutritional needs are met of children adolescent adults, older adults, people with disabilities as well as male and female. Nutritional/health messages in will be displayed on vouchers, to raise the awareness on health and nutrition concepts. Special consideration will be given to encourage PWD and FHHs by allowing a secondary family member to assist with the voucher collection and redemption. Gender and disability will be taken into consideration, making sure that the distribution is during culturally appropriate hours of the day. During and post-monitoring will be conducted by male and female staff and feedback solicited from young children, children, adolescents, young adults, adults, middle-aged adults and older adults for their preferences and suggestions for adjustments.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

ADRA Syria will ensure that the project will be implemented in coordination with all stakeholders, following a ‘do no harm’ approach. However, ADRA recognizes that due to the nature of the conflict, there is a possibility that some risk may occur. There is a risk that the food redeemed will not be shared with all family members, or household members will be put under pressure to share the food received through the vouchers with other family members. Another risk may be put on project staff by local community members to add beneficiaries that do not fit the project selection criteria. ADRA will maintain close relationships with local community members and other relevant stakeholders. Before the implementation of the project, ADRA will hold orientation sessions for local community leaders and stakeholders outlining the aim, purpose and selection criteria of the project. Verification tools will be developed to ensure impartial selection of project beneficiaries. Awareness sessions will also be held for beneficiaries about their rights and the different ways they have to report potential abuse or discrimination. ADRA has a team of well-trained members in protection mitigation measures and humanitarian principles, moreover, extensive monitoring will take place to identify and mitigate any potential risks. Monitoring will be designed ensuring that feedback is given by all project beneficiaries and relevant stakeholders through key informant interviews, PDM and a well-established complaint mechanism. COVID-19 precautionary measures will be respected to avoid the risk of staff and beneficiary transmissions.

8 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risk 1: local or countywide lock-down to prevent the spread of the COVID-19 pandemic (2nd and 3rd waves of COVID-19), limiting access to redemption points due to restrictions on movement Likelihood: medium. Impact: medium. Mitigation: In case when a lock-down is expected, ADRA will coordinate with relevant stakeholders, to see the possibility of arranging a special service for the vouchers beneficiaries ADRA will follow up on the updates from the Ministry of Health and the UNDSS office on the COVID-19 situation in Syria. Risk 2: community/family tensions/jealousy arise as the result of the intervention. Likelihood: medium. Impact: medium.Mitigation: ADRA will ensure the beneficiaries, community leaders, local authorities and beneficiaries (children, adolescents, young adults, adults, older adults, the elderly and persons with disabilities) are aware of the selection criteria of the project as well as Do No Harm principles, and are involved in the process of the design of the activity. Risk 3: depreciation of the Syrian pound resulting in lower purchasing power. Likelihood: medium. Impact: medium.Mitigation: Coordination with the stakeholders to adjust the voucher amount as needed.
