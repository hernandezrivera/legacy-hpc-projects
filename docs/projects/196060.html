Project Summary

The proposed intervention will contribute to increased and equitable access to quality education with improved education outcomes targeting children of school-going age in selected counties in Jonglei, Warrap, Central, and Eastern Equatoria States. The project will increase the enrollment of out-of-school children; ensure the availability of protective and safe learning spaces for school children through the construction and/or rehabilitation of temporary learning spaces (TLS) and latrines; hygiene and sanitation education; provision of school equipment and learning materials; training of School Management Committee (SMCs) members and Parents Teachers Association (PTA) members on effective school management and equip them to mobilize school enrollment for out-of-school children between the ages of 3 to17 years. The project will target community members with messaging and Social mobilization awareness-raising on the importance of sending children to school. In total, the project will seek to directly reach 4,790 children (2,347 boys and 2, 443 girls) and 210 adults (107 women and 103 men) in selected payams across 4 states.
The project will deliberately target communities with a high percentage of out-of-school girls and boys to increase overall school enrolment and pupil retention rates. Areas consisting of large Internally displaced persons (IDP) or returnee populations will be assessed and mapped to ensure equitable inclusion of school children from this target population. Based on JAM’s existing data, Twic county in Warrap and Pibor in Jonglei have large IDP populations and face significant access constraints to education services, whereas other locations Central and Eastern Equatoria have primary schools with insecure and unsafe outdoor classrooms, limited WASH facilities, and a high drop-out rate. The project will establish gender and disabled-friendly water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure at the established or rehabilitated learning spaces in the area.
All new or rehabilitated learning structures will be semi-permanent in nature, fenced, and have four secured classrooms that can accommodate approximately 40 learners. An office space with furniture for teachers and facilitators, three disabled-friendly latrines (1 male and 2 female) with handwashing devices. Women and girls will also have privacy provision for menstrual hygiene management, and a water access point. Community members will be mobilized for construction, while materials will be sourced locally to the best of JAM’s ability.
To increase learner enrolment and retention, JAM will support teachers, facilitators, and community structures (PTAs and SMCs) to conduct quarterly enrolment drives within the 5 km radius of the temporary learning spaces centers. Girls, IDPs, and returnees will be prioritized during door-to-door campaign sessions. To promote girls' education, JAM will identify role model women with post-secondary education attainment to engage directly with parents on the importance of education for girls. The project will also incentivize girls' education through the provision of dignity kits for adolescent girls in schools.
The project will map targeted counties to identify facilities that require school learners to travel more than 5 kilometers to access schools, schools with inadequate, absent, or dilapidated structures including appropriate WASH facilities. This will allow JAM to corroborate existing data with active consultation and feedback from the government, community leaders, and education stakeholders.

To achieve the project outcomes. JAM will leverage expertise, experience, local presence, and community acceptance. To ensure effective multi-sectoral coordination, JAM will work closely with humanitarian, government, parents, community leaders, and education stakeholders. This includes local representatives of the RRC, the Ministries of Education Gender, social services, and NGO partners.

1 - Needs

By the end of 2022, it is estimated that 3.2 million people in South Sudan will be in need of education services with 97% of them being children of school-going age (HNO 2022). Complex interrelated factors, including conflict, remoteness, flooding, and untrained teachers are some of the contributing factors(MoGEI & UNICEF Dec. 2020). At the national level, this is compounded by low public expenditure on education – 4.1% in 2018, well below the 10% minimum as stipulated by the General Education Act of 2012 and the 17.4% average for similar fragile and conflict-affected countries (Global Partnership for Education 2020). There are over 392,000 returnees and 1.7 million IDPs across the country, with both groups facing severe constraints in accessing education (UNHCR 2021). The COVID-19 pandemic, which led to the closure of schools in March 2020 exacerbated many of the pre-existing challenges of access to education, undoing much of the progress made in recent years. While other countries resorted to measures such as technology-assisted remote learning during school closures, the majority of school children in South Sudan could not benefit from such services due to limited access to the internet and electronic devices. While radio learning programs were available, these were again only accessible to less than half of the children who participated in the Education Needs Assessment ( ENA 2021).Insecurity remains one of the single biggest threats to access to education in South Sudan. Active conflict and recurring communal violence throughout 2021 led to school closures. Jonglei, the GPAA, Lakes, Upper Nile, Central Equatoria, Warrap, Unity, and Western Equatoria are some of the worst affected. Safety and security incidents in and around education facilities, as well as destruction or occupation form barriers to accessing education facilities. Learners in the 2021 ENA cited insecurity in and around schools as a major reason for learner absenteeism and school drop-out. Extreme weather conditions of drought and flooding severely impact educational attainment for children. Across the country, 30% of existing schools are estimated to be damaged, destroyed, or closed (MoGEI &, UNICEF Dec.2020). It is also estimated that by October 2022, at least 887 schools in several states were closed or damaged due to floods while several others were occupied by IDPs seeking shelter on higher ground (Education Cluster, Oct.22).Socio-cultural influences also negatively affect children's education. In Pibor county, only 56% of households reported at least one school-aged child attends formal schooling regularly (REACH 2020). A significant number of classes are conducted outside (under trees) leaving children without the protective environment of a physical classroom and often lacking access to basic classroom necessities like blackboard, desks, tables, and chairs. Long walking distance to schools, lack of access to basic teaching and learning materials, and a poor teacher incentive system further compromise the delivery of quality education. Barriers and negative impacts are multiplied for girls and young women due to negative cultural traditions. In South Sudan, 52% of girls are married before the age of 18 (UNICEF 2021). Child marriage, early pregnancy, and viewing girls as valuable for bride price (UNICEF August 2017) form key barriers. As a result, school dropout is common and decreases girls’ chances of attaining an education further reducing their opportunity to break the cycle of poverty. Access is even more constrained for children with disabilities evidenced by the lack of mobility or assistive devices in a significant proportion of education facilities. Against the above background, JAM will ensure the availability of an enabling learning environment as well as embark on efforts to increase school enrollment and retention rates and improve the quality of gender and disability-inclusive education services in 5 counties within 4 states.

2 - Project activities

JAM will implement a project aimed at increasing access to improved education facilities and increasing enrollment and retention rates in Twic East, Pibor in Jonglei State, Twic in Warrap State, Juba in Central Equatoria, and Torit in Eastern Equatoria. The project contributes to Education cluster objectives and HRP 2022 by providing lifesaving, timely and integrated multi-sector assistance to vulnerable girls and boys (3-17 years) in a safe equitable manner, improving cognitive development, enrolment, and attendance of 4,790 children (2,347 boys and 2, 443 girls). The project will ensure the availability of protective temporary learning spaces (TLS) with gender and disabled-friendly WASH infrastructure. JAM will construct 10 TLS with improved gender-segregated WASH facilities. JAM will also directly reach 110 new PTA members, 100 SMC members, and community members through various targeted activities to promote enrollment. IDPs, returnees, and households with girls and children with disabilities will be particularly targeted to increase enrolment rates within these population groups, via door-to-door campaign sessions. All TLS will follow MoGEI-approved standard design and be semi-permanent in nature, fenced, and have: two secured classrooms able to accommodate approximately 40 learners; two disabled-friendly latrines (with handwashing devices where women and girls will also have privacy for menstrual hygiene management); and a water point for access to water. Community members will be mobilized for construction, while materials will be sourced locally if available. Construction sites will be identified jointly with the MoGEI representatives at the State and county levels.PTA and SMC members will be targeted through training on effective school management and school enrolment and retention strategies to increase their capacity to engage with parents, children, or school authorities. JAM will support quarterly enrolment drives within the 5 km radius of the TLS. Girls, IDPs, and CWDs will be prioritized during door-to-door campaign sessions. To counter girls' barriers to education, JAM will identify role model women to act as change agents. The project will support the capacity building of teachers' through tailored training and the provision of essential teaching materials. By the end of the project, JAM aims to provide educational support for 4,790 schoolchildren. 7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

The project will strictly ensure all vulnerable groups including people with disabilities, women, and girls are fairly represented in all community mobilization events and local targeting committees related to the project. It will make follow-ups with schools to ensure the vulnerable groups are represented in SMCs and PTCs memberships. This will help the most vulnerable people have influence over the decision-making processes in these committees and ensure their perspectives and needs are properly considered in the project’s implementation. The project will ensure all information communicated to the target community is appropriate and tailored to local specific circumstances. The information will be delivered in local languages and will be culturally and gender-sensitive. Printed information will take into account the high level of illiteracy rate and will be presented in pictorial forms. Telephone helplines numbers will be provided for the greater community to report any cases of concern and this will be supported by the helpdesk instituted at school and community around the schools. Other complaint and response mechanisms will be available alongside the above inclusive channels.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

The project will hold an inception workshop to provide information about the project targeting and selection process, Schools and beneficiary entitlements, processes for community engagement as well as information sharing platforms. All school communities will receive sensitization about the availability of complaint and feedback mechanisms, where they will be located, and what kinds of complaints can be reported through the available channels. These will be done through school Annual general meetings, SMC&PTAs meetings, School assembly, and other state education meeting platforms. Focal persons to handle feedback and complaints will be identified and made known to the school’s community. These people will be trained on how to handle feedback and complaints and the project will encourage beneficiaries to feel free to express their views for example through phone calls, suggestion boxes, in person, helpdesk, etc.Information about the project and availability of the CRM process will be developed and printed on IEC materials that will be shared with the school’s communities and posters pasted on strategic areas for community members to see.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

At the project's inception, the Project will organize wider community sensitization events before the start of the implementation to inform the targeted schools and communities about the objective, specific activities involved and support services available. This will ensure all beneficiaries are aware of their entitlements as individuals and as a group to avoid manipulation into SEA. This platform will be used for awareness-raising to provide information on PSEA and channels of reporting and the referral pathway. Using the sensitization process and available platforms, the project will provide information about PSEA generally and also to specific target groups like school administrators, teachers, pupils, SMCs and PTAs, and community leaders. For each meeting or other activity that engages the affected population, the project will have pre-address information that will be shared to inform all in attendance about PSEA. The project will train school administrators, teachers, SMCs, PTAs, and student leaders on basic PSEA concepts, reporting, and referral mechanisms.Through printed IEC materials, the project will share key messages on entitlements and PSEA, all information will be translated in languages generally understood by the community.Where opportunities exist, the project will use local media channels, in particular, radio broadcasts about the project and its strategy regarding PSEA.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The project will carefully from time to time review feedback or complaints collected from the schools and community and provide recommendations for corrective courses of action. Based on the nature of the complaints, sensitive or non-sensitive, these will be categorized and used appropriately. Non-sensitive feedback/ complaints that relate to activity implementation or dissatisfaction with activities will be analyzed by a team of M&E, Gender, Protection and Accountability team, technical officers, and project manager, and immediate actions are taken in the subsequent tasks to correct the actions and future programmatic attention. Sensitive feedback or complaints on fraud and corruption, diversion of school supplies, abusive behavior, and sexual exploitation and abuse will be handled at the country office. The Senior management organ will review the cases and where required an investigation team is commissioned by the CD to investigate and recommend actions to management. These can result in summary dismissal of staff involved and ex-communication of other persons involved.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

To mitigate risks from violence, the project will conduct a specific risk analysis regarding the security situation in and around the targeted schools located before the implementation commences. School occupation or destruction by armed forces, active conflict, and recurrent communal conflict is common in the areas identified for intervention and thus community engagement in identifying and providing early warning for schools activities will be sought. The project will maintain the government designated time of 8 am -4 pm to ensure pupils, students, teachers, and members of the PTAs and SMC don't come too early or leave too late. Coordination with local protection partners and the UN system to obtain regularly updated information on the security situations and forage protection support when necessary will be done. All activities will be assessed to ensure issues that will be stored do not become a risk factor for school attacksTo mitigate the risk of SEA, the project will provide regular training to JAM field staff on Child Protection (CP), PSEA), GBV, and gender equality to enhance awareness on prevention and protection form of sexual exploitation and abuse and other forms of sexual violence, child protection, so as to reduce and protect from the risk of being a perpetrator. JAM will ensure policy implementation and sign-off for every staff member of its PSEA policy and Declaration, Safeguarding, Child protection, and others, so staff knows they will be held accountable. JAM train teachers, SMCs, PTAs members on CP, PSEA and GBV so that they are able to identify risks, act and report for further management. But also to ensure they do not become perpetrators of violence as guardians of the pupils while at school and not victims as well. JAM will conduct a specific risk analysis to identify factors that expose women and girls to sexual violence within the education structure set up and take the necessary protection measures. Complaint and feedback mechanisms- suggestion boxes will be put in place in all schools, use the senior male and senior female teacher as focal persons for school’s helpdesk, and form a leadership club to ensure children can report to their leaders who will, in turn, pass the information directly to JAM staff for further management.JAM will work with the school to identify and provide safe and secure avenues for children, teachers, and members of the community to present their complaints. Use of telephone lines to encourage them to report on any act of abuse and seek assistance, the phone numbers will be printed and provided on large banners in every school. Confidentiality will be maintained at all times to ensure reporters anonymity. The project will use various channels to inform the target/affected population and raise awareness eg. posters, banners,information sharing platforms like meetings, training etc. The Project will organize wider community sensitization events in and around selected schools and before the start of the project to inform the target communities about the objectives and activities of the project. For every activity, the pre-address checklist will be revised, developed, and used to ensure it provides information on safeguarding, GBV PSEA, and channels of reporting.To mitigate this risk of unethical data handling, JAM will enforce the confidentiality code that all staff working with Persons affected by crisis use.
