Project Summary

After over seven years of violence, the Syria remains one of the most dangerous area for children. The CP needs across Syria are immense, far outweighing the available provision of necessary services. Conflict affected girls, boys and their families are facing appalling protection, neglect and deprivation risks on a daily basis, exacerbated by conflict‑related stress, exclusion or separation from their families.
This project aims to provide life-saving assistance and to promote a protective environment for conflict-affected children and their families, while strengthening their resilience and protection. SC will be establishing and supporting community-based child protection programming, provide specialised child protection services and CP capacity building initiatives.
In terms of community-based child protection, SCI will establish/support 78 CP Committees and Networks in NE and NW Syria through trainings, regular meetings and action planning to identify, prevent and respond to CP issues at the community level. SC will also provide child protection and PSS activities through child and adolescent friendly spaces, targeting 10,998 children and adolescent boys and girls, and will run child and adolescent clubs which children can engage in child-led activities/initiatives. Outreach will ensure that vulnerable and excluded children, including children with disabilities, are able to participate in activities. In order to strengthen children’s protective environment within the family, SC will engage 2,050 parents and caregivers in parenting groups and mother toddler groups, with priority given to vulnerable caregivers, such as child mothers. At the community level, awareness raising campaigns will be conducted with 50,000 girls, boys, women and men on key topics identified by CPCs, child clubs, and community members, including violence against children, the prevention of separation and mine risk awareness. Child protection situation monitoring will also be conducted across SC operational sites, as well as rapid assessments in new locations based on influxes and changes in the population. CP monitoring teams will also conduct mine risk education awareness, and refer locations where explosive hazards have been identified to mine clearance agencies.

Through case management, trained CW's will support 3,570 vulnerable and at-risk children, including unaccompanied and separated children, cases of child marriage and child mothers, child labour, and children experiencing violence, abuse, exploitation and neglect, including child survivors of sexual violence. Appropriate alternative care will also be provided/supported for UASC, with a focus on community-based and family-based care. Capacity building will also be undertaken with case management teams, relevant authorities and foster parents to strengthen the quality of alternative care. SC will also maintain a children’s emergency fund to respond to needs which cannot be met by other service providers for vulnerable and at-risk children, based on children’s case plans.

SC will also work to strengthen human resource capacity both internally and at the NES and NWS levels through capacity assessments, capacity building plan development, training roll-out and through coaching and supervision support. Training will target 1000 women and men, including SC staff, CP actors, relevant ministries and authorities, and non-child protection actors.
SC coordinates with a range of other actors in the implementation of CP activities, particularly for case management referrals. For example, depending upon the issues identified, external referrals will be made to local stakeholders for alternative care and SC also coordinates with actors such as (but not limited to) KRC (Kurdish Red Crescent), MSF, IRC and PHC's when referrals to medical services are required. SC will also continue to maintain active participation in sub-cluster meetings and other relevant coordination forums.

Needs

The Syrian war remains one of the most dangerous conflicts for children. The situation changed significantly in 2018 with the Government of Syria (GoS) intensifying its military offensives and consolidating control over large areas including Homs, Eastern Ghouta, south Damascus and Dar’a. In NES, although the liberation of Ar-Raqqa has reduced the movement of displaced persons, the ongoing offensive in Deir Ez Zor continues to displace families into SC operational areas. In NWS, the implementation of the DMZ agreement continues to fail, and more recently, experienced a setback as HTS seized political and military control from the Turkish-backed NLF in the area. As such, the child protection needs across Syria remain unmet. Conflict affected children are facing appalling protection risks daily, exacerbated by conflict‑related stress, exclusion and/or separation. Protection actors have identified significant risks including child marriage, child labour, family separation, lack of birth certificates, sexual violence, domestic violence, neglect, child labour (including the worst forms of child labour), physical risks associated with mines and UXOs, and child recruitment. When looking at differences between girls and boys, there are some variation in protection needs and barriers. For example, caregivers are often less likely to allow adolescent girls to participate in psychosocial support activities due to their roles within the home and concerns about safety. Due to this, many girls report feelings of isolation and restrictions on their movements which can have negative consequences of their well‑being. While boys have had to take roles and responsibilities beyond their age, such as primary breadwinners/head of households. Child marriage has also been reported as a critical issue affecting children, and particularly adolescent girls, while boys are also affected. Younger children have reportedly been affected by neglect due to increased responsibilities and stress affecting parents, and due to the increased number of adolescent mothers as a result of child marriage. The Ending Violence in Childhood report (2018) shows that violence in childhood can be deeply destructive, including restricting education opportunities and leading to depression and behavioural problems. Recent SC mental health assessments in grades 1-6 show 20% of children in the "abnormal" range (17% girls; 23% boys) and children in grade 1 showing the highest overall levels of abnormality. SC’s Invisible Wounds report found that 84% of adults and almost all children cited ongoing bombing and shelling as the number one cause of psychological stress, and 71% of parents said that children increasingly suffer from frequent bedwetting and involuntary urination. Against a backdrop of violence, continuous displacements and worsening socio-economic conditions, the 2018 WoS CP governorate level report highlighted that 86% of assessed communities in Idlib, and 92% in Raqqa reported UASC as a core concern. In the communities, 25% stated they have personally experienced the occurrence of family separation. Respondents cited common causes for separation: child marriage for girls (23%) and child recruitment for boys (19%). UASC with diminished protective factors are frequently forced to resort to negative coping mechanisms. Culturally, many Syrian children that are separated from their parents or primary caregivers live in kinship care arrangements (HNO, 2018). However, the deteriorating economic situation and breakdown of family and community safety nets has disrupted this. For instance, grandparents might not be able to take care of their grandchildren due to age, health issues or worsening economic situations (IRC Protection Monitoring NWS, July 2018). In NES, it was reported that UASC are also at increased risk of child marriage and recruitment, indicating that it is also a consequence of separation (SCI, UASC Assessment, Oct. 2018).

Implementation of activities

To contend with the aforementioned Child Protection (CP) challenges, resulting from prolonged exposure to war, stress and uncertainty, in line with the Protection Sector Results Framework, Save the Children (SC), with partners, aims to implement: Community based CP for girls and boys is available in targeted locations CP Committees (CPCs) established and/or supported and trained to prevent and respond to CP concerns at the community level Child and adolescent groups supported to actively engage in developing child-led CP initiatives The provision of safe and protective child and adolescent spaces for girls and boys to engage in structured, sustained child protection programmes, including psychosocial support (PSS) provided through static and mobile interventions, designed in consultation with children and communities. This will include the implementation of SC’s Child Resilience programme and Healing and Education through Arts (HEART) programme in appropriate locations Outreach to engage the most vulnerable children and adolescents in PSS and CP activities, including children with disabilities and adolescent girls The implementation of SC’s Common Approach to Parenting Without Violence, including mother and toddler groups and positive parenting groups. SC will focus on involving caregivers from particularly vulnerable and at-risk families in the programme, including young mothers and child mothers Outreach for awareness raising and the facilitation of community events to prevent and respond to CP issues, in consultation with CPCs and child clubs. CP and GBV situation monitoring conducted together with children and women through the implementation of participatory consultation and observation tools Specialised CP services (case management) for girls and boys are available in targeted locations Individual case management support to girls and boys, service providers and families, including outreach, registration, assessment, case planning, follow up, direct service provision, referrals, monitoring, case review and case closure Individual Protection Assistance (NFIs, transportation costs for referrals, etc.) provided to vulnerable and at-risk children based on their case plans through a children’s emergency fund The provision of appropriate, community-based, alternative care for UASC, including ongoing monitoring and support The provision of capacity building opportunities to strengthen family-based care based on SC's Alternative Care Assessment findings, including training for case management teams and training for foster parents Strengthen human resource capacity to respond to CP concerns in Syria CP technical capacity assessments conducted with partner organisations and internal and partner capacity building plans developed SC and partner staff are trained on CP in line with CP minimum standards and gender and GBV mainstreaming Non-CP sector staff are trained on child on CP mainstreaming and integration Relevant CP and education authorities are trained and supported to ensure safe and effective CP and PSS approaches Development of alternative care guidance and staff and community members are trained to support safe and sustainable community-based care Based on the changes in context in the NES and NWS, SC is currently implementing a case management contingency action plan in order to prepare in case of a need to suspend or change modality in operations. This includes developing new strategic partners, implementing capacity building plans with national NGOs and relevant authorities, building community capacity to prevent and respond to CP concerns, and revising data protection protocols in case of suspension and/or relocation, in line with the above activities.

Protection Risk Analysis

Risk: SCI or partner staff violating SCI CSG rules resulting in harm to childrenAll SC staff, volunteers and representatives sign the Child Safeguarding Policy and Code of Conduct, child safeguarding risk assessments are conducted for all project sites and mitigating actions and put in place, all staff are trained on the Child Safeguarding Policy and Code of Conduct, with regular refreshers, and a confidential reporting mechanism and disseminated. Risk: Affected populations and SC staff and volunteers, are placed at risk of sexual exploitation and abuse, including harassment. Training will be provided to SC staff and volunteers on SC’s Anti-Harassment Policy and Code of Conduct. SC will also continue to provide trainings on (PSEA) through SC’s PSEA focal points. Confidential reporting mechanisms will be maintained, with reports thoroughly and confidentiality investigated with follow up action plans implemented. Risk: Community tension due to service delivery locations or types of services offered in one community Continuously ensure service mapping, consultation with community on program interventions, regular communication with different communities on availability of services including host communities and returnee populations. Expansion of services to non-camp locations where needs are present, to avoid concentration of services in camps. Regular consultations with communities, FGDs, feedback boxes Risk: Inadequately trained service providers and/or low-quality specialized CP/PSS services results in doing harm or perpetuating unintended consequences to girls, boys and their caregivers availing of the services. This can include re-traumatization without referral to higher-level PSS. It can also result in traumatizing service providers themselves. With more specialized services, we will ensure clear messaging on what services are available to survivors and ensure strong support mechanisms are in place for PSS professionals and those delivering PFA. Staff training and capacity assessments will anticipate their limits/capacities in providing more specialized PSS and CP services. Regular trainings, coaching and supervision of staff and partners will ensure the quality standard of interventions. Feedback mechanisms will be set across all services and centres to ensure adequate community feedback. SCI will also ensure an appropriately resourced complaints response mechanism is in place with clear guidelines for ensuring feedback is actioned upon. Risk: Resistance from local authorities to accessing and implementing programming. SC has established relationships with relevant local authorities and stakeholders, such as local council, education directorate, ministries, community gatekeepers and religious leaders. The stakeholder will be, involved throughout program design and implementation. Feedback will be sought through meetings will be held with relevant authorities. Risk: Barriers to access for marginalized groups Specific measures are taken to ensure input is received from marginalized groups such as women and girls, children with disabilities and the elderly. Feedback is used to inform planning, implementation, monitoring and evaluating activities. Mitigation measures are brainstormed with the community to ensure that girls and boys (incl. children with disabilities) have equal access to SC activities. Additionally, as needed specific measures are taken to ensure activities are age and gender sensitive. Risk: Stigmatization facing children receiving specialized services Case management principles including confidentiality, non-discrimination, ascent of children and consent of caregivers (if available), and ‘Do No Harm’ are strictly adhered to during implementation. Children and caregivers will be actively engaged in the case management process and case management M&E tools implemented to ensure minimum standards are maintained.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

-Safety & Security (S&S)All staff movements must be pre-approved and refresher security trainings are regularly conducted. The Hostile Environment Awareness Training is mandatory for NES deployments; local staff have been trained on Advanced Personal S&S. SC’s partners engage with relevant stakeholders and receive timely updates. SC works on a common response to unacceptable conditions.-AirstrikesSC supports partners to immediately report security incidents to be analysed and to develop and implement comprehensive safety plans.-Loss of accessSC and partners conduct thorough security assessments and maintain updated contingency plans. If necessary, SC will suspend programs and work to resume activities. If this is not viable, SC would assess possibilities for relocation. SC launched an initiative with OCHA to provide external training on international humanitarian law and negotiation with armed actors.
