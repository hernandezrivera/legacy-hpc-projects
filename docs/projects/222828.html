Project Summary

In the 2025 Joint Response Plan (JRP), Save the Children (SC) will operate 2 Health Posts, a Primary Health Care Center (PHCC), and a Field Hospital across five camps in Ukhiya and Teknaf. These inclusive gender-responsive services will ensure equitable healthcare access for vulnerable groups including maternal, neonatal, child, and adolescent populations, individuals with disabilities, and those from diverse sexual orientations, gender identities, expression and sex characteristics (SOGIESC).
The field hospital, PHCC and HP will implement the Minimum Package of Essential Health Services (MPEHS) according to health sector guidelines, in collaboration with the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW). The service intervention of Save the Children (SC) will provide 24/7 indicative cesarean sections and Basic Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care, along with Laboratory Medicine including blood storage & transfusion, and interventions to prevent mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) of infections like HIV, Hepatitis C, supporting outbreak control. Moreover, SC will continue offering non-communicable disease (NCD) services, outpatient care, emergency services, and sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services. Furthermore, the hospital will focus on survivor-centered care against gender-based violence (GBV) providing clinical management of rape (CMR) and support for intimate partner violence (IPV) and nutritional support for children under five through stabilization services, SOGIEC support, WASH, and MHPSS (counseling, mhGAP, clinical psychology). Rehabilitation for disabilities includes assistive devices, 3 Mobile Medical Team (MMT) will operate under Emergency Preparedness Response (EPR). SC will ensure Infection Prevention and Control by an IPC focal point, aligning with Global Medical Team standards. HIS and MEAL will oversee data reporting and focus on e-MIS for 2025. CHWs will boost clinical services via health promotion, SBCC, and engage PLWs, adolescents, SOGIEC groups, and gatekeepers in health awareness and gender-responsive practices. SC will ensure low-cost, safe referrals to higher-level centers.
Aligned with SC’s localization strategy, health interventions in Camps- 4, and 17, will be implemented through local partners ‘PHD’ and technical support from CDD and Bandhu Social Welfare Society; where Camp 21 will be run in the mixed method. SC will carry on as a SAG member, actively participating in working groups like the newborn subcommittee, SRH, EPR, and referral technical groups, providing technical support to enhance the overall health workforce in the Rohingya response. SC has also contributed Maternal and Child Health cards through the SRH working group and will have a plan based on resources and funding.
Recognizing the importance of understanding climate-influenced infectious disease transmission, SC will focus on evidence-based research initiatives, including epidemiological surveillance, to assess climate impacts on disease patterns and support interventions such as early warning systems. To ensure the quality of healthcare services, SC uses a robust Monitoring, Evaluation, Accountability, and Learning (MEAL) system to ensure accountability, impact, and continuous improvement based on the MEAL framework. SC will also maintain multiple feedback channels (e.g., toll-free hotline, feedback box, children consultation, FGD, one-to-one interview, online FRM) to ensure accountability. A Gender Focal Point will monitor indicators to ensure gender-specific practices, supporting SC’s vision for a gender-transformative program. SC’s “Do No Harm” policy includes safe programming through Medical Incident Reports, child-friendly spaces, and Datix for secure online reporting on Child Safeguarding and PSEA.
This integrated approach highlights SC’s commitment to delivering inclusive, accessible, and equitable healthcare that fosters resilience and meets the unique needs of affected populations.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

In Cox’s Bazar, Bangladesh, at present 1.48 million people (Rohingya refugees and adjacent host communities) need healthcare services. According to the UNHCR population factsheet (September 2024), the Ukhiya and Teknaf areas currently host around 1 million Rohingya refugees, 52% of whom are children and largely dependent on humanitarian aid agencies. The Rohingya population lives in conditions that compromise basic needs, particularly in sexual, reproductive, maternal, newborn, and child Adolescent health (SRMNCAH), with limited access to healthcare services. Meanwhile, the surrounding host communities also face significant healthcare needs.To cover their overall health needs, 56 active Health Sector partners are providing essential healthcare services from 102 Health facilities including HP, PHCC, and CEmONC Centers. Additionally, Upazila Health Complexes and Sadar Hospital play a pivotal role in managing cases with critical needs.According to the SRH WQ Q2 bulletin, 8,328 facility-based deliveries were recorded, with 67% involving Rohingya refugees and 33% from host communities. This marks a 10.1% decrease from the previous quarter. Live births accounted for 97.8% (8,148), while stillbirths were 2.2% (180). The cesarean section rate was 8.4%, within the recommended range, with 45% performed for refugees and 55% for host communities. Over 300 monthly community-based deliveries highlight a high number of unskilled births, contributing to maternal and perinatal mortality. To reduce maternal and perinatal mortality; SC will step in and continue SRMNCAH which will minimize the referral burden in this response.The Health Sector's 2024 bulletins reported a surge in communicable diseases, with 10,131 confirmed dengue cases and 210 cholera cases by September, mostly affecting Rohingya refugees. Outpatient consultations and inpatient admissions in September were 7.5% and 15% higher than the year's average, driven by new arrivals and unregistered patients. Acute Respiratory Infection (ARI) and skin diseases contributed to 21% and 13% of consultations, respectively. Additionally, seasonal variation and weather shifts may drive endemic disease outbreaks, keeping transmission rates high in the over-crowded camps and sustaining the need for essential healthcare services.According to the Health Sector Strategy on Viral Hepatitis B and C among Rohingya refugees (2024–2025), the World Health Organization's (WHO) surveillance data for the refugee response in Cox’s Bazar showed a 38% positivity rate for hepatitis C (HCV) in the samples collected from the refugees. Besides, the prevalence of major Non-Communicable Diseases (NCD) such as hypertension and diabetes were 14.5% and 8.4%, respectively. Despite various initiatives, the rising burden of communicable and non-communicable diseases highlights the need to strengthen laboratory medicine at the camp health facility level.However, the Health Sector is currently overwhelmed by the existing referral system. Additionally, recurring extreme weather events and fire incidents underscore the urgent need for robust climate action and Emergency Preparedness Response (EPR) plans in health facilities to safeguard lives and resources. There is a gap in integrated health services leading to beneficiary sufferings in terms of movement from facility, service availability and accessibility.

2.3 - Age & Disability

According to UNHCR Factsheet September 2024; 30,273 Rohingya individuals, indicating a total of 3,02% individuals have been identified as Persons with Disability among the Rohingya population, where 15,195 are female and 15,078 are male. Among them, 6.18% of total individuals have been identified with at least one specific need. The same factsheet also illustrated 1,370 older female; 589 older male are at risk in terms of need. Moreover, 1,352 unaccompanied/ separated female children and 1,158 unaccompanied/ separated male children have been identified with specific needs based on this factsheet. SC, in collaboration with technical partners, screens and identifies persons with disabilities, along with their needs for therapeutic services and assistive devices. Disability Support Committee (DSC) has been formed to build self-reliance through training in life skills such as health education, nutrition, gender inclusion, Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR), MHPSS, and behavioral competencies. To foster resilience, SC will support both existing peer support groups, and committees for adolescent girls, boys, and persons with disabilities and also establish new peer support groups and committees for adolescent girls, boys, and persons with disabilities.SC offers emergency aid and healthcare rights for Rohingya children, including EPI, IYCF-E, C-MAMI, and consultations. The health program also targets the geriatric population, recognizing their specific needs related to incontinence, nutrition, and rehabilitation. This project will ensure that older individuals have access to clinical care, laboratory services, medications for managing NCDs, therapeutic services, and preventive health education. SCI will ensure addressing specific vulnerabilities and developmental needs to support the rights and well-being of all individuals in challenging health, safety, and security conditions.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

Save the Children's existing accountability framework covers a rigorous process where (i) information is shared in a planned manner ensuring that every project has a different specific plan to share with the support of a dedicated accountability team for RR and the host community (ii) participatory feedback reporting is ensured to each sector directly working with children in camps and host community and (iii) feedback analyzed and reported through online reporting format. As a part of SC’s core value, the accountability framework applies both proactive and reactive channels. (e.g. Community meetings, feedback box, exit interviews, toll-free hotline number, helpdesk, 8 different child-friendly tools, post distribution monitoring, home visit) to collect feedback. SC accountability team ensures capacity strengthening, monitoring information sharing and feedback reporting to project and partner staff, and volunteers, and conducts over 100 assessments during the design and implementation phase to understand and reflect on the accountability requirement. It produces knowledge products and is reflected in quarterly/monthly and bi-monthly need-based reports to influence the program in decision-making. SC MEAL team routinely conducts the needs assessment, consultation, baseline, midline and end-line survey/assessment where accountability is an integral part of the design with an objective of adaptive management. The team has been instrumental in J-MSNA 2023 (e.g. in 2023 SCI conducted Children Consultation, Multi Sector Needs Assessment). In 2024, another MSNA is undergoing to inform the design of multi-sectoral humanitarian and development programming for diverse groups affected by the protracted Rohingya refugee crisis and host communities in Cox’s Bazar district where accountability will be applied as well.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

Save the Children (SC) is an active member of the Protection from Sexual Exploitation, Abuse and Harassment (PSEAH) Network led by the Inter Sector Coordination Group (ISCG) and shares its capacity with the platform members. Apart from this mainstream PSEAH, SC promotes PSEAH awareness, prevention measurement, reporting, and response mechanisms in the health project. In this regard, SC raises staff awareness through training (staff and volunteer)/ inductions to all staff within 7 days of joining, and a provide refresher to all staff and volunteers once a year, disseminates PSEAH mandates in the staff meetings, develops learning materials (posters, flyers, videos, Safeguarding Pocketbooks, Volunteer Flipchart etc.), contribute to community capacity strengthening by establishing community champions. All the health facilities are posted and ensure visibilities with printed IEC material on PSEAH. Also, SC supports safer recruitment practices (background checks, volunteer vetting, PSEAH statements in job advertisements), PSEAH risk identification and mitigation measures in program deliveries. SC has a zero-tolerance approach to any PSEAH concern and also strictly maintains confidentiality, and ensures availability of an accessible, and culturally appropriate Feedback and Response Mechanism (FRM). It takes the necessary actions after receiving any PSEAH complaint that is in line with the Standard Operating Procedure (SoP) for the PSEAH Complaint and Referral in the Rohingya response along with the survivor-center support approach. There is a dedicated team of 3 staff in SC Cox’s Bazar area office for conducting safeguarding investigations. SC also contributes to creating partner capacity in conducting safeguarding investigations, and technical support to ensure visibility of relevant IEC materials at the facility level.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Climate change significantly threatens human health by increasing diseases such as dengue, cholera and skin diseases while variability in climate- like heatwaves and floods- exacerbates waterborne and vector-borne illnesses. Extreme weather events raise child mortality, and hospitalizations, and damage healthcare infrastructure, disrupting essential services.SC is committed to climate-resilient healthcare infrastructure, implementing environmentally friendly practices including low-carbon solutions such as solar systems, waste reduction, sustainable waste management, and reliable safe water supply systems to enhance structural resilience. SC will also treat hospital wastewater to reduce speeding of iatrogenic infections and disease transmission within the surrounding camp environment and introduce vertical tree plantations to enhance green cover, reduce heat absorption, and mitigate disaster-related infrastructure risks.To reduce health impacts from climate change, SC empowers female community health workers, midwives, and frontline healthcare providers on climate-related health risks, through training, awareness, and health day observances. These efforts equip women and girls with knowledge and skills to protect their health against climate-related risks. In collaboration with the health sector and Community Health Working Groups, SC tailor’ DRR messages for vulnerable groups, including women, children, and persons with disabilities, to enhance disaster preparedness.SC will further enhance adaptive capacity by addressing climate impacts on vulnerable groups by developing an anticipatory action framework for early alerts, disease surveillance, and community resilience initiatives through capacity building and inclusive messaging. Additionally, this project, emphasizes evidence-based public health initiatives targeting climate-sensitive and infectious diseases.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

Since the Rohingya influx, Cox’s Bazar has experienced several cyclones and major fire incidents, resulting in loss of life and resources. In response, SC has developed an Emergency Preparedness and Response (EPR) plan for each health facility, including an inventory of medicines and medical supplies. This project will also ensure the EPR are in place and operational. Besides, the facility-based emergency medical response team delivers immediate life-saving health services and coordinates emergency referrals during outbreaks or disasters, ensuring rapid action regardless of facility status. The team performs triage, stabilizes critical cases, and maintains clear communication with local health authorities for coordinated support. Regular assessments and preparedness drills ensure ongoing readiness to protect health services and patient safety. SC’s EPR staff, volunteers, and partners received training in emergency preparedness, Basic First Aid (BFA), and Psychological First Aid (PFA) to effectively respond to disasters events and provide post-disaster support. In collaboration with the Mobile Medical Team working group, SC has established 3 MMTs—one core team and two surge teams—to support emergency response through a catchment-based approach.SC emphasizes the importance of risk-informed business continuity plans for its own and partner facilities, focusing on hazards like cyclones, flash floods, landslides, and fires. Moreover, periodic assessments of SC and partner facilities are conducted based on hazard vulnerabilities, following risk mitigation to ensure service continuity for disaster preparedness and response. As part of emergency preparedness, SC Community Health Workers (CHWs) collaborate with the SMS team to ensure effective support and resource sharing. In coordination with CHW WG, SC CHWs participate in emergency simulations and disseminate early warning messages to all, including vulnerable groups such as children, women, and persons with disabilities.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

This project emphasizes adolescent and youth empowerment as key to fostering resilience. SC’s child-led, adolescent-centered care approach integrates with SRH needs to address the needs of adolescents and young groups within 10-19 years. By ensuring adolescent-centered care, SC will empower adolescents and youth groups to raise their voices and incorporate their needs in the project design. It will be ensured through a feedback response mechanism. Adolescent peer teams and youth-led monitoring empower adolescents to actively participate in the project activities by making decisions on their interests, encouraging a sustainable, community-driven approach. Besides, adolescent sexual reproductive health skilled force activities will be continued. SC also enrolled “Girl Decide Module” through the active involvement of girl children at the camp level by reflecting on needs and interests through the adolescent and youth-led project. SC’s Health sector will also support its education, child protection, and other sectors to ensure their health rights irrespective of gender and disabilities, promoting their resilience, inclusivity, and community ownership.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

SC health projects are designed to enhance self-reliance and resilience through Health awareness programs, Community campaigns, and training focused on health education, encompassing preventive care strategies, hygiene practices, and basic first aid techniques. Providing capacity enhancement training for refugee community health workers or peer educators diminishes the community's reliance and establishes a reliable network of health advocates within the community. SC actively supports refugees improve their physical and mental health by providing direct healthcare and access to tailored Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) services. MHPSS service assists the refugees in addressing their mental health issues and improving their well-being through developing coping strategies. Additionally, establishing peer support groups led by community gatekeepers, and facilitating group sessions by CHWs, this project will address various health issues and strengthen community resilience.

4.2 Do project activities in the host community link to development initiatives, and/or align with development priorities in the Cox’s Bazar district? If yes, briefly describe.If yes, briefly describe.

SC is Conducting Host community project activities that align with development initiatives and the Cox’s Bazar District administration’s planning for 2025-2027. By Engaging local government officials and stakeholders during the project design phase, the project objectives correspond with district priorities that focus on enhancing health center services, including healthcare and Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) support at the district child hospital. Additionally, this project provides Social and Behavior Change Communication (SBCC) materials designed to raise awareness and enhance the learning experience. By identifying existing needs for comprehensive emergency maternal and obstetric care in Cox’s Bazar district and the upazilas, SC field hospital will serve as a CeMonc Service center and referral hub to improve healthcare access for local communities and Forcibly Displaced Myanmar Nationals (FDMN).
