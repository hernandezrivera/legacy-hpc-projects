Project Summary

The context of Syria dramatically changed during 2018 leading to more displacement towards Northeast Syria (NES) and Northwest Syria (NWS), to either camps or within host communities in cities. Save the Children (SC) works to address the educational needs of children through different projects, locations and approaches. Evaluations were carried out for the latest education projects funded by the Norwegian Ministry of Foreign Affairs (NMFA) and the Swiss Agency for Development and Cooperation (SDC - in NWS only) in 2018 and these learnings, alongside current programming, were explicitly used in the design of this Action.
Children’s educational and protection needs are increasingly complex and must be addressed with flexible education service models with the eventual goal of reintegrating children into formal schools. Assessments conducted at enrolment ensure students are provided with the appropriate level and type of education services and self/home learning measures are used for children who are forced to keep moving with no access to traditional education services. Trainings for teachers and facilitators are offered in order to ensure quality learning outcomes and monthly participation activities are conducted with parents, teachers and members of the wider community. Research shows multiple benefits to classroom based SEL in education in emergencies, including a stronger preparedness for learning and increased concentration and sense of control. SEL is widely regarded as both a preventative and curative intervention for children. Through this project, SC will use its new Social Emotional Learning Foundations (SEL-F) programme. In addition, a comprehensive capacity building plan will be developed to ensure Child Protection (CP) is mainstreamed in Education services and that minimum standards are maintained.
The areas for implementation in NWS were selected based on numbers of out-of-school children and the capacity of the hosting communities to accommodate them within existing schools. Vulnerability criteria also priorities newly displaced children, unaccompanied and separated children (UASC) and children with disabilities. The protection severity rating of the locations selected is five out of six on average and it has been identified that the number of people in need for protection services is 313,772 in the targeted areas. In NES, SC’s Education team has worked in six camps - Newroz, Roj, Alhole, Ein Issa, Areesha and Mabrooka. These camps include very different groups of populations such as Iraqi refugees including Arabs and Yezedians, Syrian IDPs and foreign families).
SC recognizes the need for having a gender balanced recruitment and training strategy to ensure that dialogue with communities, implementation of activities, and coordination with stakeholders take the needs of both genders into consideration. An estimated 50%+ of the targeted children are girls, but girls will still be explicitly targeted and prioritized for inclusion. An equal number of female and male beneficiaries will be targeted by resourcing a gender balanced number of facilitators. SC teams identify barriers to participation and inclusion and implement measures to mitigate these, for example by separating parent/community group meetings, where cultural norms dictate to facilitate female participation; or by ensuring males are also recipients of our key messaging on education as they are usually the gatekeeper in household level decisions. In addition, diversity must be considered very carefully, especially regarding religion, ethnicity or nationality. The contents of education activities must be neutral and suitable for all children in the learning space.
This action is in line with SC Whole of Syria strategic plan. In NWS SC is co-leading the Education Cluster together with UNICEF and in NES SC established and has led the Education Working Group (EWG) for the education sector coordination since 2017.

Needs

Syria remains the largest and longest running humanitarian crisis in the world. An estimated 400,000 civilians have been killed or injured, while over half of the population, approximately 13.1 million people, have been displaced. Of this figure, approximately one million people have migrated to Europe, over 5.6 million are living as refugees in Syria’s neighbouring countries and approximately 6.3 million remain internally displaced within Syria’s borders, including 2.8 million children. The conflict, now in its eighth year, has destroyed livelihoods and exhausted coping capacities of families with many now utilizing negative strategies to provide for their immediate needs. As such, access to life-saving assistance remains a priority. People have often been displaced multiple times, with continuous disruption to education for children. Security concerns often suspend school for extended periods of time, and some children cannot access learning spaces due to long distance and heightened risks on the way to school, with girls disproportionately impacted. Families who have been displaced lack access to learning materials and books at home, resulting in a deficiency in home-based learning support for children, crucial to basic skill development, especially at the youngest ages. The formal education system is in need of school rehabilitation and learning materials and the Ministry of Education needs support in the capacity building of their staff. Many families have neglected education as they have had to prioritise work and livelihood, or they are continually on the move, resulting in many children requiring assistance to continue learning from home. Vocational education is key in Syrian society as many children have lost the opportunity or desire to go to school and would therefore benefit from the training to start a safe vocation. ECCD children have been always neglected in Syria as there are very few qualified staff in this area and there is no specific curriculum used for children of this age. In NES, the Education team conducted a rapid needs assessment in the camps in December 2018. This revealed key needs including additional learning spaces; students’ education materials, teacher trainings and PSS. The assessment also helped SC to understand the key factors that prevent children from attending school. For girls, the main reason is that they are expected to help with household chores. For boys, the main reason is that they are expected to work to support their families. Implementation of activities

In order to respond to the dire needs outlined, SC aims to implement via the following activities through three main approaches: Access and Quality LearningThis objective focuses on increased, immediate access for the most marginalized student populations to responsive, protective, and quality-ensured learning environments.- TLSs providing ECCD (including Early Literacy and Math), basic education and recreational activities- Distributions of stationary, hygiene kits, student and teacher kits and winterization kits- Mobile education using SLP materials- Child participation and safe access supported through school level safety planning activities and the new Improving Learning Environments Together in Emergencies programming in NES- Teacher trainings to ensure better quality of education provision, including the Inter-Agency Network for Education (INEE), TICC (Teacher In Crisis Context), hygiene promotion and the new TiCC Peer Coaching and Mentoring packages- Facilitators and camp management personnel trained on Education in Emergency, TICC, positive discipline, case management, classroom management and SC core policies- Regular training of community-based volunteers Home and Community-Based InterventionThis objective focuses on improved capacities and resilience for communities to support learning and protection at home and in schools.- SEL-F programme consisting of a scripted SEL curriculum that is age and stage appropriate for Grades 1-11- International Social Emotional Learning Assessment (ISELA) to assess the impact of SEL programming on children’s wellbeing- Monthly participation activities such as TLCs (Teaching Learning Cycles), PTAs (Parent-Teacher Associations) and community committee meetings- Using SC’s Community Action toolkit including activities such as community Book Banks to strengthen community participation in learning- CP services integrated into Education programming with both sectors mainstreaming MHPSS approaches, such as case management services through trained teams (50% female) to vulnerable children including children at risk of violence and exploitation, child labour, out-of-school children (OOSC), unaccompanied and separated children (UASC) and children with disabilities (CWD)- Child Friendly Spaces (CFS) established within schools to provide children with structured psychosocial support- Peer-to-peer support groups for adolescents- Community Coordination Centres (CCCs) to support children in at-home learning and to maximize their learning capabilities. Two CCCs to be established soon.- Six vocational centres are running to support out-of-school adolescents Understanding and Advocating for Safe AccessThis objective focuses on enhanced understanding and responsiveness to recent displacement trends, capacity gaps, and critical advocacy messaging to support safe school access for the most vulnerable children.- Increase community level understanding on hazards facing children’s access to education- Align with SC’s global leadership around advocacy to support the Safe Schools Declaration (SSD) to reduce the likelihood of attacks on schools and mitigate the negative consequences- Information campaigns on the Guidelines to Protect Schools from Armed Attack- Child Protection Committees and child clubs representing all sections of society and made up of at least 50% women- Work closely with the Ministry of Education, Ministry of Youth, Humanitarian Affairs Office, Department of Women and camp managements to improve their knowledge of education

Protection Risk Analysis

Risk: Security accidents or fires happening inside the learning spacesMitigating measure: All staff, guards, cleaners and facilitators must be trained on safety and security standards and fire fighting. Risk: Community tension due to service delivery locations or types of services offered if one community is served and a neighbouring community not served/cannot access or if information offered is not consistent between communities. Mitigating Measures: Continuously ensure service mapping, consultation with community on program interventions, regular communication with different communities on availability of services including host communities and returnee populations. Expansion of services to non-camp locations where needs are present, to avoid concentration of services in camps. Monitoring Mechanism: Regular consultations with communities, FGDs, feedback boxes Risk: Resistance from local authorities to accessing and implementing programming. Mitigating measures: SC has established relationships with relevant local authorities and stakeholders, such as local council, education directorate, ministries, community gatekeepers and religious leaders. The stakeholder will be, involved throughout program design and implementation. Monitoring Mechanism: Regular meetings with relevant authorities. Risk: Barriers to access for marginalized groups Mitigating measures: Specific measures are taken to ensure input is received from marginalized groups such as women and girls, children with disabilities and the elderly. Feedback is used to inform planning, implementation, monitoring and evaluating activities. Mitigation measures are brainstormed with the community to ensure that girls and boys (incl. children with disabilities) have equal access to SC activities. Additionally, as needed specific measures are taken to ensure activities are age and gender sensitive. Monitoring Mechanism: FGDs, feedback mechanism Risk: SCI or partner staff violating SCI CSG rules harms children; damages the reputation of the organisation; and can lead to loss of funding and prosecution. Mitigating measures: All SC staff, volunteers and representatives sign the Child Safeguarding Policy and Code of Conduct, child safeguarding risk assessments are conducted for all project sites and mitigating actions and put in place, all staff are trained on the Child Safeguarding Policy and Code of Conduct. Partners have received CSG training via Hurras. Partners have their own reporting mechanism as well as CSG focal points in the field and main CSG focal point in Turkey. Risk: Affected populations and SC staff and volunteers, are placed at risk of sexual exploitation and abuse, including harassment. Mitigating measures: Training will be provided to SC staff and volunteers on SC’s Anti-Harassment Policy and Code of Conduct. SC will also continue to provide trainings on the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) through SC’s PSEA focal points.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Safety & Security (S&S)All staff movements must be pre-approved and refresher security trainings are regularly conducted. The Hostile Environment Awareness Training is mandatory for NES deployments; local staff have been trained on Advanced Personal S&S. SC’s partners engage with relevant stakeholders and receive timely updates. SC works on a common response to unacceptable conditions. AirstrikesSC supports partners to immediately report security incidents to be analysed and to implement comprehensive safety plans. Loss of accessSC and partners conduct thorough security assessments and maintain updated contingency plans. If necessary, SC will suspend programs and work to resume activities. If this is not viable, SC would assess possibilities for relocation. Only in a worst-case scenario would project termination be considered. Work permitsMeasures are in place to mitigate the Gov. of Turkey (GoT) legislative environment hindering SC’s ability to operate, I.e. using a third-party HR company to overcome work permit challenges. Diversion of resources to terrorist/ proscribed groups In 2018, interviews were conducted to identify areas for increased risk mitigation; an action plan was developed. SC launched an initiative with OCHA to provide external training on international humanitarian law and negotiation with armed actors. Partners have their own systems of anti-terrorism vetting checks for all contracted suppliers and SC’s Syria office vets against the third-party database for procurements $750+. ShellingWhilst the most frequent attacks of shelling take place in southern rural Idlib and northern rural Hama, no area is immune.Escalation of military activity at Turkey/Syria borderSC program locations and contingency plans are under constant review and the Hibernation, Relocation and Evacuation plan is shared with staff. Offices and guesthouses are located away from military bases and have the necessary S&S infrastructure.Attacks by armed groupsSecurity assessments are conducted on potential project sites. Proximity to military locations is monitored and a back-up vehicle is used in high-risk areas. Staff transit must be pre-approved and take place during daylight.Improvised Explosive DevicesSC coordinates closely with Tetra Tech, new project locations are cleared by the Mines Advisory Group and follow up trainings in Mine Risk Education are planned for staff.Road accidentsDaily vehicle checks; all vehicles are equipped with tracking devices and satellite phones; mandatory use of seatbelts.Disruption to cash transfer operationsIn NES, SC is conducting a review of Hawala delivery to identify mitigation measures/additional suppliers.
