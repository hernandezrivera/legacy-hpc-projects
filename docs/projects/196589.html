Project Summary

Since its initiation by the Syria Strategic Steering Group in 2017, the Whole of Syria (WoS) Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) Programme has supported the interagency humanitarian response in the joint implementation of international PSEA commitments. The WoS PSEA programme is a multi-donor, comprehensive response, designed to address the risks of SEA posed by the complex and protracted humanitarian emergency in Syria. The PSEA programme is designed to cover diverse contexts through an established, robust interagency PSEA Network consisting of more than 350 focal points from 186 UN agencies, INGOs and NGOs, and cluster/working groups. The programme strives to decrease the risk of crisis‐affected communities experiencing incidents of sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) by humanitarian workers and provide technical and operational support to humanitarian actors to prevent and respond to SEA cases. The WoS PSEA Network operates an inter-agency complaint hotline in NWS which offers humanitarian staff, beneficiaries and communities a way to make complaints related to SEA and other misconduct by aid providers.
Leadership and Coordination: The PSEA program, through its team of dedicated staff, will maintain coordination capacity and provide leadership and strategic guidance of the PSEA Network hubs in Northeast Syria (NES), in Northwest Syria (NWS), and at the Whole of Syria (WoS) level. To sustain local leadership and sustainability, the programme will continue to expand its outreach to stakeholders involved in the Syria response through close collaboration with the NWS/NES NGO Forums, working groups, clusters, and network members.
Capacity Building: The PSEA programme will build the capacity of humanitarian workers through interactive trainings based on IASC standards. The training plan includes mandatory focal point training and training of trainers (ToT) to ensure the presence of qualified trainers across the response, as well as sector-specific trainings for clusters and other stakeholders as needed. Capacity building activities on PSEA Risks mitigation, using tools which were developed by the PSEA programme in 2021. Engagement of Senior Management will be strengthened, and organizational commitments solidified through the organization of tailored high-level workshops. The programme will continue to provide technical assistance, mentoring and capacity building on SEA investigation techniques.
Community Mobilization and Response : The programme will work with IPs to conduct direct consultations and awareness raising sessions with affected populations and humanitarian workers in NWS and NES. Noting that awareness raising delivered to beneficiaries is effective and increases the reporting of SEA incidents, the programme will work to integrate PSEA awareness materials into members’ protection awareness activities. Building on lessons learned from the PSEA programme in NWS, an inter-agency complaint mechanism will be established in NES, SOPs will be developped for the referral of all cases to GBV services providers (if needed).
Integration into the broader humanitarian infrastructure: To enhance local leadership and sustainability of the PSEA network, the program will mainstream PSEA into the humanitarian infrastructure, through integration of PSEA messaging into key multisectoral tools, assessments, and guidance documents and will work closely with clusters/working groups to mainstream PSEA across all sectors. Further, the programme will support PSEA risks assessments to support the overall response and mitigate SEA risks.

3 - Needs

SEA by aid workers and actors involved in the provision of aid directly contradicts the principles upon which humanitarian action is based and represents a protection failure on the part of the aid community. SEA inflicts harm on those whom the humanitarian community is obligated to protect, as well as jeopardizes the credibility of all humanitarian actors. Despite the significant progress made in instituting international commitments on PSEA in the Syrian Response thus far, needs remain high, as evidenced by the 2022 household survey carried out jointly by HNAP/PSEA. Key findings from the survey continue to show that affected populations remain highly reliant upon humanitarian assistance for survival. At the national level, 8 percent of households (HH) that received humanitarian assistance stated that they or members of their HH were asked for favours in exchange for humanitarian assistance or services (including requests for cash, labour and personal relationships, or SEA). Out of these, 38% of female-headed HH were asked for personal relationships in exchange of assistance or service compared to 1% of male-headed HH. SEA in Syria continues to be underreported due to its sensitive nature and the number of actual violations is expected to be higher. Unfortunately, the majority (92%) of Syrian HHs who receive requests for favors in exchange for assistance did not report these incidents, largely due to concerns over confidentiality and fear of losing access to assistance.

4 - Implementation of activities

The programme is coordinated by IOM, under the responsibility of the UN Syria leadership (Regional Humanitarian Coordinator and Humanitarian Coordinator). It will make available funding for the Senior PSEA coordinator’s position and/or implementation of activities inside Syria based on requests/priorities from various hubs (Damascus, northwest and northeast). Since its inception in 2017, the WoS PSEA Network supported the humanitarian response in the joint implementation of international PSEA commitments. Through its established PSEA Networks of 350 focal points across 186 UN agencies, clusters, INGOs and NGOs in the WoS, the programme aims to decrease the risk of SEA and provides technical and operational support to humanitarian actors to prevent and respond to SEA allegation. These focal points act as a bridge between the respective PSEA network and the organization. They receive PSEA related training from the PSEA Network and are provided with a package of PSEA related tools and resources that the focal points introduce to their organizations to build PSEA related internal capacities. Furthermore, the PSEA Network through Implementing partners directly conducts capacity building and awareness raising materials targeting community members, local authorities and non-traditional providers of humanitarian assistance. It also runs an inter-agency community-based complaint mechanism in NWS and will establish one for NES.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risks: 1) Physical security risk due to conflict activity and barriers to access; 2) If community messaging is not culturally sensitive and suitable for communities, it could cause a reduction in community members’ trust in existing complaint mechanisms or implementing organizations; 3) lack of timely referral to requested survivor assistance may create additional protection and health risks for the survivor; 4) lack of confidentiality and safe information-sharing protocols may result in additional harm or retaliation towards survivor, perpetrator and/or community members Mitigation Measures: 1)Through interagency collaboration, monitor security risks and barriers to access to ensure the safety of staff and beneficiaries; 2) provide agencies’ access to culturally sensitive and tested PSEA awareness-raising materials created by the PSEA network and review other PSEA related materials used by network partners to ensure culturally sensitive and consistent with standards for PSEA messaging; 3) ensure timely referrals of requests for survivor assistance received by the network coordination to GBV experts for assessment and assistance provision and follow-up with service providers and/ or survivor on the outcome; 4) follow information sharing protocols when sharing information about SEA allegations, and ensure that network partners are familiar and also utilize these protocols and practices.


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

The PSEA Network ensures that all activities are guided by the principle of “Do No Harm”. New activities and materials are only implemented following consultations with coordination fora, beneficiaries, and community consultations. In order to ensure appropriate interactions among staff, the network ensures that all staff are trained on PSEA, aware of IOMs internal reporting mechanisms, strictly follow its Code of Conduct, and have received training concerning confidentiality and engagement with vulnerable populations and SEA survivors. Furthermore, the PSEA network ensures that all partner organizations have the following standards in place:1) Internal SEA reporting mechanisms and organizations have a code of conduct that includes a comprehensive section on PSEA. 2) The organization adopts the Humanitarian Code of Conduct (CoC) for the Syria Cross-Border Response (2020) and/or ensures that their own CoC is consistent with it. All staff, including contractors, sub-contractors, daily wage workers and volunteers have understood and signed the CoC. 3) All staff are trained regularly on PSEA .4) The organization follows up on received complaints with due diligence, with respect to consent, confidentiality, and time-sensitive, survivor-centred approaches. 5) All field activities are supervised by humanitarian staff, including female staff members to mitigate PSEA and other protection risks.


9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks: • Deteriorating political/security situation with repercussions for access.•Insufficient funding. Assumptions: • Safe and secure access to communities to allow for awareness-raising activities amongst the field staff and beneficiaries. •Beneficiaries have access to multiple reporting mechanisms (such as an interagency hotline and organizational complaint mechanisms or CBCMs) that are easily accessible and of consistent standards of operation. • Inter-agency collaboration facilitates communication and planning. • Funding is sufficient for sustaining of PSEA activities. • Inter-agency coordination will mainstream PSEA monitoring, prevention and response consistently. • The PSEA Networks in all hubs will sustain high participation of agencies and effective PSEA mainstreaming within their policies and programming. Mitigation measures: • Continued advocacy on the importance of PSEA among all relevant stakeholders including donors, the Government of Syria, and humanitarian leadership and sectors/clusters. • Open and transparent dialogue on PSEA amongst the humanitarian community and with key stakeholders. • Implementation using a consensus-based, and inter-agency (PSEA Network-centered) approach.
