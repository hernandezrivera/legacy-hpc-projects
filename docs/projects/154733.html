Project Summary


The project proposed to run 2 Community Centres (Tel Afar and Ramadi), where ZOA would provide
* PSS to adults (400)
* PSS to children (900 children, using CPWG approved modules for structured child PSS)
* Parenting programmes (800 parents and caregivers)
* Case management (200 cases)
* Legal assistance/ counseling (500 cases – 200 children secure civil documentation, up to 100 legal assistance for children, up to 200 adults are supported in legal matters (documentation, divorces, etc.))
* Legal awareness raising from centres (2000 individuals)
* Protection monitoring in the neighbourhoods of the centres
From Ramadi, ZOA will also deploy a mobile team to the village along the Falluja-Ramadi strip, which will provide
* Child PSS (300 children)
* Legal awareness (2000 individuals)
* Links to legal assistance and case management from Ramadi Community Centre
In West Mosul, ZOA will work through its existing Community Centres to train 100 so-called 'community builders' on community-based PSS and peer-support groups, and provide them with a toolkit and curriculum as guidance to set up their own group. These male and female community builders will be selected by the Community Centre staff, according to their capacity, and will be empowered to be change makers in the community.

Needs

To date, approximately 1.9 million internally displaced persons remain in Iraq. Among them, over 1.3 million reside out of camps. These IDPs, both the ones that are looking to return in the coming months, as well as those who might remain in displacement for longer, protection needs prevail. Nearly half of the people in need are children. Having witnessed death and destruction, children of Mosul, Tel Afar and Ramadi have lost their childhood early on. At risk of exploitation, forced into early marriage or child labour, the situation for children look bleak. Save the Children reports that almost half of the children interviewed in Mosul feel grief all, or a lot of the time, adolescents fear walking alone or being away from their parents. Less than 1 in 10 children could think of something happy in their lives, and 72% of caregivers report feeling depressed or unhappy. Adults, too, lost loved ones, homes, livelihoods, and still live in fear, indicating not feeling comfortable in the settings in which they currently reside. Protection risks and livelihoods needs in Anbar and Ninewa are closely related. People who lost their documentation during displacement, or hold expired documentation, are obstructed in accessing various services, exacerbating their vulnerability. They cannot register children in school, are obstructed from obtaining food basket support, and have trouble finding work. Access to civil documentation has become increasingly restricted, particularly for persons with perceived affiliation to extremists. There’s a lack of legal awareness, counselling and actual aid to address issues, courts are not easily reached (especially for rural communities, due to distance and lack of funds to for transportation), and courts are overwhelmed with the amount of cases. Many hesitate to renew their documentation at government offices, fearing detainment due to perceived links to IS. The MCNA found 13% of the returnee, 19% of out -of- camp IDP, and 9% of non-displaced households with children showing signs of psychological distress. Adults’ stress conditions (related to displacement, trauma, lack of livelihoods) leads to more cases of child neglect and domestic violence. Child labour and sexual violence, including child marriage, are exacerbated by lack of livelihood opportunities of care givers. Recent peace-building exploration workshops between ZOA and its partners indicated the need for peace building activities, and activities aimed at peaceful co-existence and social cohesion. Social tensions throughout Iraq exist between IDPs, returnees, host communities, as well as various tribes and religious and ethnic groups. These are both inter- and intra-communal tensions, which require peace-building on various levels.

Activities

The project proposed to run 2 Community Centres (Tel Afar and Ramadi), where ZOA would provide * PSS to adults (400)* PSS to children (900 children, using CPWG approved modules for structured child PSS)* Parenting programmes (800 parents and caregivers)* Case management (200 cases)* Legal assistance/ counseling (500 cases – 200 children secure civil documentation, up to 100 legal assistance for children, up to 200 adults are supported in legal matters (documentation, divorces, etc.))* Legal awareness raising from centres (2000 individuals)* Protection monitoring in the neighbourhoods of the centres Protection monitoring in the neighbourhoods of the centres In Tel Afar and Ramadi, ZOA proposes a community-based response that would include provision of protection services through Community Centres. These centres will provide psycho-social support (PSS) to children ranging between the ages of 5-14 as well as adults, both in group setting (based on Protection Cluster approved curricula) and individual psychological support. Case management is available for complex cases, and all PPS will be led by psychologist and social workers. The Community Centres are also tools to increase legal awareness, provide legal counselling, and where necessary also legal assistance on obtaining documentation. From Ramadi, ZOA will also deploy a mobile team to the village along the Falluja-Ramadi strip, which will provide * Child PSS (300 children) * Legal awareness (2000 individuals) * Links to legal assistance and case management from Ramadi Community Centre ZOA’s response along the Fallujah-Ramadi strip requires a flexible, mobile approach. Child PSS will be provided through mobile teams, reaching out to children in remote areas with regular group-based child resilience programmes (based on Protection Cluster approved curricula). Cases with severe mental health issues will be referred to other service providers in the area, or to the centre in Ramadi, while ZOA will support potential costs for transportation. The same communities will be targeted with legal awareness raising efforts by trained facilitators and supported with legal counselling and legal aid by lawyers in the project, for issues such as lacking documentation. In West Mosul, ZOA will work through its existing Community Centres to train 100 so-called 'community builders' on community-based PSS and peer-support groups, and provide them with a toolkit and curriculum as guidance to set up their own group. These male and female community builders will be selected by the Community Centre staff, according to their capacity, and will be empowered to be change makers in the community. All Community Centres also provide other services, including life skills and training, non-formal educational activities, information services, as well as community events. Current Community Centres are used by other organisations and communitymembers to provide a range of services and access communities in urban neighbourhoods. ZOA aims to run Cash for Work schemes under the Emergency Livelihoods Cluster in the same areas. Through the protection monitoring in the neighbourhoods, specific attention will be given to children at risk of, or engaged in, child labour. ZOA's Cash for Work scheme will target adult family members of these children, while engaging the children and their care givers (where possible) in PSS schemes, as well as any other protection service necessary. All activities are carried out in collaboration with local organisations which have expertise in Protection and peacebuilding, and have extensive understanding of social networks and functioning of services in the neighbourhoods. Sustainability is guaranteed through the engagement of community builders, as well as the commitment of ZOA to seek ways to maintain community initiatives in the longer-run at minimum costs (which could potentially be carried by community leaders or otherwise, which is not yet realistic but would be in the future). High quality staff training benefits the expertise on mental health and PSS in targeted locations, which could be disseminated further in the future. Note that the proposed community centres are not Community Resource Centres. Peaceful co-existence activities for the proposed project are small scale due to the short time-frame, as well as the awareness of the humanitarian mandate in this. Proposed peaceful co-existence throughout the project includes the training of 100 individuals (Community Builders) who set up peer-support groups. This is primarily for (informal) community based PSS, but also allows for stronger engagement with (young) people through community events and joint activities to enhance a sense of unity. In groups, they will be encouraged to develop activities that foster peaceful co-existence (through recreational activities, civic engagement, etc.)

Protection Risk Analysis

1. In community-based protection schemes, there's a risk of trained staff or Community Builders over-estimating their capacity in caring for complicated cases. Rather than referral, they might try to solve cases themselves, which could potentially be harmful. 2. Staff could get overwhelmed by the needs and psychological trauma they witness around them. 3. Community builders drop out after certain amount of time of engagement. 4. Duplication with other actors implementing in the same areas. 5. The security situation deteriorates putting both staff and beneficiaries in danger. 6. Sexual exploitation and abuse.

Mitigation Measures

1.Staff and Community Builders will be trained before commencing their involvement in the project. Part of that training will be on the importance of referral, as well as the concept of 'quality over quantity'. 2. Staff care is done through support to staff, regular evaluation of cases in groups, exchange of practices between staff in other centres, as well as regular outings to build the teams and attend to needs in the team. 3. Community builders are expected to be engaged for a period of 3 – 9 months, and ZOA is aware that this engagement might differ for each person related to their person activities. Therefore, the way the community builders work is that, in their peer-support-group, they identify 1 co-leader who learns to engage with community by setting up groups, but is also engaged as a volunteer in other community based activities and events. ZOA will follow up with / monitor each community builder and 1 individual from their group. 4. Continued engagement with PSS actors in Ninewa and Anbar, through alliances and cluster coordination. 5. Increase in security could lead to inaccessibility of some areas, increasing difficulty for ZOA to implement the project and deliver results. This is mitigated by continuous monitoring of security development by each ZOA's security staff, who will advise on how to proceed. In case of deteriorated access, ZOA might temporarily suspend activities, and in extreme and sustained cases, may relocate activities to other areas with similar needs. 6. Another risk identified by the consortium is the risk sexual exploitation and abuse. The most vulnerable to this risk are women and children, especially girls and young women. ZOA recognizes that the problem of sexual exploitation and abuse exists within humanitarian spaces, and will therefore take all measures to prevent its occurrence on the part of its staff, partners, vendors and associates. ZOA is committed to the Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA), and adheres to Codes of Conduct. All staff and partner staff are trained in the understanding of the Code of Conduct (training of three sessions at start of employment, which is repeated annually) Child Protection policies will be adhered to by all staff. They affirm ZOA's commitment to the welfare of children and their protection from abuse and exploitation and is consistent with ZOA;s vision, mission and core values of human dignity, faithfulness, stewardship and justice. The policies are aimed at deterring, minimizing and removing opportunities for child abuse and exploitation to occur in our country programs. ZOA and partners are committed to ensure that all staff, contractors, volunteers, interns, and partners are aware of the problem and issues of child abuse and exploitation, risks to children and their safety. We provide support when concerns arise regarding possible abuse and exploitation are signalled.

How is your project accountable to affected population

ZOA adheres to the CHS throughout the programme cycle, and has designed and will implement the project in a way in which beneficiaries are, have been, and will be, involved in all stages, through for example the following: • Leading up to the project proposal, ZOA has conducted assessments regarding people’s needs through current community centres. Further assessments will be conducted closer to implementation of the project, where vulnerability assessments will serve as a basis for beneficiary selection. • Implementation is done through participatory community approaches. Such an approach ensures that the most vulnerable community members (women headed household, disabled, etc.) are well represented amongst the beneficiaries. • To ensure a Do No Harm approach, efforts are made to ensure that relationships with the targeted population and its local leaders are enhanced, and programmes are implemented to improve relationships, reduce tensions, and promote peaceful pathways out of conflict situations. Understanding the context in which we operate is paramount to ensure success of interventions, and negative consequences in the operation areas are avoided. • Two-way communication is ensured through feedback mechanisms such as frequent field visits from project and MEAL staff. MEAL activities will play a vital role in continuously understanding the context and conflict dynamics, especially what typically connects or divides community members. Beneficiary's feedback will be recorded by all partners, shared with the project team on a monthly basis and will be incorporated into future actions to ensure accountability to beneficiaries. The feedback will be critical for the direction of the programme. • Project final review at the end of the project will be conducted in a participatory manner, with representatives from the community and the local authorities, teams ZOA and partners, in order to discuss results from the post distribution monitoring, and beneficiaries feedback on the project, and identify lessons learned. By definition, a Community-based approach builds on support and understanding of community structures, empowering individuals in the community to protect their own (children, youth, etc.). Choices on who to target, how to roll out programmes, etc., will be taken in collaboration with community members, with whom we aim to build spaces in which boys, girls, men and women are safe and free to play, express, and process the impact the crisis has and/or had on their lives. ZOA’s PSS programming started and developed based on constant interaction with beneficiaries about the relevance of the programme, which will continue. Complaint mechanisms will make beneficiary accountability possible, as well as transparency towards the community about the selection of beneficiaries. Beneficiary selection for legal assistance and case management is never a sole decision of 1 staff member, but rather an informed decision of at least 3 staff members with different disciplines. At the same time, strict confidentiality is guaranteed for case management and legal assistance; staff is trained for this.
