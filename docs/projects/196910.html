Project Summary

A coherent response that is locally driven is more important than ever in Sudan, not least because funding levels are expected to stay the same during the year. Ensuring an agile and cost-effective humanitarian system, where coordination is seen as an enabler, and the humanitarian programme cycle is lighter, will be priorities during the year. The office will also continue investing in access and humanitarian negotiations. During 2022 CMCoord forums were set up across several states in Sudan; this will continue in 2023. This is important because of the increasing access and bureaucratic impediments imposed on humanitarian operations following the 25 October 2021 Coup d'état.
OCHA will maintain a robust field presence in seven states (West, North, Central Darfur and South Darfur – covering also East Darfur; South Kordofan – covering also West Kordofan; Kassala – covering Kassala, Red Sea, and Gedaref; Blue Nile) and the Abyei box, ensuring adequate humanitarian coordination across the country. As new areas that have been difficult to access over the years open up, OCHA Sudan aims to establish a presence in more remote areas to respond to the increased workload effectively.
The direct beneficiaries of OCHA's coordination services in Sudan include the Humanitarian Coordinator (HC), the Humanitarian Country Team (HCT) and the Inter-Cluster Coordination Group (ICCG) members, implementing partners, i.e., both UN agencies and I/NGOs, donors and government of Sudan counterparts.
In 2023 OCHA Sudan will continue to support strategic and operational coordination through the HCT, the ICCG, and Area HCT/ICCG at the sub-national level.
Ensuring humanitarian access and space through monitoring and advocacy efforts will remain a strong focus. OCHA will work on setting up an operating model that is predictable, flexible, and fit for purpose to respond to both sudden and slow-onset crises, including recurring natural disaster events such as floods.
OCHA Sudan will continue strengthening its leadership role in assessing, planning, coordinating, and monitoring the response in Non-State Armed Group (NSAG) control areas, including Jebel Marra, Blue Nile, and South Kordofan. It will continue to show leadership in humanitarian access and civil-military coordination to ensure the most vulnerable are reached with assistance.
OCHA managed Sudan Humanitarian Fund (SHF), which allocates funding through the Standard Allocation modality and the Reserve for Emergency modality. With its well-developed Reserve for Emergency (RfE) modality with three instruments available – prepositioning of stock, rapid emergency response, and RfE projects- it is ideally placed to assure humanitarian actors have timely access to funding to respond to new or deteriorating emergency shocks. At the same time, its Standard Allocation is ideal for addressing, through humanitarian interventions, the root causes, the secondary impact of these emergencies and managing chronic needs.

1 - Needs

Humanitarian needs in Sudan will be at an all-time high in 2023 - 15.8 million people - driven by conflict, a political and economic crisis, increased insecurity, localized violence in parts of the country, protracted internal displacement that is yet to find durable solutions, coupled with dry spells, floods, and disease outbreaks. Of the 15.8 million people in need, about 11 million need emergency assistance for life-threatening needs related to critical physical and mental well-being. This is a 21 per cent increase compared to the previous year. Meanwhile, all people within the PiN require life-sustaining support to meet minimum living standards. Most people in need are women and girls - 8.2 million or 57 per cent, while more than half of people in need are children. For a second year, conflict continues to displace an increasing number of people. About 3.7 million people in Sudan are displaced, many of them for decades. 2021 saw the erosion of the protection of civilians and the resurgence of large-scale attacks against civilians, particularly in Darfur. Over 440,000 people were displaced because of conflict in 2021 – this is four times higher than in 2020. Between January to September this year, over 211,000 people were displaced by armed attacks and inter-communal violence. Hundreds of villages have been burnt, household assets taken, farmland damaged, and livestock looted. 546 civilians, including women, have been killed, 846 injured, many others physically abused, and abducted. This led to populations relapsing to life-threatening living conditions and deteriorating food security.Prolonged dry spells, erratic rains, and subsequent crop failure have deteriorated food security. The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) estimates that 11.7M people will be food insecure during the lean season from June to September 2022. This includes 3.1 million people in IPC phase 4 (emergency) and 8.5 million in IPC phase 3 (crisis). This is a two million increase from last year. WFP's Comprehensive Food Security Assessment suggests that even more, up to 15 million people, are facing acute food insecurity during the same period. Food insecurity exists in all of Sudan’s 18 states and has worsened in 16. The ten most affected localities in the Darfurs have been ravaged by nearly two decades of protracted conflict and displacement. Rates of malnutrition are increasing across Sudan at a concerning rate, especially among children. Over three million children under five years old suffer from acute malnutrition. Sudan’s health system has long been under extreme stress. The health infrastructure at the federal and state levels remains under-staffed, under-resourced, and under-equipped to cope with large-scale outbreaks. Climate-related events, including floods, continue to pose a challenge. In 2022, over 349,000people across Sudan were affected by rains and flooding. The flooding season in Sudan lasts from June-September. Gender-based violence continues to be a life-threatening concern exacerbated by conflict, forced displacement, and recurring disasters such as floods. Over 2.7 million people need lifesaving gender-based violence prevention, mitigation, and response services. Survivors lack access to quality specialized lifesaving services, with the clinical management of rape, psychosocial support services, case management, and referral mechanisms still unavailable in over 80 percent of localities. People with disabilities are disproportionately affected by violence, while adolescents and young girls continue to be exposed to the harmful practices of child marriage and female genital mutilation.

2 - Activities/Outputs

Provide support to the HC and HCT to enable effective leadership in strategic coordination, including the integration of key cross-cutting issues such as gender, environment, and accountability to affected people in humanitarian actions.2. Lead the inter-sector coordination mechanisms at national and sub-national levels in accordance with IASC guidance and support clusters in the preparation of cluster response plans and their strategic monitoring indicators. 3. Establish and maintain dialogue with affected people, including in IDP settlements in Darfur, to ensure the provision of basic services to vulnerable people. This includes evidence-based liaison with cluster coordinators at the state and Khartoum levels to address any gaps reported. 4. Support the HCT in developing the Humanitarian Response Plan informed by evidence-based analysis of assessed needs with clear measurable objectives.5. Ensure that inter-agency risk analysis is undertaken, and minimum preparedness actions and advanced prepared actions are routinely carried out in accordance with the Emergency Preparedness Framework.6. Develop and publish on a regular basis communication and information products using a standardized and integrated approach. This includes the coordination of humanitarian public information and advocacy efforts.7. Analyze and provide access reports to guide and inform decision-making by the HCT and advocacy and negotiate for access.8. Promote the adherence to humanitarian principles by humanitarian actors to ensure principled interventions in line with the HCT Minimum Operating Standards.
