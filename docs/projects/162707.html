Project Summary

The project aims to provide food and non-food assistance to families displaced by the conflict using electronic vouchers. CRS proposes a 12-month extension to its joint OFDA and FFP project to meet the immediate food and NFI needs of 27,450 individuals in approximately 3,660 affected households in Mezam Division. The extension is based on priority needs of the population, local dynamics and learning from the current response. CRS will continue to work with the Archdiocese of Bamenda through its social arms Caritas Bamenda and Justice and Peace Commission. Together the partners will provide food and NFI assistance using electronic vouchers (e-vouchers) on CRS’ Cash Asset Transfer Platform operating on the RedRose ONESystem and customized for CRS.

Besoins / Needs

WFP’s recent Emergency Food Security Assessment in NW and SW shows that 1.5 million people are food insecure with 906,461 (60%) in the NW. WFP reports that 50% of IDPs in the NW are food insecure, with 13% severely food insecure, and 60% of people with inadequate food intake. In both the NW/SW regions, IDP HHs are more vulnerable than local populations as they adopt food-related coping strategies that are more severe and with more frequency. The average reduced Coping Strategy Index (rCSI) is comparatively high among IDPs (29) in the NW. CRS’ project baseline shows that targeted HHs have adopted high frequency food coping strategies. More than 70% have resorted to irreversible emergency coping strategies such as begging and selling assets, which compromises their productivity and ability to cope with future shocks. More than half of the IDP HHs (52%) have borderline to poor food consumption scores. Host HHs are also engaging in severe coping strategies eroding their capacity to support IDPs in the community. The deteriorating food security situation is driven by severely disrupted livelihoods, limiting household food production and income to purchase food and meet their basic needs. In addition to forcing people from their homes, the threat of violence and the continued enforcement of ghost town days prevent people from accessing their fields and livelihoods. Prior to the crisis most of IDP families relied on agriculture and livestock breeding as their main livelihoods. In 2018, 88% of IDPs reported that as a result of the conflict, they do not have access to land for agriculture and 89% said that their financial resources were insufficient to satisfy their family’s basic needs. Unable to return home, farming families have left crops rotting in the fields unharvested, and untended livestock have been stolen. According to FEWSNet this has resulted in the third consecutive below average season. Some residents say that in current situation the lean season may start as early as January.
