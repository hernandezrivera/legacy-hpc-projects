Project Summary

Since the beginning of the conflict in December 2013, IOM has been providing life-saving humanitarian assistance to internally displaced persons (IDPs), returnees, and host communities in conflict-affected and hard-to-reach locations across South Sudan. In 2021 IOM will continue to provide integrated emergency life-saving primary health care, routine immunization, and surveillance and response to disease outbreaks to underserved and conflict-affected IDPs, returnees, migrants/travelers, and host communities through six static clinics, 15 mobile clinics, eight rapid response team (RRT) deployments and one PoE screening site. The static and mobile clinics are located in Wau (Western Bahr el Ghazal State), Malakal (Upper Nile State), and Bentiu (Unity State) protection of civilians (PoC) site, as well as outside of PoC locations. The health RRT will be deployed to any area across South Sudan that faces acute man-made or natural disasters with unmet health needs. IOM will use the DTM intention to return survey results to quickly deploy the health rapid response team to areas of highest return so that acute returnees get the appropriate basic health care that supports them to settle in the area of return. IOM integrates comprehensive HIV/AIDS/TB prevention, care, and treatment, systematic nutrition screening, mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS), clinical management of rape services (CMR), and COVID-19 screening, prevention, and mitigation activities in any of its health interventions. MHPSS services are provided in 13 recreational and counselling centres in Wau Naivasha IDP site, Bentiu PoC, Malakal PoC, and in respective host communities in the greater Wau area. Recently, IOM has started MHPSS programming in the Abyei Administrative Area (AAA) as well.
In addition to the direct service delivery mentioned above, IOM plans to strengthen the Ministry of Health (MoH) capacity through a diaspora health project whereby South Sudanese diaspora communities will be engaged in the provision of direct health services and on-the-job training of MoH staff on selected priority medical intervention, including an emergency maternal, child and neonatal care. IOM will also work closely with the MoH Directorate of the International Coordination Office to strengthen port health and conduct a survey to understand the perceptions of the border communities and institutions on COVID-19 preparedness and response. The outcome of this survey will inform MoH and humanitarian actors on the strength and gaps observed in the current response.

1 - Needs

Access to primary health care (PHC) services continued to be unavailable for a large majority of South Sudanese, with only 44 percent of the population having access to basic health services, while 25 percent of IDPs and returnees have no access to basic health services and live in settlements further than 5 km from a functional health facility. In addition, an estimated 6.35 million people (52.6% of the population) faced Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity, of which 2.1 million people faced Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. The protracted conflict, unstable political situation, economic deterioration and inflation, frequent disease outbreaks, massive flooding, increasing food insecurity, coupled with the COVID-19 pandemic and the lockdown measures, have created a disabling environment for a well-functioning health system. Out of the 2,300 health facilities, more than 1,300 are non-functional. Of the functioning health facilities, the majority are poorly equipped and staffed, while humanitarian and development partners support 57 percent. The prevailing health system challenges are exacerbated by poor housing structures, lack of safe water supplies, infection prevention and control measures, healthcare waste management, expansive malnutrition, a looming threat of COVID-19, and poor infrastructure to procure, transport, and store essential pharmaceutical and vaccines. As a result, South Sudan continued to have some of the worst health outcome indicators globally, including:• Eight counties having measles outbreaks (Tonj East, Magwi, Bor, Kapoeta East, Tonj South, Wau, Pibor and Ibba), • Seven counties across five states, namely Northern Bahr El Ghazal, Western Bahr El Ghazal, Warrap, Lakes, and Eastern Equatoria, had polio outbreaks in 2020, • Maternal mortality ratio of 789 per 100,000 live births—fifth highest in the world, • The under-five mortality rate is at 99.2 per 1,000 live births,• Only 19 percent of births were attended by skilled health workers• More than 50 percent of children are not fully immunized through routine childhood vaccinations• Health needs were the second amongst the top seven most commonly cited priority needs for women across almost 2,000 assessed settlements in South Sudan in October 2020. Protracted conflict, exposure to violence, displacement, and natural hazards continue to affect the physical and mental health of the South Sudanese population. In 2021, the country continues to face major gaps in the provision of adequate mental health care. Inpatient psychiatric care is only available at Juba Teaching Hospital, and the training capacity for specialized services remains limited. Mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) services are offered by humanitarian partners and national organizations and are not fully integrated into existing health care facilities. The outbreak of COVID-19 had an additional negative impact on the level of distress observed in the general population and particularly on persons living in confined areas such as protection of civilians (PoC) and collective sites where preventative action such as physical distancing and regular handwashing presented a challenge. Over the first half of 2020, Juba Teaching Hospital, MHPSS humanitarian actors, and protection partners in South Sudan observed an increase in suicide cases and suicide attempts for young adults, adolescents, and children. Different factors contribute to this situation, including the lack of socio-economic opportunities, pressure on the heads of households or those responsible for the family's income, alcohol and substance use, and family conflicts.

2 - Project activities

IOM implements four response modalities to reach various population groups, including those in PoC sites, IDPs outside of PoC sites, conflict-affected host communities, people living in hard-to-reach areas, populations affected by disease outbreaks, and cross-border and international travellers. The four modalities are: • Emergency Primary health care services through static health facilities;• Emergency health care services through mobile and outreach clinics;• Deployment of health rapid response teams; and• Strengthening port health through screening of travellers at major points of entry. The clinical package of services that will be provided in the four modalities of response include:• Preventive, and curative health services for common morbidities;• Surveillance, early warning, alert and response to epidemic prone diseases;• Routine immunization for children;• Systematic Nutrition screening; • Sexual and reproductive health services that include Basic emergency obstetric and neonatal care services (BEmONC); antenatal and postnatal care, PMTCT, family planning services;• Clinical management of rape (CMR); • HIV and tuberculosis (TB) testing and treatment;• Integrated mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) through focused services provided in recreational and counselling centres associated with IOM clinics and via tele-communication (regional MHPSS hotlines);• Non-communicable disease (NCD) management; • Emergency reactive mass vaccination campaigns and case management;• Improving water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) in health facilities; • Health promotion and development of key messages in collaboration with MHPSS teams; and referral to secondary level of care;• Training and capacity building for health personnel, from both the Ministry of Health (MoH) and national non-governmental organizations (NNGOs);• Capacity building for MHPSS mobile teams, for social worker trainees of the Ministry of Gender, Child and Social Welfare (MoGCSW) who use an integrated MHPSS approach; and• MHPSS Mainstreaming for health workers, protection actors, national and international non-government organizations; Coordination of the MHPSS Technical Working Groups at national and state level (Wau, Malakal, and Bentiu);• Screening of travellers at the identified priority border points of entry; and• Survey to evaluate the 2020 COVID-19 preparedness and response actions.

3 - Project indicators

The success of the project will be measured against the achievement on a set of selected indicators as set out by the Health Cluster. The indicators are tallied daily at the facility level and entered in IOM Migration Health Information Web Application (MIWA) data base and reported through the EWARS weekly. Data is analyzed and reports generated on biweekly and quarterly basis for internal IOM for monitoring and donor reports prepared in view of informing on impact and as per agreement with the specific donor. Qualitative information generated from community consultative meeting reports and beneficiary service satisfaction surveys will also serve as input to measure the success of the project.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

IOM Health and MHPSS units in Juba and respective field locations have several PSEA focal points. The focal points are trained and guided by the IOM Protection Officer and the MHU program manager on PSEA, their roles, responsibilities, and expectations. The PSEA focal points and the field health coordinators raise PSEA as one of their agenda point during the project orientation community meetings. PSEA messages and reporting lines written in local languages will be posted in visible areas and discussed during community meetings.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

At the launch of any intervention, IOM calls for a meeting with community representatives, local authorities and other stakeholders to create a common understanding of IOM’s mandate, priority sectoral and geographic area of intervention, and the project’s available feedback mechanisms. IOM strives to conduct focus group discussions with specific groups, including women, elderly and people with disability. In addition, IOM has a protocol for service provision for people with disability in IOM health facilities.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

IOM complaint and feedback mechanism have a variety of confidential ways, including verbal, telephone and written complaint mechanisms. The community dialogue sessions and the house-to-house health promotion session also contribute to collect feedbacks from the most vulnerable people like disabled, elderly, bed ridden individuals. In addition, IOM has trained all its program managers on mandatory disability streamlining training. Quantification of the prevalence of disabled people in the PoC and basic need assessment surveys was also done in collaboration with the INGO Humanity & Inclusion. The collaboration will continue to ensure that streamlining is institutionalized. IOM Health and MHPSS units in Juba and respective field locations have several PSEA focal points. 7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The Health and MHPSS coordinators collect and analyze all feedback from the community meetings and the quarterly beneficiary satisfaction surveys regularly. An action point is drawn on identified issues and followed up on until all cases are concluded and feedback provided to the communities. Continuous regular quarterly surveys will measure if the issues still persist. The retrieved information continuously informs programme adaptation and improvement.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

Health services will be provided in various modalities to meet population needs and access, including static and mobile clinics and rapid response team mechanisms, which provide life-saving health services to conflict-affected populations in hard-to-access areas. Therefore, this project will lessen the need for communities to leave their habitual place of residence to receive assistance. This will also reduce women and girls' exposure to sexual violence when accessing humanitarian assistance, concerning the distance to and location of humanitarian assistance and services. IOM consults with local communities to identify a relatively safe site that also considers the different vulnerabilities of people with disability, elderly, girls, boys, men and women before establishing a point of care site. The area based/Integrated one stop service/shopping strategic approach of IOM also encourages beneficiary to come to one spot in group mitigating violence that they could face if they were coming in smaller numbers. IOM strives to provide integrated health, MHPSS and WASH services in any area of intervention. Adequate staffing to provide services with minimum waiting time helps patients and care takers return back home before it gets dark. Priority in service provision will be given to elderly, people with disability, pregnants and other vulnerable groups. IOM also reports any protection related incidents to the protection cluster and OCHA focal person for access so that protection is always kept high in the agenda.

12.1. Does your project contribute to any of the two Collective Outcomes, i.e. Food insecurity and Gender-Based Violence?

Yes

12.1.4. Quality, timely and inclusive protection services (including medical care, legal support, safety and mental health psychosocial services to children, women at risk and GBV survivors

IOM actively participates at the national and state GBV Cluster and Health Cluster coordination meetings to contribute to an improved prevention, sensitization, case identification, referral and support system. The Health and MHPSS units provide awareness raising messages at both community and health facility level, continuously trains its staff on compassionate car, and provision of CMR services for survivors. In close collaboration with the GBV Sub-Cluster IOM, through the MHPSS Technical Working Group, is strengthening the capacity of GBV service providers in delivering psychosocial support to GBV survivors.

12.1.2. Decreased proportion of populations facing IPC 3,4 and 5

The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) is a common global scale for classifying the severity and magnitude of food insecurity and malnutrition. The IOM health intervention will contribute to reducing the IPC phases through its health promotion, preventive and curative health services. Typical areas of intervention that contributes to this outcome include: Health promotion on good feeding practices, community disease prevention and hygiene and sanitation sessions, routine immunization services, early detection and treatment of common morbidities.

12.1.3. Equitable access to cross-sectoral basic services to vulnerable populations (including women, children, the elderly, persons with disability and older persons)

Improved access to preventive and curative health and mental health services through 21 static and mobile clinics, deployment of 8 health rapid response teams.

12.1.1. Decreased prevalence of global acute malnutrition among children under age 5 in severity levels 3 and 4

Poor nutritional status weakens people’s health and vice versa. IOM through provision of preventive, promotive and curative health services breaks the vicious cycle enabling people to actively work and support their family. House-to-house and facility based hygiene promotion and health education helps minimize the incidence of communicable diseases while early detection and treatment helps reduce the impact of morbidity including malnutrition.
