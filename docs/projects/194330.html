Project Summary

2021 saw a deterioration in the humanitarian situation in South Sudan. The country was confronted by social and political instability due to violence and a series of interconnected shocks, including conflict, persistent and unprecedented flooding, inflation, and the impact of the economic weight of COVID-19 and the global impacts of the Russian-Ukraine war. This has led to massive internal and cross-border displacement, further straining of scarce resources, livelihoods, basic services, and increasing protection risks, particularly for the most vulnerable groups. Insecurity, fueled by sub-national inter-communal violence, crime and wide-scale impunity, continued to hamper the country’s roadmap to peace. Over 8.9 million people (including 4.6 million children) are estimated to need some form of humanitarian assistance and protection in South Sudan in 2022.
The April 2022 agreement between SPLA-IG and SPLA-IO leadership to unify army command structures and forces has reduced political tension. However, the slow implementation of the remaining elements of the 2018 peace deal and the decentralized command structure of the armed forces continue to limit the Transitional Government of National Unity’s (TGoNU) ability to enforce orders nationwide.
The majority of the targeted households in Aweil South and Tonj North Counties have poor food production capacities and limited sources of income generation, making them extremely vulnerable. Production remains low and the majority of produce is transported from Sudan and Uganda driving up already high prices. Poor utilization of land and a lack of available agricultural inputs, technologies, the lack of extension services and government support programs, and poor crop production skills of farmers (including pest management and post-harvest handling practices) are major limitations to local production in the targeted project area Most farmers are primarily subsistence farmers relying on rain-fed to meet their food and cash needs. They operate at subsistence level with an average farm size of 1.8 ha. Cultivation is mostly often carried out by women which limits the area households can cultivate.
Agriculture and food security are extremely vulnerable to climate shocks. Key climate change factors include unpredictable rainfall pattern, recurrent droughts, floods and excessive heat results into crop failures. The four consecutive years of flash floods is one of the factors that amplified the food insecurity. Nutrition and resilience are strongly interlinked, households that are nutrition secure are better equipped to withstand external shocks.
FLDA’s proposed program “Food assistance, production and strengthening ability to shocks” aims to deliver lifesaving humanitarian assistance through provision of food assistance that meets short term hunger gaps while helping households build longer term food security and resilience against future shocks and stresses. The proposed program contributes to Food Security and Livelihoods cluster objectives - CO1, CO2, and CO3 that are aligned with the SDG 2 “End Hunger - achieve food security and improved nutrition, and promote sustainable agriculture.” The program will target vulnerable populations, especially women, girls, elderly, disabled, PLW, children U5, HIV & the chronically sick amongst IDPs, refugee, returnees or host communities. FLDA is well placed to implement the above program as it has been operating in the location since 9 years, having established a strong working relationship with local authorities, built trust with communities, delivered inclusive and impactful programs that mainstream gender and protection. FLDA is an active member of the coordination mechanisms and ensures community participation in all phases of the program cycle. WFP’s CFA and FAO’s RALP in Aweil South will further help FLDA address food security and nutrition concerns amongst vulnerable groups. FLDA’s integrated approach will strengthen synergies and the impact.

1 - Needs

In Warap state, during the period of Oct to Nov 2022, an estimated 740,000 people (57.1% of the population) are facing Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity, of which 413,000 people are in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) acute food insecurity and 327,000 people are in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. During this period, all counties are classified in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. The main drivers of food insecurity in Warrap State are conflict and insecurity, climatic shocks in the form of floods and dry spells, high food prices, animal disease outbreaks, crop pests and diseases, and reduced income at household level. During the post-harvest period, the food security situation is expected to deteriorate slightly, with an estimated 746,000 people (57.6% of the population) likely to face Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity. Of these, 413,000 people will likely be in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) and 333,000 people will likely be in Emergency (IPC Phase 4). During this period, all counties are classified in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. The negative effects of the insecurity in the Amiet/Abyei region drive the slight deterioration of the food security situation during the post-harvest season, coupled with an anticipated earlier than normal depletion of harvests, continued high food prices, and reduced market functionality because of insecurity. It’s expected that during the lean season, the food security situation is likely to deteriorate further. It’s estimated that 885,000 people (68.2% of the population) is expected to face Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity. Of these, 472,000 people will likely be in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) acute food insecurity, and 413,000 people will likely be in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. In the targeted county of Tonj North with a population of 263,566 person, between Oct-Nov 2022 (66, 000 persons); Dec 2022-March 2023 (79,000 persons); and April- July 2023 (92,000 persons) will be under IPC Phase 4 respectively. Whereas in Northern Bahr El Ghazil, during the period of Oct to Nov 2022, an estimated 580,000 people (56.9% of the population) are facing Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity, of which 337,000 people are in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) acute food insecurity, and 173,000 people are in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. During this period, all counties were classified in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. The food security situation will improve during the post-harvest period with an estimated 533,000 people (56.9% of the population) likely to face Crisis (IPC Phase 3). Of these, 349,000 people are in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) acute food insecurity, whereas 184,000 people are in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity During the lean season period, the situation is likely to deteriorate with an estimated 656,000 people (66.3% of the population) anticipated to be in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity. Of these, 379,000 people are anticipated to be in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) acute food insecurity, and 277,000 people are likely to be in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. Specifically for the targeted county of Aweil South with the population of 141,217 persons during the period of Oct-Nov 2022 (28, 000 persons); Dec 2022-March 2023 (21,000 persons) and April- July 2023 (36,000 persons) respectively will be classified under this IPC phase 4. Food insecurity is mainly driven by the depletion of food stocks for households whose agricultural activities were affected by climatic shocks such as flooding and dry spells; a reduction in access to livestock products as animals start to migrate with the onset of the dry season; the hoarding of food stocks by traders waiting for the lean season that is characterized by high food prices; and closure of the trade routes between South Sudan and Sudan which is affecting market functionality.

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

With funding from FAO, FLDA has signed Letter of Agreement on the Resilience Agriculture Project (RALP) that aims at providing the much needed knowledge and skills, quality inputs and access to technology for farmers and their groups or cooperatives to increase productivity and production through use of climate-smart agriculture (CSA) and nutrition sensitive-agriculture, improved post-harvest handlings and aggregation capacities, agro-processing and value additions and market opportunities while also enhancing adaptive capacity to climate risks. The overall target beneficiaries are 4 756 households, of which 3500 will be supported with crop and vegetable kits, 500 with small ruminants and poultry, and 750 (25 groups) for resilience building activities relating to tree planting and agro-forestry, seed production, vegetable production and crop value additions, 6 local artisans. The RALP target vulnerable households in Aweil South. With funding from WFP FLDA has a running FLA on conditional cash transfer (investments in assets, training, or communal work groups/ planning committees etc.) distribution to food insecure households in Aweil Centre. This CFA project is benefiting 1,969 household. The CFA project was based on lessons learnt, market assessment and recommendations of the implementation of similar projects in Aweil South, Aweil East and Aweil West counties by different cooperating partners (CP). These similar project were also being funded by WFP. Equally important, based on FLDA’s independent summative evaluation ( 2019-2021) it came out clear that beneficiaries and stakeholder in Aweil South preferred cash opposed to food.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Based on the sphere and protection standards the beneficiaries rights to humanitarian assistance will be observed during the whole project management cycle. The proposed project incorporates community feedback and complaint mechanisms including: complaint desks at distribution sites, anonymous and non-anonymous reporting mechanisms to a focal person at each of FLDA’s field offices, post distribution monitoring (PDM), Ad-hoc supervisory and beneficiary feedback meetings conducted by senior monitoring staff PSEA is mainstreamed across FLDA's policies and programming. The beneficiaries will be informed of SEA impacts and levels of reporting mechanisms, anonymous and non-anonymous through AAP messaging. FLDA as an organization considers SEA as gross misconduct and calls for immediate termination of staff who commit such an offence. Community based protection is an essential component of FLDA’s human security work. Human security recognizes that vulnerable people are exposed to a constant series of threats and risks and focuses on need to retain a minimum quality of life defined as freedom from fear, freedom from want and freedom to take action on one’s own behalf. FLDA acknowledges the importance of these rights; all its programs are designed to empower the poor, vulnerable and excluded to exercise their rights and acquire a voice, to emphasize the need for protection and respect and assist the most vulnerable by increasing their safety with dignity, by developing local prevention and response strategies and organizing their resources to reduce exposure to harm. For example; FLDA will go directly to the communities to deliver the vouchers and where necessary; female staff will deliver vouchers and provide community sensitization and outreach to female beneficiaries; where required, the disabled and elderly will be assisted to use the vouchers/cash. FLDA will engage with community leaders at each step and will invite women’s open participation to voice their views and concerns in case of dissatisfaction. The proposed project incorporates community feedback and complaint mechanisms, including: complaint desks at distribution sites, anonymous and non-anonymous reporting mechanisms to a focal person at each of FLDA's field offices, post distribution monitoring (PDM), Ad-hoc supervisory and beneficiary feedback meetings conducted by senior monitoring staff.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

With FLDA’s ongoing projects under FAO and WFP, in both Aweil South and Tonj North counties of NBEG and Warrap States, feedback mechanism from vulnerable beneficiaries has been tracked in several ways through formulated project management committees comprised of both Females and Males of all vulnerability. Beneficiaries free hotlines number have been stipulated at project sites with guidance of complaint help desk committees who engage with the organization staff to make sure that beneficiaries receive their feedback as soon as possible. Involvement of local authorities has also been of great importance in regard to CFM mechanism. FLDA engages with communities through the program cycle, especially through its awareness building activities, specialized food security, livelihoods and nutrition consultations, house visits and household level interactions. FLDA also conducts regular meetings with key stakeholders and wider community that are not necessarily its direct beneficiaries, informal women’s groups, farmers/livestock breeder groups, project management committees, at distribution sites for livelihoods kits and food, and through trainings. Community elders, leaders, key representatives, especially women and youth, are consulted on a regular basis for feedback on programming, quality of services and staff performance. Coordination meetings with national and international NGOs at Juba and county level permit FLDA to keep abreast of local updates, have a deeper insight into community needs, challenges and negative coping mechanisms so that these are addressed through programming and advocacy and help communities live safe and dignified lives. The project is founded on various reports and assessments, including FLDA’s food security assessments, evaluations, surveys, observations and consultations, both with men and women, FLDA identified widespread needs regarding access to food, and limited economic opportunities and livelihoods, among IDPs, returnees and vulnerable host community members. Needs identified during the baseline assessment and consultation with vulnerable population has informed this project design.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

The protection environment is volatile and not sufficiently stable or safe, especially for girls, women and children as well as people with disabilities. Safety risks are expected from the distance for distribution points that potentially expose children and women to violence and insecurity from people and wild animals. All these risks will be addressed through working with all relevant stakeholders to identify central distribution areas and ensuring that protection, safety and security measures are put in place prior to distribution. All project interventions will be inclusive for differently vulnerable populations especially women, girls, men & boys, elderly, disabled, PLW, children U5, HIV & the chronically sick staying in either IDPs, refugee, returnees or host communities. FLDA will mainstream protection by supporting integrity, dignity, and safety for all targeted populations. Women and children will be prioritized and seek counsel during the implementation process. Protection will be supported through women and youth consultations and representatives, targeting the most vulnerable, and support rights-based and participatory approaches. Awareness building exercise on protection concerns will be conducted before the needs assessment so that communities make informed decisions on location of project intervention. FLDA developed community feedback mechanism whereby protection issues will be collected and facilities, services and assistance will be used to mitigate and improve the protection environment.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Key marginalized stakeholders such as women, youth, elders, informal groups will be specifically consulted, and their awareness raised in order to hold informed consultations and bring out their concerns. Targeting criteria, beneficiary numbers, expected norms of engaging, define outputs per modality and benefits will clear. The roles and of each stakeholder will be developed during inception meeting and adhered to during project implementation, monitoring and evaluation that will enhance learning and improvement among all concerned. The project will address ‘Do no Harm’ to ensure that interventions do not contribute to intra and inter-communal tensions by affected population. The project considers specific protection needs of all population groups and the modality to address them. Rights awareness messaging will be disseminated prior to all distributions, trainings and through PMCs. Referral pathways for SGBV victims and survivors will be established and utilized for case management. Self-reported cases of SBGV will be referred to local protection partners for care and case management. A security lens will be applied to targeting of communities and households in order to mitigate any potential conflict related to program exclusion. Every FLDA office has an appointed security focal point who advises, develops, and updates local security procedures for the protection of staff and beneficiaries. Also, FLDA receive information regarding the security situation through the UNDSS and the International NGO Security Forum. FLDA will use these resources to keep its staff, beneficiaries and assets safe. FLDA utilizes community mobilizers as well as mobile teams to reach the most vulnerable where FLDA may not be able to reach. FLDA regularly engages in RRMs to reach IDPs and returnees - where static services may be inaccessible. Moreover, in all operational field sites FLDA maintains means of movement including motorbikes, donkey carts, and vehicles to reach all targeted beneficiaries.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

FLDA acknowledges that extreme weather hazards, from floods, made more frequent and intense by climate change, continue to uproot people across the targeted operational area. In addition to the health threat posed by Covid-19, the pandemic has also devastated economies, reversed development gains and increased humanitarian needs. Rising costs, persistent instability and recurring disasters have deepened vulnerabilities, leading to unprecedented levels of food insecurity. The targeted project location are prone to floods. FLDA main stream Disaster Risk Reduction/Management and natural resource management in its programming. Guided by the early warning/ early response systems, scenario based plans are developed and implemented. Established community structures (Community Based Disaster Reduction Committee) capacity to respond to disasters such as flood are strengthened. This project will address the identified climate risks and vulnerabilities by prioritizing investments that help to increase agricultural resilience, build capacity of farmers and institutions to implement climate-smart agriculture; nutrition-sensitive agriculture; improve access to drought-resistant seeds and other climate-smart technologies and climate-proofing infrastructure, strengthen and support value chain and access to market interventions and enhance resilience livelihoods. Furthermore, the project encompass interventions to do with DRR, and Natural resources management.

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

FLDA adheres to the guiding principles and approaches that underpins the Minimum Standards that are in accordance with “GBV Guiding Principles”: Some of these standards includes; As an organization, we employ community-based approach that ensures the affected populations are engaged actively as partners in developing strategies related to their protection and the provision of humanitarian assistance. This approach involves direct involvement of women, girls and other at-risk groups at all stages in the humanitarian and recovery response, to identify protection risks and solutions, and build on existing community-based protection mechanisms. FLDA ensures that survivors have the right to choose to whom they will or will not tell their story, and any information about them should only be shared with their informed consent. All actions taken should are guided by respect for the choices, wishes, rights and dignity of the survivor. The role of helpers is to facilitate recovery and provide resources to aid the survivor. FLDA ensures that the a rights-based approach that seeks to analyse and address the root causes of discrimination and inequality to ensure that everyone has the right to live with freedom and dignity, safe from violence, exploitation and abuse, in accordance with principles of human rights law. This principle is related to the survivor-centered approach that creates a supportive environment in which survivors’ rights and wishes are respected, their safety is ensured, and they are treated with dignity and respect. A survivor-centred approach is based on the following guiding principles: FLDA operational strategies/intervention are underpinned by the humanitarian principles of humanity, impartiality, independence and neutrality should underpin the implementation of the Minimum Standards, and are essential to maintaining access to affected populations and ensuring an effective humanitarian response. In our operations we ensure that a “do no harm” approach that involves taking all measures necessary to avoid exposing people to further harm as a result of the actions of humanitarian actors are mainstreamed. GBV survivors receive equal and fair treatment regardless of their age, disability, gender identity, religion, nationality, ethnicity, sexual orientation or any other characteristic. We ensure that the safety and security of survivors and their children are the primary considerations. In order to effectively be held accountable to our stakeholder, increase coverage and minimize duplications, FLDA follows the principles of Partnership.
