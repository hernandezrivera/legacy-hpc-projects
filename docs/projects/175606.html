Project Summary

The development objective of the project “Strengthening child protection among internally displaced people, refugee returnees and host communities in Terekeka, Bor South and Rubkona Counties of South Sudan” is to enhance the protection and security of vulnerable children, unaccompanied and separated children and children who have experienced violence, exploitation, and abuse. Salvation and Light Development Action (SLDA) will implement the project for a period of 12 months. The project targets Terekeka, Bor South and Rubkona Counties of South Sudan which are classified in Emergency [Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) Phase 4] acute food insecurity. Sex breakdown estimated on 54% women (13,500 beneficiaries) and 46% men (11,500 beneficiaries) since women population is higher in target populations. The total cost of the project will be USD 490,000.
The project provides support to the conflict-affected girls, boys and women who are exposed to child protection risks and gender based violence in South Sudan. The project includes providing case management and family tracing and reunification services, psychosocial support, reintegration interventions for unaccompanied and separated children (UASC), at risk and children victims of violence, and children associated with armed forces and groups (CAAFAG), monitoring of grave child rights violations, GBV interventions, and critical, frontline emergency services. The project will establish a referral mechanism for gender responsive child protection services.
The project provides timely protection response and prevention services to children from IDP, refugee returnees and host communities background who are facing protection risks and threats. Likewise, the project ensures that vulnerable children, UASC and children who have experienced violence, exploitation, and abuse have the skills, opportunities and positive coping strategies required to return and reintegrate into their communities in safety and dignity. In particular, the project provides lifesaving and critical services for vulnerable children, UASC and children who have experienced violence, exploitation, and abuse suffering from psychosocial distress, increased violence and potential neglect, abuse and exploitation as a result of the conflict.
By the end of the project SLDA will provide lifesaving critical services for vulnerable children, UASC and children suffering from psychosocial distress, increased violence and potential neglect, abuse and exploitation as a result of the conflict. Targeted children will have access to protective environment and appropriate age and gender specific activities within the Child Friendly Spaces (CFS). Additional individual support trough individual counseling, case management and caregivers parenting skills improvement will be associated to the collective activities in order to better address the identified cases of most vulnerable and at risk children.
Three main outcomes are: 1. Address the psychosocial needs of vulnerable children, UASC and children who have experienced violence, exploitation, and abuse, through psychosocial activities, including life skills, sport and awareness, creative activities, and focus group discussions, within CFS and individual counseling. 2. Mobilize effectively and meaningfully caregivers and community members around child protection needs and risks and encourage their participation to a more effective referral system, through community risk mapping, participative vulnerability assessment, awareness events and child protection campaign; hygiene and GBV sessions, and positive parenting and peer to peer support. 3. Ensure vulnerable children, UASC and children who have experienced violence, exploitation, and abuse receive proper case management, and close follow up, through the provision of services (referral for health and education) and non-food items and food, psychosocial support to caregivers.

1 - Needs

In the first half of 2020, the Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM) reported 75 instances of spontaneous population movements comprised of 145,628 internally displaced people (IDP) and 21,771 returnees across the seven states and Abyei Region. Localized conflict accounted for the vast majority of displacements (83%) which includes communal tensions including cattle raiding without a known or direct involvement of national actors. A further 11% fled floods and 7% fled armed conflict with direct involvement of national actors. According to Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO) 2021, an estimated 2.7 million people, including 2.3 million children, are at risk of violence, exploitation, abuse, and neglect and in acute need of immediate child protection in 2021. An estimated 2.102 million people faced Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. A total of 34 counties including Terekeka, Bor South and Rubkona Counties were classified in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. During December 2020 to March 2021, an estimated 1.79 million people are likely to face Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. During April to July 2021, an estimated 2.47 million are likely to be in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. Children in South Sudan continue to be at risk of many violations, stemming from the impact of the armed conflict that ravaged the country for more than 20 years. This is exposing children to high levels of violence, including gender based violence, exploitation, abuse and deprivation. It is estimated that children make up 62% of the total population of the country (HRP 2019) and lack basic social services that could contribute to their wellbeing. Above 20,000 children have been registered as unaccompanied, separated or missing since December 2013, with more than 8,600 cases still open (UNICEF). Since 2014, some 3,700 incidents of grave violations against children have been documented and verified by the UN. The Country Task Force on Monitoring and Reporting has recorded 651 incidents of recruitment and use of children which resulted in the number of 5,988 verified cases of children recruited and used in armed forces and armed groups between the first quarter of 2014 up until the second quarter of 2019, creating a high need for disarmament, demobilization and reintegration support. More than 80% of the refugees are women and children. Many refugee children are exposed to violence and traumatic events. More than 5 million children remain displaced, in need of humanitarian support, and at high risk of recruitment, abuse, exploitation, neglect, profound distress and death. 3 million children are estimated to be severely food insecure and about 1 million children suffer from psychosocial distress, with only about 266,000 currently being reached by psychosocial support interventions. More than 2 million school-aged children are out-of-school, with some 1.3 million children at risk of dropping out of education altogether (War Child Holland). The CP Sub-Cluster recognizes that all children are at risk in the entire county however the level of children’s exposure to abuse, neglect, exploitation and violence is different. Areas hosting protracted IDP caseloads, areas experiencing resurgence of inter-communal conflict resulting new displacement such as Jonglei, Warrap, Lakes, Unity, and; the greater Equatorias all have significant child protection needs. According to the Initial Rapid Needs Assessment conducted by Salvation and Light Development Action from February 01 – 07, 2021 in Terekeka, Bor South and Rubkona Counties, it has been identified that there is a growing need for returnees’ population; many of the returnee community’s children have no basic child protection services. Psychosocial distress; recruitment, family separation, child labour, sexual violence, and danger and injuries among children are commonly identified and reported child protection risks in Terekeka, Bor South and Rubkona Counties.

2 - Project activities

Above 61% of the affected population are children. There is need to urgently provide child protection services to the children in all the areas identified above otherwise continued lack of services will further worsen the protection of children and subsequently increased their suffering thereby creating generational gaps in the society. Response to child protection issues requires a multifaceted, sustainable, integrated and coordinated approach including strengthening child protection community structures. IDP, refugee returnees and host communities require much attention from the humanitarian agencies. Women, adolescents and children are at heightened risk of violence and exploitation. In particular, services and age specific programmes for adolescent boys/girls are limited, leaving this group idling and exposed to life threatening risks. The project interventions are aligned to the CP Sub-cluster priorities for the 2021. Project activities have been designed both in consultation with local communities and with the aim of reducing the impacts of the identified drivers of food insecurity in the target locations. This project intends to rehabilitate vulnerable children, UASC and children who have experienced violence, exploitation and abuse, then reintegrate them into community life, strengthening both community child protection responses and the networks supporting the children. The project provides psychosocial support (PSS) and care for the children; builds the capacity of project staff to operate efficiently and effectively; and strengthen community systems to monitor gender-based violence. The project will work closely with CP Sub-cluster and Education Cluster on the implementation of the school-based PSS interventions for school age children, and children with protection concerns identified in the target locations. A special focus will be put on gender issues ensuring most vulnerable categories needs, particularly those of adolescent girls, will be addressed with appropriate activities. Recruitment of women will be highly encouraged where possible. Peace promotion and dialogue are important components of the project and will be direct particularly focusing on adolescents role within respective communities. The project focuses on the needs of child-headed households who are at great risk of food insecurity as well as protection and health issues. The project provides small scale income generating activities, gender specific services, and psychosocial support and medical treatment for vulnerable children, UASC and children through referral to the adequate services. Training for community animators (IDP, returnees and host communities) and key SLDA staff is vital to ensure the quality of the project since protection activities are based on capacities and skills of those dealing directly with children and their caregivers. The following activities will be priorities:Activity 1: Provide community based psychosocial support activities and case management for girls, boys and caregivers in extremely vulnerable situations including through mobile teamsActivity 2: Provide comprehensive case management services and referrals to extremely vulnerable children including through cash based assistance for UASC and CAAFAGActivity 3: Community Based Child Protection (CBCP) , life-skills, capacity building and outreachesActivity 4: Reintegration services for UASC as well as children formally and informally released from armed forces and armed groupsActivity 5: Conduct behavior change messaging on child protection risksActivity 6: Conduct service mapping in target Counties to inform referral pathway establishment/review and/or strengthening existing referral pathways Activity 7: Conduct community outreach and engagement activities to disseminate key information on child protectionActivity 8: Establish and strengthen child friendly spaces to provide age specific recreational and creative activities Activity 9: Cash transfer

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

Injections of cash or the use of commodity vouchers with local traders benefits the recovery of trade and the local economy. Evidence shows that cash transfers reduce the negative coping strategies used by beneficiaries. When goods are available and accessible locally, cash or voucher-based interventions have the potential to be more rapid than bringing supply from out of country. When beneficiaries use local markets, agencies also eliminate logistical and import costs.

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

The project believes that using cash or vouchers recognises that beneficiaries are best placed to decide what their households need, and gives them the flexibility to choose according to their own diverse needs and priorities. Similarly, in allowing beneficiaries to control what goods and services their households need, cash transfers are considered by many as more dignified than receiving goods in kind, and recognise beneficiaries as active participants in providing for the welfare of their families in an emergency situation, like South Sudan. Likewise, the availability of cash gives households a sense of restored power over their immediate situation. In addition, there is evidence that receiving cash can at least temporarily empower women within the household. Access to financial structures such as bank accounts may empower beneficiaries, in particular women, in the longer-term as well. The project will support the immediate nutritional needs of vulnerable HHs through the provision of cash transfers. Using transfers, HHs will access, either through direct distribution or via local markets, sufficient food to meet daily caloric and nutritional requirements.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

This project will promote quality programming with a focus on inter-sector synergies and integrated programming to mainstream protection, gender, and accountability to affected populations. The project ensures that all social mobilization, community engagement and surveillance mechanism will be developed and implemented in conjunction with women’s groups.The project prioritizes the mitigation, response and prevention of gender based violence (GBV) and Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA). The project will train women’s groups on the requirements to promptly refer survivors of sexual exploitation and abuse for assistance through existing GBV pathways. Working with the protection cluster and other development partners, SLDA will implement Accountability to Affected People and Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse in line with the IASC Commitments. It will put in place a collective mechanism to ensure participation of affected people in planning and programming of humanitarian interventions, and to regularly monitoring of their satisfaction and priorities. SLDA will put in place reporting mechanisms and guidelines to ensure that cases of sexual exploitation and abuse are immediately reported and followed up on, through our clear guidelines for reporting. Any allegation of abuse or exploitation of an adult or child associated with a SLDA programme will lead to the immediate suspension and removal from the location of the accused individuals. The project intervention’s will incorporate protection principles and promote meaningful access, safety and dignity in provision of project resources and services. Safety and dignity, and avoid causing harm for the target beneficiaries will be the priority. The interventions will prevent and minimize unintended negative effects under this project, which can increase people's vulnerability to both physical and psychosocial risks. The project will provide meaningful access to all the beneficiaries. Women, youth and target communities will access services without discrimination. This will target Individuals and groups who may be particularly vulnerable or have difficulty accessing project resources and services especially people living with disabilities, girls and teenage mothers.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

In this response, the Community Feedback and Response Mechanism (CFRM) will be set up and made functional at community levels. The CFRM will deliver a mechanism that enables the beneficiaries and community members to provide feedback and seek responses in relation to activities of this response and other development actors in their communities, in a manner that is safe, non-threatening and accessible. The initiative will promote beneficiary accountability and feedback and response mechanism to other development actors. The project will measure the adequacy of interventions and address concerns and complaints of most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls). Access barriers could be at following level i.e. within IDP, refugee returnees and host communities and age and sex. SLDA will engage CBCPN, involve, and consult community elders to inform and design sensitization campaigns to raise awareness on child rights. The message will be tailored to age and gender appropriate needs. SLDA has strong principle of do no harm; programme staff are trained on ensuring that humanitarian interventions do not put beneficiaries at risk and does not discriminate service delivery on basis of ethnicity, and sex. Similarly, SLDA has community feedback and response mechanism to ensure that voices of targeted communities are included in the programme. The feedback is collected through farmers groups, cooperatives and VSLAs, and community elders. SLDA ensures the safety and well-being of women and children who come into contact with the organization is promoted through the promotion of a zero tolerance policy towards the abuse and exploitation of women and children during our programing. SLDA is accountable to ensuring all staff, partners and volunteers on this project receive adequate training on PSEA, child protection and safeguarding.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

The project applies community engagement approach to encourage organizational and government leaders to take on roles as facilitators, supporters, and collaborators—offering them enhanced perspectives while giving a stronger voice to tribal citizens and stakeholders. Drawing on local knowledge from diverse groups creates solutions that are both practical and effective. Local communities are creators and owners of the project and catalysts for change rather than simple beneficiaries of programming. Through community engagement, project beneficiaries play a meaningful role in the deliberations, discussions, decisions and implementation of the project activities. The project will leverage all aspects of community engagement to promote gender equality, ensuring consultation with women specifically, building women’s skills and expertise, creating spaces for leadership and decision-making, and engaging women and men in dialogue that advocate for positive social change that promotes greater equality, opportunities and respect for women and girls. The project will strengthen women’s agency and empowerment by actively supporting women’s participation in leadership positions through the project. To ensure that women’s voices and opinions are incorporated into the planning and implementation of activities, women’s free and active participation in community consultation processes will be enhanced by employing inclusive facilitation techniques, and holding meetings at times and locations that encourage women’s participation. Gender-responsive approaches will also be adopted throughout implementation such as moving trainings closer to female participants’ households, establishing shade shelters for children to safely play while mothers receive field-based training, and engaging men to understand the mutual benefit of supporting women’s agricultural skill development. The project will engage with potential existing networks, including traditional and cultural structures, government structure, schools and youth programmes, and churches and faith-based organizations. The project will give following considerations for working more inclusively and engaging different stakeholder groups:- Engage tribal leaders, community action groups, and affected populations to participate in the overall development and execution of programmatic action.- Solicit input from all affected parties to discover what is working, what is not working, and what needs to be accomplished for a successful implementation of project activities.- Engage with community and association groups as starting points for approaching and communicating with the populations targeted for engagement. - Build trust with the affected populations. - Develop a different communication approach to know the attitudes, beliefs, and behavior of the intended beneficiaries in order to craft the most effective messages to encourage community involvement.- Constructively engage with youths to widely disseminate information to others within the community.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

SLDA recognizes the right of beneficiaries and community members to give feedback and seek response from projects affecting them. Consultative meetings will held with the learners, communities and local leaders. The beneficiaries and community members will be encouraged to give feedback and seek responses from the projects affecting them. They will be sensitized right from the start of the project through kick off meetings and community consultation workshops on key deliverables including community roles and responsibilities. Community members will participate actively, transparently and without discrimination in analysis, planning, design, implementation, monitoring and evaluation of all interventions proposed. Views and feedback will be collected from the CBCPN from the start (design) of the intervention through key information interviews. The key informant interviews will conducted among the local leaders and community leaders over the period. All stakeholders will be involved in all assessments including assessment among IDP, host community and other interest groups. In essence, SLDA will approach the principle of participation from equity perspective and advocate for equal participation of different groups. Based on the cluster reporting system, the project will put in place and implement and monitoring and reporting system, progress, best practices and lessons learned will be presented in cluster meetings based on the 5Ws formats developed by the cluster.
