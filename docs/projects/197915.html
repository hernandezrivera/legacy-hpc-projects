Project Summary

Since 2016, the North West and South West regions of Cameroon have witnessed a socio-political crisis which has deteriorated overtime with violent clashes between armed forces and Non-State Armed Groups (NSAG) which have led to loss of lives and property, internal displacement, attacks on education and a growing climate of fear and incertitude. Much of this violence is targeted at local communities and, in particular, at schools, with teachers frequently facing attacks in the form of kidnapping, harassment, decapitation and killing, while children have been exposed to a number of protection risks such as harassment, exploitation, abuse and recruitment and some have been killed due to the crisis. The NSAG have adopted school boycott/disruption strategy since 2017 to apply pressure on government in the form of Monday ‘ghost-towns’ and other spontaneous calls for ‘lockdowns’, especially on commemorated national days, sometimes lasting for several days and even weeks. The school boycott strategy has had a severe impact on children’s ability to attend school, learn and retain previously learnt content. Calls for a return to normal functioning of schools from different individual, national and humanitarian actors have been challenged by other separatist groups. In addition to the threats and direct acts of violence leading to school disruptions, other factors such as social media misinformation, false alarms and panic have led to the further disruption of school activities. The ongoing violence has left some 700,000 children out of school in the Northwest and Southwest regions and over the last four years, increased attacks on schools have been recorded and over two thirds of the schools are now closed. On its part, the reaction by the international community has not been robust enough in terms of condemnation and action to ensure that the right to education is upheld. The Norwegian Refugee Council has referred to the crisis in Cameroon as one of the most neglected in the world. This research seeks to identify and provide an overview of all the converging factors leading to disruption of school activities, outline a timeline of school attacks, and provide an educational needs assessment for children of school going age in the Anglophone regions. It provides a brief background to what is now known as the Anglophone crisis, a timeline on attacks in schools and other factors including, social media misinformation, false alarms and panic, leading to further disruption of school activities. Around 7 million learners are currently affected by schools’ closure, of these an estimated 15% of children live in the NWSW region. In order to ensure education continuity and mitigate the impact of school closing, the Education Cluster is developing an Education response plan in line with National response strategy that includes measures to mitigate the impact of school closure. These measures include rapidly adapting distance education and community sensitization on COVID- 19. Plan International aligns the HPD strategies with the National response strategy, NWSW COVID 19 Readiness and Response Guidance in the NWSW, Education Cluster, COVID-19 Risk and Mitigation plan, the NWSW Education Cluster Response Annex and Plan Global Education COVID 19 Response considerations. Plan on the ground will coordinate and work closely with the WASH and Protection partners including Plan’s WASH, NFI and CPiE thematic areas, to ensure vulnerable girls and boys affected by the conflict and closure of schools and non-formal learning spaces due to COVID 19, access education and minimize on the lost time for learning.

1 - Besoins / Needs

The education needs for girls and boys in the North West (NW) and South West (SW) regions have been exacerbated during the past 6 years by armed conflict. For most of these children, the hard-gained learning progress from the conflict has been reversed as functional schools and non-formal learning spaces have been either damaged or closed. These leaves children at a major disadvantage, especially girls and children living with disabilities who are already limited by socioeconomic and cultural barriers. More so, primary and Secondary school-aged children have become more vulnerable and are further exposed to a myriad of protection risks. Over the past 6 years, there has been growing anxiety among parents and students who are not sure if they will be able to prepare well for the public examination let alone sit for it. Already instead of staying at home, adolescent girls and boys are seen contributing to increase household income in streets and market places exposing them to contamination risks and other child protection related risks (recruitment, exploitation, abuse, etc): Some resolve to negative coping mechanisms including drug use and prostitution. Both teachers from formal schools and facilitators from non-formal learning spaces have been impacted negatively as they do not have accurate information and training to manage these children within the crises contexts. All children, teachers, facilitators and communities at large require gender and age-appropriate information on the importance of education, inclusive education, health education (especially with respect to cholera which has caused multiple deaths within the regions in 2022 and COVID-19). Teachers and facilitators also require psychosocial support training to be able to deal with the current situation and manage children better within the learning context. There is further need to identify out of school children and possible temporal learning spaces which are safe, inclusive and provides protection for these children to be able to continue learning, recover on lost learning and have a chance at normalcy in their development. More so, to ensure that impact lasts even after the project ends, there is a need to support children and adolescent clubs will be established with a goal to raise awareness in their communities on specific topics including their rights, gender equality, recognizing and reporting violence, bullying, hygiene education including MHM, comprehensive sexuality education, thereby reducing their chances of being abused, exploited, trafficked, etc.Plan International aligns the HPD strategies with the National response strategy, NWSW COVID-19 Readiness and Response Guidance in the NWSW, Education Cluster, COVID-19 Risk and Mitigation Plan the NWSW Education Cluster Response Annex and Plan Global Education COVID-19 Response considerations.
