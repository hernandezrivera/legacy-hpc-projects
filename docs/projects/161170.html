Project Summary

AVSI intervention aims at addressing the urgent needs related to water access, sanitation and hygiene in the area of Ikwoto, through an intersectoral approach that involves targets typically related to food security, nutrition and health. The overall objective is to increase equitable access to safe water to meet population basic needs and reduce morbidity of the most vulnerable in severe food insecurity.
The interventions proposed, therefore, address two main urgent needs of, respectively: water accessibility, of both the population and the communities of refugees returning to South Sudan, through the drilling and installation and rehabilitation of boreholes in target areas in high need due to the hydrological stress conditions and/or with increased water demand caused by the returned South Sudanese refugees. Second, prevention to water-related disease outbreaks such as cholera and Ebola through awareness campaigns at multiple levels (schools, health institutions and community). On the other hand, it aims at targeting a longer-term change towards good hygiene and sanitation practices in order to ultimately interrupt the circle of diarrheal diseases linked with malnutrition, to be achieved through the distribution and construction of hygiene and sanitation equipment and facilities (hygiene kits, hand washing, chlorine, soap, latrines). Specifically, hygiene kits are to be provided to screened in SAM children and their mothers, or caretakers, through the supervision of the already existing network of CNV (Community Nutrition Volunteers) and Nutrition assistants active in 14 OTP/SC supported by AVSI within the County of Ikwoto. Importantly, the construction of latrines for rural house-holds is associated with the formation of a group of skilled laborers, mainly made by youth, for the installation of rural latrines. Once formed, the group will construct latrines at house hold level at subsidized price through the provision of conditional vouchers to identified beneficiaries. The hardware component is necessarily combined with awareness activities as campaigns at village level, the creation of hygiene clubs at school level and institutionalization of community groups, and the strengthening and of existing Community Water Community Management Committee (CWMC) or water bodies at the County or Payam and Boma level.
Both type of interventions are characterized by the high involvement of local institutions and practitioners.
Importantly, it is highlighted how in this approach, that involves local institutions as well as promotes key player for community good practices, AVSI relies on existing social structures, focusing on strengthening them in order to introduce elements of innovation that are welcomed by the communities through constant and persistent actions of sensitization (through field-based staff and constant visit to beneficiaries and awareness campaigns). Further, the intervention wants to support the sensitization of communities on SGBV issues specifically related to WASH context with SGBV participatory risk and assessment exercise at water access and sanitation sites.

1 - Needs

According to the SSD WASH Sector Analysis 2018, WASH coverage is still far below the regional average. In fact, AVSI’s multisector survey in Ikwoto found that 34% of HHs must walk more than 1 hour to access a functioning borehole forcing 6% of HHs to use surface water (ponds) as drinking water when possible. Moreover, according to the SSEC national Assessment in 2018, 68% of schools lacked proper WASH facilities in SSD. With AVSI’s long term commitment to education and consequent routine gathering of information, AVSI also found that in some of the areas in which it implements projects, the average number of pupils per latrine is 150, well above the SPHERE standard. From the AVSI multisector survey, 38% of HHs had soap at home while only 14% of HHs used latrines to defecate. Poor WASH leads to poor nutrition, in fact, according to AVSI’s June 2019 SMART Survey, the Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) rate for the former County of Ikwoto was 9.2% (6.6 – 12.8 95% C.I.), and the severe acute malnutrition (SAM) rate was 2.0% (1.0 – 4.0 95% C.I.). GAM indicates a poor nutrition situation while SAM is classified as critical according to WHO classification. 26.9% (22.4-31.9 95% C.I.) of children were found to be underweight while 4.3% (2.7-6.6 95% C.I.) were severely underweight. The rate of chronic malnutrition in Ikwoto is also worrying with a level of stunting considered "serious" at 31.9%. In fact in EES, Ikwoto in particular, chronic malnutrition is the highest compared to the entire country, highlighting the need to prioritize stunting prevention interventions. The high rates of malnutrition in SSD are attributed to several factors including high levels of food insecurity, poor infant and young child feeding practices as well as high morbidity due to a weak health system in the country. Poor quality and diversity of food (Minimum Acceptable Diet: 7%, Minimum dietary diversity: 15%) and high prevalence of diseases (up to 40%). According to IPC key message of August 2019, in EES, an estimated 560,000 people (52.6% of the State population) were facing Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity in August. Ikwoto was classified to be in Crisis. The outcomes were driven by low cereal stock and high food prices, the situation was also further worsened by reduced physical access to markets and poor purchasing power. According to projections, food security should improve within EES during the typical harvest season, Sep-Dec 2019.This reduction is attributed to the seasonal availability of harvests and livestock products. However, in some locations, particularly the predominantly pastoralist areas of Ikwoto (Lomohidang South Payam), incidents of cattle raiding are likely to contribute to household vulnerability to food insecurity. The overall improvement is believed to be a short lived relief for some of the population as figures are likely to return to around 50% again during the post-harvest season of Jan-Apr 2020 due to decline in harvested grains where households start to rely more on markets for food, but face continued high prices. The onset of the lean season will be characterized by depleted stocks and reduced access to livestock products and wild foods. Currently Ikwoto has 27 functional health facilities with only 4 PHCCs capable of providing comprehensive integrate maternal child health care, most of the facilities have got inadequate skilled personnel and basic equipment’s hence poor health care system. Nutrition services implemented currently are targeting treatment of acute malnutrition which is not comprehensive and with minimum integration. The Nutrition sites are not adequate and not easily accessible to majority in hard to reach settlement. In addition, there is only one stabilization center in the county which is not supported and lacks most of the vital required facilities and equipment.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

One objective of AVSI’s projects in EES intends to reinforce the resilience of displacement-affected people by improving the nutrition, food security and education of returnees from Uganda. AVSI has recently conducted two surveys, one with refugees in Uganda and another in South Sudan, to assess the needs of displacement-affected people, including their perspective on repatriation. In 2019 AVSI launched a project, financed by the Italian Agency to Cooperation and Development (AICS), specifically targeting displacement-affected people. It must be mentioned, in fact, how the area of Torit, being an area bordering Uganda, experiences a high migratory flows of refugee families in between Uganda and South Sudan. The targeted rehabilitations, in fact, target places and villages highly exposes to these migratory flows.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

AVSI, given its steady presence in EES since 2005, has had the opportunity to establish over the years a deep relationship with local authorities, both at the county and at the state level. AVSI foresees the presence of staff that support local authorities in deﬁning needs, implementing intervention strategies and putting into action planned activities. AVSI has indeed been working in close collaboration with the County WASH Departments in implementation, adding value to its interventions with a signiﬁcant component of technical skills, knowledge and equipment capacity building of County WASH Department and technicians, schools and health facilities personnel. AVSI trained the technicians in 2018, and continues building on their capacities in 2019, intending to proceed with the 2020 project. Furthermore, in 2019 AVSI formed technicians on the data collection of water bio-chemical quality testing, given the complete absence of water quality data in the area. Given the positive response and high interest from the local authorities in conducting this exercise, due to the current complete absence of data on the topic, it is proposed to expand and continue the exercise. In addition, also key members of communities are directly involved in the activity implementation, with a speciﬁc attention to gender component. Selected women in CWMC are formed and sensitized on Participatory Hygiene and Sanitation Transformation (PHAST) and Child Hygiene and Sanitation Transformation (CHAST) tools during a series of workshops and constant support of AVSI-long running internally funded program, to then be encouraged to play a key role in community-based actions, as per community awareness on transmission routes and sensitizations towards good practices, ultimately leading to women playing an active role in societal change.

The identified activities are part of the priorities mentioned in the main national planning documents, such as the National Development Strategy 2018–2021, the National Gender Policy 2013 and in particular the South Sudan Comprehensive Agriculture Master Plan 2015–2040.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

The conditions of protection are far from stable, let alone safe. In Ikwoto County, AVSI’s multisector survey found that around 15% of children and adolescents are reportedly subject to repeated physical abuses and harsh punishments, 8% were discriminated against, and 7% sexually abused. Moreover, mental distress is also quite common, with almost half of the population seeking the repeated intervention of some healer (whose qualiﬁcations are diﬃcult to assess but presumably inadequate). 34% of women and girls report GBV such as physical assault (24%), rape (4%) and forced marriage (19%). These ﬁgures are most likely much lower than reality due to underreporting. In the face of such threats, leveraging on the expertise gained in relation to its projects, AVSI promotes the development of capacity in the ﬁeld of protection for all its project oﬃcers, actively seeking the inclusion of women in all its interventions, including with local civil associations of women. AVSI implements projects that integrate education components on protection, as a means to provide protection especially to young children and women. Furthermore, specifically to the proposed WASH activities, the improved access to water sources has in itself an indirect result impacting the mentioned categories. Thea it, provided that women and young children are the categories assigned for water collection, hence exposed to violence during the undertaking of such duty, providing accessible water sources and reducing the walking distance will inevitably have positive effects on their exposition to risk. Furthermore, the planned GBV risk analysis is an activity explicitly targeting protection issue.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

The sustainability of the intervention is ensured through a number of strategies. First, by the participatory approach engaging with local authorities and societal structures, enabled by the long-standing relations of AVSI with the local stakeholders and partners, this specifically includes the strengthening and training of governing bodies (CWMC) over water structures and management, community meeting for participatory planning and implementation as an assumed approach, finally the strengthening of WASH department at County level through capacity building. Secondly, the engagement of cash based interventions (CBI) as conditional vouchers for the construction of rural latrines and promotion of safe-water storage facilities at house hold level are designed to foster ownership over proposed innovation of more diﬃcult cultural acceptance, as per latrine and their usage. Third, the focus on cost recovery for WASH structural interventions on water accessibility, with the aim of creating in communities the consent on payment for safe water provision service to enable constant monitoring and future repairs. Furthermore, the planned CVM analysis aims at an in depth-study of the willing to pay of the communities for which water services, a key information to plan for sustainable solutions and ensure sustainability of intervention –for future repair of water systems and technicians. Finally, it is fundamental to mention how the proposed activities are integrated with other running projects, increasing their potential impact and longterm duration continuity of intervention and integration within AVSI programs. All the structures resulted from the project will be handed over to the assigned offices, including the drilled and/or rehabilitated boreholes.

8.18 - Please indicate how the risks selected above will be mitigated.

Firstly, as is often reported from communities, humanitarian assistance is at times channeled to more organized communities, leaving other communities (even more vulnerable populations) less assisted. To minimize this, AVSI always attempts to cover large as well as small communities in both central and more peripheral locations. For the second risk, rather than lack of awareness of referral services, there is more a problem of lack of access to psychological support to traumatized children and victims of GBV. AVSI focuses on prevention and mitigation by providing safe spaces to children, promoting female education and gender inclusion in general. Lastly, the delivery of humanitarian assistance may always spark intercommunity conflict over access to resources. To mitigate this risk, AVSI is always transparent in its selection criteria and strives to implement activities that have sustainable and long-lasting benefits for the entire community.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

AVSI has in place a tested system of yearly multi-sector surveys that through the employment of more than 400 questions (including question on GBV, hazards, protection, disabilities, education, gender inclusion, etc.), a large number of interviews (more than 400 HHs) capable of providing detailed reference at the subcounty (payam) level, an analysis centred on specific groups (single mothers, families with members with disabilities, poor farmers, etc) and the participatory discussion of such findings with the staff and representatives of the communities ensures not only that projects are designed on the base of the specific needs of the different groups of the population, but also that such knowledge is interiorized by AVSI staff and becomes the base of an informed conversation and a common understanding with local communities, ensuring feedback from the most vulnerable within the community. In the last 12 months AVSI has produced 5 of such surveys which, along with other researches aimed at monitoring the results of AVSI’s projects, are accessible on the “Assessment” section of the Humanitarian Response’s website. The questionnaire used for the Multi-Sector Survey can be accessed at this webpage https://ee.humanitarianresponse.info/x/#Yztl. The most recent example of one of AVSI’s feedback mechanism happened in the Lake States in August where AVSI staff validated the finding of two researches on challenges faced by Persons with Disabilities (PwD) and on Gender Based Violence. The 3-day validation meeting was attended by key stakeholders including PwD. AVSI ensured that at least half of the 50 attendees were females in positions of authority at the institutional or community level.
