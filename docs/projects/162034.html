Project Summary

Tdh aims at targeting both host community and Rohingyas living in camp 27 to ensure a safer environment, through the provision of a full WASH package for both areas of intervention.
Over one and a half years after the largest and fastest intake of Rohingya refugees into Bangladesh, the situation in the Kutupalong megacamp in Cox’s Bazar has stabilised, but the Rohingya are still extremely vulnerable. Tdh’s WASH response in the megacamp began in November 2017, and focused on addressing the most essential and basic needs of the population through the construction and rehabilitation of facilities and infrastructures (latrines, tube wells, washing cubicles, waste disposal pits, etc.), the distribution of hygiene kits and mass hygiene promotion activities. Currently in a turning point of the crisis and moving towards a post-emergency situation, Tdh’s strategy encompasses the three objectives outlined in the 2019 Joint Response Plan – deliver protection, provide life-saving assistance and foster social cohesion – as well as lessons learned from past projects to work towards making a more sustainable and deeper impact in the camp.
As identified at sector level and by Tdh’s teams in the field, the issues at stake now revolve around an effective and appropriate use of the existing facilities through ongoing maintenance and rehabilitation, together with reinforcing community mobilization and participation in WASH activities. The community will be consulted at all stages of the intervention to reinforce their sense of ownership, to tackle specific vulnerabilities and needs (e.g. women, youth, children, elderly, people with disabilities, etc.) and improve the quality of the access to WASH facilities. The inclusion of the specific needs is deemed necessary, as all beneficiaries are not exposed to the same level of vulnerability in the camp, and as social and cultural factors need to be considered. For example, an adequate gender-responsive intervention is essential, as the role of women in sanitation and water supply is central and requires special care: Tdh is now implementing a specific Gender in WASH approach as a core tenet in all WASH projects.
The approach aims at engaging the targeted community at early stages of the implementation of project, to emphasise active participation and to sensitise the community on its responsibilities in the improvements of sanitary conditions and the overall access to the services in the camps.

Needs

As per UNDP’s joint assessment released in July 20191, in WASH, the assessed population in Balukhali and Teknaf – directly affected by the influx - reported experiencing declining water resources. In addition, with an approximate 10,000 tons of additional solid waste produced per month, waste management appears as a health, hygiene and sanitation priority in the camps, settlements and host communities. Furthermore, in light of the recent outburst of incidents between host communities and Rohingyas, resulting from the underlying tensions between the two communities, it appears as a key priority to shift towards comprehensive and integrated approaches targeting both groups. Indeed, in parallel of a degradation of infrastructures and roads, an increase of the prices combined to a collapse of the wages, host community members mentioned their concern about their own safety and security. A majority of the population assessed by UNDP in Balukhali and Teknaf reported feeling directly impacted by the crisis, growing anxiety, all while expressing sympathy for the Rohingyas. Then, combined approaches for vulnerable Bangladeshis and Rohingyas is a matter of social cohesion on the short term, and a milestone for building sustainable peace on a longer term. Tdh, in August 2019, launched its own multi-sector assessment in host communities in villages directly impacted by the refugee crisis, through consultation of the targeted population and in-depth observation. In this frame, Tdh assessed the four villages of Whykhong, Nhilla, Sabrang and Teknaf, to better understand the gaps and needs in these areas, poorly reached with humanitarian aid. Among the key findings were a critical lack of access to safe and constantly available water for vulnerable families in the area, with only 42% of respondents that reported having access to water constantly. As for sanitation, 57% of latrines are reported in poor condition and 34% of the latrines are unfunctional, while 8% of the population still practice open defecation. Finally, needs in waste management, hygiene promotion, soap distributions and capacity building appear as crucial to be met to enhance the general living conditions of the villages assessed. Furthermore, Tdh, as a lead agency in children aid, assessed crucial needs in schools present in the area, especially in terms of facilities. In camp 27, as reported in the Tdh RASTA, 94% of the respondents listed drinking water as one of the top 4 priority needs and only 7.8% said they had access to sufficient drinking water. Government officials cited the high salinity of the water supply as one of the reasons for urgent attention to be given to water infrastructure, and health officials listed watery diarrhoea as one of the main concerns. Furthermore, Bangladesh is prone to natural disasters, especially flooding during the monsoon and cyclone season that can impact access to services, cause epidemics and additional incidents such as landslides and fires. Seasonality affects safe and dignified access to services of populations and needs to be considered while designing humanitarian response.Also, 73% of Rohingyas and 56% of the host community responded that the latrines they could access were either dirty and in a state of disrepair but operational or not operational at all.Access to hand-washing facilities remains a challenge, as 80% of the Rohingya assessed reported difficulties to access soaps.Secondary data reported that current conditions are threatening the health and wellbeing of community members and are exacerbating related safety concerns. In all of the Rohingya settings, women and girls expressed concerns with using WASH facilities, especially latrines and bathing places: latrines are not gender-segregated, lack of light and open defecation at night is unsafe. In terms of Menstrual Hygiene Management specific difficulties mentioned by women in FGDs in both communities included insufficient water, lack of area, etc.
