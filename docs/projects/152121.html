Project Summary

Since the beginning of 2015, the north-east of Nigeria has witnessed an increase in violence perpetrated by Non-State Armed Groups (NSAG), causing a major humanitarian crisis. The intensification of attacks has resulted in prolonged insecurity, exacerbating the plight of vulnerable civilians and triggering waves of forced displacement as well as violation of the human rights resulting in forms of Gender Based Violence (GBV). More than 1.9 million individuals have been displaced in Adamawa, Borno, and Yobe State. Despite the fact that some displaced individuals have started to return home after relative security has been restored and more areas are now accessible to humanitarian actors, the situation on the ground remains dire and some remain in inaccessible areas.
There is an urgent need to expand the provision of integrated basic Primary Health Care (PHC) services in north-east Nigeria especially in Borno state, to prevent further deterioration of the health status of affected populations. Health services also need to be made available and accessible at the different levels of care, supported by a well-functioning referral mechanism, at and between the community, primary and secondary/higher care levels. While the Ministry of Health has made efforts in integrating the mental health through primary health care, there is a need to further this approach to ensure increased access to mental health services.
Through this twelve-month project, IOM will support provision of health services and support national coordination in MHPSS in North East Nigeria, as well as capacity building to health actors.

Needs

The crisis in North-East Nigeria remains one of the most severe in the world. In the three worst‐affected states of Borno, Adamawa and Yobe, the insurgency has displaced 1.9 million people. Over eighty per cent of IDPs are in Borno State, the epicenter of the crisis, and over sixty percent are living among host communities while other IDPs are living in formal and informal camps, collective settlement centres, and transitional centres and in host communities. The insurgency has led to a meltdown of socioeconomic and development structures, the destruction of health facilities, security, dysfunctional legal and justice systems amidst increasing safety and security concerns. Although ensuring reliability of health data is a challenge, maternal mortality ratio in Borno is as high as 2,000 per 100,000 live births (national average of 576 / 100,000 live births) while under five mortality is 192/ 100,000 (the national average of 157). Health facilities were targeted during the conflict, and as a result, it has been estimated that 60% of health facilities have been damaged or destroyed. The disruption in supply chain of medicines and medical supplies has compounded the problem. The dire living conditions of these IDPs together with the often poor water and sanitation conditions contribute to increased health risks and higher vulnerability to the spread of outbreak prone communicable diseases, such as cholera. IDP returnees in newly accessible areas or IDPs living in “Satellite camps” have reportedly limited or even no access to health care services. Millions of people in Nigeria’s northeast need assistance to ensure their basic survival. An estimated 3.9 million people are food insecure, and more than 400,000 children suffer from severe acute malnutrition. Only 30 per cent of health facilities are functional in Borno State, where malaria is endemic and cholera and other diseases affect the population regularly, often in life-threatening ways. Public services and the institutions that provide them have collapsed in areas where conflict has raged. Nearly half a million homes and 700 public buildings have been destroyed by the conflict, along with 1,200 schools, nearly 800 health facilities, and 1,600 water supply sources. In Borno State, the vast majority of public servants have not yet returned to conflict-affected areas, in part because they remain inhospitable.There is an urgent need to expand the provision of integrated basic Primary Health Care (PHC) services, to prevent further deterioration of the health status of affected populations. Health services also need to be made available and accessible at the different levels of care, supported by a well-functioning referral mechanism, at and between the community, primary and secondary/higher care levels. While the Ministry of Health has made efforts in integrating the mental health through primary health care, there is a need to further this approach to ensure increased access to mental health services.The 2018 cholera outbreak across the BAY states has been particularly deadly. At least 18 LGAs have been affected with 4,250 cases reported, including 112 associated deaths, with women and children disproportionally affected. This includes 33 per cent of cases with 24 per cent associated deaths in Borno State; 48 per cent with 28 per cent associated deaths in Adamawa State and the remaining in Yobe State.

Activities

Activity 1: Establish three Mobile Health Teams providing an integrated standard PHC service package, including referrals to secondary care. Activity 2: Identify and train local community volunteers on health promotion and community based health interventions (i.e. Middle Upper Arm Circumference nutrition screening, defaulter tracing and distribution of long lasting insecticide treated mosquito nets) Activity 3: Support the national disease surveillance system and contribute to control of disease outbreaks and other public health events; Activity 4: Refurbishment and equipping of 10 selected public health facilities in order to resume their function to provide integrated primary health services; Activity 5: Conduct training for PHC workers with the use of the WHO Mental Health Gap Action Programme Intervention Guide (mhGAP IG) and PFA using already trained Trainers; Activity 6: Strengthen referral and provision of specialized Mental Health services at tertiary and secondary health facilities (e.g. federal neuropsychiatric hospital); Activity 7. Ensure mainstreaming of protection, gender and MHPSS approach
