Project Summary

In 2019, NRC’s overall objective in Information, Counselling and Legal Assistance (ICLA) programming is that Iraqis affected by displacement are able to claim and exercise their rights vital for achieving durable solutions. To achieve this, NRC has two primary objectives: that target groups 1) claim and exercise HLP rights; and 2) possess legal identity, including civil documentation. These objectives will be achieved through a multiplicity of ICLA activities, including the delivery of information sessions, provision of legal assistance, advocacy and training on issues such as Housing, Land and Property (HLP) and Conflict Dispute Resolution (CDR). NRC's ICLA interventions are consistently coordinated and integrated with other sectors and departments, including Camp Management, Education and Shelter.

Needs

An ongoing protection need faced by IDPs and IDP-returnees in Iraq is a lack of legal identity, including civil documentation. The ‘golden square’ of civil documentation in Iraq is considered to be national ID, civil ID, residency card and public distribution service card. Documents recording key civil life events, such as birth, marriage, death and missing persons’ certificates are also critical for accessing a range of related rights. Displaced adults and children without these cannot access healthcare, the job market, government pensions or enrol in school, finding their right to freedom of movement severely restricted. Families who lived under IS control lack valid documents (REACH MCNA, Sept 2018). Some possess IS-issued documentation, which is not considered valid by the GoI, while others lost documents as they fled IS-controlled areas or when IS and anti-IS forces routinely confiscated documents (Amnesty International, 2018). These concerns are pertinent for female headed-households (HH) with alleged or perceived affiliation with IS. Many children born in Mosul, Hawija or Anbar either hold IS-issued birth certificates, or due to fear of retribution for holding those, have no documentation. Despite Iraqi law providing that any child born to either an Iraqi father or mother has Iraqi nationality, the reality in practise differs. In the absence of a death certificate or the filing of a missing person’s report for one or both parents, the names of both parents must be recorded. Thus, where a child is born to a survivor of rape and/or to a father allegedly affiliated with IS, the mother would not provide his name due to the high risk of violence and discrimination. Mothers have expressed concerns that such children would be excluded from society, at high risk of illiteracy, de facto statelessness and joblessness (Amnesty International, 2018). Without addressing these barriers, Iraq risks repeating familiar cycles of exclusion and revenge. Families with alleged or perceived affiliation with IS face more barriers than many and are more likely to lack essential documents, even where they originally once possessed them.Damage and destruction of property during the conflict has contributed to displacement, and prevents some returns. In household assessments, 83% of remainees surveyed report that the main Housing, Land and Property (HLP) reason they cannot return to their place of origin is because their house was destroyed (HLP SC Dashboard, Aug 2018). In another assessment, when weighed against other considerations including security, having a damaged or destroyed house was the main reason for 45% of IDPs not intending to return within the next year (REACH, Movement intentions, Sept 2018). In some cases, property damage contributes to secondary displacement, with 16% of recently surveyed returnees considering further displacement stating that the main reason is because their property is destroyed, damaged, looted or occupied (REACH, Movement intentions, HNO, Sept 2018). Compensation claims for damaged property are bureaucratic, involving local police, muhktars, National Security, as well as local and national compensation committees, and are difficult for women-headed HH. Due in part to the mass destruction of property, there is a shortage of affordable and adequate housing in areas of return, causing a barrier to return, particularly when combined with livelihood concerns. Monitoring indicates that for 11% of people still in displacement the main HLP-related reason they are not returning is because they cannot afford rent due to lack of livelihoods (HLP SC Dashboard, Aug 2018). This is compounded by insecure tenancy arrangements; price increases due to the lack of housing; and fear of or actual threats of eviction (HLP SC Dashboard, Aug 2018). In September 2018 15.5 % of IDPs out of camp in Haditha, 16% of returnees in Ru’ua, 18.4% of IDPs out of camp in Mosul, and 30.4% of IDPs out of camp in Mosul reported being at risk of eviction (REACH, MCNA, Sept 2018). 32% of IDPs in a recent survey reported that they do not intend to return home due to HLP damage/destruction, secondary occupation or lack of proof of ownership. In parts of Iraq, families with members allegedly or perceived to be associated with IS experience their property seized, occupied or destroyed, sometimes to prevent returns (REACH, HNO analysis, Sept 2018). Occupation of a less sensitive short-term nature has also been relatively common, sometimes resulting in disputes dealt with by customary actors. Of the 3% of people who reported continued displacement because their house was occupied, 57% reported occupation by armed actors, while 25% said other IDPs or tribes (HLP SC Dashboard, Aug 2018). Matters are complicated by IS destruction of property records and land registries. IDPs in informal settlements established on state land face forced eviction, with examples in Salah al Din, Baghdad, Anbar and Ninewa (UNHCR protection updates, 2018).

Activities

Considered a “lifeline” in Iraq, possession of legal identity, including civil documentation is critical for accessing a wide range of services and rights. NRC’s Information, Counselling and Legal Assistance programme (ICLA) will continue to support access to key Iraqi documents through the provision of information/rights awareness sessions, legal counselling and direct legal assistance. Identification and service provision to out of camp populations will be assisted through collaboration with NRC’s community centres, which hold regular information and counselling sessions. Where CM mobile site management is being carried out in rural areas, we will work closely to identify target groups. NRC deliver information and counselling through joint sessions with Education colleagues for parents, teachers, particularly focusing on the importance of documentation for enrolment in formal education systems. Information will be disseminated through social media, via animated videos to be developed in 2019. This will accompany the existing information provision through group and mobile outreach sessions, local language leaflets and hotline operation. As HLP assessments are currently primarily done through MCNA and other generalist tools, HLP-specific assessments will also be carried out. Following the research on undocumented children and female-headed households, NRC will consider how best to programme and advocate for access to these documents for families perceived or allegedly affiliated with IS. These are challenging cases, to which there is no clear solution at present given the sensitivities and involvement of security personnel. Any proposed programming on this will be preceded by risk and protection analyses. For life-saving medical treatment for displaced people only possible outside the country, NRC will begin providing legal assistance to obtain a passport through referrals from medical actors such as MSF, managed at a national level. Assessments show that 18,000 respondents said that ownership documents for their property were never issued, and another 8,000 had documents confiscated, lost or expired (HLP SC Dashboard, Aug 2018). However, less than 1% of respondents said that not having ownership documentation was the main HLP-related reason they cannot return to their original home (HLP SC, Aug 2018). This can affect access to shelter interventions and compensation. For these, proof of ownership and other forms of HLP due diligence is important for not doing harm, not increasing the risk of conflict, and for increased security of tenure, all of which contribute to durable solutions. NRC will continue to include ICLA on HLP documentation issues, including on the importance of written tenancy documents and proof of ownership to reduce the risks of eviction. The relevant documentation will depend on the type of Shelter intervention and what is feasible, based on a policy of ‘secure enough’ and will include work with informal customary actors and/or community-based tenure mapping where relevant. NRC will provide ICLA for property compensation, ideally with a local NGO partner – including for Shelter beneficiaries before rehabilitation work takes place and in collaboration with advocacy. NRC will explore ways for this to be an entry point for increasing women’s security of tenure. NRC will carry out ICLA through the formal system on inheritance to women and men, emphasising the rights enshrined in Shari’ah and state for women to inherit property. NRC will endeavour to ensure that women who are entitled are included on the formal deeds, and will not process claims where such women are excluded. This is likely to give rise to some sensitivities, but not as much as negotiations or involvement with inheritance disputes, given the strong entrenched cultural norms on women’s ownership of property in Iraq. Iraq’s conflict has caused or aggravated a range of complex and sensitive HLP disputes, including in relation to occupation and ownership. Some of these are beyond the current capacity and mandate of ICLA staff to resolve safely, particularly relating to families with perceived IS affiliation, longstanding geo-political/ethnic conflicts, or secondary occupation by armed forces. Based on NRC’s collaborative dispute resolution (CDR) strategy/policy, we will continue to provide training on HLP, women’s HLP and CDR to duty bearers. The target group for this intervention will be duty bearers and service providers who have an impact on protecting displaced people HLP’s rights. This includes customary justice actors who are involved in HLP decision making but may include government officials, mediators and local NGOs. We will carry out legal assistance (using CDR methodologies such as mediation and negotiation) on a sub set of general property disputes, including intra-community short-term secondary occupation. Where relevant, NRC will carry out the above activities in close collaboration with local partner, Mercy Hands.

Protection Risk Analysis

While the proposed project activities all explicitly aim to protect beneficiary rights, there are nonetheless other types of protection risks that may arise out of the activity implementation that must be considered and incorporated into the project design. Four related protection risks have been identified in the needs and response section above, consideration of which has indeed already shaped the response strategy and scope of proposed activities. In short, these are: 1) risk to civil documentation beneficiaries related to perceived IS affiliation 2) risk that women will be excluded from HLP processes/rights and their tenure of security is not strengthened due to prevailing gender power dynamics and practices 3) risks of threats of or actual GBV for women in relation to claiming or exercising HLP rights that, to some degree, are not accepted in local communities due to uneven power dynamics and socio-cultural reasons 4) risks to project staff and less powerful beneficiaries if very sensitive HLP disputes, are tackled. Examples of such types of disputes include those related to armed actor illegal/secondary occupation of property, property disputes or violations carried out by tribal actors to punish families perceived to be affiliated with IS and prevent their return; secondary occupation and/or ownership disputes inflamed or accentuated in the recent conflict, but with their origins in ethnic, religious and/or broader historic geopolitical tensions.

Mitigation Measures

Mitigation measures against these possible risks have already been incorporated into the project design and scope of proposed activities. In terms of civil documentation beneficiaries and risks connected with IS affiliation, NRC’s policy of not asking outright about affiliation is a first key measure. While NRC does plan to collect some data related to the challenges connected with perceived affiliation in 2019 in order to have an evidence base for advocacy, this will only be collected where beneficiaries mention (without our prompting) possible affiliation or other related pre-decided proxy indicators (such as a female mentioning their husband was taken to prison during the conflict and they have not seen him again). This will be collected, not through NRC’s ordinary ICLA database, but through password-protected excel sheets with minimal information (no case details) which will only be collated together by ICLA project managers. In addition, NRC will ensure that our counselling and legal assistance activities are carried out in private spaces to ensure confidentiality. The current project design takes into account the risk that women will be excluded from HLP rights, or conversely put at risk of GBV for exercising HLP rights that are counter to the dominant socio-cultural practise in some areas of Iraq. In short, NRC will test different approaches to the former and speaking with target groups in different areas as to where the balance between these two interrelated protection risks. From prior experience and research, we know that in some places (often urban areas) of Iraq, we can push further to increase women’s tenure security – such as being included on tenancy or possibly even ownership documents – particularly when we have leverage such as rehabilitation into shelters. However, in other types of cases, such as disputes over women’s property through inheritance or divorce, we know that if we were to take on in the formal state system or encourage women to go through these processes, the risks would be very high for them. Thus, at this stage we will not be engaging in the latter. We will however, try to increase awareness and acceptance of women’s HLP rights through training and capacity building of formal and informal duty bearers, including by drawing on locally legitimate sources of law such as Iraqi state law and Shari’a principles. In terms of the risk to staff and beneficiaries in very sensitive, complicated HLP disputes, we will be limited our interaction. There is little prospect that ICLA staff – at this stage – will be able to solve this level of dispute with either very asymmetric power dynamics, historic geopolitics or arms involved. Thus, our CDR interventions will be not include these type of disputes, but rather focus on intra-community, recent displacement-related conflicts.

How is your project accountable to affected population

NRC is committed to setting up systematic and effective mechanisms to collect beneficiary and non-beneficiary feedback, and to provide on-going feedback to NRC during its ICLA projects. NRC takes guidance from the Core Humanitarian Standards (CHS) definition of Accountability and understands Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) as a process of using power responsibly, taking account of, and being held accountable by the people we seek to assist. Specifically for ICLA, our target group are provided the opportunity to participate and influence project design in some new areas through participatory assessment and response. This recently took place for NRC’s UDOC and ICLA programme in Sinjar (Ninewa). Our project design is also informed by qualitative research that draws on focus group discussions and in-depth interviews with affected populations. For example, our recently launched ‘Reconstructing Justice in Iraq’ report used these methodologies, and will inform our HLP programming going forward. Similarly, ongoing research on undocumented children and women’s headed households, as well as our work on women’s HLP in Iraq both included a qualitative component that analysed input from these vulnerable populations and is designed to inform programming. As with other NRC projects, a complaint and response mechanism will ensure that beneficiaries and communities have the opportunity to register feedback and any complaints. This mechanism includes complaints and suggestion boxes at static project sites, which for ICLA is primarily at UDOC community centres and inside our offices. The mechanisms also includes a dedicated complaint and feedback hotlines, email, and face to face sessions between staff and beneficiaries. NRC uses this feedback to resolve any issues that may arise. This occurs through our M & E and accountability team compiling and receiving the feedback. When they come across trends that relate to ICLA, or issues with ICLA services, we will discuss these together and consider adjusting programming, or any remedial action that needs to be taken. People served by NRC staff will be informed on NRC’s Code of Conduct and how to report any breaches, employing the community members and beneficiaries as part of the accountability system.
