Project Summary

Mercy Corps has been implementing Child Protection activities and interventions since the onset of the crisis in 2013. Mercy Corps works with other partners implementing child protection by building clear and precise referral pathways to enable vulnerable children to have access to the services such as nutrition centers, psychosocial support centers, case management, child-friendly spaces, and primary health units.
This project proposes to protect children from all forms of violence (including abuse, neglect, and GBV), raise awareness at the household and community level, strengthen preventive and response measures (including parenting and caregiver training, teacher training and school capacity building, provision of adolescent and youth life skills courses, and functioning and effective community based measures such as community mobilizers, help desks, and reporting and referral mechanisms), and establish and expand child friendly spaces (specifically to increase enrollment and participation amongst girls and women, children, adolescents, and youth living with disabilities, and to incorporate the involvement of community members invested in expanding protection of all children).
In Rubkona, strengthening and rehabilitating Child Friendly Spaces (CFS) and Youth Centers (YC) will provide static resources for community members to house help desks and also provide focused and non-focused MHPSS activities for children, adolescents, and youth. Mercy Corps has been present in the county for Child Protection implementation and awareness raising for over three years, and currently supports the operation of two YC in Bentiu and Rubkona towns. Two previously-supported CFS (one in the Bentiu PoC and a second in the small town of Dingding) have been closed due to a lack of funding, but are needed in the communities in order to offer the same supports to expand and enhance Mercy Corps’ reach in the county.
In Panyijiar, flooding has displaced many families who sought refuge in schools, churches, and higher ground areas in 2020. The issues of child abuses have been reported among IDP families, specifically congested areas. Mercy Corps proposes to mitigate these risks through local community leaders to support the enforcement of local laws to protect children, especially vulnerable girls and boys in the community. Additionally, the two Mercy Corps Youth Centers will operate as CFS to support the health and wellbeing of children within this context and to expand and strengthen child protection measures at the community level.
Mercy Corps proposes a broad range of complimentary activities to address this humanitarian need and to alleviate the immediate suffering of children, adolescents, and youth, and fortify the supportive ecosystem around them to reduce vulnerabilities and enhance resilience, healthy coping mechanisms, and other important life skills.

1 - Needs

The Child Protection Sub Cluster has identified 2.7 Million people in need of child protection, with children making up 2.3 Million, and the remainder consisting of parents and caregivers as well as teachers, school staff, and youth practitioners. In Panyijiar and Rubkona counties, the target population for child protection interventions totals nearly 63,000. According to the December 2020 Bentiu PoC Report, there are more than 30,000 children age 5-17 years—who are particularly vulnerable—have missed out on school and other protective measures as services and CFS remain closed or otherwise limited. The ongoing emergencies and crises in South Sudan, further exacerbated in 2020 by the spread of COVID-19, has left glaring gaps in child protection provisions for children and adolescents, as well as gaps in services with school closures, the additional burdens at the household level, and limitations placed on static services and supports due to social distancing and reduced group size requirements. In 2021, these two counties are identified as IPC 4, extreme, suggesting that nearly half of all children are at-risk of dropping out of school, which is a cogent threat to child wellbeing as schools have been closed for the year, long school academic layoffs a known barrier to children returning to school. A further concern for children living in the Bentiu PoC and the surrounding communities is the evidence of an increase in child-headed households, due to parents and caregivers returning home or to new settlements for economic opportunities while choosing to leave children behind for a variety of reasons, including protection concerns and schooling. Thus, further awareness raising at the family and household level is required, in addition to strengthening support systems and protective factors such as CFS and YC, and finally, durable solutions such as resilience and critical age- and gender-appropriate life skills. With the closure and reduction of many services over the past year, children have been limited in their ability to experience and enjoy elements of childhood as labor practices increase—either domestic chores or informal work outside of the home—and household abuses can persist with fewer checks and opportunities for reports or interventions, and a decreased likelihood of protection or justice. Furthermore, abuses happening within the household are on the rise, and likely under-estimated. Local behaviors towards child protection is still worrying, with increased risks still including child and forced marriages, and other risks and threats of violence perpetratd against girls and women. All these forms of abuse or practices impact the psychosocial wellbeing of children in all aspects of their lives, furthering protection concerns. As schools are set to reopen, child protection actors need to be ready to mainstream protection in schools, enhance capacities in youth and community centers, and maintain agility and flexibility to respond to stresses to an already fragile system, particularly as the threat of subsequent waves and variants of COVID-19 spread across South Sudan. In order to help children and their communities become more resilient in the midst of regularly-occurring threats, humanitarian actors also need to help to establish resilience solutions to reduce the vulnerabilities of girls, boys, women, and men, including those living with disabilities. In order to support the protection of children, their surrounding context and supports need to also be addressed, supported, and developed. This includes building the capacity of their parents and caregivers, their teachers, school personnel, youth development practitioners, and other frontline workers, in addition to developing community institutions such as schools, youth centers, and other child-friendly spaces, to create a strengthened coordinated approach to protect children at a community level.

2 - Project activities

Mercy Corps’ key activities proposed for 2021 in the targeted areas to increase and expand Child Protection services in line with IPC 4 indicators to include: 1) updating child protection reporting services and case management mechanisms; 2) expanding mental health and psycho-social (MHPSS) supports at the community level, through schools, youth centers, child friendly spaces, and other community centers; and, 3) strengthening community-based child protection approaches, including coordination and capacity building. Mercy Corps proposes to broaden the reach of help desks, develop and place strategic referral IEC, and update partnerships and cluster collaboration to create a supportive environment where children feel safe and comfortable disclosing abuses which require effective reporting and referrals services to higher levels of specialized MHPSS interventions via case management and necessary counseling. A combination of these help desks in static locations (schools, youth centers, and other identified child friendly spaces) as well as community mobilizers meeting children and adolescents where they gather or spend time will create a multi-pronged approach to identifying and referring cases. Mercy Corps plans to train youth facilitators and mentors to deliver lower level MHPSS interventions through focused and non-focused individual, group, and targeted programming to support the healthy development and recovery of children, adolescents, and youth who require some additional support. These facilitators will be trained to facilitate participant-led life skills sessions covering key topics such as identity, teamwork, communication, peace and conflict, respect, responsibility, and others. To make YC and CFS accessible and inclusive of all children (including boys, girls, and children living with disabilities), rehabilitation projects will repair classroom blocks and establish gender-segregated latrines. Critical community feedback and a history of low levels of female participation indicates a clear need for latrines which are private, secure, and gender segregated, as well as accessible, to ensure that MHPSS and life skills programming, as well as all other protective measures offered through CFS and C are equitably distributed in the community. Awareness campaigns will target community members and will be designed to be far-reaching and address diverse audiences. Key messages will be shared and community members will be sensitized on the importance of child protection issues which are locally and contextually relevant. Local community members will then be identified and mobilized to carry nuanced messages further into communities through clubs, community gather spaces, family structures, and households. These community mobilizers will lead the initiative to identify the community’s expressed needs and re-centralize child protection initiatives to extend community structures and away from NGOs and humanitarian actors. This mobile and flexible community cohort will be able to navigate COVID-19 guidelines, limitations on group sizes, and meet the hardest to reach, and often most vulnerable within a community, specifically those living with disabilities. These static and mobile support systems will also be replicated in school settings. In addition to CFS and YC facilitators and personnel, school personnel, both women and men, will be appropriately trained to identify suspected cases of child protection needs and receive disclosures of child protection violations from children. Moreover, these school-based professionals (starting with parent-teacher association leaders) will also be trained and supported to mobilize for community outreach to better maintain positive and healthy connections with schoolchildren in the event of subsequent or extended school closure time, to better ensure that child protection measures at the school level are not only dependent upon the school being open in a traditional sense.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

Underpinned by Mercy Corps’ internal Safeguarding policies and Community Accountability and Reporting Mechanism (CARM), this project will fall under the supervisions and supports which cover all programming and community-based activities. Specifically, the zero tolerance policy states: treating beneficiaries with dignity and compassionately is a matter of priority, no act or behavior exploitation and abuse shall be condoned. Trainings will be done with teachers, PTA and SMC members, and other parties engaged in the project on all issues relating to protection. Close collaboration and coordination will be done with protection teams in issues to do with protection, and all partners, participants, and beneficiaries will be made aware of the various organizational challenges through which they can report or otherwise claim their grievances. Moreover, a key component of this project will be to highlight referral services and pathways, including the capacity development of school institutions as well as the individual roles of teachers and other school personnel. This project will work in collaboration, consultation, and coordination with other partners in the area to consolidate workable referral pathways for the survivors to access services without hindrance when needed.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Involvement of all stakeholders, especially those who are most vulnerable, will enable the definite achievement of goals in a timely manner. Community engagement meetings will draw upon participants from all groups of beneficiaries, regardless of age, ability, gender and position in the community, including school children. Ensuring that a diverse range of perspectives, lived experiences, and voices are represented in these conversations, prioritizing all types of community members will continue be the prerequisite of accomplishing Mercy Corps’ project goals and aims. Messages will be tailored to suit age- and gender-specific groups. Information, education and communication materials will be in local languages (e.g. Nuer) for Rubkona and Panyijiar counties. School and community meetings will also be held where necessary to target specific groups separately, for example parents or teachers, (men and women), schoolchildren (girls and boys), children (and families of children) who are living with disabilities, and others as necessary.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Ranging from informal feedback such as conversation and communication in school locations to official report feedback mechanisms such as post-distribution monitoring (PDM) and the Community Accountability Reporting Mechanism (CARM), and steps in between, Mercy Corps includes various feedback and complaint mechanisms throughout all stages of implementation to ensure integrity and joint ownership in projects, activities, and outcomes. Additionally, all team members share responsibility in guaranteeing the inclusion of these feedback systems, from program and project team members working in the field, to specifically-assigned CARM Officers, Monitoring, Evaluating, and Learning team members, as well as country and global headquarters supports and quality assurances. The cultural shift away from using the term “beneficiary” in favor of “participant” is another organizational shift towards increasing the role, dignity, and ownership over the projects on the part of the affected and supported population—the participant. This proposed project will continue to utilize and improve upon tools, mindsets, and culture specifically designed to solicit and on-board regular feedback (including complaints) from members of the targeted community.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

The project will work with CCCM and other partners on ground and use various media to ensure beneficiaries are aware of the project activities and the feedback channels available. Sensitization meetings will be done with different groups to ensure information reaches all communities in the project target areas. Specifically, feedback from parents and caregivers as well as teachers, youth facilitators, community mobilizers, and others working to support the protection of children will have opportunities to provide feedback for adaptation and implementation throughout, as was indicated in their inclusion in the needs assessment for the completion of the current project proposal.Where appropriate, children (boys and girls, as well as children living with disabilities) will be asked for their feedback to onboard in the current project cycle. For example, children being supported by case management will will have opportunities to advocate for themselves and the type of care and support they prefer to receive, as is appropriate for their situation. Furthermore, as focused and non-focused MHPSS interventions contribute significantly towards the Child Protection project proposal, participatory-led programming will keep adolescent participants at the center of sessions and themes.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

Panyijiar and Rubkona counties have enjoyed relative calm, yet some risks still remain, particularly during specific times and in specific locations. Protection cluster partners and advisory groups will be consulted in the lead up to registrations and distributions to ensure that beneficiaries, children (both boys and girls), families, and people living with disabilities can receive education-related goods and services can receive support with safety and dignity. Mitigation measures and risk assessments will continue to be a component of distribution and registration activities conducted by Mercy Corps and partners. Prioritizing participant perspectives, including members of marginalized community and minority groups, this project will have an inclusive implementation of included activities, that comprise of all the stakeholders’ participation: regardless of sex, race, religion, language (mother tongue), and ability by a way of conducting community engagement meetings for collective decision making, school meetings, and other wide ranging consultation mechanisms. Women, girls and youth have been and will highly involve in the consultations and decision-making processes. Multiple, simultaneous community-based approaches will be leveraged to break down socio- and cultural-normative practices which exclude or otherwise limit the participation of women and girls. These approaches will address the multiple community perspectives, especially those of women and girls, and include those of power-holders, to ensure safe and sustainable progress supported by the community. A key component of this project will be to highlight referral services and pathways, including the capacity development of school institutions as well as the individual roles of teachers and other school personnel. This project will work in collaboration, consultation, and coordination with other partners in the area to consolidate workable referral pathways for the survivors to access services without hindrance when needed.

12.1.4. Quality, timely and inclusive protection services (including medical care, legal support, safety and mental health psychosocial services to children, women at risk and GBV survivors

A variety of quality and inclusive protection services will be established, upgraded, and strengthened in this proposed project. Namely, the various levels targeted in mental health and psychosocial support to address the mental and physical health and wellness needs of children, their parents and caregivers, and the broader community. Lower levels of MHPSS support and awareness will target community members at the household level as well as within schools, youth centers, and other child friendly spaces. Sensitizing these groups, raising awareness, and building basic capacity is a key step in our community-wide approach to deliver quality and inclusive protection services at its broadest reach. Planned implementation and delivery of lower level MHPSS interventions for children, adolescents, and youth will provide safe spaces of focused and non-focused activities and supports which many young people require in an emergency context to regain normalcy in their lives, including the return of healthy relationships, coping mechanisms, and age- and gender-appropriate development opportunities. Non-focused interventions will allow participants to benefit from child friendly spaces and utilize the resources and opportunities as they identify and prioritize for themselves. Targeted and focused MHPSS and life skills interventions will also contribute to the health and wellness of participants by improving their knowledge and skills and through promotion of healthy habits and behaviors which contribute to protection outcomes. Children, adolescents, and youth in need of higher level, specialized supports, will be able to benefit from updated and strengthened referral pathways. Starting with capacity building of frontline workers and community members, accessing care and navigating appropriate referral pathways will place children into the appropriate care necessary to address their specific needs. These referrals to specialized supports will include medical care, GBV survival specialists, and others. The present project proposal does not explicitly include legal support as current capacity and expertise is low. Other partners working in this space will be consulted on a case-by-case basis as they are available, but this is a significant gap in justice services facing the whole of South Sudan which the project cannot sufficiently address or overcome.

12.1.2. Decreased proportion of populations facing IPC 3,4 and 5

Protection concerns, specifically child protection and GBV concerns and threats within the community which make children vulnerable and contribute to the severity of humanitarian contexts, will be addressed in this proposed project. While the risks that exit cannot be altered (e.g. conflict, flooding, pandemics), strengthening community networks, reporting and response systems, and awareness at the individual, household, and community levels will better prepare the community to be resilient in these contexts. Communities which are mobilized and sensitized to the threats and drivers to child protection concerns and who are participating in the mitigation and response strategies are better situated to build sustainable mechanisms to protect children through mainstreaming protective measures as well as context-appropriate child-friendly responses and referral pathways. The project's targeted activities and outcomes aiming to reduce the suffering of children, including addressing child protection concerns and GBV prevalence, is in direct correlation to the humanitarian needs in the target areas (both Panyijiar and Rubkona Counties) causing them to register as IPC 4.

12.1.3. Equitable access to cross-sectoral basic services to vulnerable populations (including women, children, the elderly, persons with disability and older persons)

This project works with other sector services to ensure the protection and safeguarding of children, adolescents, and youth, as well as their families, households, and communities. Working strategically with other sectors such as General Protection, Education, WASH, GBV, and health services, systems and structures will be established in collaboration as well as referral pathways and follow-on specialized support to ensure that the most vulnerable, including victims of abuse and neglect, are appropriately supported. Child protection mainstreaming in schools will help to ensure that protective measures are taken to mitigate against threats and drivers of violence. Infrastructure considerations will be made and Child Protection and GBV specialists (along with community members themselves) will be consulted on solutions and pathways forward. Furthermore, teachers, school personnel, and school management will be trained on Child Protection in Emergencies, PSEA, GBV, and referral pathways. With this increased capacity, schools will be prepared to establish school-based mechanisms to support and respond to children in severe need. Training frontline workers on child protection and GBV, and establishing functional referral pathways will ensure that vulnerable children receive the appropriate response and care according to their need. While teachers and school personnel will not become specialists in medical or GBV responses, they will have the capacity and resources to appropriately navigate appropriate pathways which can better address the child's suffering. Creating a robust and healthy ecosystem of care around children which are localized in the community and components of existing systems and institutions will help to create equitable access to care and services. Specifically, this cross-sectoral approach directly contributes to this level of care and consideration for vulnerable populations.
