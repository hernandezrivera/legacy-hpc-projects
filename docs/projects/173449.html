Project Summary

Save the Children (SC) aims to prevent the undermining of breastfeeding practices and to improve knowledge of optimal infant and young child feeding practices among affected communities in Rohingya refugee camps. The targeted beneficiaries are caregivers and their children 0-23 months. The life-saving steps to provide appropriate feeding and care for children 0-23 months requires specific expertise and technical support, which SC demonstrates with IYCF-E activities since 2017 in line with the IYCF-E Working Group. As such, SC is well placed to provide a strong lead in IYCF-E response to the affected communities. SC will also focus on facility and community-based behavior interventions to enhance the awareness and practices of PLW in IYCF. SC has 8 health posts in 8 camps where IYCF-E services will be delivered through IYCF-E Counsellors and nutrition volunteers.

1 - Identified Needs

The levels of acute malnutrition among Rohingya refugees living in Cox’s Bazar remain extremely high. Rohingya refugee children under the age of 5, pregnant and lactating women (PLW) and adolescent girls represent particularly vulnerable groups, with high levels of acute malnutrition. The reasons for this are multifold and include: limited support for mothers and young children, lack of safe breastfeeding spaces, suboptimal hygiene and sanitation practices, poor WASH facilities (in particular low access to safe water), and low dietary diversity. There is thus a need to not only to maintain, but to further scale-up and improve, the quality of nutrition services currently available in the camps. The latest Nutrition Sector survey indicated that across all camps, Rohingya refugee children under two years old (U2) and Pregnant and Lactating Women and Girls (PLW/G) represent particularly vulnerable groups, with high levels of acute malnutrition . In Camps 25 and 27, 46% and 38% of HHs respectively were below the acceptable threshold of the food consumption score (JRP 2020). At 10.9%, Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM), levels in all camps in Cox’s Bazar are serious based on the SMART standards. Adding to this, 59.6% of children (6-23 months) suffer from anemia (JRP 2020). 60.9% of Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) cases had access to services, a 10% decline since 2017 (JRP, citing a 2019 survey). It is clear that repercussions of the pandemic are likely to cause more harm to children than the disease itself by exacerbating already poor diets and low uptake of infant and young child feeding practices (ICYF). Refugee children in particular may also be more susceptible due to high levels of undernourishment, one third (aged 1-5) being chronically undernourished (COVID-19 Gender Equality Analysis 2020). Only 64% of infants (0-5 months) receive exclusive breastfeeding, 55% of children are breastfed up to two years and 26% have access to minimally acceptable diverse diets (JRP 2020). Furthermore, high vulnerability among the refugees has been reported in the recent Refugee Emergency Vulnerability Assessment (REVA) conducted by World Food Programme. The REVA indicates that refugees are adopting negative coping mechanisms such selling of assets, food ration and other humanitarian relief items to obtain cash. These practices have been identified as comprising the food security of households and resulting to inadequate food intake. Similarly, the REVA indicates that while the majority of the refugee households still rely on general food assistance provided by humanitarian actors, their diets are high biological proteins and micronutrients which offer dietary diversity. Moreover, SC conducted a MHPSS assessment in 2019 and found concerning signs of developmental delay and malnutrition in Rohingya children, implying that nutritional status, psychosocial distress, lack of cognitive stimulation (education) and psychological trauma are all contributing to developmental delays. Much of this is still valid in the camps. Continuing the efforts in nutrition messaging and social behavioral change communication (SBCC) in camps and host communities with partner organizations is also necessary.

2 - Activities or outputs

(a) Regular screening for acute malnutrition through MUAC/oedema and referral at community level, and health facilities; (b) Sensitization of community leaders and administrative leaders on objectives of mother to mother support groups; (c) IYCF-E orientations for key community groups such as majhees, religious leaders and fathers with children under 2 years of age; (d) Development of context specific IYCF- social behaviour change communication materials; (e) Observation of calendar events such as world breast feeding week and child health; (f) IYCF-E capacity building initiatives targeting community health workers , nutrition volunteers and community mental health workers on Infant and young child feeding in emergencies (IYCF-E); (g) Referral of children under five years of age with severe acute malnutrition for community based management of acute malnutrition (CMAM) at Outpatient Therapeutic Program (OTP), children with moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) and PLW to targeted supplementary feeding program (TSFP) and blanket supplementary feeding program (BSFP) run by other partners
