Project Summary

RENHW uses an integrated, multisector approach to improve nutrition outcomes in Jonglei State. The project objectives are: 1) increase access to and use of quality primary health services, 2) improve access to quality and standardized nutrition services for
pregnant and lactating women (PLW) and children under 5 (CU5) and 3) improve WASH services in health facilities and communities. Ayod, Duk, Pochalla, Twic East, and Uror are the target counties for the RENHW project.

1 - Needs

Health: Health care in South Sudan, especially in Jonglei, are suboptimal, leaving more than half the population without primary health services. Ongoing conflict and flooding in 2020 damaged existing health facilities, reducing South Sudanese already limited access to essential health services. These factors contribute to the high under-five mortality rates (90.7 deaths per 1,000 live births) and maternal mortality rates (789 deaths per 100,000 live births) within the country. The country has only one skilled health professional for every 65,574 people and only 44% of population lives within 5 km of a health facility. Nearly 75% of child deaths result from preventable diseases, such as diarrhea, pneumonia, and malaria. In addition, only 14% of pharmaceutical commodities are accessible in the country. A 2018 study by CRS found that the distance to health facilities and costs related to transport were significant barriers to health seeking behavior in RENHW’s target counties.

2 - Project activities

Comprehensive primary healthcare services, including reproductive health services, communicable and non-communicable disease services, social behavior change communications, and community outreach through community health workers (CHW) and Care Group structures.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

Safeguarding and protection are mainstreamed fully in all activities and approaches. Information on PSEA is shared with beneficiaries through IEC materials posted in health and nutrition facilities as well as through Care Group structure. Within the nutrition and health facilities, staff conduct health education sessions during which PSEA messages are shared. There is also a Protection Partner in each county that conducts trainings on safeguarding for all staff, partners, and volunteers.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

There are different literacy rates and limited access to technology and assets, such as radios and phones, which can contribute to the exclusion of the mostvulnerable from accessing information that is in written form. Given differential access and barriers to information, CRS has tailored communication approaches to help meet the different information needs of various groups. For example, RENHW leverages Care Group structures, engages female and male volunteers and health workers, uses picture messages, and shares information in spaces where women and girls are likely to frequent to ensure feedback is captured from the most vulnerable community members. In addition, the project also ensures that project participants are provided with the option of providing feedback in their local languages either through direct engagement with project staff or through local stakeholders. The project’s FRM has been popularized at the community level and participants are aware of the numerous channels through which they can provide feedback.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Information is provided through health facilities’ healthy spaces, CHWs, and theCare Group structure. IEC materials, translated into local languages with clearand culturally appropriate illustrations and images that are easily understood, are used to provide information on the project goals, services offered, and context-specific feedback and response mechanisms for target communities.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Quarterly review meetings with project stakeholders are held to beneficiary feedback, review lessons learned and coverage data, evaluate behavior change adoption, and adaptively manage mobile unit activities. These meetings are also used to monitor evolving protection risks and review established mitigating measures. In addition to quarterly meetings, participant feedback is reviewed during Project Steering Committee monthly meetings and action plans developed and tracked to ensure that feedback received is adequately addressed. In accordance with COVID-19 protocols, the project team also conducts community engagement events during which project participants and community stakeholders are encouraged to share feedback.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

CRS will continually monitor current affairs and the national and local safety and security environment and take all necessary precautions and safety measures to ensure the safety and security of project participants, CRS staff, partners, and consultants. CRS will look for ways to adapt to rapidly changing situations andminimize disruptions to planned activities. CRS will undertake an integrated protection risk analysis across all proposed sectors. CRS and consortium partners will mainstream protection in the provision of services proposed in sectors and subsectors. In addition, context-appropriate complaint channels for safe and confidentialreporting will be established with fair and impartial feedback and response mechanisms set up in collaboration with the communities. A KAP survey and community consultations will inform materials and supplies needed for communities to practice promoted nutrition and hygiene behaviors. WASH NFI and MenstrualHygiene Management (MHM) kits will be distributed alongside culturally appropriateeducation and messaging on nutrition and hygiene through Care Groups and ‘HealthSpaces’. CRS will consult with community members, especially adolescent girls andwomen, on preferred materials, supplies, and distribution approaches, including safetyconsiderations, to inform the provision of health and nutrition services and WASH NFI/MHM kits, mitigation of any potential protection risks related to the activities, and barriers to access. In addition, all CRS staff and partners are CRS trained on the code of conduct and PSEA policy. All partners that CRS works with are also required to have an organization code of conduct and PSEA policy.
