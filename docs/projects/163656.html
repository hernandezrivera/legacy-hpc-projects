Project Summary

Migrants living in Libya face significant protection concerns for a wide range of reasons, including violence, exploitation, hazardous living conditions, and abuse at the hands of smugglers and traffickers. IOM will work to ensure that migrants requiring specialized assistance as well as mental health and psychosocial support are appropriately supported while alleviating suffering and maintaining human dignity. The project’s main line of work are related to service provision to vulnerable individuals one the one hand, and capacity building of duty bearers on the other hand. In addition, in depth analysis underpinned by accurate data is crucial and IOM will produce evidence base that can service for advocacy purposes as well.

1 - Needs

The ongoing armed conflict has augmented pre-existing risks and vulnerabilities of migrants, IDPs and conflict affected populations in Libya. This has resulted in an acute need for the provision of services to migrants living in conflict zones or displaced by the conflict, including access to safe spaces, and increased protection concerns. Migrants living in Libya (both in detention and in urban setting) face significant protection concerns, including violence, exploitation, hazardous living conditions, and abuse at the hands of smugglers and traffickers. Many migrants find themselves in vulnerable situations both physically and psychologically. Concerns are also highly gendered, with women and girls from migrant and IDP communities facing gender-based violence Gender-Based Violence (GBV) risks). Many fear moving outside their communities alone due to these issues, further limiting access to services. Geographically, the most severe needs are in the east and south of the country. Migrant children are particularly at risk of trafficking and kidnaping, and IDP children are at risk of forced recruitment by armed groups. According to the 2019 Libya Multisectoral Needs Analysis (MSNA), 60% reported that migrant and refugee children face safety and security concerns in their (baladiya), 27% reported encountering unaccompanied or separated children living in their (baladiya) and 56% reported encountering children working in their (baladiya). East Africans typically receive the worst treatment by both host communities, Libyan security forces, and armed groups compared to migrants from other regions. According to the MSNA 45% of East African migrants reported feeling in danger and 48% reported movement restrictions. There are additional rifts between Arabic and non-Arabic speakers. Irregular migrants or migrants who may have lost their documents in transit are at risk of being arrested and detained by law enforcement and militias at any time when out in public. They are arbitrarily arrested by law enforcement, and kidnapped, ransomed, and trafficked for forced labour and sexual exploitation by militias. At the same time, Libya’s economy relies heavily on migrant labour, and migrants continue reaching the country.Displacement is on the rise with approximately 149,315 IDPs identified by DTM as of January 2020 who have been forced to leave their homes due to armed conflict since 04 April 2019. The conflict also affected migrants held in the detention centers as well as those in urban locations and their protection remains a concern. Migrants in urban locations face a range of risks including exploitation, forced labor, harassment and abuse and are often at risk being caught in the crossfire of the clashes together with other vulnerable populations.Migrants have continued to face issues particular to their precarious status in the country, with reports of non-Libyans being refused access to collective shelters, and the situation for migrants in detention becoming extremely dangerous. For migrants in Libya, the lack of access to a safe space is associated with an array of secondary protection risks, including exposure to indiscriminate shelling/shooting, arbitrary arrest, physical abuse, robbery, abduction, human trafficking and psychological distress.

2 - Activities

1. Produce data, research and monitoring to improve information on protection risks faced by migrants in Libya to inform advocacy and programming; support internal in-depth studies and conduct comprehensive research into counter human trafficking and child protection in Libya. 2. Build the capacity of duty bearers, humanitarian actors and community members to identify and assist vulnerable migrants through training and capacity building initiatives; 3. Provide specialized services for migrants (GBV, child protection, victims of trafficking (VoTs), etc.), in and outside of detention settings. This includes conducing protection assessments identifying assessment to identify vulnerable migrants and provision of tailored assistance including referrals to specialized services, as well as the provision of assistance such as temporary shelter in host families or in safe shelters, telephone call home to loved ones. 4. Establish safe spaces for vulnerable migrants in Libya. The shelters will represent a safe place where vulnerable migrants will be supported while tailored assistance is provided, and a safe return is ensured. 5. Provide alternatives to detention. IOM has already established a nascent network of families from migrant communities who are willing and able to host vulnerable adult migrants released from detention centers. Host families are identified with the support of migrants’ embassies as well as migrant community leaders and are provided with non-food items (NFIs) and stipends by IOM. 6. Raise awareness on prevention strategies relating to human trafficking, risks associated with family separation and irregular migration, protection risks and services available. The awareness raising will be done on a community and individual level, with a focus on community-based targeted prevention measures. Context specific awareness raising materials will be developed to address the information gap among vulnerable groups and equip them with the information.

4 - Protection risk analysis

Due to the nature of the project the possible protection risks are taken into consideration and mitigation measures built into the response. However, there are conflict related risks that we anticipate will impact the implementation, such as; access issues related to security and government officials denying IOM staff entry, large internal displacements and direct and indiscriminate attacks against civilians.Due to the sensitivity of the topics discussed during the assessments we conduct, there is the risk of retraumatizing the children or adult individuals we interview. Since some of the assessments occur at the detention center there are concerns to their safety if privacy is not adhered to.

5 - Mitigation measures

IOM activities will be flexible and adaptable to any changed context or to better suit the unique needs of those affected, this includes ensuring flexibility in implementation activity for other priority areas to respond in a timely manner. Also, maintaining effective and positive relationships with Libyan officials will remain critical to ensure access to detention facilities and regarding security. IOM will continue to coordinate with the protection sector and relevant international and national actors.IOM staff have all been trained on survivor centered approaches, safe interviewing techniques, the importance of respecting confidentiality and not to conduct interviews if privacy and safety is not guaranteed to ensure that the risks mentioned above are kept to a minimal. IOM protection staff have an internal referral mechanism with IOM Medical and MHPSS teams for further support and professional guidance.

6.1 - Other accountability mechanisms

An IOM hotline provides a feedback mechanism to migrants receiving assistance. Posters with the IOM hotline are on display in interview rooms encouraging beneficiaries to call the free number with complaints, queries or feedback. In addition, monitoring and evaluation tools measure the satisfaction of services provided, and interventions are tailored based on beneficiary feedback. All IOM staff are provided with PSEA training, and informed of reporting mechanisms. IOM has strict internal procedures to deal with misconduct.
