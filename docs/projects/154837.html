Project Summary

The Danish Refugee Council (DRC) in Iraq proposes a 12-month intervention in line with the HRP strategic priorities to support families and communities affected by the 2014 conflict. The proposed program will be part of a DRC multisector response to support those who return to reintegrate into their communities but face a host of challenges, including ongoing violence and loss of homes and livelihoods; as well as displaced households who wish to return but are unable to do so for a variety of reasons and face escalating protection risks; and finally, host communities who have been impacted by the conflict, including through destruction of infrastructure, lack of available services, and population influx. The program aims to continue providing services to most vulnerable communities, based on both sector-specific and integrated needs assessments, and builds on DRC’s achievements implementing Protection, WASH, Shelter, and Camp Coordination and Camp Management (CCCM) activities under previous grant awards in 2015-2018. The program will be implemented in both camp and non-camp settings.
The multi-sectoral program will be strengthened through further integration of proposed activities, including through beneficiary selection modalities, referrals, and assessments, using an “area-based” approach where possible. In line with this approach, DRC expects a large proportion of the individuals targeted by this project to benefit from multiple DRC services. The program also incorporates increased attention to training of local authorities, civil society, community leaders and other duty bearers to identify and meet the needs of populations of concern through technical support.
To implement the Shelter/NFI components DRC intends to incorporate a greater focus on market-based approaches to the delivery of assistance to support the economic recovery of the targeted communities. This will depend on the beneficiary specific needs and individual context, not often are women volunteers for construction related labour at such special provisions will be made in this case. DRC will also continue to implement a strong protection focused programme to insure the prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse, consideration for those with specific needs due to disability, access constraints and gender as well as integrated HLP support as part of the multi-sectoral objectives of the program. Household activities will be tailored to the needs of those benefiting, with particular attention to better access for those with disabilities.

Needs

Return movements following the conclusion of military operations are complicated by continued lower-level violence, serious protection risks, lack of infrastructure, shelter and housing issues and disruption of markets and employment options; these issues often lead to secondary displacement. During displacement and upon return, men, women, boys, and girls have acute needs for shelter, WASH and protection including legal documentation, with the HRP recognizing shelter as a key concern. A DRC-led Shelter assessment in Anbar found that 10% of the houses assessed in Fallujah City and 25% in Saqlawiya have sustained category III or IV war damage and are in most cases burnt or destroyed and in need of reconstruction, leaving the homeowners displaced in other buildings, often disused due to war damage. The assessment found FHHs with limited financial means to meet basic needs residing in category II damaged buildings. The damages included missing window panes, doors, water tanks, sanitary system, concrete works, plastering and roof insulation which leave occupants particularly vulnerable during seasonal weather extremes, particularly those with weaker immune systems including children, chronically sick and elderly. Privacy and security were primary concerns for FHHs, and it was found that rural residents were less likely to be able to afford shelter repairs independently, compared to urban areas. In Fallujah city, DRC identified a shortage of jobs and livelihood opportunities despite the functioning construction market. SAD has both long-standing unmet needs among protracted IDPs, returnees and host community members, as well as emerging needs stemming from new population movements. In Tikrit city, Al Alam and Shirqat, IDPs, and returnees have needs for shelter repair and upgrading. Over 3,000 households have returned to central areas of Hay Alasri and Aljadida in Beiji, despite DRC's findings that 20% of houses are completely burnt or destroyed, with critical repairs of damaged houses urgently needed. The needs include doors, windows, electricity works, handrails, bricks and concrete block works, water network connections, sanitation works, closure of wall damages, concreting, and plastering. DRC identified the potential for market-based approaches for the provision of shelter items, as businesses including construction material shops are starting to reemerge. This would provide temporary employment opportunities for men and women also according to their preferences.DRC undertook a comprehensive MSNA in early 2018 in Ninewa Plains which, in line with those of CRS and Reach, identified shelter and destruction of property as key concerns for vulnerable families. The high costs associated with repair or reconstruction have delayed rehabilitation, with residents stating that due to the lack of livelihoods or savings, they do not have the resources to cover construction costs. The majority of the shelters (75%-100%) in villages located close to the front lines of the battle have sustained category III and IV damage and are largely unpopulated as the population remains in displacement. Similar villages in Wana sub-district (Sumhela and Amar Khubir) reported near-complete destruction of shelters. Approximately 1,250 families unable to return to those villages were reported to be occupying houses of other families, who will displace them when returning themselves. As the trends of returns continue, its anticipated that some camps will be consolidated into others. It is expected that during camp consolidation within Al Alam the movement of tents from one sector to another will increase the damage of the already poor tent conditions and as such tents will require replacement. In addition, sector 1 requires upgraded drainage infrastructure to prevent flooding for those who are unable to return and will remain in the camp.In Qayyarah Airstrip Camp, also expected to remain, the drainage system requires upgrading to concrete channels as the current system causes the formation of pools of stagnant water, posing health and road damage. The current stormwater system is not adequate to contain and divert the stormwater from the hills and surrounding Jedda 4, 5 & 6 IDP camps, and regularly floods in the camp.

Activities

In line with the first Shelter Cluster Objective, DRC will maintain adequate living conditions for vulnerable displaced people in camps through adequate shelter and NFI support in Qayarrah Airstrip and Al Alam IDP camps through:- Upgrading drainage systems for flood mitigation using the engineering expertise in the DRC CCCM and WASH teams, where possible creating temporary employment opportunities.In Qayyarah Airstrip alone, DRC intends to:- Meet the NFI needs of new arrivals (in line with Shelter Cluster NFI technical guidance), as the Airstrip continues to receive newly arriving families that need to be accommodated as they arrive with few possessions; and- Improve road conditions in specific areas within the camp, and at the main approach to the camp. Following the second Shelter Cluster Objective, DRC will assist highly vulnerable people out of camp (as identified by the CWG socio-economic vulnerability tool) transitioning to durable solutions through the provision of adequate shelter support in priority areas of displacement and return in Anbar, Salah al-Din and Ninewa. DRC will focus on areas not covered by high budget Stabilization Actors and consider historically underserved small urban towns, peri-urban locations, and rural populations through:- SOK distributions (300 USD/HH) for highly vulnerable IDPs out of camps, identified using CCCM established camp exit surveys, secondary data from partners and Clusters, and referrals from DRC teams. DRC intends to consider the use of the local labor and construction market through means such as vouchers, local purchase, and local labor to install shelter solutions based on the findings of construction material and labor market assessments;- Emergency shelter repairs for highly vulnerable returnees whose homes have sustained category II and III war damage: to provide access safe, dignified and weatherproofed shelter, DRC will provide support through direct repairs or conditional cash modalities with full technical support valued at 2,000 USD in coordination with the CCI and CWG Iraq and in line with Shelter Cluster minimum standards and beneficiary needs and preferences;- Integrated HLP support in line with the HLP Sub Cluster-produced HLP Guidance Note to ensure that compensation procedures are understood, and that informed consent is given to address the shelter conditions under DRC programming. This will include legal follow up support to recover documents where required. - Support to the local council and municipalities to strengthen adherence to housing construction standards from the Ministry of Urban Planning, and respect for legal planning procedures to ensure any independent reconstruction is to appropriate standards.- Establishment of Framework Agreements for works and services carried out under this programme to ensure rapid response in locations where local business strengthening cannot be achieved due to the lack of productivity in the local economy.

Mitigation Measures

1. DRC is registered in Iraq and has strong links and productive relations with authorities, particularly at the local level, which allows for a smooth implementation of projects. DRC regularly engages with these contacts to inform them about the humanitarian principles guiding DRC's work to obtain buy-in.2. DRC will work closely with the local authorities, host and beneficiary communities and through the humanitarian architecture to ensure that distributions meet safety and security requirements in the target location, including distributions in a safe and secure location and preventing beneficiary crowding at distribution sites. The RPA team, hosted by DRC, can influence the decision to desist due to protection risks, and the results from DRC Protection teams ongoing protection monitoring influence all aspects of DRC programming.3. DRC work alongside colleagues at DDG, who will frequently undertake non-technical surveys in areas of infrastructure works and provide mine risk education (MRE) within communities. 4. DRC has an extensive security setup utilizing a low-profile approach. Furthermore, security and contingency planning is in place, and staff have the ability to remotely manage and support on-the-ground activities if necessary from the field offices in each of the project governorates and or area offices if staff are forced to evacuate. This setup allows DRC to continue operating even with the possibility of increased insecurity. If the project cannot be implemented in some locations due to access, these activities are in line with the strategy for other locations and can be diverted until a time that implementation in the original site is possible.5. DRC Shelter teams operate in the same established locations as DRC Protection teams, both teams have promoted DRCs complaints and feedback mechanism, and IDP call center. All distributions are accompanied by IEC materials that promote the reporting of abuse cases, and all post-distribution monitoring questionnaires of Shelter items include questions on PSEA to ensure that if issues were not prevented then they are followed up and learnings fed into the programme design.6. DRC will pay particular attention to ensure the inclusion of all sectors of the community, including the elderly, those with disabilities and chronic sickness and women. This will be by undertaking gender separated focus group discussions, and developing individual housing designs at the household with the consultation of vulnerable groups with specific needs.7. DRC will hold initial meetings with the District Council, as well as the local leaders including the Mukhtar, Tribal Council, Sheiks, and other elected community representatives to outline the practical and geographical parameters of the project, as well as the implementation methodology and minimum standards. Throughout the project, there will be periodic meetings with this body to address any concerns arising within the community that hasn't been reported to DRC complaints and feedback mechanism which is publicized at the program onset during the household assessment.

How is your project accountable to affected population

DRC strives to be accountable to those whom we assist. We seek to achieve this through our Accountability Framework, Complaints and Feedback Mechanisms, and certification of compliance with international standards. DRC’s global Accountability Framework defines the organization’s commitments, that is, its aims, standards and procedures and how it ensures that it is accountable for them. DRC’s accountability framework comprises a global AF which applies to all international operations in DRC. DRC’s international operations were certified as compliant with the Core Humanitarian Standard, following an external audit in May 2017.A strong monitoring, evaluation, accountability, and learning (MEAL) system is an integral part of all DRC interventions throughout the different phases of humanitarian response and other programming. DRC’s independent field-based MEAL Officers conduct assessments and monitor activities under the supervision of the MEAL Coordinator, in collaboration with the Project and Area Managers.A MEAL plan outlining outcomes, indicators, baseline, targets, activities, and status of implementation will be created to guide implementation. The MEAL plan will guarantee that the initial project proposal and log frame is not only adhered to in the process of implementation but done so in a timely, effective and efficient method. A major part of the MEAL plan is the beneficiary satisfaction survey (similar to a PDM) asked by an impartial team who report whether the needs of the men, women, boys, and girls in the household have been met.Beneficiary satisfaction is measured by rating household conditions of privacy, security, climate protection, and hygiene, using the comparison of the baseline and end line to determine whether the intervention assisted the household as per its design.Compliance spot checks are used to ensure designs meet Shelter Cluster Standards. The MEAL team manages the country-wide and multi-channel beneficiary feedback and complaint mechanism including a hotline, an email address, feedback boxes and mobile help desks at distributions. This mechanism provides project participants and other concerned individuals with different avenues to report complaints and submit suggestions.
