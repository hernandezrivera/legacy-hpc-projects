Project Summary

GC implements integrated protection services to prevent, mitigate, and respond to harm, exploitation, and abuse for the most vulnerable boys, girls, men and women in northern Syria in Idleb and Hama governorates. Under this program, we will manage 13 safe spaces and 16 mobile teams. Safe spaces, in the form of Community Centers (CCs), Child Friendly Spaces (CFS) and Women/Girls Safe Spaces (WGSS) will be service centers used to provide consistent services to communities. Mobile teams will support the static facilities to provide services to those not able to access the safe spaces, raise awareness in the wider community, quickly respond to new movements and emergencies, and conduct assessments. Mobile teams will also be ready to respond and provide emergency response services to IDP displacements and any influx of IDPs. The objective of the project is to enhance the prevention and mitigation of protection risks, and respond to protection needs through supporting the protective environment in Syria, by promoting international law, IHL, HRL and through quality principled assistance.

Needs

With the Syrian conflict in its eighth year, over 13 million people remain in need of humanitarian assistance; 5.6 million have acute needs. Nearly 2.5 million people are estimated to be in need in Idleb and Hama (HNO 2019), with approximately 270,000 IDPs living in camps in Harem District alone. The security situation has gradually deteriorated even further across Northern Syria over the past few months as a result of intensified hostilities in northern Hama and southern Idleb. The continuous violent fighting has resulted in unprecedented humanitarian toll, with regular attacks by GoS/GoR against civilian infrastructure threatening access to safe drinking water, health care, and other essential services. Children continue to face serious protection risks, driven by poverty, family breakdown, and lack of opportunities. Adolescents are at increasing risk of recruitment into armed groups, early marriage for adolescent girls, and exploitation in hazardous work to support their families. Few schools exist for older children and they are unable to find safe, healthy employment due to years of missed schooling and lack of training. Youth are engaged in hazardous work, face school dropout, abuse and neglect within the family and few places to play. PMTF data (April-June 2018 PMTF Quarterly Report), 75% of KIIs report children in their community are at risk of exploitation and almost all KIIs reporting that some or most of children under 18 work in their communities. Our teams also see a high number of CP cases with medical needs, either due to injury from conflict, lack of nutrition, or a result of emotional trauma. Families are often unable to pay for services or unwilling to provide the necessary support. Parents may lack the coping mechanisms to deal with their own trauma and stress, taking it out on their children. Children who have lost parents or been abandoned are often moved around to different extended family members who also face challenges or are uninterested in providing the necessary emotional and physical support. Women and girls also suffer from conflict-related and social pressures. Gender-based violence affects women and girls in Harem in ways public and private, with issues like stigma against widows, resistance to women working, sexual harassment, physical and emotional violence in the home, and restrictions of movement. Early and forced marriage are some of the most frequent reports from our CP, GBV and protection teams. Both social pressure and economic hardships push families to send girls to marriage. Adolescent girls often have no way to push back against their families unless they are able to provide income for their families, or find a way to support their school attendance. Crowded camp conditions leave women and girls more open to harassment when traveling outside their homes. Women are also at risk when accessing services or receiving aid, as suggested by recent reports of sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) either by humanitarian workers or other local actors that facilitate or are seen to facilitate access to aid. According to PMTF, two-thirds of KIIs reported that there were no services for people with disabilities and almost all said there were no none for those with special needs. This is confirmed by our teams who report high numbers of protections cases where the head of household has a disability, resulting in additional burdens and protection risks for the family. Family members with disabilities are often isolated as caregivers may not know how to integrate them or be afraid of the stigma in the community. Activities that specifically target people with special needs in their homes is needed to reach them and provide support to access services. Support for elderly individuals is also needed, with many elderly-headed households where older family members are left to look after young children, resulting in higher protection risks for themselves and the children.

Implementation of activities

GC has been working in northwest Syria since early 2016. From our office in Gaziantep and sub-office in Qah, GC will be responsible for overall program strategy and design, reporting, grant administration and compliance, procurement, vendor and sub-award payments, monitoring and evaluation (M&E) and quality assurance/quality control (QA/QC), and capacity building of staff. Program staff will oversee direct implementation of all proposed activities. Our Syrian staff will enter Syria as appropriate to monitor program implementation. The program will deliver protection, child protection, and GBV services through static and mobile protection services. Our Child Protection services engage children in activities designed to support positive psycho-social well-being and coping strategies. Our static facilities and mobile However, there is a need to expand the mobile services to communities farther from our static facilities to reach communities that are not receiving similar services. It is also recognized that static facilities are frequently accessed by children that may be less vulnerable, so increased outreach to those that are not able to come to centers can be reached. Our YEP Center in Atmeh Camp is one of few centers providing adolescents with services and we have seen more than 100% increase in attendance over the past year as a result of a diversification in our available activities. Our parenting skills courses are also crucial to improve overall household well-being by helping parents to deal with their own stress and anecdotally we have seen significant improvement in child well-being as a result. An additional four WGSS and linked mobile teams provide GBV prevention and response services to 4,000 women and girls on a monthly basis in the four locations in Dana Sub-District, providing empowerment activities for women and adolescent girls and response services for survivors. Our robust case management team has extensive experience and are a trusted source of support for women and girls in the area. These teams include experienced case management staff that provide high quality case management as well as counselling and structured PSS in CP and GBV. These teams are also available to receive referrals of complicated or sensitive cases from the HELP teams. We also integrate activities with other sectors where possible, including with the WASH services – with close coordination and joint activities to increase the benefits to the communities. With the WASH team, we also coordinate to target distributions to highly vulnerable households and individuals identified by our protection teams. The WASH team has provided health and hygiene sessions to children in our activities and we have in return trained their hygiene promotion staff to identify potentially vulnerable or at-risk individuals during their household visits to refer to protection teams. They have also been trained to identify potentially vulnerable individuals and refer them to our teams. The teams will continue to target schools, hospitals and other community infrastructure that allows the protection activities to benefit more people and also to build on the work of the other sectors.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

The Bab al Hawa border crossing between Turkey and Syria will remain open and accessible for humanitarian goods and staff, without extended closures, and/or alternate border points are opened and accessible. If the border closes for humanitarian goods or commercial traffic and our ability to receive essential items or funds, we will consider other, less accessible means of delivery.GC staff continue to have consistent access to target locations while maintaining acceptable levels of independence, impartiality, and neutrality. We will ensure this by continuing to utilize our community engagement team to liaise with the relevant camp stakeholders, and continue to reinforce that we are a humanitarian agency that abides by the humanitarian principles of independence, neutrality, and impartiality. Should our level of access change significantly or impact our ability to work within our humanitarian principles, we will look to identify alternative locations or solutions.Active violence does not directly impact GC's offices in Gaziantep, or Qah for a sustained period of time; Violence in the targeted areas remains at a level which allows GC to implement the program, with both armed and unarmed parties to the conflict allowing access to vulnerable HHs for provision of services. We will regularly assess the risk to our beneficiaries and staff of violence in our project locations. If the risk becomes unacceptable we will suspend activities temporarily and close permanently if needed, according to ethical closeout procedures and in communication with the Cluster. Camp managers, Local Councils, relief committees, and community stakeholders will collaborate with GC, and facilitate access for delivery of humanitarian assistance. We regularly engage with local actors to coordinate activities where appropriate. We are also expanding our use of community networks to both implement some activities and encourage local acceptance of our activities.
