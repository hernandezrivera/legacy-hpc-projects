Project Summary

RI’s integrated programme aims to prevent and treat malnutrition and under nutrition and promote good health among vulnerable groups. In 2020, RI will scale up integrated services among nutrition, FSL, health and WASH sectors through the implementation of its projects in Greater Upper Nile. This strategy will help address interrelated and under-lying causes of malnutrition, such as food insecurity, suboptimal childcare and feeding practices, and lack of safe water and sanitation.
Compared to 2019, RI will increase the volume of preventative activities and build on the successes of community-based management of acute malnutrition.
RI’s aim is to support South Sudanese including refugees, internal displaced persons, returnees and vulnerable population. Our response will prioritize life-saving treatment services targeting children under five years and pregnant and lactating women, in compliance with relevant national guidelines, these being the most vulnerable groups in terms of malnutrition due to their increased biological and physiological needs. Geographically, our response will prioritize the greater upper Nile (Maiwut, Nasir, Ulang and Longechuk) characterized by a high malnutrition, food insecurity (IPC Phase 4 and 5), conflict and internal displacement. RI Nutrition sites will act as hubs for integrated programming. While its health activities sector will provide malaria testing and treatment among severely acute malnourished children being treated at the Nutrition sites, WASH activities will provide access to safe water and NFI kits to severely malnourished children’s families and the food security activities will improve dietary diversity through kitchen gardens at nutrition sites to demonstrate recommended feeding practices. RI nutrition sites will integrate early childhood development stimulation activities and will be considered as potential entry points for identifying and responding to GBV survivors and helping them access referral pathways to be established by RI.
To further localize RI’s response and to ensure sustainability, RI will strengthen the County Health Department (CHD) responsibilities to oversee nutrition interventions, to coordinate the response with various stakeholders at the county level and provide stakeholder capacity building on the new national guidelines of community based management of malnutrition and maternal, infant, and young child nutrition.
RI will provide technical support to CHD to review county level nutrition intervention strategy that is expected to include nutrition referral needs of all payams and Boma of targeted counties. RI will reinforce the nutrition information system and use of nutrition data at the county and community level by employing gender-sensitive approach to mitigate obstacles compromising women and girls’ ability to receive nutrition support. Women’s participation will be encouraged in programme design and feedback mechanisms. RI will also collaborate with existing and planned development interventions on nutrition, such as those funded by the World Bank in the greater upper Nile. RI aims to achieve four objectives, including: (i) Increasing equitable access and utilization of quality life-saving nutrition services to address acute malnutrition amongst the most vulnerable and at-risk U5 children and pregnant and lactating women; (ii) Preventing under-nutrition by increasing equitable access to early detection and treatment of acute malnutrition amongst maternal, infant and young children; (iii) Increasing access to integrated nutrition, health, WASH, FSL and protection interventions; (iv) Enhancing nutrition situation monitoring, analysis and utilization of nutrition information for early warning and decision making.

1 - Needs

Protracted conflict and displacement in South Sudan have drastically reduced the availability of and access to health services, safe water, food and livelihoods opportunities, aggravating high malnutrition amongst children under 5 and PLWs, morbidity and mortality amongst the most vulnerable, especially impacting life, safety and dignity of women and children. Continued conflict and worsening economy has led to mass displacements. With the revitalized peace agreement (RPA) resulting in movement of returnees, pressure is exacerbated on scarce and depleted local resources and significant portions of the population continue to face food insecurity and nutrition concerns. With open defecation practices and lack of access to clean water, health and WASH outcomes are compromised. Upper Nile has some of the highest numbers of people in need in South Sudan, with an estimated 845,000 people in IPC Phase 3 (Crisis) as of August 2019. According to the July 2019 Humanitarian Snapshot, Upper Nile has the highest number of IDPs in South Sudan, at 323,579. While many displaced people have returned to their place of origin in Upper Nile over the past year, fighting in Maiwut displaced around 20,000 people in June 2019 and continues to contribute to lack of humanitarian access. Upper Nile counties are highly dependent on natural resource-based livelihoods, including agriculture and livestock rearing, which have been significantly disrupted by conflict, displacement, inter-communal fighting, cattle raids, child abduction and climate shocks, heightening tensions, displacement, and deaths and aggravating malnutrition. While Longechuk, Maiwut and Ulang counties are in IPC Phase 4 (Emergency) food security conditions, Nasir county is in Phase 3 (Crisis). Further, unusually heavy seasonal flooding is affecting large areas of South Sudan, with approx. 700,000 people having been affected, as per authorities. Mid-September 2019, Mathiang town in Greater Longechuk experienced a six hours heavy rain causing floods and displacement of approx. 1,873 households and 11, 238 individuals. Assessments reveal that critical needs include access to therapeutic foods supplies, safe drinking water, anti-malarial and other basic drugs. The flooding and lengthy periods of accessibility only to limited food items has impacted nutrition amongst the most vulnerable. Access to regular health and nutrition services, continues to remain a challenge due to long distances, remoteness, and floods in the rainy season. In Maiwut, August 2018 SMART survey revealed a GAM rate of 10% with poor health and food security being an aggravating factor in the county. Longechuk had a GAM rate of 12% while Ulang had a GAM rate of 11.1%. South Sudan 2019 Humanitarian Needs reveals that 860,169 children under 5, 596,944 pregnant and lactating mothers, and some 4,000 older people in PoC sites are estimated to be acutely malnourished. Greater Bahr el Ghazal and Greater Upper Nile continue to have highest burden of acute malnutrition for the 4th consecutive year. Persistent insecurity in target counties contributes to increased severe acute malnutrition cases. RI has been operating in Upper Nile since 2006 and is well-placed to respond to the multifaceted needs leading to high malnutrition rates.

6.6 - Will beneficiaries' rights be legally recognised?

Beneficiary rights are recognized through the rights-based programming approach and protection mainstreaming whereby the safety, rights and dignity of beneficiaries is paramount and determines project design, service provision and implementation methodology. Beneficiary needs, rights and voices have informed project activities and will be key to informing advocacy with government and non-government actors. Further, interventions will factor-in the rights of host communities, IDPs and returnees, bringing the target communities on board the finalization of selection criteria, beneficiary selection and site selection for infrastructure activities, messaging modalities and needs and right-based information dissemination

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

The project includes perspectives of displacement-affected people through baseline assessments, FGDs, key informant interviews and KAP surveys, conducted amongst target host communities, returnees and IDPs, with community elders, women and local authorities. These assessments aimed to understand needs and challenges, priorities and protection concerns amongst displaced communities.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

The project is linked to government/community systems since engagement with community is central to all interventions through project life, including design, implementation, monitoring and quality evaluation. The community is engaged during site selection for MMUs, in providing storage space for MMU nutrition supplies. Community volunteers are involved closely in community mobilization and awareness building activities. Skills and knowledge development of community based CNVs and awareness building amongst Lead Mothers on nutrition issues is core to addressing nutrition concerns. In addition, project activities are in coherence with the BHI and in close coordination with the County health Department. RI holds regular meetings and joint monitoring visits with community elders/representatives, ROSS, CHD director and local Commissioner. Regular meetings will be organized with informal groups of women for timely assessment and response to nutrition needs.

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

The project is aligned with the national objectives through its coherence with the nutrition cluster objectives, contributing to (i) increased equitable access and utilization of quality preventative nutrition specific service delivery for children, adolescents and women; (ii) increased equitable access and utilization of quality lifesaving nutrition services for early detection and treatment of acute malnutrition for girls and boys under five years of age and pregnant and lactating women affected by acute malnutrition; (iii) strengthen nutrition information system for evidence-based nutrition response; (iv) increase equitable access to nutrition sensitive interventions from health, WASH, FSL sectors through integrated programming benefiting IDPs, returnees and host communities, ensuring safety and dignity of affected populations

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

The protection environment is not sufficiently safe or stable, impacting lives and dignity of the most vulnerable groups. Safety concerns are heightened in the target counties due to a volatile local environment of inter-communal conflict, cattle raiding and child abduction. Protection concerns are high, especially amongst women, girls, children and persons with disabilities, whose vulnerability exposes them to GBV and life-threatening health and nutrition conditions and insecurity related to WASH facilities and services.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

In order to ensure sustainability and handover, the response will focus on local capacity building for nutrition service provision, close and strong collaboration with the local authorities and concerned departments and ROSS as well as adherence with the Boma Health Initiative. The project will ensure gradual handover of facilities and services to CHD while building capacity and mentoring health staff. Community engagement, consultation and dialogue will be central to all interventions through life of project to ensure local ownership and sustainable solutions. Through preventive health promotion and information dissemination as well as capacity building on safe practices in Health, nutrition and WASH, communities are expected to build resilience and invest in long term solutions

8.18 - Please indicate how the risks selected above will be mitigated.

Potential gender-responsive interventions directly impacting lives, safety and dignity of women will be discussed with men before interacting directly with women of the community. Female staff will be assigned to implement community mobilization, health awareness building and provide SRH, MHPSS and MNCH consultations, counselling and service provision. Local and non-relocatable staff will be trained for positions that don’t require technical degrees, aiming to give more responsibilities to promote local ownership of interventions addressing malnutrition. Lack of access to/awareness of referral services for survivors of protection incidents (GBV, child protection, for example). Project activities include establishment and strengthening of referral pathways for survivors of protection incidents. RI will work with other local actors, including protection, health, WASH and nutrition actors as well as local authorities to design and ensure the functionality of the referral pathways. RI will follow up on the ground and advocate with OCHA, donor community, local authorities, clusters and the NGO forum. Humanitarian assistance and service provision will be brought closer to target communities (for ex. Mobile Medical Units and weekly visits to each target boma/village for vaccinations, medicine provision, supporting referrals, ensuring safe distribution points) and sound feedback mechanisms will be put in place to ensure that accessibility constraints and distance from service delivery points do not expose women and girls to risk of sexual exploitation and abuse. Awareness will be raised amongst community members on the value, importance and benefit of the feedback mechanisms. Further, RI will put contingency measures and plans in place (including the pre-positioning of supplies, increased capacity building amongst the local community) to ensure continued delivery of the services.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

Feedback mechanisms will be embedded into project activities as part of ongoing monitoring visits and post distribution monitoring. As part of accountability to vulnerable communities and ensuring feedback from them, RI will employ two types of complaint and feedback mechanisms: (i) responsive and proactive, permitting direct and indirect beneficiaries to provide feedback and/or lodge complaints, even anonymously to organizational staff (and senior managers in case of complaints against staff). The system will respect the local culture but will not be impeded by local power holders. RI will integrate interventions to improve accountability to affected populations in all project sites. During the project initiation phase, community meetings will be held to inform the assessment and identify cultural barriers that can affect nutrition outcomes. During implementation, exit interviews will be conducted with patients who visited the RI supported clinics and community-level meetings and focus group discussions will be organized on a quarterly basis to receive feedback on the services provided and address any concerns.A complaint mechanism will be established in collaboration with the community to ensure collection of complain from the beneficiaries in a confidential and transparent way where need in order to address them in timely and objective manner. Quarterly monitoring will be also undertaken to ascertain how the project is being implemented and its impact on beneficiaries. Feedback and complaint mechanisms will be diverse and complimentary including: complaint boxes at nutrition facilities (mobile and static) sites; anonymous and non-anonymous reporting mechanisms to a reporting focal point in each of RI's field offices and health care facilities; Supervisory and beneficiary feedback meetings conducted by senior monitoring staff.
