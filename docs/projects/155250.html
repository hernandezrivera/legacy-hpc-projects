Needs

Children represent 55 per cent of the Rohingya refugee population in Cox’s Bazar (CXB) District (495, 651 children according to UNHCR-RRRC Family Counting data). Of these, 284,119 (147,742 girls and 136,377 boys) have been identified by the as children in need of immediate assistance (31% of population). Since the onset of the emergency, UNHCR has continuously consulted with the refugee community through regular focus group discussions managed though Community Outreach Members. Taking into account gender and age considerations, UNHCR conducted a comprehensive Community Assessment in November 2017, and participated in an interagency UNICEF-led Rapid Needs Assessment in December 2017 focusing on Child Protection and Education to better understand the protection environment of girls and boys in both refugee camps and host communities. Currently, 6115 UASC have been identified and documented. In addition to dealing with the emotional distress of being separated from their primary caregivers, many UASC are at heightened risk of child trafficking, abuse and exploitation. Girls, who make the majority of UASC, are particularly vulnerable to abuse, violence, sexual exploitation, and neglect while boys are particularly at risk of labour exploitation. Other known prevalent risk factors for boys and girls include child marriage, child pregnancy, child marriage, domestic violence, physical and mental disability. Through community consultations, LGBTI children were identified as a particularly vulnerable group having a high risk of exposure to violence and with a corresponding limited access to services. Due to their invisibility, accurate numbers are yet to be verified. According to the Multi-Sector Needs Assessment commissioned by UNHCR (Sep 2018), 49% of households reported the main safety concern for boys to be kidnapping and 41% reported the main concern for girls to be sexual violence. The assessment also showed that only a very small number of adolescents (e.g. just 3% of 15-17 year olds, of which girls were only 1%) were attending Child Friendly Spaces (CFS). With limited access to education and/or livelihood opportunities adolescent boys and girls are at a high risk of child marriage and child labour: many families expect girls to marry, and boys to start earning money once they turn 12-14 years of age. With 14% of the population between the ages of 12-17, a lot more has to be done to deliver protection to adolescents and to positively engage them through participation, lifeskills provision and resilience-building. These statistics also show that even where the CFS facilities are available, a majority of children do not attend these services. Over the year, parents have been choosing to send their children to madrassas as shown in the Joint Education Needs Assessment conducted by the Education Sector; where 66% of parents reported Madrasahs as the first choice of learning spaces trusted for their girls to provide secular education, compared to 22% identifying NGO learning centres. For parents of Boys 50% of them reported Madrasah as trusted spaces and 40% NGO learning centres.The CFS model will have to be adopted to respond to the needs of the community to provide safe access to basic learning. There is a need to engage the madrasa educators on importance of PSS activities for children as well as mainstreaming child safe guarding principles. Advocacy with the Government to allow access for refugee children to national services in accordance with the national child policy and applicable laws ongoing.

Activities or outputs

In coordination with its partners and the CPSS, UNHCR’s child protection interventions will address violence, exploitation, abuse and neglect through both preventive and remedial actions. Based on case management approach and the “Do No Harm” principle, UNHCR plans to provide remedial services through referral, provision of psychosocial support (PSS) and aims to change behaviour of refugee girls and boys, as well as of duty bearers through awareness raising, capacity building and support to community structures, CiCs and other local stakeholders involved in child protection. To further inform our programming, continuous child protection situation monitoring will be mainstreamed to inform response not only to CP but other sectors as well. Activities will include: 1. Case management. Improving access to quality child protection services is a priority for all children at risk including unaccompanied and separated children. Specialised case management services will be provided for girls and boys at heightened risk. 2. Capacity building. The capacity of partner staff will be strengthened and developed through their own training, joint training with UNHCR and training initiated through the Child Protection Sub Sector. 3. Child-Friendly Spaces (CFSs) will enable children and adolescents to actively engage in age-appropriate recreational and psycho-social activities in a safe space. 4. Community-based approach. The community is at the centre of UNHCR’s protection strategy. UNHCR and its partners will engage with parents, girls and boys as well as key community representatives to understand the protection environment of children and adolescents. Furthermore, UNHCR and its partners will be supporting community driven initiatives around issues that the community identify as affecting children. Community Based Psychosocial Support will also be implemented to improve resilience and well-being of the community members. 5. Coordination. UNHCR and its partners will coordinate their activities through the interagency Child Protection Focal Point system, through participation in coordination meetings at camp and CXB level and through the use of the existing referral pathways. The project also includes a multi-sectoral approach to response activities, where UNHCR child protection staff actively coordinate their activities with both other UNHCR units but also with all UNHCR partners to ensure the mainstreaming of child protection into partners’ projects and programmes directly implemented by UNHCR across sectors. 6. PSEA (Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse). UNHCR has prioritized SEA prevention and response activities in Cox’s with staff, operationally, and, in the interagency context. 7. Sustainability. Sustainability will be ensured through the envisaged transmission of the CFSs and the child protection community based structures to the refugee and host communities. By strengthening community and integration of community based child protection, the community will have ownership of the CP protective structures which in turn will ensure their sustainability. 8. Social Cohesion- UNHCR and its partners will ensure social cohesion between host and refugee communities by applying the principle of non-discrimination. As part of system strengthening, UNHCR and its partners will continue to collaborate with the Ministry for Social Welfare through the Department of Social Services. Referral pathways for identified host community children at risk will be developed and strengthened in coordination with partners and the CPSS. 9. Disaster Risk Reduction and Environment- UNHCR and its partners will continue awareness raising to the community on family separation. In efforts to protect the environment, UNHCR and its partners will continue ‘greening’ projects where children at CFSs plant and care for vegetation.
