Project Summary

According the 2024 HNO, the number of people in need of life saving services is 5.7 million. 15.4 million people in Syria – 66% of the population – currently face acute food insecurity which may further accelerate all forms of under-nutrition among vulnerable children and women, translating to approximately 2.7% increase from 2023. This has been exacerbated by reduction in food assistance levels due to funding and resource constraints plaguing WFP, as well as the devaluation of the SYP that has increased the cost of imported commodities and domestic food prices. The rising food insecurity will negatively impact on the nutritional status of the most vulnerable groups particularly children under 5 (CU5), pregnant and lactating women (PLW) as well as the approximately 153,000 children under 5 living with disabilities. Wasting prevalence varies from 5-10% among women and girls across Syria. Children with malnutrition are at an elevated risk of mortality and morbidity. 506,529 children who are currently suffering from acute malnutrition will require appropriate, timely and sustainable life saving treatment interventions. Furthermore, at least 1 in 4 children suffering from anaemia in Syria emphasizes the detrimental effects of sub-optimal diets lacking essential nutrients, hence the need for robust anaemia treatment and prevention interventions.
According to the SMART survey conducted in NES in December 2023, the prevalence of combined GAM was low (<5%) in Al-Hassakeh, Aleppo, and Ar-Raqqa, but higher in Deir-ez-Zor (8.4%). The combined SAM revealed low prevalence of between 0.2% to 1.3% in the survey areas. The same survey found underweight levels of between 10.3% and 15.0%, which are classified as medium based on the 2010 WHO thresholds. Stunting prevalence ranged from high in Al-Hassakeh (26.6%) to very high in Aleppo (34.9%), Ar-Raqqa (35.8%), and Deir-ez-Zor (33.4%), showing the need for a targeted, holistic response to prevent and treat malnutrition. Results of the SMART survey conducted by NWS Nutrition Cluster in September 2023 in the Aleppo and Idleb governorates showed an increase in GAM prevalence from 5% in 2022 to 5.6% in 2023, consistently high stunting rates (22.4% in 2022, 22.3% in 2023 – above the “critical/extreme” level of 20% set by the global nutrition cluster), and declining rates of exclusive breastfeeding (56.3% in 2022, 36.4% in 2023). IYCF practices assessed in Aleppo and Deir-ez-Zor were poor in both SMART survey areas.
Caregivers of children under 2 years and pregnant and lactating persons will be provided with MIYCN services in the target areas. CU5 will be screened for acute malnutrition and treatment will be provided at the facility or through referral. The NGO will follow-up all cases of malnourished children until they complete their treatment and achieve the desired nutritional outcomes. Gender and disability considerations will be integrated into the design of activities.
The nutrition teams will work with the health teams to provide integrated, inclusive services – inclusive of screening, treatment, IYCF awareness, early initiation of breastfeeding, delivering nutrition supplies, and applying CMAM to treat malnutrition cases and referral for complicated cases and acutely malnourished infants below 6 months for inpatient treatment services. All PLWs and caregivers of CU-5 within the intervention population will be linked with MPCA support to enable improved dietary practices; particularly, improved dietary diversity for reduction of micronutrient deficiencies amongst infants and young children and PLW, and improved child growth and development. Mothers requiring PSS and GBV support will be referred to the protection teams for additional assistance.

The M&E team and health team will ensure protection of health data and control accessibility to only authorized personnel. ///// Protection Risk Analysis:Risk 1: Lack of or inadequate information provision, feedback and participation of affected communities due to access challenges. Likelihood: high, Impact: medium. Mitigation: Establish/strengthen local partnerships, build the capacity of partners to effectively engage communities, strengthen community-based health and nutrition services, ensure beneficiary feedback included in TPMs. Risk 2: Barriers to access or exclusion of some vulnerable and minority from accessing health and nutrition services for different reasons such as children and adults with disabilities and denial of access to certain areas that have shifted lines of control. Likelihood: medium, Impact: high. Mitigation: Analyze and disaggregate all data by sex, age and disability. Analyze the needs of vulnerable and minority groups throughout the program cycle with identification of risk factors and rights violations impacting service provision for beneficiaries. Risk 3: Breach or leakage of sensitive data and information containing patient names, medical conditions and health care provider names into the wrong hands maybe can put people and communities at risk of targeting, shame, abuse and even death. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: Electronic data and information should be properly protected and backed up. ///// PSEA:Risks: Women and girls not able to access services or participate in income generating activities due to sexual harassment; exploitation and abuse by aid workers; female heads of HHs and adolescent girls in particular risk of sexual exploitation in exchange to services (Voices from Syria, 2022); risk of not reporting SEA for fear of retaliation; lack of awareness on reporting channels; stigma and shame and fear of losing on the services making it difficult for the NGO to hold staff/partners involved accountable. Mitigations: the NGO has a Child Safe Guarding policy which includes PSEA. To mitigate the risk of PSEA during the assistance delivery, the following will be included: mandatory training and regular refreshers on Child Safeguarding, Safe programming and PSEA policy to the NGO and partner staff; working closely with the WoS PSEA TF to strengthen feedback, reporting and response mechanisms for SEA; conducting community awareness and sensitization sessions on PSEA with emphasis on reporting and ensuring safety of survivors through referrals; regular participatory safety audit to identify protection and safety concerns.
