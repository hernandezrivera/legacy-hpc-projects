Project Summary

The proposed action aims to restore the access of boys and girls to quality education and a protective learning environment through capacity-building of teachers, the provision of catch-up classes, psychosocial support and recreational activities for children, awareness-raising and sensitization sessions, and the rehabilitation and repairs of damaged schools in conflict-affected areas in Derna.
ACTED will enhance access to formal and non-formal education for vulnerable school-aged children affected by protracted crisis (Sector Objective 1) by providing catch-up classes to out-of-school and displaced boys and girls. Catch-up programs will address the priority needs of vulnerable children who are out of school, who are in enrolled in school but have fallen behind because of the conflict, from displaced families, as well from disadvantaged groups (girls, children with disabilities and additional needs, associated with armed groups, from minorities, from poorer socio-economic groups, etc.). ACTED seeks to bridge the learning gaps created by conflict-related disrupted access to education. ACTED will also aim to address the physical and infrastructural barriers to safe and protective learning environments through the rehabilitation and repairs of educational facilities. The rehabilitation of the selected schools aims to create safe and inclusive learning spaces for children of all backgrounds and abilities.
Also under Sector Objective 1, ACTED will engage with local communities to enhance access to education in Derna. By conducting awareness-raising sessions for parents, caregivers and children on key issues pertaining to education and child protection, as well as to support building acceptance of the project and participation of beneficiaries. Awareness-raising sessions will focus on topics related to children enrolment in schools and the importance of prioritizing children’s education.
To improve the quality of education services in protective learning environment (sector objective 2), ACTED will conduct psychosocial support and recreational activities. In a context where both parents and teachers noted signs of distress and psychological consequences from the conflict, these activities will respond to the children’s wellbeing needs through an integrated approach combining education and protection interventions. The activities will also seek the participation of parents and community members to contribute to reinforce family and community support systems and foster an enabling and supportive environment for young people, aiming at building resilience and social cohesion.
To strengthen the capacity of teachers, education personnel and sector members to deliver a timely and coordinated education response (sector Objective 3), ACTED will conduct capacity-building of teachers on education, classroom management skills and on child protection. Doing so aims to address the issues of underqualification and underperformance of teachers reported in the assessment, in order to reinforce the quality of teaching in formal schools. This activity will also cope with the negative effects of the conflict on teaching staff, which limits their ability to create an inclusive and caring environment, by addressing issues related to their wellbeing and their emotional and psychosocial condition.
This action offers the opportunity to complement and expand existing programmes where ACTED has a demonstrated technical capacity in the East of Libya. To implement this action, ACTED will rely on its on-the-ground experience implementing education and child-protection projects in the East of Libya through its 2016 UNICEF-funded project, which operated child friendly spaces and provided psychosocial support (PSS) in six different areas of Benghazi. ACTED is also enhancing the resilience of and strengthening the protective environment of conflict affected children in Benghazi through its current UNICEF education and child protection project.

1 - Needs

The scale and intensity of the protracted humanitarian crisis continues to increase in Libya, substantially affecting the well-being and mental health of children exposed to severe protection risks. Although there has largely been a return to formal education following the end of the Derna conflict in February 2019, the low quality of public education, the lack of recreational activities, and limited availability of psycho-social support have compounded the problem and hindered the development of conflict-affected children. ACTED’s EiE Needs Assessment conducted in December 2019 reveals clear gaps in education service provision as well as child protection needs in the wake of the armed conflict in Derna – echoing the strategic objectives set by the education and protection sectors for 2020. Educational facilities were destroyed in the conflict and those functioning have substandard infrastructure. A lack of qualified teaching staff, overcrowded classrooms and poor classroom management negatively affect the attainment of students. The closure of schools for eight or more months during the conflict in 2018-2019 left children in Derna behind the curriculum for their age. 22% of households had one or more school-aged child out of education, with the cost of education constituting the most important barrier to education. Children with disabilities and non-Libyan children face stigma and bullying in schools. The psychological impact of the conflict on children is evident. 87% of households with children stated they had noticed signs of distress in their children during the conflict. Parents, teachers and students spoke to the ongoing psychological impact of the conflict on children after the end of hostilities, who also lack recreational spaces and activities. IDPs face the highest level of HH vulnerability with a lower ability to meet basic needs and higher adoption of negative coping strategies. At HH-level, results of the assessment were disaggregated by gender of head of HH. This revealed that enrolment of children in education is higher among female-headed HHs (90% had all children enrolled) than male-headed HHs (75%). Access to non-formal education, however, was lower among female-headed HHs (11% access non-formal education opportunities for their children) than male-headed HHs (18%). The barriers for girls to access education were explored in FGDs and the HH survey. Teachers identified that transportation for girls to school can be a protection risk and this can lead to families preventing girls from attending school given the perceived risk of public transport to reach schools. Female students reported feeling unsafe when travelling to or from school on foot in the absence of available transport and being exposed to harassment.ACTED’s EiE Needs Assessment also reveals that: -Parents recommended the maintenance and rehabilitation of school facilities. -87% of HHs with children stated they had noticed signs of distress in their children during the period of conflict in Derna. This was high across displacement groups, with 94% of non-displaced HHs reporting signs of distress in their children, 92% of IDPs and 84% of returnees. -Parents identified the negative effects of the conflict on teaching staff which limits their ability to create an inclusive and caring environment and students identified violence of teachers towards students. -Among HHs in Sheiha with school-age children, 22% had one or more child not enrolled in education, with the remaining 78% of HHs reporting all children enrolled. 25% of HHs currently access non-formal education such as remedial or catch-up classes. -Teachers reported that children with disabilities face bullying, isolation and hostility from other students and acknowledged that teachers do not give students with disabilities specific consideration.

2 - Activities

ACTED will conduct trainings for non-CP actors, targeting teachers and school personnel, to provide them with key skills and knowledge—notably on child protection, classroom management, positive discipline, gender-sensitive pedagogy, curriculum planning, child rights, inclusion of children with disabilities, and basic psychosocial support.ACTED will also provide vulnerable children, including out-of-school and displaced boys and girls with catch-up classes. ACTED seeks to bridge the learning gaps created by conflict-related disrupted access to education. The content of the classes will be identified by the schoolteachers in line with the curriculum taught in schools and adapted to the age range of the participating children.ACTED will also give the opportunity for conflict-affected children to access psychosocial support and recreational activities. ACTED will establish a range of appropriate, structured, age and gender-specific recreational activities directed to vulnerable children, in which psychosocial support (PSS) and social and emotional learning (SEL) activities will be integrated. ACTED and CSO partners will conduct awareness raising sessions with parents, caregivers and children from the selected schools to sensitize beneficiaries on key issues pertaining to education and child protection, as well as to support building acceptance of the project and participation of beneficiaries. They will also emphasize the issue of children with disabilities’ integration in formal education. They may also address topics such as: prevention and protection of Violence Against Children (VAC), gender-sensitive parenting and education, mine risk prevention, positive coping mechanisms by both parents and teachers, health, and nutrition.Finally, ACTED will rehabilitate and repair damaged schools in conflict-affected areas to address the institutional, physical and infrastructural barriers to safe and protective learning environment.

4 - Protection risk analysis

Risk 1: The Security Situation in Libya Prevents Access to Beneficiaries and Endangers Field Staff: As the security situation remains unpredictable in Libya, a sudden escalation of the conflict could limit the access to areas of implementation. Should the situation escalate, it is possible that direct implementation would no longer be possible without risk to staff or beneficiaries. Travel disruption remains a possibility--periodic outbreaks of violence of all kinds (tribal, military, political, extremist, criminal) have been known to greatly increase the danger of travel between cities in Libya, or sometimes temporarily block travel entirely.Risk 2: Access Constraints Posed by Local Actors: There is a risk that local authorities place constraints on programming, reducing access to target areas. While ACTED has so far been able to secure permissions for access, both local and national authorities are rather unstable in Libya, and regular changes in leadership may affect the attitude of local authorities towards international NGOs and their activities even if agreements were previously signed. Risk 3: Risk of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (SEA). The involvement of humanitarian workers in acts of SEA is a serious violation of the ‘do no harm’ principle, and humanitarian organizations’ obligation to protect populations affected by crises. Risk 4: Data protection: Upholding the beneficiaries’ rights to protection of their personal data remains of paramount importance. Personal data must be safeguarded from unauthorized access and unlawful processing or disclosure, as well as accidental loss, modification or destruction.

5 - Mitigation measures

R1. ACTED dedicated security staff continuously monitor conflict developments in order to anticipate access concerns and modify procedures in advance. ACTED has in place standard operating procedures designed to minimize the exposure of staff and beneficiaries to security threats. Should changes in conflict dynamics cause a deterioration of the security situation, threatening the safety of staff or beneficiaries, ACTED will potentially adapt the program if needed in coordination (whether delivering assistance through partners, proxies, or by shifting the area of activities to more secure locations).R2. ACTED liaison staff and management actively works to develop positive relationships with authorities on both the local and national levels to obtain the necessary authorizations, as to reduce the likelihood of such measures impacting ACTED programs. Nevertheless, in the case that local authorities prevent access to the designated areas of intervention, ACTED will potentially adapt the program if needed after consultation with the relevant donor.R3. ACTED’s CRM provides a mechanism for beneficiaries or community members to report any instances of SEA on the part of ACTED staff or partners. Where possible, ACTED will ensure a gender-balanced group of staff for all program field teams. ACTED also has a Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse policy that all staff have to abide by, and any violation of this policy results in immediate termination of staff. Furthermore, through the GBV sub-sector PSEA task force, ACTED will facilitate PSEA training for its staff as well as the staff of its partners, in order to mitigate the risk of sexual exploitation and abuse occurring as a result of ACTED activities in Libya. R4: Access to all sensitive specific data will be strictly limited to those staff members directly involved in processing it and working copies will be deleted once no longer needed.

6.1 - Other accountability mechanisms

Participation, influence, and feedback of beneficiaries: ACTED’s ACTED AME Unit will conduct FGDs with parents, caregivers, and community members in order to receive feedback and suggestions and improve the project activities accordingly to ensure that none of the activities are indirectly harming any beneficiaries. Consultations will be held in the start-up phase of the project to adapt the project design and the implementation methodology to the priorities of beneficiaries and communities. ACTED will also continuously monitor the quality and effectiveness of project deliveries including by collecting feedback from beneficiaries directly and through the complaints and response mechanism (CRM), ensuring that the proposed action remains relevant to the needs of beneficiaries throughout the project implementation. ACTED’s CRM will be a key means of ensuring accountability towards beneficiaries and external stakeholders. The CRM is managed by AME staff to ensure independence from program teams and for which confidentiality is a fundamental principle. All complaints are confidentially registered and recorded by the CRM Focal Point in a password-protected database and categorized based on the level of sensitivity. The CRM Focal Point leads to resolve complaints raised, with the involvement of program, AME or senior management staff, depending on the case and its severity. Information is shared on a strictly need-to-know basis and restricted as much as possible. The CRM will be systematically communicated to all beneficiaries, including the children participating in the center, their parents and/or caregivers, and community members participating in awareness sessions, in order to ensure that awareness of the CRM is high. Within this project, ACTED will actively work to ensure that the CRM is able to be accessed by any parent or caregiver regardless of their level of literacy, to ensure that marginalized groups can access it—including female headed households. This served as an active line of communication for ACTED to receive any grievances from community members regarding the implementation of the project activities or any suggestions for changes. These calls therefore directly translate into recommendations to programs for improvement of programming, and the resolution of such complaints communicated directly back to the beneficiaries.Accountability to at-risk beneficiaries: Although the project does not aim to directly provide specialized services to at-risk boys, girls, women, and men, at risk children will be identified through ongoing protection monitoring of children by qualified staff throughout the project duration.To help ensure that at risk children have access to a range of specialized services, ACTED will seek operational synergies and linkages with local humanitarian CSOs operating in Derna. ACTED will ensure the establishment of referral pathways to strengthen the coordination at the local level and facilitate the exchange of information as well as to further build the acceptance of ACTED as a key actor for the humanitarian response in the area. ACTED has already created a network of protection CSOs that it collaborates with in Derna, through its 2019 UNHCR-funded project, wherein ACTED provided capacity-building to protection actors in the East of Libya, including with CSOs and representatives from the municipality of Derna, Civil Society Commission, and Libyan Red Crescent Society. These local actors will continue to support the implementation of the proposed intervention.ACTED will also explore opportunities for referrals and synergies with other international humanitarian actors currently present or planning to operate in Derna, including DCA (mine risk education), PUI (medical assistance), ICRC (cash assistance and NFIs) and NRC (ICLA and shelter).
