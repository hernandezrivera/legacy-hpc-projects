Project Summary

This project will encompass the deployment of an integrated mine action response across accessible areas in Syria.
Dense, widespread contamination across Syria – both conventional and improvised – is preventing the safe return of displaced populations, increasing risks to physical safety, restricting access for humanitarian actors and disrupting early recovery efforts where hostilities have ceased. The prevalent use of improvised landmines and booby traps by ISIS has further escalated the threat to Syrian populations. Increased access to areas formerly under ISIS control, together with largescale Internally Displaced People (IDP) movements, has led to a significant increase in civilian casualties from improvised devices and Explosive Ordnance (EO), highlighting the threat posed by contamination. Items are commonly found in civilian homes, community infrastructure and agricultural areas. Across Syria, information on contamination is limited due to restricted mine action activities, however it is expected that following years of war and multiple military offensives the contamination from conventional as well as improvised devices is high. Contamination is likely to be found across all areas including agricultural land, residential areas and urban settings.
This project will aim to deploy an integrated mine action approach for WoS where access is permissible. The project will include manual and mechanical clearance operations, where operations will have a humanitarian focus to remove and destroy explosive items that present a risk to life and limb or is blocking access to community structures. All tasks will be prioritized internally against determined indictors to determine those with the highest humanitarian impact. National teams will be trained and deployed with International oversight to ensure activities are conducted safely and to International mine action standards. The project will also deploy Community liaison (CL) teams to deliver Risk Education (RE) sessions in order to promote safe behaviours, train community focal points to deliver safety messages to wider communities and provide RE to humanitarian workers. The CL teams and the manual clearance teams will also conduct Non-Technical and Technical Survey respectively to help provide a more detailed picture of the contamination profile to guide a safer humanitarian response and future clearance efforts.

Needs

According to the 2019 Syria Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO), 10.2 million people continue to be at risk and exposed to explosive hazards across Syria, with higher risks in urban areas. Displacement patterns across Syria remain extremely fluid, across NES the majority of displacements during 2017/2018 occurred within the governorate of origin. Across NWS, chronic displacement, increasing the risk from contamination, has occurred in cycles, correlating to escalation in conflict. The overall contamination profile in Syria is varied and largely unmapped. Restricted access has resulted in a limited mine action response, especially in NWS. Limited activities are being conducted in these areas and are mainly focused on Risk Education (RE), contamination mapping and victim assistance through the assistance of partner organizations. In NES integrated mine action, including clearance and technical surveys, are possible through direct implementation. In NES, the contamination profile in areas previously under ISIS control consists of improvised landmines, booby traps and improvised UXO found in villages and agricultural lands. Many of these items are comprised of sensitive operating mechanisms and huge amounts of explosives, which are sensitive enough to be triggered by a child but powerful enough to disable a tank or destroy an entire building. Contamination in the form of booby traps is commonly found in civilian homes, schools and community infrastructure. These items are difficult to detect and present a huge risk to families hoping to return and rebuild their lives. To date, a total over 75,000 explosive items have been removed in NES, the majority of which have been of an improvised nature. This includes over 20,000 improvised landmines, 40,000 small arms and light weapons, over 2,000 improvised UXO and over 8,000 conventional UXO. In NWS, intensive and consistent clashes between armed forces, which include recurrent air bombardments, have resulted in conventional contamination across the north west. Furthermore, intra-opposition armed groups struggle have also lead to the use of conventional and improvised contamination to secure control of areas. Restricted access in these areas to international NGOs has meant no substantial contamination mapping has taken place to date and evidence remains largely anecdotal. The high levels of violence and localized conflict means the threat from contamination remains extremely high, especially for the large numbers of displaced communities, often facing multiple displacements, and returning communities. Community Liaison (CL) teams in Idleb and Aleppo have reported a total of 524 EO victims between May 2016 and November 2018, with the majority of accidents due to conventional UXO (31%), followed by conventional mines (27%) and cluster munitions (25%). Furthermore, the teams have gathered 126 basic contamination surveys since May 2016 from the villages where they have conducted RE sessions. The majority of the contamination has been found in access roads to homes and agricultural land (33%), followed by contamination found in agricultural/pasture land (29%), then private homes (18%), then “other” areas (12%) and lastly in community infrastructure such as schools, clinics and water/electrical plants (8%). During conflict and displacement, patterns of mobility and lifestyles evolve, thereby forcing women and men to redefine core aspects of their identities. For instance, women may be forced to take on more income-generating activities and responsibilities, be more active in the public sphere and make family decisions, in addition to continuing to take care of their families. While this adds to women’s vulnerabilities during conflict and displacement, it can also present an opportunity for empowerment through employment opportunities.As stated, in areas where hostilities have ceased, women, girls, boys and men are affected by contamination with explosive hazards in different ways.

Implementation of activities

The following mine action capacity - depending on the accessibility - will be deployed across Syria; Activity 1: Deploy a national clearance capacity to remove and destroy conventional and improvised devices in urban and agricultural settings The teams will work with local authorities and communities to identify areas for prioritised clearance, including transit routes, critical infrastructure, urban and agricultural settings. Where possible the organisation ensures to recruit both men and women into the roles of deminers, ensuring HR policies and operating contexts are sensitive and tailored to encourage gender equality. All deminers will be trained to international mine action standards. Clearance teams are also trained to conduct Technical survey of suspected Hazardous areas. This will assist in prioritising areas for full clearance and contribute to the limited mine action contamination profile across Syria. Activity 2: Deploy a mechanical asset capacity to support manual clearance activities Obstructions in front of buildings will also be removed to provide safe access for manual searchers. Mechanical assets increase the efficiency and effectiveness of operations, increasing clearance rates by twofold in some settings. In area where there has been high levels of destruction, mechanical assets are key in enabling access and removing rubble. Furthermore, mechanical assets increase the efficiency of clearance rates, especially in agricultural settings. Activity 3: Deploy a community Liaison capacity to deliver Risk Education activities and conduct contamination surveys directly and through partner organisations The teams will deliver tailored RE sessions to conflict affected communities, including boys, girls, men and women. Depending on the location of operations, the teams will prioritise different age and genders following analysis of known victim data information and experience of the context in terms of who is most at risk. All CL teams consist of one man and one women to ensure a gender balance and that all sub sectors of the community can be reached. Where possible the teams will also liaise with other NGOs to provide safety briefings and RE sessions to support a safe humanitarian response. The teams will also conduct Non-Technical survey or basic contamination surveys depending on the level of access and training/oversight that is possible. Non-Technical Survey is enables a more thorough assessment and identification of the risk, creating polygons of the suspected hazard which can then be reported to clearance teams for future clearance. All information from contamination surveys are shared with the wider humanitarian community and various hub protection working groups to help guide a safer humanitarian response. The CL teams will also work closely with other humanitarian actors to respond to suspected areas of contamination that may be blocking aid relief efforts.

Protection Risk Analysis

Integrated mine activities in Syria are a pre requisition to enable safe delivery of humanitarian relief and early recovery efforts. This project will address several cross cutting protection themes. Mine Action capacity is extremely limited across Syria. This project will support building the technical capacity of a national capacity in clearance and Risk Education activities for future clearance activities. Explosive hazards present a huge protection risk to IDPs, returning communities, NGO workers and local authorities. This project will support the removal of explosive hazards that are currently blocking safe access to critical infrastructure, preventing a safe returns process and presenting immediate risks to those transiting through contaminated areas. Furthermore this project will work with other INGOs to identify areas where humanitarian relief activities have been blocked by the presence of contamination. The Community Liaison capacity will work to raise awareness of contamination both improvised and conventional to help promote safe behaviours amongst the affected population and encourage communities to actively report contamination that is identified. The main protection risk as outlined in the protection risk analysis is conducing RE activities to at risk groups ( 4.1) is that “ 1.1 Familiarization may cause the civilian populace to become overconfident or complacent to the threat of explosive hazards “. The project will mitigate against this through the emphasis of “Stop, Warn and Report” messaging during the delivery of RE session and during the training of community focal points. This will be monitored through the internal quality assurance surveys which are taken after an RE sessions to ensure the quality of the sessions and the changes in behaviors from participants. The teams provide regular refresher trainings to ensure that the field teams understand the importance “Stop, Warn and Report” messaging and as means to monitor the effective communication and training of RE messages. In addition, the CL teams will aim to deliver RE sessions to areas that are presumed to be contaminated in order to emphasis the importance of safe behaviors and will target IDPs and host communities to promote safe behaviors amongst all sectors of the community. Lastly, the training of community focal points to deliver RE messages once the teams depart from an area will allow the spread of safety message and promotion of safe behaviors after the RE sessions have been delivered to the communities.The risks associated with performing clearance activities as outlined by the hub are detailed in “4.2 Conduct survey and explosive hazard removal”; of these the main risks which will affect this project are “2.5 5 Decision to conduct clearance in particular communities is perceived to be based on discrimination” and “2.6 Risk that local authorities may dictate mine action priorities”. Risks will be mitigated by sending community liaison teams before and after clearance to ensure clear communications on mine action activities to local communities. The CL teams will also be constantly engaging with local community to maintain awareness of local ethnic sectarian balances which will be used to assist in the prioritization of clearance activities. Furthermore, balanced local recruitment is promoted in diverse areas in order to mitigate against any issues of biases. In regards to the prioritization of clearance activities, the hazardous areas reports are compiled by the CL teams as a way to include community participation in the priority setting for clearance tasks. Furthermore, all of clearance tasks are identified and targeted in line with humanitarian principles which are explained clearly when cooperating with local councils.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Access routes used in country become inaccessible due to permissions being denied or because of security considerationsAlternative routes to operational areas are identified and assessed on an ongoing basis. Extreme weather conditions restrict operational delivery and reduce productivityWeather has been taken into account at project planning stage and alternative activities will be planned if required. Local authority stance towards permissions to operate in and around operational areas changesStrong relations have been developed with local authorities across accessible areas in Syria, including relevant Civil Councils. The programme will maintain to keep a close relationship to help prioritize areas for clearance and keep the local authorities updated on all activities. Security situation deteriorates in area of operationsThe programme has significant experience of working inside Syria and other conflict-affected countries, as does its partner organisations. Thorough security and emergency procedures have been developed and implemented according to the context. The project design mitigates the risk of not being able to deliver objectives in full through;• ‘live’ Security policies and procedures in place that are being reviewed on ongoing basis, including medevac, hibernation and relocation/evacuation policies and procedures• Regional and country specific security managersThorough risk and security assessments are conducted before deploying teams into any new areas of operations.
