Project Summary

FAO will contribute to Nutrition Cluster efforts to reduce malnutrition prevalence among children, women and their households by integrating nutrition-sensitive approaches across its food security and livelihood responses within the Food Security Cluster.
More specifically, FAO will integrate tailored nutrition awareness materials into livelihood programmes targeting farmers, agropastorists, pastoralists and fishers to improve dietary diversity and encourage the production and consumption of fresh and nutrient rich foods (targeting at least 50 000 households). Education campaigns will be led by empowered village-level nutrition champions, who will disseminate acquired knowledge and work with their communities to improve dietary diversity.
In addition, FAO will complement Nutrition Cluster food and nutrition education in rural communities, including through training and demonstrations on food hygiene, food preparation, food storage, food preservation and small-scale homestead processing tailored to women and with a focus on nutrient retention. Knowledge, Attitude and Practice (KAP) analysis of fish, milk and meat products will be conducted through a nutrition lens in five key locations to develop and fine-tune nutrition sensitive programming. In addition, context-specific materials and campaigns will be developed for food waste and food loss prevention, reduction and management in both rural and urban areas. 50 small school gardens will be established, where children (age 10+) learn about the food path, including the provision of vegetable gardening kits, micro irrigation systems and teachers’ notes. FAO will continue efforts to promote fish consumption through at least 20 tastings, cooking demos and nutrition education campaigns.
Gender will be mainstreamed in all activities (e.g. each village will have 1 female and 1 male nutrition champion). Also, due attention will be paid to women’s engagement in project activities are not a burden or take time away from other caretaking and other key roles.
This profile links to FAO resilience activities (including as part of the FAO/WFP/UNICEF Joint Resilience Acton 2018-2022) which ultimately aims to improve household food security and nutrition through integrated approaches.

Needs

Unacceptably high levels of acute malnutrition persist in Somalia despite current interventions. In the past year, the overall level of acute malnutrition has improved from Critical to Serious (from a median GAM of 17.4% down to 14%) comparing assessment findings from Gu 2017 and Gu 2018 (FSNAU). This is attributed to improvements in food security, reduced outbreak of disease and intensive interventions targeting malnutrition. However, 588 200 children are acutely malnourished – with nearly 1 in 5 expected to be severely malnourished – between August 2018 and July 2019. High concentrations of acutely malnourished people are in IDP settlements. Malnutrition is very prevalent in the rural areas especially within pastoral and agro-pastoral areas due to loss of productive assets.The primary focus of the Nutrition Cluster response is to save lives through therapeutic and supplementary feeding, in-kind food distributions and vital services. At the same time, it is critical to reduce the incidence of acute malnutrition and prevent relapses. A concerted effort is needed to address the underlying causes of malnutrition, across sectors. In rural areas, there is enormous scope to address key drivers of malnutrition including through the food and market environments by increasing the production, local availability, consumption of diverse nutrient-rich foods (e.g. fish and vegetables), and nutrition education for behavior change, among other key activities. It is essential to ensure that impact pathways are traced to allow for the identification of best practices and challenges during implementation.

How is the project linked to durable solutions/resilience?

Protection is mainstreamed throughout the project cycle - from design to community sensitization and use of feedback mechanisms to address problems/complaints as they arise. Grievance mechanisms are communicated by implementing partners to council members, elders and the community, as well as through face-to-face sessions with beneficiaries. FAO Somalia also uses radio campaigns, ad-hoc Call Centre exercises, communication through field monitors and Third Party Monitors, and its Hotline number(displayed on vouchers and sent via SMS). Other valuable feedback and complaints comefrom the radio Ergo’s Common Feedback Project (CFP) initiated by the HumanitarianCountry Team of Somalia. These feedback loops inform programming to ensure thatassistance and services both reach and properly engage vulnerable populations. The project links toand actively engages government, community & other local networks. This includes, forexample, the active participation of local government, community animal health workers(CAHWs) and Livestock Professional Associations (LPAs) in animal health campaigns; aswell as the sourcing of quality inputs from established networks of local seed traders andagrovet dealers. These activities and partners link to longer term projects, including forexample continuous capacity development & strengthening of CAHWs and LPAs, while theseeds distributed under humanitarian projects are local high performing seed varietiesthat have been recovered and multiplied through years of development work. To achievesustainability, technical training is provided for each activity to IPs, and to beneficiaries onhow to maximize their use of project inputs, among other topics. FAO also assesses the efficacy of training using the Kirkpatrick model, which evaluates: (i) immediate utilization of training, (ii) knowledgegained through testing, (iii) application of knowledge gained, & (iv) wider impact of thetraining. The proposed activities link to FAO Somalia's wider Resilience Programme andFAO/WFP/UNICEF Joint Resilience Action. FAO will actively seek to sequence, layer andintegrate funded activities with those of UN/NGO partners to maximize impact.address the multifaceted needs of affected households.

Protection risk analysis & Mitigating measures
Preventing risks of exclusion based on displacement

FAO's beneficiary target includes rural IDPs, who are especially vulnerable to exclusionas rural livelihoods rely on land use and other natural resources. FAO will adapt cashand/or livelihood packages to rural IDPs' distinct needs and surrounding realities.Other highly vulnerable groups include destitute pastoralists who lost all livestockassets, migrated to coastal areas and want to engage in fisheries livelihood activities.FAO deliberately targets both IDPs and members of host communities, which contributes to reduce social tensions and build cohesion. Communication messages and community mobilization campaigns in target communities explicitly state the vulnerable groups targeted by the intervention, including IDPs. Recent M&E findings show that livelihood support provided to returnees and host communitieshelped in achieving social cohesion and harmony, as reported in focus group discussions. A key objective of FAO’s emergency livelihood programme is to prevent displacement in the first place by delivering timely assistance into remote rural areas. Livelihood activities are designed to protect and boost food production and nutrition through in-kind and technical support, providing rural families with the means and incentive to remain productive on their land and within their community. All cash programmes are linked to productive livelihood activities – whether through cash-for-work on rural infrastructure or Cash+ programmes that pair UCTs with seasonal livelihood inputs.

Preventing risks of exclusion based on gender and age

Activities at the village level ensure active participation of both women and men (e.g. nutrition champions are both male and female). Young children are also key participants in achieving project outcomes, through their participation in school gardens. As part of its Call Centre surveys, FAO Somalia verifies and ensures that beneficiariesidentified in the selection process meet the selection criteria (female, male, age, IDP,household heads, etc.). FAO’s beneficiary selection criteria ensures that at least 30percent of beneficiaries are women-headed households across the programme. Selectprogrammes – such as Cash+ Livestock – are designed to respond to the specificneeds and protection issues of pastoral women and other vulnerable groups duringpeak times of vulnerability: i.e. during seasonal migrations periods when women,children, elderly and other vulnerable family members stay behind with few smallanimals to meet their needs. All Cash+ Livelihood interventions are designed toenhance the engagement of women in productive livelihood activities (e.g. vegetablegardening, small livestock rearing and fish handling/processing). FAO’s fieldmonitoring process through its own staff or TPM assesses and reports impact bygender.

Mitigating the impact of armed conflict and violence

Insecurity caused by armed groups and clan conflicts pose significant threats to thewell-being, nutrition and food security of affected populations. Insecurity is a main reason whypeople abandon their livelihoods and migrate in search of safety. In contrast, foodand nutrition security, strong social networks and better livelihood opportunities are key reasonspeople opt to stay where they are. Insecure and conflict-affected rural areas are largelycut off from aid. FAO has a wide network of local NGO partners and fleld monitors whoare embedded in remote rural areas and able to deliver assistance to people in need.Many operations must and can be managed remotely. FAO's risk management includesrobust due diligence verification to prevent diversion of project funds to illegal entities,including Al Shabbab or other armed groups, although risk remain. Food insecurity and malnutrition can be both a cause and a consequence of violence in rural areas. Conflict-sensitive livelihood programmescontribute to addressing both sides. Interventions to sustain rural livelihoods reducefood insecurity, malnutrition and poverty in conflict affected areas, while also protecting at riskpopulations (especially youth) from joining extremist groups or illicit activities (e.g.piracy), undergoing informal migration or adopting other harmful coping strategies.Livelihood and integrated nutrition programmes within this HRP build social cohesion around sharedresources and services – bringing together men and women from different villages,clans and sub-clans. Other examples of social networks in rural areas includefarmer/pastoral/fisher field and life schools, water user associations and women-ledproducer groups.
