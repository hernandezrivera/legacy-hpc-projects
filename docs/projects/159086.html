Project Summary

The project targets 30,700 individuals (3795 men, 11775 women, 4875 boys, 10255 girls) in Morobo, Juba (CES) and Kapoeta South Counties (EES), using an integrated approach including health, GBV/protection and education. The project will provide equal access to safe water and sanitation for both genders, enable conflict-affected population to use safer hygiene practices to reduce WASH related risk and morbidity/mortality among vulnerable individuals and malnourished children, to break the vicious circle of diarrheal diseases/nematodes associate with malnutrition.
To reduce the burden for women and girls associated with water collection, 10 water points near Child Friendly Spaces, identified together with GBV/child protection partners, will be rehabilitated in Morobo County. Water Management Committees (one per b/o) trained in water resource management/maintenance and HHWT use, and trained hand-pump mechanics will assist, providing access to clean and safe water to 2,000 individuals (people per b/o 200:1-Sphere standards).
For nutrition integration, 10 water points at OTP/TSFP and 5 community boreholes will be rehabilitated and 5 community ones constructed in Kapoeta South, in consultation with women/girls through FGDs led by LCED and GBV partners, providing access to safe drinking water to 2,000 individuals, trained in hygiene promotion, including importance of improved hygiene at household level and health impact on the status of the child, conducted by trained community hygiene promoters. Also, household packages for famine prevention will be distributed to 1,000 caretakers of SAM/MAM children, PLW and any other at-risk group, selected in consultation with nutrition/health and GBV partners.
Improvement of hygiene conditions in all targeted Counties will focus on areas with high GAM rates, risk of famine, and cholera/EVD outbreaks, targeting 1917 vulnerable HHs, including caretakers of CMAM children identified with the assistance of health/nutrition partners, with distribution of hygiene kits (water storage containers, water purifiers, soap, etc.). This will be paired with hygiene promotion using the PHAST/CHAST approach to improve hygiene behaviors, to reduce diarrheal diseases and encouraging effective management of WASH services. 60 trained Community Hygiene Promoters (20 per County) will conduct house-to-house visits and hygiene awareness sessions in health and nutrition clinics, on diarrhea prevention, hand washing, MHM, infant and young child feeding (IYCF), water treatment, food storage and waste disposal. Additionally, key messaging including HIV/AIDS, EVD and cholera prevention will be passed on during distribution and trainings, as an EPR mainstreaming.
WASH-GBV mitigation will be prioritized in all the targeted counties, after conduction of a GBV risk analysis, with protection/GVB partners on ground. 10 latrines (5 in Juba, 5 in Morobo and 10 in Kapoeta South) near health centers will be rehabilitated and 10 constructed, in consultation with women/girls through FGDs led by LCED and GBV partners, making them gender-sensitive (separated and with lockable doors) in order to improve sanitation for 500 individuals (people per latrine 20-50:1-Sphere standards), including provision of hand-washing facilities in each institution. The siting, rehabilitation and repairs of WASH facilities will be done through thorough GBV risk analysis (FDG with women/girls, conducted by female staff with a GBV/protection partners) to ensure gender-appropriate and need-tailored interventions, minimizing risks associated with access, safety and security, including SEA. 4800 women and 3200 girls of reproductive age will be trained in menstrual health management and receive MHM kits, enabling them to maintain their personal hygiene, privacy and dignity, and improve school attendance. Additionally, 200 women in Juba County will be trained on how to make reusable sanitary pads for sustainability purpose, through a Cash-For-Work modality.

1 - Needs

5.5 million people in South Sudan lack access to safe clean water, due to the long conflict, displacement, destruction of infrastructures, and health, education and WASH facilities, with women and girls being the most affected by lack of access to water and sanitation (HNO, Oct 2019). According to WHO (Aug 2019) 600 million people practice open defecation in South Sudan. Only 29% of the South Sudan population has access to a borehole, tap stand or a water yard within a maximum 30-minute distance without facing protection concerns, while the remaining has to walk long distance to access safe water or rely on surface water/unprotected water sources (HNO monitoring report, 2019). More than 90% practice open defecation due to lack of access or cultural behaviors, while only 13% of the population has access to WASH items (FSNMS, 2018). According to the DTM Round6, 210,463 IDPs and 129,144 returnees are located in Central Equatoria State, and 69,339 IDPs and 98,190 returnees in Eastern Equatoria State. The increased of displaced population, both static and in transit, has exerted pressure to already limited resources among the host communities hence limiting the coping abilities to withstand the shocks.WASH and health needs are high in Greater Kapoeta, especially pastoral areas, where poor access to latrines, safe boreholes and health facilities increase the risk of waterborne diseases (REACH, Sept 2019). Widespread open defecation poses a high health risks, particularly considering cholera hotspots including Juba and Kapoeta South Counties (WHO, Aug 2019). More so, Kapoeta South has a global acute malnutrition rate of 16.7%, indicating a critical need to provide WASH services at OTP/TSFP centers and provision of famine prevention packages to SAM/MAM households.In Morobo County and Greater Kapoeta, there are few operational boreholes in rural areas, destroyed by the conflict/not properly maintained, as well as perceived lack of access to safe water points (REACH, Sept 2019). This has caused reduced per-capita water consumption (under 10 L/person/day), increased use of unprotected water sources, increased risk of waterborne diseases, increased time for water collection at the expense of other essential tasks, increased conflict over communal resources, and higher GBV risk (abduction of young girls and boys, forceful marriages, killing, rape).Most displaced households in Greater Equatoria are in dire need of basic hygiene kits (including water storage containers, water purifiers, soap, etc), due to displacement, looting and deterioration of items, lack of access to the markets and little purchasing power. Hygiene practices, and especially hand-washing at critical moments are reportedly low across CES and EES (REACH, Sept 2019). This increases risk of cholera and EVD outbreaks and highlights the need of spreading preventive hygiene messages and establishing hand-washing facilities.High rates of open defecation in the road sides and bushes poses a great health risk and a security concern to women, girls and the community at large, while lack of MHM Kits for women and adolescent girls of reproductive age (limited by lack of functional markets or purchasing power by these marginalized groups), pose sanitation and protection concerns that need an immediate remedial action. The critical health needs, insufficient food access, high cases of SAM and GAM, and poor WASH services may contribute high levels of morbidity and mortality if not responded rapidly. Inadequate WASH services contribute not only to disease outbreak, but also to increased SAM cases. Lack of improved sanitation and limited knowledge of hygiene practices contributes to poor feeding habits of children leading to malnutrition. Data from IOM, OCHA and partners revealed that displaced persons are settled in areas without clean water or improved sanitation, and have little opportunity to practice proper hygiene in what turns out to be flooded areas for most of the year.

8.18 - Please indicate how the risks selected above will be mitigated.

Community consultations will be conducted including gender specific FGDs, to identify gender specific needs, and encourage vulnerable/voiceless groups like women to actively participate in the design and implementation of projects, ensuring that that GBV risks are identified and mitigated.Rehabilitated latrines will have locks and stable walls, and provision of washing facility at health centers will create behavior change, preventing occurrence of wash-related infections. All-female FGDs co-chaired with GBV partner will allow to include views of women on siting and design of WASH facilities and on their participation in project activities, including the appropriate way of providing feedback. Rehabilitation of water points will consider the need to reduce travelling distance and time spent, reducing exposure to GBV risk, also through messaging by field staff/volunteers encouraging women and girls to fetch water in groups.Safety audits with protection/GBV partners, local authorities and WASH cluster, will identify safe activity sites for staff and beneficiaries. Distribution will be done in consultation with women to identify safe & accessible distribution sites and schedules to prevent any SGBV risks related to access. To mitigate any other GBV risks, a PSEA policy shall be part of the training for staff and community, including sharing with the community GBV referral pathways, PSEA reporting channels and CFM. Privacy and safety will be key during MHM kit distribution, to avoid stigmatization in the access to serviced, and distribution/demonstration shall be done by LCED female staff/volunteers. Lastly, women will be prioritized for Cash-For-Work activities, and their participation will be ensured by hiring them as WMC members, hygiene promoters, volunteers (60% female). Similarly, 10% host community members will be hired in those roles, to prevent conflicts with beneficiaries, while the community at large will benefit from asset rehabilitation.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

Community consultations will be conducted at inception phase of the project, as integral part of the multi-sector analysis in the targeted project locations, including community leaders and FGDs with representatives of the vulnerable groups (women/youth/elderly/PWDs), to ensure all views are captured and agree on the appropriate CFM. LCED staff will also sensitize the community on what to expect from the project, their roles, responsibilities and participation modalities, so that they are aware on what to provide feedback on. In each location LCED will form a Village Representation Committee and a Water Management Committee, including representatives of women/youth/PWDs and other vulnerable groups, IDP/returnees and host community leaders, and sub-chiefs. WMCs and VRCs provide great insight for activity planning and project design, definition of targeting criteria, Complaint and Feedback Mechanism, as well as context-specific information, changes, and conflict early warnings. Their composition will ensure that each vulnerable and minority group is taken under consideration. The VRCs will be responsible for ensuring that beneficiaries and community are aware of the priorities for the selection of beneficiaries for distribution, and how vulnerable households have been verified, and will also assist LCED in the distribution of WASH items (including a complaint desk).Community Hygiene Promoters, VRCs and WMCs will continuously report to LCED on the progress of the project activities, and beneficiaries will be encouraged to provide feedback during the monitoring activities on quality, satisfaction, impact and conduct of LCED staff. LCED will make efforts to ensure women participation in the monitoring of project activities including providing feedback. Finally, both VRCs and WMCs will assist the LCED team by mediating in case of discontent among the population in regards to the prioritization of the beneficiaries, quality of items, and any other complaint raised.
