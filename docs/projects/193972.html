Project Summary

This project aims to provide timely multi-sector life-saving assistance to crisis-affected people (IDPs and host communities of Red Sea and Blue Nile) through WASH, Education, FSL, GBV, to 96,300 individuals (29000 women, 20300 men,23000 boys and 24,000 girls). 96,300 for (WASH), 16500 (Protection), 10000 (Education), and 9300 FSL. IAS will target the IDPS affected by the current conflict and the tribal conflict prioritized and vulnerable communities in Blue Nile, (Damazine, Altadamoun, Wad Almahi and Kurmuk localities) and Red Sea in (Port Sudan, Sawakin, Sinkat, Algunub, Tawker and Dordeib localities).
IAS will work in a complementary way to address WASH, Education, Protection and FSL to the same targeted students, boys and girls and their families, for schools, IAS will make sure the WASH and Protection gaps are addressed besides the Education where the child-friendly spaces, learning materials, water supply and sanitation and hygiene services will be provided to the targeted student and their families will be provided with Food Security and Livelihoods to support their living conditions. IAS will target vulnerable populations such as women, children, CWDs, and the elderly, considering gender-sensitive issues. Activities to be implemented with WASH will include the provision of water trucking, rehabilitation of 6 water yards including solarization, operation and maintenance of 10 water yards, drilling of 20 new boreholes equipped with hand pumps, rehabilitation of 30 hand pump, conducting water treatment and water quality monitoring to 20 water point, implementation of sanitation and hygiene to scale up the sanitation and promote the hygiene practices of at least 109,830 among communities and institutional set-ups in the targeted communities of Blue Nile and Red Sea, through education interventions IAS will aim to prioritize immediate critical life-saving education needs. Security, access to most vulnerable and marginalized girls and boys at risk of exclusion from education targeting 10,000 children(4500girs, 3500 boys 1000male1000female), about 2000 children with disabilities access child-friendly learning spaces,10000 will be supported with recreational materials,6000 access to safe drinking water in schools,10000 will benefiting from structured, school-based psychosocial support activities,6000 will be supported with teaching and learning materials, in addition 100 teachers/facilitators supported with monthly incentives. The protection intervention will be in GBV targeting 6800 (3060 women, 2040 men, 1020 girls and 680 boys), CP targeting 3500 (1400 boys, 1050 girls, 525 men and 525 women) and general protection targeting 6500 (2600 women, 1950 men 975 boys and 975 girls), including PSEA & provide PSS services to children with disabilities with a referral mechanism. Under the GBV activities, IAS will rehabilitate 4 Women centres, targeting 3000 vulnerable women and girls, conduct 10 awareness campaigns targeting 4000 vulnerable and conflict-affected by GBV at localities levels. Moreover, training of 4 CBPNs to support the delivery of the PSS services and referral pathway.
The FSL component aims to provide life-saving support to 9300 beneficiaries 4,180 Male & 5,120 Female) through improved access to agricultural inputs and services, provision of livelihoods for 1,000 Conflict-affected HHs (5000 beneficiaries (2,180 Male & 2,820 Female). The intervention aims to address immediate FSL needs through the direct provision of agricultural inputs, including the provision of quality assorted seeds, mainly legumes including chick pea, pigeon pea and ground nuts, besides vegetable seeds including okra, watermelon and tomatoes along with cereal seeds (sorghum & maize ), in addition to the provision of hand tools to increase reliance, dietary intake and diversification and will place special focus on women who are involved in farming activities in targeted areas. In addition, 1050 farmers will be targeted by training.

1 - Needs

Clashes between SAF and RSF have been ongoing since 15 April, several cities have faced heavy bombardments and clashes, and associated unexploded ordnance, causing hundreds of civilian deaths and casualties, as well as damage to critical water, health care and power infrastructure. The fighting has also reached West Darfur, South Darfur, North Darfur, South Kordofan, North Kordofan, Blue Nile, Kassala, and the Red Sea (HRP2023)Since the beginning of the armed conflict between the Sudan Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) in multiple locations of Sudan, including Khartoum, thousands of people have fled the capital city. Port Sudan, as the main port of the country, has witnessed a significant influx of displaced persons of different social conditions and nationalities resulting from the ongoing conflict in Red Sea There are more than 32,000 affected population displaced from Khartoum, 10,850 of them and more expected are staying in 18 gathering points within Port Sudan and 1300 individuals are in Swakin. A multi-sector needs assessment on the displaced populations in Port Sudan in gathering sites was conducted on 15 and 16 May to provide an overview of the current situation faced by refugees, vulnerable internally displaced persons (IDPs) and other displaced persons in the area.The mixed nature of profiles in gathering sites creates a need for screening mechanisms to identify and assess individual protection needs (including child protection concerns, allowing GBV survivors to come forward in safety and ensuring that those with international protection needs are all given information on asylum procedures). A number of those identified have expressed a need to return to their country of origin or to be relocated to third countries seeking safety. All such operations should be handled with respect to international guidelines and protection principles. No collective protection risks were noted in the gathering sites. Nevertheless, the sites are makeshift shelters in inhabitable locations such as clubs and community spaces. As such, privacy issues have been cited and there is a lack of adequate WASH facilities. Noting that those in the sites have recently suffered (secondary) displacement and have lingering psychological effects. By nature of the locations, concerns rise over the safety of women and children due to the lack of separate sleeping and WASH facilities. The overarching socioeconomic effects of conflict disproportionately affect those identified in the sites, as they lack the cash to provide for their basic needs of food, water, and sanitary items.Education: Displacement came at a time when final examinations at many levels were about to take placeAccording to the Education sector Red sea and Blue Nile one of most states need for establishing safe and child-friendly learning spaces, equipped with both learning and recreational supplies. Distribution of Education in Emergency supplies to both schools and safe learning spaces and support for the facilitators in the learning centers. Furthermore, providing psychosocial support through counselingFSL: Lack of access to food assistance and shortage of cash could have detrimental consequences to the different populations, as it could lead to negative coping mechanisms and malnutrition.According to HRP 2023 more than 360,000 IDPs, 65000 returnees and 14000 are refugees need for emergency support in Blue Nile, moreover, Blue Nile has witnessed Inter- communal fighting between the Hausa and Hamaj tribes. This led to the loss of lives from both parties, physical injuries, burning of houses, looting of household goods and destruction of public infrastructure. Several people in settlement towns were displaced.WFP expects the number of acutely food insecure to increase to 19.1 million people in the next 3-6 months if the conflict persists.20 The highest food insecurity levels are expected in West Darfur, West Kordofan, Blue Nile, Red Sea and North Darfur.

5.2 - Impact on environment/natural resources

Due to our experience and lessons learned in the multi-year DFID funded IWRM projects in Red Sea and Darfur, IAS uses IWRM principles to enhance the environment in the design, implementation, and closing of our WASH humanitarian projects. For 2022, IAS will be implementing Hafirs using the community-involved catchment approach, the assessments wil also be based on catchment while coordinating with WES the decisions governing all things related to the intervention, including environmental actions. Rather than continuing to use up existing precious water by drilling more boreholes, this approach guides rainwater (90% of which is normally lost) through the use of sand dams and other subsurface dams to replenish existing wells. The environmental impact for safe water provision is naturally very positive in terms of supporting healthier lifestyles, a better quality of lives, and supporting plants and agricultural projects, etc., but with the use of cement and construction materials, great care will be given to cleaning up after each project site; that each water point intervention area will be cleaner than before IAS commenced the project. Inclusive community/ main actors project design, initiation, and planning meetings will be the stage for environmental protection and planning advocacy. Beneficiaries will be tasked with developing a long-term environmental plan and will sign-off and be accountable to the authorities on their progress. For every water point, IAS will conduct Environment Impact Assessments (EIAs). Taking the data from these surveys, we educate targeted beneficiaries to take the necessary steps to improve the environment. Communities will be involved in cleaning up water points and latrine construction sites. Fences and shrubs will be set up around the area. Conservation of natural resources, especially water, will be central themes in WMC meetings throughout the project, including close-out activities, including inspection, ensuring that the environment activities were carried out and the environment enhancement plan was completed for the future each beneficiary community where IAS is intervening. As well as budgeting for water point maintenance needs, WMCs will be requested to work with the community to budget environmental enhancement activities. Finally, those receiving environmental advocacy and planning training will be encouraged to bring their knowledge into the local schools.

6.2 - Explain how your project aims to ensure protection mainstreaming during its design and implementation

IAS, will apply different standards and best practices in emergency response including SPHERE standards, LEGS, Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR), Natural Resource Management (NRM) and "Do No Harm'' approaches in the implementation of this project activities. It will be taken into consideration that the transfer of resources (project inputs and services) into a resource-scarce environment can trigger conflict in the communities. Careful assessment of the local context, examining how humanitarian assistance would interact with the current situation in the target areas, and the creation of options for project revision to improve its quality will be carried out. this assessment will include analysis of: (i) dividers and sources of tensions between groups: systems and institutions; attitudes and actions; values and interests; experiences; symbols and occasions; etc.; (ii) connectors across sub-groups and the local capacities for peace; (iii) the project in terms of why; where; what; when; with whom; by whom and how and its impact on dividers/tensions and connectors/local capacities for peace. The partners will work together to ensure that the project is implemented in a way that can strengthen local capacities for peace, build on connectors that bring communities together, and reduce the divisions and sources of tensions that can lead to destructive conflict. To ensure that no negative impact should result from this project the following will be undertaken: just distribution of resources, creating or strengthening networks of relationships across divisions, strengthening good governance, the use of participatory processes for decision making, supporting traditional or indigenous mechanisms for conflict resolution and reconciliation, inclusion of diversity of ethnic or religious groups, gender, and youth in project activities and leadership structures. Selection of beneficiaries will be performed with due consideration of “Do No Harm” principles, ensuring fair representation of different vulnerable groups regardless of their ethnicity, religion or gender. Criteria for selection of beneficiaries will be socialized with communities and ample opportunity will be provided to raise concerns or complaints. Selection criteria for all project activities will focus on female-headed HHs, child-headed HHs, orphans, elders and persons with disabilities. The population of the targeted localities; Al Ganab, Dordeb and Jobayt Elma’aadin in Red Sea state Al Kurmuk, At Tadamon, Baw, Ed Damzine and Wad Elmahi in Blue Nile state include a high proportion of vulnerable individuals; targeted HHs might have lost a family member during the conflict, mainly men, which explains the considerable number of women-headed HHs among the targeted communities; those women have to act as the breadwinners for their children and often their elders on their own. Enabling them safe and dignified access to basic services is part of the protection in this program.

7.2 - Please outline how the project or your agency will inform and interact with inter-agency approaches to accountability to affected people and feedback mechanisms, including SEA and abuse complaints?

Affected populations will be involved in all stages of this project. For example, when the project is approved, engagements will be held with affected populations, including women, boys, and girls to share about the objectives, the duration, and the key activities. IAS will also disseminate the complaints reporting mechanisms to ensure that the affected persons are able to understand how to raise complaints, but also know what to expect from IAS when a complaint has been raised. The targeted communities will participate in Project Focal Persons and selecting of water management committees. In addition, the community members’ views will be sought during project monitoring visits to ensure that the project results into the desired outcomes. IAS will continue to popularize the whistleblowing and complaints response mechanism with the community members to enable the communities to raise complaints or give feedback on the projects or conduct of the IAS staff or the staff of implementing partner organizations. Quarterly project reflection meetings will be held in which affected populations will provide feedback on what is working, what is not working, and solicit feedback from the targeted communities on the changes, both expected and unexpected caused by the project. Project monitoring tools will also be designed to capture feedback from the affected populations to ensure that the project is effective in respond to the needs, challenges, and feedback from the community. The most appropriate mechanisms will be established in consultation with community members, taking into consideration literacy rates, accessibility to communication devices and the presence of suitable sites for establishment of complaint/suggestion boxes; to ensure accessibility of all community members (men, women, children, elders and disabled people) and provision of timely response. Good internal procedures and confidential reporting systems will be implemented to ensure the confidentiality and safety of all community members. The communities’ feedback will be acquired and verified through different approaches including regular monitoring activities and feedback, complaints and response mechanisms including CFRM-Complaints Feedback Response Mechanism, focus group discussions, semi-structured interviews, key informant interviews, community meetings, establishment of complaint/suggestion boxes in project sites and communicating the partners’ contacts and addresses with community members and committees,
