Project Summary

To address the needs of highly vulnerable children and communities, War Child Holland proposes to work in Raqqa and Deir Ez-zor governorates with three local partners to provide integrated Protection services to children and community members to December 2019.
War Child and implementing partners will set up CFS in schools where possible, and where necessary, will establish non-formal centres to act as safe spaces for activities. Each of these locations will be assessed against the War Child CFS checklist to ensure they are fit-for-purpose, and lightly rehabilitated where required. They will then be equipped with minimum standard safety equipment, winterization materials, and stationery and recreational kits. Referral pathways will be mapped for all project locations and regularly updated throughout the lifecycle of the project.
Structured age and gender-appropriate PSS activities (Ideal, Little Fellow, SRA) will be integrated into the lesson structure. For 14-18 year olds, War Child will run a Peace Deal at each centre. Newly tested in Lebanon, with strong evidence to support its impact in enhancing conflict awareness and peacebuilding, the refreshed methodology offers adolescents the opportunity to create and implement their own ‘peace action plan’ and each group will be offered a small grant of $250 to implement their plan. In addition, in two locations, a group of girls aged 12-18 will be invited to join the She Deal, to pilot the intervention in Syria and develop more evidence on interventions designed for adolescent girls, aligned with the GBV sub-working group. Using the safe identification and referral training, children with specific child protection concerns will be referred to dedicated case workers in each location.
For caregivers and teachers, War Child will offer sessions in their Caregiver Support Intervention (CSI) to assist with addressing their own trauma and build resilience. To develop the capacity of the educational system to accommodate and properly support children affected by conflict, the action will include a coaching programme for educational professionals. In each of the implementation areas, a network of coaches will be trained and supported to conduct classroom observations and assist teachers with their professional development.
At a community level, mobile teams of facilitators will be trained by a dedicated agency to deliver sessions to both children and adults on prevention and response to ERW. The teams will travel to provide regular activities related to mine risk throughout the identified areas. They will also be utilised to offer basic services to communities not covered by other protection actors (PFA, recreational sessions, awareness raising, protection referrals). Awareness raising campaigns on child rights and child protection issues will be conducted on a quarterly basis.
For all field staff (including facilitators and teachers), before activities begin War Child will provide training on Safe Identification and Referral, Psychological First Aid, and Child Protection in Emergency Minimum Standards, as well as Child Safeguarding induction (which will be based on the updated WCH policy and disseminated to current and previous WCH partners). In addition, recognising the severe lack of specialized case management services in Raqqa and Deir Ez-zor governorates, War Child will work with its implementing partners to identify case workers, provide intensive case management training, fast track a team of case supervisors, and offer coaching sessions to the case supervisors.

Needs

Children and communities in Northeast Syria have severe and complex needs in an instable environment, with little support from external agencies. Moreover, since the October 2017 removal of IS control, 166,305 IDPS have returned to Raqqa city, and 244,655 to Deir Ez-zor governorate, pressuring already-fragile basic service provision (OCHA NES Situation Report 30). It is recognised that there is a protection crisis. Raqqa and Deir Ez-zor cities are categorised as six on the severity scale, and most of Deir Ez-zor governorate registers as category five, yet only 7.5% of Protection interventions were registered in the NES hub (HNO 2019; WoS Interactive Dashboard, end November 2018). The risk of unexploded devices is also extremely alarming. 10.2 million people are exposed to explosive hazards/ERW throughout Syria, and 95% of victims of explosive hazard accidents did not receive MRE prior to the accident (HNO, 2019, Mine Action Sub Cluster Victim Assistance Factsheet, November 2018). The operating environment continues to be volatile, and strong contingency planning is a necessity. Turkish forces are threatening incursions on the Northern border, US forces have announced their intention to withdraw, and frontline conflict around Hajin is displacing local communities. There were 124 security incidents in the three Northeast governorates reported in the week concluding 23rd January. Despite clear need and expanding access for humanitarian actors, only 10 NGOs reported protection services to the NES hub in November, and only 2 INGOs offer Education programming across the whole of the Northeast region. More specifically, in Deir Ez-Zor governorate, there are two protection actors reporting CP activities to the NES Forum, and both in Kisreh district.

Implementation of activities

Activities will be implemented through local partners, who will establish non-formal centres and develop mobile teams to reach additional communities.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

The major risks are security deterioration at project sites, compromised independence, and change of control with implications for the operating environment. Security deterioration at project sites has been experienced in previous programmes and readiness is built into project planning. Military dynamics are poised to change over the proposed project lifespan. Risk of fighting and shelling from the frontline are anticipated. At the early warning stage and low impact assessment, activities are suspended to protect staff and beneficiaries. If a threat is identified during activities, the area will be evacuated, and children escorted home or to a safe environment. Contingency planning also includes a shift to more mobile programming, or a relocation if necessary. Flexible context-informed approaches and mechanisms will enable the delivery of project outputs/outcomes, allowing project activities to remain relevant in a changing context. Compromised independence in the conflict is a key concern to all donors and humanitarian actors. Access to project sites is restricted by local authorities and can be subject to influences outside strict humanitarian principles. War Child is a member of the Core Humanitarian Standards Alliance, and all partners subscribe to these beliefs. In practical terms, project sites have been proposed based on the extent and severity of need from primary assessments. All partners have developed strong relationships with the relevant directorates, in order to assure humanitarian space to operate. Mitigation measures have also been built into the action design. Red lines will be articulated before the launch of any activities, official approvals will be obtained before the action, and relevant authorities/groups will be made aware of the project and commit to ensuring basic safety/access for its implementation.
