Needs

More than seven years of crisis continues to take a toll on civilians inside Syria, 13.3 million of whom remain with little or no access to protection from life-endangering risks. In 2019, new displacements as well as a growing volume of returns is expected to place pressure on communities overburdened by new internal population influxes, combining with still-fragile or devastated local economies and infrastructure to limit access to essential services. Unaddressed need for protection services continues to mount, causing vulnerabilities to compound and reliance on negative coping mechanisms, including child labour and early marriage, to proliferate. Implementation of activities

DRC has field staff present in all areas of operation who will directly implement activities. Awareness sessions, life-skills activities, support to children with special educational needs, social counselling, recreational activities, and case management will primarily be delivered from six community centres in Damascus, Rural Damascus (Jaramana and Qudsaya), Dar’a, Homs, and Hama. DRC also plans to open two satellite centres, one in rural Dar’a and one in Aleppo. The locations of the satellite centres have not been selected yet, but will likely be in Nawa (rural Dar’a), and Aleppo city. DRC will refer to Protection sector mapping of facilities when selecting locations. Additionally, DRC will use mobile teams to deliver child protection activities directly within Ministry of Education (MoE) schools across Rural Damascus, Dar’a, Homs, Hama, Aleppo, and Deir-ez-Zor. Lawyers providing legal assistance (is this activity is approved by the authorities) will provide services in community centres, satellite centres, and through mobile teams.

Protection Risk Analysis

PR1: Awareness sessions create expectation of response and/or are not fully adapted to the social and cultural environment. Risk: Medium; Impact: High. Mitigation measures: purpose of each session is clearly explained to beneficiaries; staff delivering sessions are able to provide beneficiaries with information about available services, including where possible making referrals; feedback mechanisms used to gauge reactions to sessions and inform session design; daily presence of staff in the local community, selection of staff from the local community, to ensure understanding of the social and cultural environment. PR2: Inter or intra community tension due to service delivery locations causing perceptions of discrimination or favoritism. Risk: Medium; Impact: High. Mitigation measures: DRC’s community centres in Damascus, Rural Damascus, Homs, Dar’a have longstanding presence in the local community; selection of new locations for Homs and Dar’a centres, selection of new locations for satellite centres, and of new service delivery points for mobile teams, in line with shifting severity of needs across areas done according to transparent selection criteria, targeting the most vulnerable and selection criteria clearly communicated to the community , and ensuring adequate service mapping, needs assessment, and coordination with local authorities and local humanitarian actors, as well as humanitarian leadership on the Damascus level, has been conducted before entering into new locations. PR3: Population targeted by the intervention is put at risk due to location of DRC’s facilities; including lack of accessibility, lack of consideration to local/contextual factors. Risk: medium; Impact: high. Mitigation measures: location chosen according to transparent selection criteria, targeting most vulnerable, and ensuring adequate service mapping, needs assessment, and coordination with local authorities and local humanitarian actors, as well as humanitarian leadership, has been conducted before entering into new locations. Continuous situation monitoring by DRC Safety unit, standard SOPs in place and applied in case of security incidents, closure or reduction of opening hours in centres if necessary. PR4: Exploitation/abuse by staff or other service providers. Risk: medium; Impact: high. Mitigation measures: clear and regular communication with beneficiaries, including reinforcing the message that humanitarian services are free of charge; regular consultation with beneficiaries through group discussions; operation of complaint mechanism, including regular advertisement of the system to beneficiaries; training to all staff on code of conduct and PSEA; adherence to all of DRC’s global minimum standards and guidelines for protection. PR5: Tension in community, for perceived inequality, discrimination, favoritism in distribution of special needs funds. Risk: high; impact: medium. Mitigation measures: follow DRC’s established selection criteria which ensures the most vulnerable are prioritized, depending on interviews with beneficiaries, and follow a holistic, family based approach while ensuring proper consultation and communication. PR6: Risk that existing traditional practices and community structures conflict with child protection principles. Child rights messaging is perceived as a threat. Risk: medium; Impact: high. Mitigation measures: regular engagement, communication with communities; selection of staff from community so they understand sensitivities; context analysis prior to engagement in interventions which include child’s rights messaging; use of materials and messaging tools either specifically designed for the context or adapted to suit the context. Additionally, child protection interventions which heavily involve community members in design and implementation (e.g. community-based initiatives) conducted alongside other programmes is expected to increase community acceptance for DRC’s child protection programming.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1. Currently INGOs are not permitted to deliver legal assistance inside Syria. DRC will only implement legal assistance should all necessary approvals from the concerned authorities be received; should approvals not materialize, this component of the project would be cancelled. 2. Should any of the planned areas of intervention become inaccessible due to safety concerns, DRC would consider alternate locations in line with its standard targeting criteria and procedures. 3. Should exchange rate fluctuations between donor currencies and the Syrian pound mean that DRC had less actual funds available in Syria; and/or, should this project not be fully funded, DRC may be unable to deliver all activities as planned. DRC would prioritize those communities in most urgent need of Protection assistance according to verified data from the field. 4. Should all necessary approvals not be received from relevant stakeholders (including and especially SARC) and/or governmental entities for new activities such as establishment of satellite centres, DRC would as a last resort return the related funds to donors.
