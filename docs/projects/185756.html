Project Summary

Caritas Bangladesh (CB) will build on it’s deep and long-standing relationships with communities in Rohingya camps to deliver effective community-based protection interventions that will provide essential psycho-social support and enhance the protection of vulnerable groups. The proposed proposal for Rohingya community and will be implemented at camp 4 Extension and camp 19. The CBP model of CB establishes structures in the community in which women, girls, people with diverse gender identities, people with disabilities, and other vulnerable groups can participate safely and in culturally-acceptable ways, and provides outreach from these structures to the HH by volunteers drawn from within the community. The project follows the “Do No Harm” principle and aims to ensure safety, meaningful access and dignity of affected people. CB’s Safeguarding Policy, AGD, Inclusion, Anti-Trafficking guidelines will be reflected across project activities. It engages the community to understand their perception of protection and psycho-social needs and risks, and identify appropriate, safe and adaptive responses. CB facilitates capacity building and nursing of Community Based Protection Committees (CBPC) to reflect on and prioritize protection risks and needs, identify community action plans, including actions that can be undertaken by the community. Support social and recreational activities identified by the community that are essential for their well-being as they provide outlets in a highly stressful environment and contribute to social cohesion, community bonds and social networks.
CB train and support community volunteers (female:male; 50:50), to serve as outreach workers and use their capacities in PFA, messanging and awareness raising. They are the trusted referral/linkage point for community peoples who experience violence, any protection risks, COVID 19 or have challenges accessing services general protection concerns. Remote case management system and CBP approach will be continued and strengthen to meet-up the needs, if COVID- 19 comes back again.
CB’s national policy for PSEA, is adapted for the Rohingya response context. Complaints can be collected through the several entry points and are directly processed by the dedicated PSEA focal point. Trainings on PSEA, Safeguarding and how to report is organize for volunteers and staff.
Better responding to improve the environment and activities related to DRR including monsoon preparedness CB has been working with sector and the PERU team. Capacity building of volunteers is a regular activities of CB aligned with the Protection Sector.
CB closely works with Government like- NGOAB, Deputy Commissioner, CiC and sectors for approvals and coordination. Regularly submits 5Ws and Sitrep to sectors and as active member it participats in PWG Coordination, Task Team meeting and ATWG, ADWG meeting at Cox’s Bazar level and camp level CiC meeting and protection sector meetings. CB is the Protection Focal of camp 4 Extension and regularly conduct camp level protection coordination meeting in direction of CiC and guidance of PWG. Any probable duplication of interventions in camp will be managed through well corrdination and consultation with Focal Point, CiC office and site management.
Effective complain and feedback mechanism is in place through setting Opinion & Complaint box in all offices, camp facilities, distribution points and majhi blocks, dedicated Hotline and e-mail account. CB conducts PDM and beneficiaries feedback survey regularly. These feedbacks are processed, and regularly analyzed by the Safeguarding and M&E team and shared with higher authority to action & program team to re-design the programming.

1 - Identified Needs

In August 2017, the Rohingya people of Rhakine state, Myanmar, experienced a wave of severe violence which the U.N. characterized as genocide, and approximately 750,000 terrorized Rohingya fled across the border into Cox’s Bazar District, Bangladesh; many described having witnessed unspeakable violence. As the protracted crisis enters its fifth year, over 800,000 Rohingya are surviving in crowded informal camps (density is reportedly as high as 10-11m2 per person in some camps (https://unhcr.maps.arcgis.com/apps/Cascade/index.html?appid=5fdca0f47f1a46498002f39894fcd26f) under extremely poor conditions, and are totally dependent on humanitarian aid. Rohingya are not allowed to move freely and have no access to official documents and formal legal systems. Rohingya, especially women, children, and persons with disabilities (estimated 3% of HHs have at least one person with a disability) (Inter-Sector Coordination Group (ISCG). Joint Multi-Sector Needs Assessment (JMSNA) Jul-Aug 2020. Pg. 2), face extreme risks to their safety and dignity. Aside from the hugely elevated risk of GBV, many report feeling insecure in the camps due to crime and the risk of human trafficking, while tensions within the camp. Their current conditions exacerbate the trauma many experienced, and continues to undermine physical and psychological wellbeing especially of children, women and people with disabilities. Rohingya-led participatory action research found that 88.7% reported experiencing symptoms indicative of depression, 84% reported symptoms indicative of emotional distress, and 61.2% report symptoms indicative of PTSD. (“The Torture in My Mind: The Right to Mental Health for Rohingya Survivors of Genocide in Myanmar and Bangladesh” (Participatory Action Research Report). Fortify Rights. December 2020. Pp. 10-11). There are limited opportunities for refugees women and girls with diverse age and identities, to participate and influence policy decisions affecting their lives. Some of the challenges in participation of women and girls in decision-making and activities are low levels of literacy and gender norms which restrict women and girls from public spaces or from taking a public role. Predominant camp governance structure involves unelected male block leaders, with a total lack of female representation. Abuse of power by block leaders and fear of repercussions to be a major obstacle for survivors’ access to specialized services, which has resulted in a culture of impunity that seriously affects women and girls’ health and well-being, security and dignity.” Various factors constrain refugee women and girls’ timely access to multi-sectoral services, which include social stigma and rejection, feelings of shame and self-blame, threats of retaliation, and low awareness of services and their benefits.Per the 2021 Multi-Sector Needs Assessment (Inter-Sector Coordination Group (ISCG). J-MSNA Key Findings Presentation. 17 October 2021): - 56% of HHs reported needing protection services or support- 75% of HHs reported that members in their community wanted to report a safety or security incident or access protection services in the past 12 months- A high reported over-reliance on mahjis (government appointed block leaders) and Camps-in-Charge for the referral of cases of assault or abuse Engagement of refugees is critical to build their ownership and responsibility at different stages and to ensure sustainability of protection mechanisms. Moreover, by understanding better existing positive community dynamics and drawing from them, community-Based initiatives should be promoted and ensured a supportive and protective environment for all wherever the community is in the future, considering the many uncertainties surrounding them.

2 - Activities or outputs

1. Capacity Building of Community Volunteers: CB engages local volunteers, residents of the respective Rohingya camps where they live and operate. Comprehensive trainings on special expertise and skills enabling them to work as community-based psycho-social counsellors, to identify and address protection problems, inform communities about existing services, discuss coping strategies and risk mitigation measures and facilitate an overall safer environment. The training will cover GBV, PSEA, PSS, PFA, case management, monitoring and reporting and how to develop a community-based protection plan. 2. CBP Volunteers and household visits: Volunteers make regular home visits to identify psychosocial counselling needs and vulnerabilities, sensitize households to certain protection risks, inform them about the services available in the camp and give recommendations for referral to specialized care services. 3. Formation and capacity building of Community-Based Protection Committees (CBPCs): The committee includes female, male, elderly, peoples with disabilities and GDP. Committee will develop their own TOR with assistance from CB. Committee members will receive training on protection, human rights, child rights, GBV, referral pathways, PSEA and safeguarding, prevention of child marriage, trafficking and other potential threats.4. Develop and implement community-based protection action plans: The CBPC's mission will be to reduce abuse, exploitation and violence in their communities. For this purpose, the committee members will be trained in the development and implementation of community-based protection action plan, which they will elaborate in the course of the project with the help of the community volunteers.5. Conduct awareness raising activities: Caritas plans to conduct participatory awareness-raising activities in the community on risks and threats related to intra-family & domestic violence, GBV, prostitution, trafficking and abuse of women and children, child marriage, Covid 19 and other public health threats, safety at night and corruption tactics of local leaders. 6. Psychosocial support for different age groups: Project provides counselling support to affected by GBV, violence in partnerships, neighborhood disputes or instances of child marriage. Community volunteers will be trained in providing integrated case management and psychosocial support for individuals and families at risk. Trained volunteers ensure support to affected receive high quality and timely PSS services in centre and through outreach services.7. Prevention of trafficking risks and capacity building: CB organizes and conducts session, training on the issue of trafficking, messaging at camp 4 Extension and camp 19 to increase awareness and capacity of staff, volunteers and CBPCs. The process helps to prevent, sensitize and develop a sustainable prevention mechanism through community-based approach and under the guidance of ATWG.8. Integrated community centers: CB has been operating 2 community centers at camp 4 extension and have planned to construct new one in camp 19. The center have a community hall that will be used as a training room for refugees and a meeting place for community volunteers. Separate rooms for counseling, storage and office space for staff. The center includes toilets (separated for women and men) and hand washing facilities, and accessibility features and appropriate amenities.9. Coordination and collaboration with sector and GoB: CB is the Protection Focal Point for camp 4 extension. As responsibilities it organizes and conducts camp level protection sector meeting under the direction of CiC and guidance of Sector. It provides required support to other protection actors in camp.
