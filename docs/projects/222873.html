Project Summary

UNHCR delivers comprehensive child protection intervention and response services in Cox’s Bazar, ensuring the best interest procedure, gender equality, and disability inclusion. In 2025, In collaboration and coordination with the CPSS and partners, UNHCR will continue to improve service quality for targeted children by strengthening the capacity of CP partners and other sectors’ partners, communities and government entities (DSS, justice systems) through tailored training programs including child-friendly procedures, child-friendly communication and accountability and regular monitoring. Adhering to the Minimum Standards for Child Protection in Humanitarian Action, UNHCR will ensure that interventions are conducted safely and professionally. UNHCR will mainstream and strengthen child-friendly protection and solutions intervention to ensure that vulnerable Rohingya children at risk receive quality case management and reunification of UASC according to the Best Interest Procedure (BIP) and have access to available durable solutions. Technical support and capacity building to child protection partners on BIP and family unification will be provided. Best Interest Assessment (BIA) and Best Interest Determination (BID), as case management tools for refugee children, will be used for specific groups of vulnerable children at risk including UASC and children associated with armed groups and criminal groups. Regular BID panel meetings will be organised and participated by BID panel members from CPSS, government (DSS), UNICEF, IOM, and international and national child protection organisations. UNHCR will deliver structured Psychosocial support (PSS) designed for different specific groups of children, adolescents, youths, and caregivers (Girls Shine, SAFE for adolescent boys, Khusi Khorak, Life Skills, and Positive Parenting) including referring to MHPSS and PM+. The trained workers and volunteers will organise recreational activities for children and adolescents. Peer-to-peer support for adolescents, parents, and caregivers will be initiated to increase resilience, reduce isolation, build trust and relatability, and encourage self-care. Multi-purpose centres will serve as safe, child-friendly spaces for learning and play while providing resources for parents and caregivers. Recreational activities (games, sports, arts and crafts, music, dance, and other forms of creative engagement) will serve as a foundational part of a child’s physical, emotional, cognitive, and social development and an entry point for identifying children at risk and providing them with psychosocial support. Sports and arts will be used as part of the peace and security campaign to engage and reach out to women, men, girls, and boys to gain safe spaces by and for the community. This will also mitigate risk of abuse, early marriage, neglect, recruitment, kidnapping and abduction of children. To sustain the program, UNHCR will empower the existing community-based child protection structures through a community-based approach. By enhancing training, technical guidance and material support, communities will be better equipped to develop and implement community-led initiative activities according to the community’s needs. Guided by core principles and protection standards, AAP, and PSEA, UNHCR will ensure children and families receive quality services in adherence to UNHCR, national, and international standards and can safely report any form of abuse, including sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA). Trained personnel will provide confidential channels for reporting and access to survivor-centred child protection services, ensuring a protective environment. UNHCR will incorporate disaster risk reduction (DRR) measures into child protection interventions, aiming to mitigate the impacts of potential disasters such as cyclones, landslides, fires, and floods.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

In Cox’s Bazar District, according to JRP’s projected population in 2025, Rohingya refugee children make up 52% of the registered refugee population and new arrivals. Rohingya refugee children continue to face significant child protection issues, including child marriage, child labour, child recruitment, exploitation, and neglect. These challenges heavily impact the resilience of families and communities and their ability to protect their children. Supporting community-based structures and creating an enabling environment where children, adolescents, and youth can reach their full potential while making informed decisions about their lives is a key priority for UNHCR. According to the Child Protection Situation Analysis, the kidnapping of 14-17-year-old children in KTPRC and NYPRC increased, with over 32 cases documented in June – September 2024. CPIMS+ from January to September 2024 report, the top five child protection concerns for refugee children in Cox's Bazar are as follows: neglect (31.86%), psychological distress (11.57%), physical abuse (10.48%), emotional and psychological abuse (7.84%) and child labor (7.58%), and The cases of child marriage are thought to be underreported at 4.53%, with approximately 98% of those cases involving girls. Additionally, children with disabilities (9.03%) face stigmatization and significant barriers to accessing essential services, including child protection services. The 2024 Inter-Sector Needs Assessment (ISNA) revealed that of the interviewed households that children were not living in the house, 20% indicated their children left the house to seek employment and 12% married. 88% confirmed that they have access to child protection services. Due to harmful gender norms and practices, young teenage girls experience challenges in moving safely around the camps. They have limited access to school and protection services, exposing them to early pregnancies and intimate partner violence. Families often resort to child marriage as a means of protection. On the other hand, young teenage boys face specific risks of being recruited into drug and gang-related activities. Boys are also at risk of child marriage, which forces them to abandon further developmental opportunities, such as education. The CPIMS+ 2024 report shows that 5.78% of total reported child protection cases involve unaccompanied and separated children, and child headed household, most of whom were left in the camps by parents or caregivers who sought livelihood opportunities or other means of survival. This situation leads to limited access to food and appropriate alternative family care, resulting in increased child labor and exploitation. A lack of awareness regarding various dangers in the camps contributes to incidents such as drowning and road traffic accidents. Children are also vulnerable to trafficking, abduction, and violence. While the government and humanitarian actors have collaborated to address child protection issues, more efforts are required to meet the challenges children face in their development and to ensure that their rights are fully realized.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

In coordination and collaboration with the Protection Sector, CPSS, and other sectors, UNHCR has ensured that the core principles and practices of protection are mainstreamed and integrated throughout all aspects of child protection activities, applying a comprehensive and proactive approach to safeguarding children’s well-being and rights. UNHCR will use holistic, child-centered, and AGD approaches to design prevention and response activities that prioritize the child's best interest and focus on their safety, development, and well-being. Disability inclusion and a child-friendly approach are integrated into the project to increase meaningful access to child protection services by children and caregivers through developing the knowledge and capability of staff and volunteers to communicate and deliver services in a child-friendly manner. The rehabilitation of child protection facilities and community outreach for children with disabilities or difficulty with movement are included in the project to increase accessibility to services. Community-based child protection mechanisms, local authorities, and humanitarian workers (child protection and non-child protection actors) – will be strengthened and empowered through technical support, capacity building, and materials provision. Community-based child protection structures and adolescent clubs will be engaged in identifying child protection risks and developing a prevention and response plan. The existing feedback and complaint mechanism will be regularly monitored and assessed jointly to ensure child-friendly and two-way communication between refugees and UNHCR that remains efficient in strengthening services according to the community's needs and upholding service providers' accountability and integrity. The zero-tolerance policy will be mainstreamed via training (COC, SEA) UNHCR and partners, effective investigation, survivor and whistle-blower protection, and robust action against perpetrators.

2.3 - Age & Disability

The Convention on the Rights of the Child emphasizes the principle of non-discrimination, which is vital in ensuring that all children have access to necessary protections and services. UNHCR plays a crucial role in this effort by advocating for the inclusion of children of all ages, regardless of gender or status, in child protection programs. Collaborative initiatives with various sectors, sub-sectors, and working groups have led to the establishment of children’s and adolescents’ clubs that promote inclusivity for every child, empower children to voice their needs and concerns, and ensure that their contributions are integral to service delivery decisions. Community-based child protection structures, comprising dedicated men and women, actively implement child protection interventions with UNHCR’s support. The development of child-friendly spaces, learning centers, and reception rooms reflects an understanding of the diverse backgrounds of Rohingya refugee children, fostering a sense of belonging and community among them. In 2024, UNHCR will commit to conducting disability inclusion, child-friendly communication and gender equality training for partners. This initiative aims to break down barriers faced by children with disabilities in accessing multi-purpose child and adolescent centres or child-friendly spaces. Plans are set to continue these outreach services in 2025, focusing on making all facilities accessible and tailored to meet the needs of both children and caregivers. By prioritizing accessibility and adapting services for children with disabilities, UNHCR and its partners are ensuring that every child receives the support they need to thrive

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

UNHCR is part of the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) Task Team on accountability to affected populations (AAP) focusing on preventing sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA). UNHCR has developed and implemented various AAP-related policies and guidelines, including UNHCR’s Code of Conduct (2004), the Accountability Framework for AGD Mainstreaming (2007), and the AGD Policy (2018). UNHCR has actively advocated for and integrated key elements of the AAP policy into its child protection programming, including communication and transparency, feedback and response, participation, and inclusion, as well as learning and adaptation. In collaboration with the Protection Sector, the GBV Sub-Sector, and the CPSS, UNHCR and its child protection partners are dedicated to ensuring that the best interest procedure and child-friendly approach and AAP principles are upheld throughout the project period in accordance with UNHCR, IASC and CPSS standards. The best interests of individual children and a child-friendly approach are prioritized in child protection services. UNHCR’s child protection interventions will engage various segments of the target communities, as well as partners, government agencies, and donors. This collaborative is to ensure accountability and transparency in its programs, utilizing community-based complaint and feedback mechanisms, such as Complaint Feedback Mechanism Desks, Protection Focal Points (PFP), hotlines, complaint boxes, and face-to-face interactions. These mechanisms are designed to ensure that services and assistance provided to adhere to the principles of "do no harm." UNHCR maintains a zero-tolerance policy for SEA and sexual harassment (SH). UNHCR has established internal reporting and accountability mechanisms specifically for its operations in Cox’s Bazar. UNHCR will provide technical guidance and regular monitoring for partners on AAP to effectively address concerns and complaints from refugee children and caregivers.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA) and sexual harassment is a fundamental aspect of UNHCR's commitment to safeguarding the well-being of refugees and other displaced individuals. In collaboration with the PSEA network in Cox’s Bazar, of which UNHCR is an active participant, we have successfully integrated PSEA measures and practices into all our projects. This includes proactive strategies to prevent, respond to, and address any incidents of sexual exploitation and abuse. UNHCR has appointed a dedicated PSEA focal point for the Bangladesh operation and established comprehensive policies and codes of conduct that strictly prohibit sexual exploitation and abuse by staff, partners, and refugee volunteers involved in our operations, especially in the context of child protection. These measures are designed to uphold robust PSEA standards and hold partners accountable for their implementation. Additionally, UNHCR incorporates awareness-raising activities and capacity-building programs within its child protection initiatives aimed at UNHCR personnel, partners, and the affected community. The engagement of the refugee community in the development of community-based awareness and prevention strategies is also a priority. To facilitate the reporting of incidents of sexual exploitation and abuse, UNHCR ensures the availability of accessible and confidential reporting mechanisms, including CFM desks, helplines, complaint boxes, and community-based focal points. We collaborate closely with the Gender-Based Violence (GBV) and Child Protection sub-sectors to ensure that survivors have access to comprehensive support through referral pathways that encompass both GBV and child protection services.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

UNHCR plays a crucial role in facilitating a protection-led inter-agency emergency response (PERU) in targeted camps and host communities. This initiative is guided by an Emergency Preparedness and Response Plan (EPRP) and CPSS, designed to address the risks refugee children face during emergencies in the specified areas. UNHCR incorporates preparedness measures for monsoons, cyclones, landslides, fires, and other disasters into its awareness-raising, community outreach, and community-based child protection engagement activities. In the aftermath of disasters, UNHCR is committed to implementing child protection services that specifically cater to the needs of the affected population, with particular emphasis on women, children, and individuals with specific needs. Together with its partners, the trained child protection community-based structures conduct assessments to identify and respond to these needs, including referrals, which may include the provision of temporary safe shelter, family reunification, psychosocial support, and referrals for affected children and caregivers. These services are facilitated both directly and remotely, following the findings of the needs assessments conducted. UNHCR is also dedicated to disaster risk reduction (DRR) and adheres to sustainable and environmentally friendly principles, aligning with global efforts to combat climate change and minimize the environmental impact of humanitarian operations. This commitment is reflected in the design and construction of facilities for refugees, aimed at enhancing safety and resilience of refugee children, adolescents, youth, and caregivers, using sustainable and locally sourced materials whenever feasible.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

UNHCR will integrate emergency preparedness and response (EPR) into child protection programs in contexts like Cox's Bazar. UNHCR and its partners have business continuity plan in place. Child protection personnel will receive refresher training to assess, identify, and refer vulnerable children at risk of violence, exploitation, separation, and abuse during emergencies. Community-based child protection structures will be strengthened and engaged in monitoring and identifying potential risks related to national disasters and other unrest situations to mitigate these risks. Child safeguarding protocols for emergencies, rapid family tracing, and reunification will be included in child protection emergency plans for 2025. Multi-purpose centers are designed to serve as temporary safe spaces for approximately 1,200 affected children and other populations. The centers will be managed during the emergencies by the trained child protection focal points and volunteers to ensure child friendly and safety environment. Priorities are given to the most vulnerable children and caregivers residing in the hazard-prone areas. In collaboration with MHPSS, UNHCR will ensure that first-aid psychosocial support is provided to the affected children and caregivers at the multi-purpose centers and through community outreach, including referrals to MHPSS.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

Yes. UNHCR includes and engages youth in different child protection programs. The vulnerable youth at risk of child protection concerns (under 18 years) will have access to specialized case management services, including BIA/BID and referrals according to the needs identified. Different child protection community-based structures will ensure members of youth groups (15 – 24 years old) in each structure, such as CBPC (18 years or older), parent groups (18 years or older), and youth and adolescent clubs (12 – 24 years). Youth groups will identify the child protection risks and develop and implement community-led initiatives to address the identified needs of children, adolescents, and youth groups in the community to increase the resilience and leadership to support other children and caregivers within the community.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

UNHCR promotes self-reliance through the existing child protection community-based structures by supporting capacity building and materials. Sports and arts for children, adolescents and youth will increase their life skills and self-esteem, psychosocial well-being, resilience, and sense of ownership. The community-based approach has been mainstreamed into different child protection program cycles. Community-led initiatives in 2025 will be initiated and implemented by different child protection community-based structures for their community according to the needs identified. UNHCR will collaborate with MHPSS to enhance and strengthen the current constructive and evidence-based PSS program to build the resilience of children and caregivers in the long term. Peer-to-peer support will be initiated. UNHR will coordinate and collaborate with different sectors, such as the Protection, Education, and Livelihood, to explore alternative solutions and complementary pathways for children at risk.

4.2 Do project activities in the host community link to development initiatives, and/or align with development priorities in the Cox’s Bazar district? If yes, briefly describe.If yes, briefly describe.

Yes. UNHCR and its partners are members of Cox’s Bazar Child Protection Platform, which aims to create safe environments and increase access to child protection services for all children residing in Cox’s Bazar through coordination and information sharing. UNHCR and its partners also collaborate and coordinate with community leaders, youth groups, adolescent clubs, parent groups, and religious leaders to identify child protection risks, including the impact of security concerns against Rohingya children and host community children. As part of the peace and security campaign, awareness-raising activities through ports and arts for children will be organised through community-led initiatives by child protection community-based structures.
