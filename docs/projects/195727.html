Project Summary

The main goals of the project are: to increase equitable and dignified access to quality malnutrition-specific curative services for children and women, while enhancing integrated sustainable preventive measures to halt prevalence of acute malnutrition among children and women in Ikotos Count.
The specific objectives will be; To increase equitable access and utilization of quality preventative nutrition specific service delivery for children, adolescents and women in Ikotos county by the end of 2023; To increase equitable access and utilization of quality lifesaving nutrition services for early detection and treatment of acute malnutrition for girls and boys under five years of age and PLW affected by acute malnutrition in Ikotos county by the end of 2023; To enhance nutrition situation and program monitoring, analysis and utilization of nutrition information by the end of 2023 and to increase equitable access to nutrition sensitive interventions from health, WASH, FSL, Education and Protection sectors through enhanced coordination and joint programming targeting vulnerable groups in prior.
Nutrition service delivery will prioritize provision of comprehensive and timely community management of acute malnutrition at both Nutrition centres at Boma levels and in hard to reach locations without static centres to access service. The project will as well emphasize on promoting breast feeding and optimal feeding practices among children and women through scaling up of MIYCN counselling services. Nutrition for farmers will focus on raising awareness on the importance of producing and consuming specific food items: households will be sensitized and trained on the use of new vegetables and crops that will be selected together with the communities in a participatory process; focus groups discussions will be held with the farmer groups in order to discuss the importance of diversifying the cultivation for nutrition purposes. With the lessons learnt from compounding effect of COVID-19 pandemic to already existing challenges, several revised/new guidelines, and approaches such as family MUAC shall be adhered and implemented in 203 to ensure continuity of equitable access and utilization of quality nutrition services in the context of COVID -19. Given the multi causative contributing factors to malnutrition, the project will ensure existent of operational linkages and joint programming with other relevant sectors such as WASH, health, protection and food security through effectives coordination mechanism that will stimulate integrated approach and dignified access to multi-sectoral basic service which will further reduce prevalence of acute malnutrition and eventually reduce morbidity and mortality among the vulnerable groups (children and women).
Accountability to affected population (AAP) will be mainstreamed by ensuring that programming of nutrition services approach is community/people centered in such a way that those in needs are fully engaged throughout the program implementation to ensure that the intervention is responsive enough to the arrays of the changing needs. Consideration gender and age variation of the vulnerable groups will be prioritized as well for efficiency of the response more so safety audit will be conducted at every nutrition center to ensure.

1 - Needs

South Sudan continues to face overlapping crises including floods, conflict, and food insecurity all contributing to persisting high levels of malnutrition. Preliminary results from Food Security and Nutrition Monitoring System (FSNMS) Round 28 and the 2022 SMART surveys show that the proportion of children 6-59 months suffering from acute malnutrition remains above the emergency threshold of 15 percent (very high acute malnutrition level- WHO classification) at 16.0% (15.4 -16.7) in lean season. This indicates no improvement since 2019, when the GAM prevalence was 16.2% (13.8-18.5) in lean season. The 2022 Nutrition Program data also shows lack of improvement - with increased admission of children and women with acute malnutrition by 24% compared to 2021. As per the FSNMS Round 28 and the 2022 SMART survey results, it is estimated that a total of 2,175,546 people will suffer from acute malnutrition (AM) in SS in 2023. These include a total of 1,437,735 million children under five years of which 353,606 will have SAM, 1,084,129 will have MAM, and 737,812 pregnant and lactating women (PLW) will have acute malnutrition (AM-PLW). Sadly, more children and pregnant and lactating women in SS will suffer from acute malnutrition in 2023 compared to 2022 (increased by 158, 201). Ikotos has estimated GAM of 9.4% (SAM of 3.8% and MAM of 5.6%) below the threshold, however as per South Sudan IPC analysis conducted in Juba between 3rd -15th October 2022, an estimated 474,536 of the population in Eastern Equatoria State (EES) is classified in crisis and emergency food insecurity situation. Further deterioration is likely to occur during the second projection period (April – July 2023), 578,542 people will be in Phase 3+. The most affected counties include Kapoeta East, Kapoeta North, Kapoeta South, Budi and Ikotos classified in phase 4 while Torit, Lafon and Magwi in Phase 3. Based on the FSNMS Round 2028, SMART nutrition surveys and admission trends for 2022, it is estimated that 2,175,546 people need treatment for acute malnutrition in 2023. This includes: 353,606 children from SAM, 1,084,129 children) from MAM, and 737, 812 PLW with acute malnutrition. In addition, 2,157, 514 children will require Vitamin A Supplementation (VAS), 1,015,301 Children (0-23 months) and 507,650 pregnant will require MIYCN, 1,769, 276 Children (12-59 months), will require deworming, and a total of 1,776,776 children and women will require BSFP. The major factors contributing to acute malnutrition include high prevalence of diseases. 62.5% of children reported to have been ill two weeks prior to the assessment. Diarrhea, fever, and cough were the leading symptoms reported. Existing poor sanitation conditions are likely contributing to the high incidences of illness, with open defecation reported by as high as 50% of households in 56 counties. Inadequate feeding practices of infant and young children is another major contributing factor. All Infant and Young child feeding (IYCF) indicators remain suboptimal. At national level, Minimum Acceptable Diet was 4.6%, Minimum Dietary Diversity was 18%, and Minimum Meal Frequencies was at 25%. Only 4.6% of children aged 6-23 months are receiving the required food quality at the required frequency. Additionally High food insecurity, and lack of hygiene, health and environmental sanitation services, along with inadequate maternal and child care in South Sudan continue to drive malnutrition levels higher and impact negatively on children and women’s health and well-being.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

The capillary and continuous presence that AVSI has in the communities targeted by the intervention (AVSI has more than 80% of its staff, including senior positions outside Juba), and the extensive use of staff recruited locally among people recognized by the communities, ensures that such fruitful cooperation with project beneficiaries continues throughout the implementation of the project. All project’s beneficiaries (female and male IDPs and returnees’ children, CWDs and host communities’ children) will be considered as capable and active resources rather than as passive recipients who do not have a say in the project. Thanks to a very localized approach with AVSI staff regularlypresent and usually coming from the same community of project locations, accountability is pursued also directly through its staff that can act as bridge between top management and beneficiaries. Furthermore, considering the purposely fi eld-oriented management team (officers and also project managers are fi eld based), beneficiaries and affected population at large as constant possibility to interact and fi le complain to staff at different level. Yearly, AVSI conducts surveys which results are discussed through open meetings with the community, the county and payam officials and traditional leaders (Boma and sub Boma Chiefs). During these events, AVSI explains to the community its plans for the incoming years as a means to an informed discussion among community members and receive solid suggestions. AVSI extension officers, AAP committees before selecting a person/group/community as beneficiaries, establish a case specific dialog where AVSIs expectations are compared with the one from the beneficiaries in order to evaluate possible challenges/intervention failure chance. Beside the surveys, AVSI’s beneficiaries have access to information during participatory meetings which provided platform to address issues linked to power-relations whereby some strata of the population culturally marginalized can find the opportunity to express themselves, include the view of beneficiaries in programming and allow intervention in case of issues reported thus increase transparency and accountability through awareness of beneficiaries on protection-related issues as well as project-design.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

AVSI Foundation will place protection and gender issues at the center of the intervention by engaging directly with the IDPs, returnees and host communities’ children and CWDs so that their needs, priorities, rights are addressed within the scope of the project. AVSI Foundation will always create conducive and interactive environments that encourage them to express their needs, rights and priorities. Apart from the active involvement of the rightsholders in the project, AVSI Foundation will ensure that the duty bearers (Government, PTAs/SMCS, institutions and non-state actors) play an effective role to promote the rights of the CWDs and girls. Activities will be developed with the active participation of women and parents in all stages of the implementation process, to ensure that girls’ priority needs are taken into consideration and that WASH infrastructures meet the right gender standards (IASC, 2015, Guidelines for Integrating Gender-Based Violence Interventions in Humanitarian Action: Reducing risk, promoting resilience and aiding recovery).Moreover, prevention of GBV (Sexual Exploitation) is pursued by displaying SEA code of conducts at service access points (schools, health facilitates, distribution points and community). On the other hand, safety audits are conducted, and multiple locations selected to avoid far travel of beneficiaries and reducing risks. Community members, staff and stakeholder’s capacity will be strengthened through specific c PSEA trainings.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

AVSI has in place a tested system of yearly multi-sector surveys that through the employment of more than 400 questions (including question on FSL, nutrition, GBV, hazards, protection, disabilities, education, gender inclusion, etc.),a large number of interviews (more than 400 HHs) capable of providing detailed reference at the subcounty (payam) level, an analysis centered on specific groups (single mothers, families with members with disabilities, poor farmers, etc) and the participatory discussion of such findings with the staff and representatives of the communities ensures not only that projects are designed on the base of the specific c needs of the different groups of the population, but also that such knowledge is interiorized by AVSI staff and becomes the base of an informed conversation and a common understanding with local communities, ensuring feedback from the most vulnerable within the community. In the last 12 months AVSI has produced 4 of such surveys which, along with other research aimed at monitoring the results of AVSI’s projects, are accessible on the “Assessment” section of the Humanitarian Response’s website. The questionnaire used for the Multi-Sector Survey can be accessed at this webpage https://ee.humanitarianresponse.info/x/#Yztl. Beside these surveys, AVSI has a tested system of detailed post distribution Monitoring which is used to gather precise feedbacks over the targeting, timing, transparency and overall implementation of activities oriented at the community level. During each fi eld visit, AVSI personnel is required to fill apposite forms that include a section for open-ended feedbacks and complaints. Finally, the numerous permanent bases that AVSI has across the targeted communities are equipped with mailboxes, suggestion boxes; focal point protection help desks for unanimous and isolated reporting of abuses or complaints.

12.1. Does your project contribute to any of the three Collective Outcomes, i.e. Improved Food security; Enhanced support for prevention and response to needs of survivors of Gender-Based Violence; and Flood displacement?

Yes

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

The project will contribute to the collective outcome 3 acting on different levels. The emergency activities will focus on the most vulnerable, among which flood related IDPs represent a priority group; these activities, such as emergency distribution of agricultural inputs and basic trainings, will help IDPs in finding relief from the emergency situation while building the basis for a new system of self-reliance. On the other hand, AVSI will work with Host Communities to strengthen the support network that take care of those displaced ensuring they have all the support needed. AVSI intervention aims at building the collaboration between host community and displaced people as a synergic tool to ensure the growth of the whole community and the reduction of internal conflicts over the use of natural resources.

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

AVSI’s Foundation shall ensure that the assistance during the project does not harm the safety, dignity and integrity of women, men, girls and boys receiving it, and is provided in a way that respect their rights. Protection sensitive services will as well be mainstream in the project, in fact the activities will include facilitation of child stimulation and ECD in all nutrition sites, GBV/PSEA awareness to workers and beneficiaries, referral of GBV cases to access serviceat the management sites. Likewise, AVSI will be making sure that trainings will target at least an equal number of female and male, that the monitoring will include sex and age disaggregated data, that the project personnel is trained on gender/protection topics including GBV identification, handling and referral. Equal participation will be ensured through the involvement of women and men, equally in decision making right up from induction meetings, and routine focus groups discussions. For instance, Inclusive selection process with set % for specific population components typically victims of inequalities (min 60% of females in project implementation, inclusion of elderly and people with special needs, and returnees). AVSI’s projects include activities of livelihood and capacity building especially tailored to women, individually and as women group, and is matched by educational projects that always include GBV and peace building sensitization for pupils and training on GBV and referral pathways for teachers and PTA/SMCs members. Finally, AVSI operates through numerous permanent bases distributed across the targeted communities and serves an adequate number of service sites equally distributed though out the counties to avoid crowding of beneficiaries per sites, reducing distance to access services, and thus diminish the gap for remote, inaccessible communities. Finally, AVSI will have a gender & protection specialist, a gender officer and a protection officer dedicated to the analysis, supervision and adjustment of activities from the gender perspective.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

Both in Lakes and Eastern & Central Equatoria State, AVSI will focus its intervention in the fi eld of food security and livelihoods with the main aim of reducing the proportion of population facing famine, emergency or crisis situations. All AVSI activities will be oriented to delivering basic agricultural assistance to the most vulnerable while building the capacity of the communities towards an ever-growing resilience and independence from humanitarian aid. To improve on production and utilization of nutritious locally available food at household for optimal MIYC feeding, the project will integrate nutrition to food security component, establishing sustainable solutions as demonstration kitchen gardens at household level, as well as at nutrition sites, schools, institutions (as County, Payam and Boma-based offices). AVSI will continue engaging in cereals largescale production through progressive farmers’ groups and enhancing the mechanization of cereal production in the targeted areas with common/group farms of above 20 feddan of dimension and supporting in production management and post-harvest methods. AVSI will also facilitate cooking demonstration to enlighten the mothers, fathers, and caregivers on how to prepare nutritious foods harvested from the kitchen gardens and encouraged to replicate the same at home. Mothers and caretakers of children with acute malnutrition will be link to community general food assistant to improve on household food security. People with chronic illnesses and mothers of children admitted in SC for management of severe acute malnutrition will be link to institution feeding program to receive food assistance.
