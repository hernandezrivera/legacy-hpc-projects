Project Summary

This program aims at supporting the resilience of the conflict affected people in northwest Syria, particularly in Idleb and Aleppo governorate. Shafak's proposed is designed to contribute to supporting the transition to the early recovery phase, as it supports the targeted communities with long-term assistance activities, programs that focus on building/restoring the communities' infrastructure, local economic empowerment, and initiatives that ensure engagement and participation of the community in recovering from the protracted crisis.
This program will achieve its objectives through the following:
a) providing short-term employment opportunities through Cash-for-Work activities.
b) offering rural micro-financing opportunities.
c) providing vocational and skills training to youth, men and women applicants and then support successful trainees with small-scale cash grants to enable them restart/initiate their small enterprises.
d) rehabilitating the local economic, public and social infrastructure through linking with the Cash-for-Work activity.
e) supporting local community and social cohesion initiatives.

Needs

As per the reported needs in the draft HNO 2019, the scale, severity and complexity of humanitarian needs of people in Syria remain extensive. This is the result of continuous hostilities in localized areas, new and protracted displacement, increased self-organized returns and the sustained erosion of communities’ resilience during close to eight years of crisis. Across Syria, an estimated 11.7 million people require humanitarian assistance, with certain population groups facing particularly high levels of vulnerability. Whilst there has been a reduction in violence in many parts of the country over the past year, the impact of present and past hostilities on civilians remain the principal driver of humanitarian needs in Syria. Eight years of crisis in large parts of Syria have continued to erode the socio-economic foundations of the Syrian people, thereby affecting their’ ability to sustain adequate standards of living, seek decent livelihoods opportunities, and access social services and essential infrastructure. This, in turn, has led to further strains on social cohesion and community security, as well as stresses affecting the social capital of Syrian communities. The combination of these factors has contributed to lowering the capacity of households and communities to cope with internal and external shocks, keeping their resilience under constant pressure. Given the above circumstances and the disruption generated by the crisis, particularly with regard to large-scale displacement and family separation, traditional gender roles have changed. access to resources and participation in the humanitarian response also varies across different sex and age groups. Particularly in the most traditional segments of society, the traditional division of labor in the private sphere is replicated in the public sphere, often restricting both the mobility and ability of women to engage in roles related to distribution and management of humanitarian services and may also affect their ability to access assistance. According to 2015 estimates, more than 83 per cent of Syrians lived below the poverty line, and more recent indications suggest that it has worsened since. Out of around 4.9 million potential members of the workforce(between 18 and 65 years of age), over half are without access to sustained employment, exacerbated by a severe lack of opportunities to attain employable skills and working capital, according the area-based assessments conducted in 2018. As a result, youth may resort to harmful coping strategies such as joining extremist armed groups, crime, trafficking, etc. In addition, as of 2016, the unemployment rate was estimated to stand at 53 per cent, reaching as high as 75 per cent among youth (up to 24 years). In combination with the loss of life and high numbers of conflict-related casualties and disabilities inflicted by active hostilities. The early recovery sector has a clear evidence-base to ensure the inclusion of the most vulnerable sections of the population in its strategy, identified jointly by humanitarian sectors. Prioritization criteria based on status, severity and access, will support the sector in identifying the most vulnerable groups such as female-headed households, adolescents, persons with disabilities, youth etc. Vulnerabilities can be attributed to various factors affecting the respective section of the population. These include, among others, physical profile (disability), age (the children and the elderly), gender, poverty,displacement (IDPs, refugees and hosts), barriers to market access (physical and socio-political), ethnic, and others. The context analysis of various locations inform the approach adopted for interventions under the sector. A further deepening and refinement of these analyses throughout 2019 will to provide support to the sector in addressing the specific vulnerabilities of each group in each area, through targeted programming or mainstreaming across thematic areas.

Implementation of activities

Shafak has its implementation approach with regards to the early recovery projects, and has experienced management and technical staff, since it had implemented many resilience program in north-west Syria. Shafak has its developed FSL department which will be leading this program. As part of the usual practice, Shafak starts its programs with community mobilization activities, where they meet with the community representatives and the local authorities, and conduct orientation sessions about the project activities, project approaches and assistance modalities. Shafak accountability team conduct information sharing sessions, and orient the community on the feedback mechanisms. The received feedback from the different gender and age groups in such sessions and in the similar previous projects are taken into consideration while designing the current program with specific focus on the vulnerable groups of women, youth, adolescents and elderlies. As for the implementation of the cash for work activity, this proposed action will work with communities, for example with local authorities and Civil Society Organisations (CSOs, such as relief committees, local councils and communities) to identify priority projects in the community that would help restore or protect assets, particularly related to livelihoods, water, and sanitation such as: waste management, drainage clearing, repair of agricultural roads/channels, reforestation, restoration of public infrastructure such as market places, schools, clinics/health centres and community boreholes/wells. Shafak will collaborate with the local groups to develop project proposals for the prioritized needs, and then will select the CFW projects based on a set of participatory criteria. The CFW activity will both fulfill the objectives of offering short-term employment as well as improving the community infrastructure. Cash-for-Work opportunities will be provided to households from the 'very poor' and 'poor' wealth groups who were formerly reliant on unskilled labour but are struggling to compete with increased demand for shrinking labour opportunities. Unskilled labourers will be employed for a duration of 10 days to three months, during which time they will receive wages on rates agreed as per the market surveys and Cluster's guidance. Regarding the small business activities, Shafak aims at supporting a group of selected applicants with vocational training, receive their business plans, based on the evaluation of which a narrower group will receive the cash small grant. This activity will offer 500 applicants an extensive training on various occupations (which will be determined based on the market assessment); in addtion, the action will provide structured support to 300 small businesses and enterprises in the target locations, enabling them to re-start, increase trade and become more profitable. Women will be a particular focus of this intervention and activities will be culturally appropriate to enable their participation, for example home-based activities. Both CFW and small business cash support will be in the voucher-based approach. Most of Shafak's interventions are designed in this approach seeing its benefit in stimulating the local market, and in protecting their dignity. Designing and printing the voucher is done at Turkey level to ensure the availability of the security features on the vouchers. In addition, Shafak proposes to implement a micro-financing intervention. This activity will be implemented through providing small cash credits to selected beneficiaries, who will use the credits to start/re-initiate/extend their own businesses, and will have to payback the loan after an agreed timeline and installments. The proposed program will also support the community initiatives to to promote the civic engagement community as well as the social cohesion.

Protection Risk Analysis

- limitations on movement and physical security risk due to conflict activity.- Community and family stigmatization for participating in programming, family resistance to girl’s mobility. - Low participation of girls because of family and community resistance. Shafak will mitigate the above risks through the community orientation sessions, in which the Shafak staff ensure that equality concepts are conveyed to the community.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1. Looting by Armed Groups (Low); involving the LCs and stakeholders of the society in the whole process, to avoid this matters. Putting all security procedures in place before starting distribution. 2. Aggression (by dissatisfied beneficiaries) (Medium); involving the LCs in the whole process, to avoid this matters. Awareness session and community mobilization. 3. Market Price Increased, lead to the increase in agriculture inputs prices (Medium); signing the contract with the suppliers including all the list of items with fixed prices. 4. Beneficiaries re-displaced (High); By making two lists of beneficiaries Most Vulnerable and less vulnerable, to replace the displaced beneficiaries with next eligible non-beneficiaries. 5- Losing access to the targeted location(s) (High): relocation and proposition of the activities to safer area after coordinating with the donor. 6. Bombardment activities by GoS: (Medium): suspend the project’s planned activities for two days until situation can calm down.
