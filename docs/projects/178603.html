Project Summary

Since its initiation by the Syria Strategic Steering Group in 2017, the Whole of Syria (WoS) Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) Program has supported the interagency humanitarian response in the joint implementation of international PSEA commitments. The WoS PSEA program was the first of its kind – a multi-donor, comprehensive response, designed to address the risks of SEA posed by the complex and protracted humanitarian emergency in Syria. The PSEA program is designed to cover diverse contexts, including the remote management context of the cross-border operations in northwest (NWS), the humanitarian response in northeast (NES), and the South & Central Syria response. Through an established, robust interagency PSEA Network consisting of more than 140 UN agencies, INGOs and NGOs, the program strives to decrease the risk of crisis‐affected communities experiencing incidents of sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) by humanitarian workers and provide technical and operational support to humanitarian actors to prevent and respond to SEA cases.
The program aims to build upon and deepen the impact of the PSEA program implementation in previous years. While integrating the recommendations from the recent WoS PSEA Program Evaluation, the project intends to:
- Maintain the coordination capacity for the WoS PSEA program with systematic engagement with dedicated staff leading the PSEA network hubs in Northeast Syria (NES), Northwest Syria (NWS), and Damascus, and expand outreach to include more organizations. Close coordination between the three hubs will continue and they will meet regularly to share updates, best practices and resources.
- Build the PSEA capacity of humanitarian workers through interactive trainings and deepen the impact of capacity-building initiatives to ensure the sustainability of PSEA programming.
- Provide technical assistance, mentoring and capacity building to organizations in responding to and investigating SEA cases.
- Integrate PSEA indicators and minimum standards for project development and design and ensure a PSEA lens into third-party monitoring activities.
- Support partners to conduct assessments, consultations, awareness-raising sessions and advanced training with affected populations and humanitarian workers in Syria.
- Expand project scope to reach the full range of actors engaged in humanitarian assistance, including local authorities and service providers.
- Maintain the interagency PSEA call center along with other established referral pathways, with a focus on safe referrals and provision of survivor services through GBV mechanisms.
- Strengthen PSEA mainstreaming into the humanitarian infrastructure, through the integration of PSEA messaging into key multisectoral tools, assessments, and guidance documents used by organizations in the WoS response.

4 - Needs

The need for continuing interagency PSEA interventions is reflected in the household (HH) survey carried out by HNAP and the PSEA program in 2020; community consultations conducted by the PSEA program in NES in 2020; recent WoS PSEA Program Evaluation, as well as trend analyses of SEA cases and reported incidents. Key findings from the HNAP/PSEA survey confirmed that affected populations remain highly reliant upon humanitarian assistance for survival. HHs in NWS reported the highest levels of humanitarian assistance. Regionally the reporting of favors in exchange for assistance was highest in NES, where 8 percent of all HHs who reported having received assistance also stated they had been asked for a favor, compared to 4 percent of HHs in C&S and less than 1 percent of HHs in NWS. HNAP data shows that female-headed HHs are almost five times more likely to be asked for physical or emotional relationships in exchange for assistance compared to male-headed HHs. As SEA tends to be heavily underreported due to its sensitive nature, the number of actual violations is expected to be higher. Findings from community consultations in NES illustrated that although communities had a good overall understanding of humanitarian standards, they did not believe that these standards were honored in practice, particularly when third parties distributed aid. The primary obstacles to reporting SEA included fear of losing aid, feelings of shame, lack of trust, concerns about confidentiality and retaliation, and the belief that reporting will not make a difference. There is also the risk of SEA posed to the affected population by actors outside of existing accountability lines including local authorities, community leaders and private vendors.

5 - Implementation of activities

The WOS PSEA Program has supported the interagency humanitarian response in the joint implementation of international PSEA commitments since 2017. Through an established interagency PSEA Network consisting of more than 140 UN agencies, INGOs and NGOs across three hubs (NWS, NES and S&C) the program strives to decrease the risk of crisis‐affected communities experiencing incidents of SEA and provide technical and operational support to humanitarian actors. The 2021 PSEA Strategic Plan's four programmatic pillars include:• Coordination and Management: The PSEA program intends to maintain coordination capacity with systematic engagement with dedicated staff leading the PSEA Network hubs. The PSEA program will strengthen its technical and operational support to humanitarian actors to prevent and respond to SEA allegations. • Mainstreaming, Prevention and Capacity Building: The PSEA program will continue to build PSEA capacity through interactive training, including mandatory focal point training and training of trainers to ensure the presence of qualified trainers across the response, as well as sector-specific training for clusters and other stakeholders. • Community Mobilization and Response: The program will continue to support member organizations to conduct awareness-raising sessions and community consultations, and maintain the interagency call center and referral pathways for reporting SEA.• Integration into the broader humanitarian infrastructure: To enhance local leadership and sustainability of the PSEA network, the program intends to deepen the mainstreaming of PSEA into the humanitarian infrastructure by integrating PSEA messaging into key multisectoral tools, assessments, and guidance documents.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risks: 1) Physical security risk due to conflict activity and barriers to access; 2) if community messaging is not culturally sensitive and suitable for communities, it could cause a reduction in community members’ trust in existing complaint mechanisms or implementing agencies; 3) lack of timely referral to requested survivor assistance may create additional protection and health risks for the survivor; 4) lack of confidentiality and safe information-sharing protocols may result in additional harm or retaliation towards survivor, perpetrator and/or community members. Mitigation Measures: 1) Through interagency collaboration, monitor security risks and barriers to access to ensure the safety of staff and beneficiaries; 2) provide agencies’ access to culturally sensitive and tested PSEA awareness-raising materials created by the PSEA network and review other PSEA related materials used by network partners to ensure culturally sensitive and consistent with standards for PSEA messaging; 3) ensure timely referrals of requests for survivor assistance received by the network coordination to GBV experts for assessment and assistance provision and follow-up with the service provider and or survivor on the outcome; 4) follow information sharing protocols when sharing information about SEA allegations, particularly personally identifiable information and put in place data protection measures for safe sharing for purpose, and ensure that network partners are familiar and also utilize these protocols and practices.

8 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risks: • Deteriorating political/security situation with repercussions for access.• Insufficient funding. Assumptions: • Safe and secure access to communities to allow for awareness-raising activities amongst the field staff and beneficiaries.• Beneficiaries have access to multiple reporting mechanisms (such as an interagency hotline and organizational complaint mechanisms or CBCMs) that are easily accessible and of consistent standards of operation.• Inter-agency collaboration facilitates communication and planning.• Funding is sufficient for sustaining PSEA activities.• Inter-agency coordination will mainstream PSEA monitoring, prevention and response consistently.• The PSEA Networks in all hubs will sustain high participation of agencies and effective PSEA mainstreaming within their policies and programming. Mitigation measures: • Continued advocacy on the importance of PSEA among all relevant stakeholders including donors, the Government of Syria, and humanitarian leadership and sectors/clusters. • Open and transparent dialogue on PSEA amongst the humanitarian community and with key stakeholders.• Implementation using a consensus-based, and inter-agency (PSEA Network-centered) approach.
