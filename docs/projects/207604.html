Project Summary

The proposed program aims to apply a holistic and integrated approach to promote skills and livelihood development among Rohingya refugee in Cox’s Bazar while also addressing the health and protection outcomes that both exacerbate and are the result of the lack of skills and livelihood opportunities.
The program seeks to implement a modified version of UNESCO's Accelerated Adult Learning (AAL) package. Capitalizing on its organizational technical expertise, UNFPA will supplement the AAL package by integrating gender-based violence (GBV) and sexual and reproductive health (SRH) concepts into the modules and materials that will be used to improve the literacy and numeracy of the target population, thereby providing multiple layers of benefit to the program participants to promote their protection, health, and livelihood.
The modified AAL package will integrate the modules from existing life skills education programs already being implemented by UNFPA in Cox’s Bazar. These programs - i.e. the Girl Shine Programme for adolescent girls and young women; the Champions of Change program for adolescent boys and young men; and the Girl Shine Caregiver Program for male and female caregivers of adolescents and youth - equip participants with skills & knowledge on gender equality, GBV, SRHR, menstrual health and hygiene management, and other essential social and emotional skills. These will be further strengthened with modules on entrepreneurship and youth leadership/empowerment. Introductory sessions on select vocations will also be included to further facilitate the participants' transition to the competency-based vocational training programs. The program will run for 6 months per cohort, and graduates will also be provided with kits with input and other materials to allow them to further practice their skills after their participation in the program has ended.
In addition to the modified AAL package, the program will apply an integrated approach to service delivery in line with the rationalization principles. In particular, GBV and SRH services and referrals will be made available in facilities where the AAL activities will take place. The mechanisms for beneficiary selection as described below will also ensure that not only are the most vulnerable groups targeted - such as young people, pregnant and lactating women and women at risk of GBV - but that the beneficiaries of the programme are able to access and avail of comprehensive services that will improve their overall quality of life. Where possible, graduates of the AAL program will also be engaged as community volunteers for the implementation of other GBV and SRH activities supported by UNFPA.
The program has three target population groups in Rohingya refugee camps:
1. Youth between the ages of 18 to 24 (women and men) and older adolescents aged 17 who meet the prerequisite criteria of (a) completion of the Girl Shine or Champions of Change Life Skills Education program; (b) previous experience in volunteering and community engagement; and (c) willingness to become a refugee volunteer with a focus on supporting SRH and GBV-focused interventions.
2. Indexed vulnerable households and/or individuals, including young people, pregnant and lactating young women and girls, young caregivers of adolescents and children, members of female- and youth-led households
3. Referred individuals and households who have been targeted by other interventions on SRHR, GBV, etc.
The proposed indicators of success for the programme are:
1. Number of participants in prevocational activities
2. Transition rate of prevocational graduates who enroll in vocational training
3. Improvement in knowledge of GBV and SRH topics and referral mechanisms
4. Improvement in functional literacy and numeracy.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

The 2023 Multi-Sectoral Needs Assessment (MSNA) showed that the lack of household income is resulting in food insecurity and declining health outcomes, including maternal health. According to the REVA-6 Report (June 2023), 95% of Rohingya households in Cox’s Bazar were identified as moderately to highly vulnerable, meaning they are unable to meet their essential needs without humanitarian assistance, while 38% of Rohingya households are considered moderately to highly economically vulnerable, meaning they are unable to afford the established minimum expenditure basket without assistance. Ninety-one percent (91% )of Rohingya households report that they are able to afford less goods and services in 2023 compared to 2022 (MSNA 2023). As a result, Rohingya households largely depend on food rations and other cash assistance as their main source of income, and often employ at least one negative coping strategy to make ends meet, such as borrowing money, buying food on credit, and reducing non-food expenses (e.g., education and health) to cover food needs (Joint Protection Monitoring Report, Q2 2023). These challenges are largely driven by the limited livelihood opportunities, especially in the formal labor market. In fact, 71% of Rohingya households report facing barriers to accessing income-generating activities, including the limited livelihood opportunities nearby (MSNA 2023). The lack of livelihood opportunities can also be linked to gender-based violence (GBV) prevention as poverty is widely seen as a trigger of GBV. FGDs conducted by UNFPA in camps (2023) revealed that women and girls believe that involvement in livelihood activities decreases their vulnerability to GBV, including child marriage and consequently adolescent pregnancies, sexual exploitation and abuse, coercive sex by community members, and human trafficking. The prolonged economic insecurity as a result of this lack of access to livelihood opportunities amidst ration cuts has also exacerbated the volatile peace and security situation in camps and neighboring host communities, leaving many - especially adolescents and youth - vulnerable to radicalization and recruitment into armed groups, human trafficking and drug trafficking, abductions, and other forms of violence. While more livelihood and skills development interventions are being introduced under the coordination of the relatively new LSDS in Cox’s Bazar, a significant proportion of the population could not be targeted with the vocational training based on the Government of Bangladesh-United Nations Framework on Skills Development for Rohingya Refugees and Host Communities as foundational knowledge and skills, such as literacy, numeracy, and life skills are significantly lacking in the community. This is especially true for young people as 93% of Rohingya youth have not received any vocational training as of 2022 (Assessment of Economic Opportunities for Young Rohingyas in Bangladesh (2022), Population Council). Dialogues among stakeholders implementing livelihood programs revealed the critical need for prevocational training, especially among the younger population, to prepare them for the vocational training packages that would lead to income-generating activities in camps as well as prepare them for their voluntary repatriation and reintegration in Myanmar or relocation in third countries. It is thus crucial to provide access to skills development opportunities especially among young people. These trends also indicate a clear need for a gender-transformative and age-responsive approach to livelihood, skills development, and income-generating activities. This includes prevocational training that integrates protection concerns, especially GBV prevention, and health concerns, particularly sexual and reproductive health, that are linked to the lack of livelihood opportunities.

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

In line with Principle #3, UNFPA will capitalize on its organizational mandate and strengthen the integration of GBV and SRH services in its Adolescent & Youth Center where the livelihood and skills development interventions will be implemented. Additionally, UNFPA will adopt an integrated approach to improve service delivery particularly through ongoing coordination efforts with partners from the Livelihood and Skills Development Sector, as well as across various sectors, including: the Education Sector on the interventions' alignment with prevocational training interventions; Health Sector on the interlinkages with SRH; and the Protection Sector on the interlinkages with GBV. Prior to initiating the project, UNFPA has also conducted consultations with targeted populations at the community-level in Cox’s Bazar, as well as engaged in discussions and coordination mechanisms with humanitarian actors. UNFPA has been one of the active partners in the inter-sectoral coordination mechanism since the beginning of the influx and always maintained respectful partnership and networking with relevant stakeholders and government officials. In line with Principle #4, UNFPA will ensure predictability and continuity by continuing to operate in the same areas where it has an already-established presence and with the same well-established IPs. UNFPA has been prioritizing multi-year programming and funding in its resource mobilization endeavors, and part of the proposed interventions will be financed through already-mobilized multi-year projects. Additionally, UNFPA engages local implementing partners in Cox’s Bazar and continuously supports the development of their technical and institutional capacity to operate on the ground, thereby contributing to building local capacity to deliver livelihood and skills development interventions.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

The project promotes the protection of affected populations and prioritizes safety, dignity and rights of young people. The principle of "do no harm" is incorporated throughout the project cycle, and is applied to facilities, services, and outreach activities. Through the project, UNFPA will continue to ensure that protection mechanisms and principles are in place by applying protection mainstreaming assessment tool kits, safety audits & related IASC protection standards across UNFPA-supported facilities and services.This will be monitored through regular needs and vulnerability assessments and participatory discussions with the target populations. The results of these assessments will be used to further support the special needs of vulnerable groups, such as young people, persons with disabilities, members of female-headed and youth-headed households, among other groups. Finally, UNFPA will continue to promote the engagement of the affected populations through community engagement and other awareness-raising efforts on protection topics and referral mechanisms.

3.3 - Age & Disability

UNFPA both adheres to and supports the capacity development of partners on the IASC Guidelines on Working with and for Young People in Humanitarian and Protracted Crises to provide age-responsive and adolescent-friendly services. UNFPA co-chairs the Youth Working Group which, while under the Education sector, mainstreams the concerns of young people across sectors. UNFPA has a repertoire of resources, including toolkits and checklists, on disability inclusion that it implements in its planning, implementation and monitoring mechanisms. UNFPA also facilitates the participation of people with disabilities and young people in its regular feedback and consultation mechanisms to ensure that their voices are heard throughout the project life cycle. When possible, the project will hire community teachers who are refugees and prioritize the recruitment of staff with appropriate language, gender, age and cultural competencies, including staff with disabilities, to work with Rohingya populations. Data collected will also be disaggregated by sex, age and disability to better understand the service uptake among different groups.

3.5.1 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

UNFPA has zero tolerance for sexual exploitation & abuse (SEA) and sexual harassment (SH). UNFPA has been a member of the ISCG PSEA Network in Cox's Bazar since 2017, and has appointed PSEA Focal Points for the organization. UNFPA provides training on PSEA, including obligations to report wrongdoing, for staff and non-staff personnel,IPs, vendors, and service providers. UNFPA's Office of Audit & Investigation Services (OAIS) ensures that staff can easily report misconduct through an online platform/hotline or through PSEA focal points. The organization also has a policy to prevent retaliation to protect survivors, victims, and whistleblowers. UNFPA conducts awareness-raising sessions among communities and visibly posts IEC materials in all its supported facilities to ensure that communities are aware of SEA & reporting mechanisms. As needed, UNFPA will conduct capacity assessments and support the implementation of capacity strengthening plans to ensure IPs are maintaining the required PSEA and safeguarding mechanism.

3.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

UNFPA will ensure full adherence to DRR/DRM standards, including the Government of Bangladesh’s National Plan for Disaster Management (2021-2025) & Action for Disaster Risk Management Towards Resilient Nation. UNFPA Community Mobilizers are trained to support the response to disaster-related emergencies in the acute and post-disaster phases. Basic DRR information is also shared with the beneficiaries of UNFPA’s interventions such as its Life Skills Education program, while UNFPA-supported facilities are used as temporary shelter during emergencies. UNFPA also prepositions and stores customized Dignity Kits and emergency Menstrual Hygiene Management (MHM) Kits that are quickly distributed to its project beneficiaries during emergencies to reduce the vulnerability of affected women and girls. Finally, UNFPA has its in-house climate change specialist, and will continue to explore and adopt implementation modalities based on contemporary understanding and best practices in DRR, climate change adaptation, and the application of circular economic models.
