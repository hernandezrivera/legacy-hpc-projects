Project Summary

The Sudan IPC Acute Food Insecurity Analysis (June – August 2019) estimated that 5.8 million people are experiencing humanitarian crisis with worse levels of food insecurity (IPC Phase 3 and above). Around one million individuals are facing emergency levels of acute food insecurity (IPC Phase 4) and around 4.8 million individuals are in crisis (IPC Phase 3), while nearly 11.8 million are estimated to be in stress phase (IPC Phase 2). Overall, 162 localities from 17 states have been classified out of the 18 Sudan States .
CRS will apply a multi-sectoral approach to address food insecurity, malnutrition, poor access to social services, and low resilience of communities to shocks in localities classified in IPC phase 3 and above, through interventions on food security and livelihoods (FSL), WASH, education, health and nutrition, with protection as a cross-cutting sector. It will mainstream gender equality and women empowerment across the six sectors. The approach will allow sectors to complement and leverage each other for greater impact on the lives of vulnerable communities, enabling CRS to carry out the following humanitarian support:
 Provide humanitarian support and food assistance in localities classified in IPC phase 3 and above, through cash-based transfers.
 Provide emergency agricultural and livestock support to farmers, with special focus on localities prone to natural disasters including floods and drought.
 Scale up diversified livelihoods for improved self-reliance, resilience building and social protection to all vulnerable communities classified under Phases 2, 3 and 4.
 Promote good agricultural and environmental sensitive practices with special focus on water and soil efficacy, through capacity building.
 Promote good nutritional practices at household level through nutrition-sensitive activities, such as home gardening and educational hygiene awareness on food and water safety.
 Improve information on food security and its related sectors, through regular assessments, monitoring and early warning systems.
 Participate and support capacities of national and state level Technical Working Groups on food security information systems.
 Enhance preparedness and contingency plans to adapt to different scenarios and shocks.
Interlinkages of WASH with FSL sector will ensure there is safe drinking water for communities and livestock, while interlinkages of WASH with Education sector will ensure there is safe drinking water for school children, productive water for school gardens, and sanitation facilities. Linkages of WASH with Health sector will ensure access for outbreaks-affected and ‘at-risk’ population to minimum WASH for Health package.
The project will provide WASH facilities and reduce the high drop-out rates in schools through provision of meals. It will provide teaching, learning and recreational materials to improve quality of education. The project will refurbish/rehabilitate health facilities, provide knowledge and skills on health issues for health personnel. It will develop referral systems and surveillance services for rapid response to health crisis.

1 - Needs

1.1 NeedsThe protracted humanitarian crisis has led to food insecurity, driven by social unrest, high inflation rates, soaring food and medicine prices, and shortages of foreign currency, fuel and cash. The affected 5.8 million people have adopted coping strategies to mitigate food insecurity. More than one third of households have adopted food-based coping strategies of reducing number of meals. Additionally, 54% of the households have resorted to livelihood-based coping strategies of depleting their livelihood assets and reducing expenses on basic needs. The school drop-out rates for children have increased with households reducing expenditure on education. Food availability has remained constrained despite a bumper harvest for the 2018 cropping season. Farmers and traders hoard their agricultural produce, regarded as a more reliable form of savings compared to the fast weakening local currency, and this has reduced food availability in the market. Large segments of the population rely on market purchase to fulfil their food accessibility requirements, and this is dependent on prices, income and availability of cash . More than half of the households are spending at least 75% of their expenditure on food and are unable to invest in livelihood assets. Similarly, 58% of the households cannot afford the local food basket due to limited purchasing power resulting from high inflation. Prices of cereals were in July at near-record levels in most markets across the country. Reduced purchasing power has limited the ability of urban workers to support rural populations. The Sudan IPC Acute Food Insecurity Analysis reported low food utilization, with 25% of households lacking adequate dietary diversity, and 20% and 30% not consuming Vitamin A and hem-iron rich foods respectively, exposing them to potential micronutrient deficiencies. Over one in three children under five (CU5) years of age are too short for their age (stunted) and more than one in six are too thin for their height (wasted) . Additionally, several localities reported inadequate access to clean drinking water, and unsafe methods of food preparation and conservation. Sanitation and hygiene are poor as most households are without latrines, resulting in high incidence of diarrheal diseases. The few available deep wells and boreholes are unprotected, posing a health risk to the communities. The above-average rains in August caused high levels of flooding in flood-prone areas, which resulted in destruction of productive assets and infrastructure, and increased the prevalence of water-borne diseases. However, the above normal rainfall improved pasture conditions, water availability and livestock body condition and production, with resultant effect of increasing the need of vulnerable households to restock. The humanitarian crisis affects women the most, as unfair patriarchal structures, attitudes and beliefs limit social and economic engagement of women in livelihoods, markets and community structures that affect their lives. Women are mostly confined to the home and must be home before dark to take care of domestic chores. Schools face poor WASH facilities, with high drop-out rates for school children due to hunger and malnutrition. The quality of education is low because of lack of teaching, learning and recreational materials. Vulnerable people have limited basic water, sanitation and hygiene services. The SAM and outbreaks-affected and ‘at-risk’ population lack minimum access to WASH for Nutrition and Health packages. Vulnerable people face limited health facilities, with staff poorly equipped with knowledge and skills to provide health services. The referral system is poorly developed to provide specialized health services. They also do not have surveillance systems to rapidly respond to health crisis.

5 - Environment Marker

B+: Medium environmental impact with mitigation

5.1 - Impact on environment/natural resources

With an Environment Marker of code B+ for the SHF project, CRS will use the OCHA/UNEP Sector Guidance to identify likely impacts on the environment and natural resources and develop mitigation measures for each sector (FSL, WASH, education, health & nutrition). It is expected that the FSL sector might have environmental harmful impacts from promotion of winter season cultivation of vegetables and increased numbers of school and homestead gardens that will cause depletion of underground water reserves. Use of improved seed might lead to biodiversity loss of indigenous vegetable varieties. Increased animal production might degrade pastures and watering points, while increased use of veterinary drugs and their poor disposal will affect the environment. Promoted income generation activities (IGAs) as alternative livelihood options might cause environmental degradation from the activities on agri-food processing and fisheries. Construction and rehabilitation of fisheries, schools and health and nutrition facilities will emit carbon dioxide, methane and other waste like dust from use of heavy machinery. CRS will use the OCHA/UNEP Environment Marker and its Sector Guidance to assess the expected impact of the SHF project on the local environment and natural resources, design mitigation actions for each sector, and monitor the adequacy and effectiveness of the mitigation/enhancement measures. This will ensure that any negative impact on the local environment and natural resources is reduced as much as possible for sustainability.

3=Yes, room for improvement

6.1 - Explain how your project aims to ensure protection mainstreaming during its design and implementation

Protection risks continue to be a challenge for women, girls and other vulnerable groups in the complex socio-economic political context of Sudan. Consistent with CRS’ values to uphold the dignity of all human life and promote justice across the globe, CRS will continue to be an active member of collaborative efforts to mainstream protection in humanitarian programming and develop shared protection tools and approaches. In Sudan, CRS has a dedicated full time Gender Focal Point, who is supported by the CRS East African Regional Gender and Inclusion Technical Advisor. CRS mainstreams protection to: (i) prioritize safety, dignity and avoid causing harm in its adherence to the ‘Do No Harm’ principles, (ii) ensure equality and meaningful access, (iii) include mechanisms for beneficiary accountability, and (iv) emphasize participation and empowerment to meet SHF minimum requirements. It will carry out protection community-sensitization sessions for strengthening capacity of community workers, volunteers and community-based protection structures (local Sheikhs, mother & widow groups, community-respected Hakamas, farm protection committees/Ajawids, etc.) to identify protection risks and provide protection services for vulnerable women, children and victims of sexual gender based (SGBV/GBV). CRS will refer for specialized protection services cases of personal hygiene kits (PHKs), SGBV/GBV prevention, clinical management of rape (CMR), and psychosocial/counselling for vulnerable people (women, girls, adolescents, men, boys, elderly & PWD). It will refer cases for specialized protection services to Government protection units (Combating Violence Against Women & Children [CVAWC], family & child units within the Police under the Justice Ministry, and councils for child welfare & violence against women under the Social Welfare Ministry, Ministry of Internal Affairs and Ministry of Security & Social Development) for sustainable delivery of quality protection services. CRS will use the Community-Based Complaints Mechanism (CBCM) for target populations to support the development of self-protection capacities and assist people to claim their rights to shelter, food, water, sanitation, health and education. It will provide latrines with separate entrances for boys and girls to protect the safety and dignity and ensure that latrines are accessible to PWD. It will also sensitize PTAs and students on protection during activities to promote girl-child education.
