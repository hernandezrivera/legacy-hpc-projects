Project Summary

The proposed intervention will safeguard quality, equitable and inclusive basic education for vulnerable, school-aged, Palestine refugee children in the West Bank and East Jerusalem. Through this project 20,560 students (9,853 boys and 10,707 girls) will continue to learn and to benefit from having access to quality education in a context of increased violence as a result of the protracted crisis and continued military occupation. This intervention targets 42 UNRWA schools that are affected by frequent military incursions, clashes and settler violence and are located at high-risk areas namely East Jerusalem, Hebron H2, Area C, as well as UNRWA schools that are currently affected by the increased deteriorating situation in Nablus and Jenin.
This intervention will aim to increase safe access and provide psychosocial support for 20,560 vulnerable refugee students and 783 teachers, in addition to other non-teaching staff. Activities aim to support UNRWA school staff and students who are affected by the education-related violations to enjoy a protective environment as well as to assist children and school staff in vulnerable schools in preparing and responding to emergencies.
UNRWA will work to scaling up the scope of its ongoing core interventions in response to the emergency situation in the West Bank, both in terms of increasing target groups and increasing the frequency of interventions, such as providing recreational activities in camp schools, as well as proposing other new activities to respond more in-depth to this situation, and to the needs of schools identified by the needs assessment under this emergency situation, an example of which is the protective presence - gatekeepers in the school.
Finally, this intervention also aims to improve the confidentiality, safety and accessibility of complaints mechanisms, including complaints of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (SEA). The project provides multiple child-friendly channels to receive and respond to complaints which are adapted to the target population. UNRWA has developed ad hoc SoPs on the management of cases of SEA which abide to CBCM SoPs. Complaints of SEA will be handled by PSEA focal points who will ensure a survivor centered approach in how the complaint is handled and support is offered to the survivor. UNRWA West Bank
Field Office works closely with the PSEA Network and will undertake compulsory reporting and external referrals.

Implementing Partners

The United Nations Relief and Work Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East (UNRWA) has a fundamental humanitarian operational presence in the West Bank, both geographically and in terms of sectors of intervention. Next to the Palestinian Authority, UNRWA is the biggest service provider in the West Bank. In total, the Agency serves 1,111,062 Palestine refugees and other registered persons (UNRWA, Registered Population Dashboard, January 2022), which amounts to one-third of the population in the West Bank, of whom more than half a million are women and girls. UNRWA was established by the General Assembly in 1949 and is mandated to assist and protect some 5 million registered Palestine refugees in the region. As the main service provider for Palestine refugees in the region, UNRWA delivers primary education, health care, relief and social services, infrastructure and camp improvement, microfinance, and emergency assistance. The Agency has extensive experience providing emergency assistance to Palestine refugees during humanitarian crises to save lives, preserve human dignity, and prevent and respond to the impacts of such crises. In the West Bank, the Agency's programmes seek to reduce the impact of the protection crisis stemming from over 50 years of the Israeli occupation by addressing the critical needs of the most affected individuals. In terms of response, UNRWA provides critical services to those exposed to protection threats, including psycho-social counselling, medical assistance, cash-based interventions, and the provision of dedicated programming to specific vulnerable groups.

1 - Needs

The increase in violence and protection-related incidents is impacting refugee livelihoods, children's education and the psychosocial well-being of the refugee community. According to the 2022 (MSNA), 52.1 per cent of refugee households in Areas A and B sent their children to live elsewhere on at least one occasion as a coping strategy to avoid threats and/or violence by Israeli settlers. Over 50%of refugee households in Areas A and B reported at least one child showing signs of psychosocial distress (compared to 15 per cent for non-refugees). Also, according to the 2022 MSNA, 26.6% of refugee household in the WB with school aged children reported Protection risks while commuting to school as the main reason for dropping out of school in the year before. Amongst the refugee households in the West Bank that reported the way to/from school to be unsafe, 24.5% reported firing tear gas on school or students as their main concern, while 12.8% households reported military presence around schools as their main concern. As reported by the HC on 18 October, since the beginning of October alone, 15 Palestinians, including six children, have been killed by Israeli forces during search-and-arrest operations, exchanges of fire, or in confrontations between Israeli forces and Palestinians. The northern West Bank has suffered the most from the incursions this year; Jenin and Nablus governorates alone account for more than half of the Palestinian fatalities. On average, there have been 14 ISF operations per week in the refugee camps so far in 2022. (UNRWA Protection data). ISF operations often include the use of sound bombs, tear gas, and destruction of private property, shouting and intimidation, which are likely to cause trauma for inhabitants in these densely populated refugee camps. UNRWA staff and operations are also regularly impacted by these operations, which often causes for example schools and health centres to close for periods. In some occasions, school children were trapped for hours in UNRWA installations while fighting erupted. Children (boys and girls) attending UNRWA schools are exposed to several education-related vulnerabilities including protection concerns, lack of MHPSS support and outdated school infrastructure. These factors undermine the quality of education, generating and contributing to higher school dropout rates. 20,560 UNRWA students in Area C, H2, EJ and those living in refugees' camps continue to face challenges in accessing schools. The fulfilling of their right to education is severely impacted by Israeli Security Forces (ISF) violence and armed search operations in schools; harassment, intimidation, and settlers' violence while commuting to schools.In these areas, where girls need to walk long distances to get to school, they are at risk of dropping out under parental pressure due to fear for them, while believing that boys can walk to school. In areas, s close to the Israeli labour market, boys are vulnerable to dropping out. Often, girls with disabilities are more likely to leave school under parental pressure to hide the existence of a disability.Also, according to UNRWA data, ISF is more likely to use live ammunition in refugee camps; 48% of injuries occurring in refugee camps are caused by ISF use of live ammunition, compared with 14% of injuries in non-camp locations.Exposure to violence during such incidents is a key source of stress and anxiety among children, parents, and teachers, who are increasingly in need of mental health and PSS. However, major capacity gaps in school-based PSS, particularly in the most exposed areas, have prevented the educational system from properly addressing this need. This has resulted in an extra burden on UNRWA teachers and has increased the risk of long-term mental health disorders among affected children (boys and girls). The situation is further aggravated for the 1,852 students (1,085 girls and 767 boys) with disabilities enrolled in UNRWA schools who face a higher risk of dropout, due to gaps in adapted facilities, inadequate transportation means, and limited staff capacity to accommodate for their needs. As per the MSNA, 88.9% of households perceived that children with mental or physical disability faced additional challenges in accessing education services, with the biggest challenges being: bullying, lack of adapted infrastructure, lack of adapted classrooms, and transportation and travel constraints.UNRWA is working within this challenging context to continue providing quality, inclusive, and equitable education to Palestine refugees, in addition to the context of military occupation and the challenges it provides. Some remote UNRWA refugee communities, especially in Area C, have struggled with access to education, and especially with remote learning. The vulnerability of the target population to protection risks following rights violations and the relative power imbalance between humanitarian workers and refugees requires UNRWA to ensure that safe complaints and feedback channels based on consultations with beneficiaries, are accessible and known to the target population, including feedback and complaints on goods and services and more serious complaints related to misconduct and sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA). Consultations undertaken by the PSEA Network for the oPt confirm the need for all organisations to strengthen SEA policies and processes. The MSNA found that 8.5% of refugee HHs reporting awareness of how to access and use complaint or reporting mechanisms (compared to only 1.7% of non-refugee HH). While this is a significant improvement and an indication that UNRWA’s efforts to raise awareness on how to access and use complaint or reporting mechanisms are producing results, there is still a need to strengthen these mechanisms. UNRWA will guarantee coordination through the periodic meeting it attends along with other cluster members, in addition to sharing relevant reports.

2 - Activities

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

The project adopts a do no harm and conflict sensitivity approach, including specificmeasures to ensure that identified risks are addressed within the action andunintended negative consequences are minimised. UNRWA has developed an internalguideline for ethical practices in the context of briefings, field visits, communicationsand others. UNRWA’s Case Management SOPs ensure that protection cases areidentified and referred to specialised support services. SOPs cover compulsoryreporting on SEA and support to survivors. A dedicated psychological consultationhotline is available to all refugees. UNRWA has existing guidelines for disabilityinclusion, gender equality, PSEA and child protection which are all fully applied in thisproject. In UNRWA's Protection Strategy (2012), the prioritisation of safety and dignityis explicitly address as a key protection standard where UNRWA programmes deliverservices in a manner that ensures the safety and dignity of beneficiaries and staff.UNRWA ensures that its actions do not cause unintended harm or result in reducedprotection of beneficiaries, communities or staff, including risk mitigation for SEA. Ona biannual basis all of UNRWA's activities are subject to a protection mainstreamingaudit, which assesses the integration of protection principles in UNRWA's work. Thisaudit included extensive interviews and focus group discussions with beneficiaries,and sought to assess UNRWA's application of the four main protection principles,including the principle of Do No Harm. UNRWA ensures that vulnerableindividuals can access assistance by external referrals, UNRWA has developeddedicated case management SOPs and internal/external referral pathways, to ensurethat the comprehensive needs are met. In the planning of its advocacy approach andstrategy UNRWA engages with a range of community representatives, includingprotection committees, village councils, camp resident committees – as well asholding focus group discussions including for women, children, youth and the elderlyin target communities.
