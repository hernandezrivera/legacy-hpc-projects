Project Summary

This project aims to enhance women and adolescent girls' access to second-chance education and learning opportunities. UN Women has five Multi-Purpose Women’s Centers (MPWCs) in Camps 3, 4, 4 extension, 5, and 18. UN Women will have 3 more MPWCs in the Host Communities. MPWCs have been providing safe spaces for women and adolescent girls for accessing learning opportunities and skill building trainings and livelihood activities, as well as psycho-social services, counseling and referral services for GBV cases. Learning and education opportunities are the key component of this project.
UN Women has adapted its global Second Chance Education Programme approach and content to Cox’s Bazar context and harmonized with the learning framework for the Rohingya adolescents. The learning pathways provided include basic literacy (in Burmese and/or English); numeracy; lessons on life skills such as sexual and reproductive health and menstrual hygiene; awareness raising sessions on gender-based violence, negative consequences of early and child marriages, and human trafficking; as well as basic financial literacy, digital literacy and management skills.

1 - Identified Needs

There is a very high level of illiteracy among Rohingya women – particularly among adolescents who did not have access to education in Myanmar. This leads to unfortunate regeneration of repressive gender roles and harmful practices like child marriage, which cannot be tolerated and needs to be addressed diligently and urgently. Even before Covid-19, the Rohingya women and adolescent girls in the have been facing challenges in accessing opportunities and services in the Camps. The Rohingya community is highly conservative and there is deeply rooted gender norms in the Rohingya society, which leads to women and adolescent girls having limited mobility and therefore limited access to education and learning opportunities, information, assistance and services, low level of participation to decision making, low level of participation to livelihood activities; and high level of GBV. In the refugee settlement, learning centers for children up to 14 years old have been established. Out of the primary school-aged children (aged 6-14), 57% of all girls and 60% of boys have attended the services of learning centres since arriving in Bangladesh. However, for adolescents over 15, particularly girls, there are very few gender and age-appropriate learning centres and educational facilities for them. The education sector has reported that only 10.3% of the 54,000 adolescents and youth (ages 15-24) that the sector has targeted have enrolled in any education activity so far. According to the recent MSNA report, 49% of individuals aged 3-24 were reported to have attended learning centre and in all age categories (6-14, 15-18, and 19-24), the prevalence of males is more than female in terms of attendance, which further stresses the lack of female education in the Rohingya community. The lack of support for at-home learning will likely lead to many adolescent boys and girls dropping out. However, the increase in unpaid care work and the low valued placed on girls’ education will put adolescent girls in a worse situation. Apart from that, Host community children also learn on their own now that schools have closed. Approximately 90% of children learn on their own for an average of two hours a day. There is very high level of illiteracy among Rohingya women – particularly among adolescents who did not have access to education in Myanmar. This leads to unfortunate regeneration of repressive gender roles and harmful practices like child marriage. With Covid-19 the existing inequalities have been exacerbating, and the Covid-19 crisis have been disproportionately affecting women and girls, as highlighted in the Rapid Gender Analysis on the Impact of COVID-19 in Cox’s Bazar (conducted by the Gender Hub, UN Women, OXFAM and Care). However, their access to information and health services was lesser, due to the restrictive gender norms such as purdah. Meanwhile, the policing of and violence and discrimination against women also increased. Education has been one of the most powerful tools for empowerment of women and girls, through raising their awareness, self-confidence and self-respect; by increasing their status in the community, and through linking them with life skills and livelihood opportunities. With education, women and girls change their and their families’ lives for better and take their communities to a higher level. Therefore, the project aims to contribute to enhance empowerment and resilience of Rohingya women and adolescent girls in the refugee camps and host community through development and delivery of a learning programme. The planned learning programme will include basic literacy (in Burmese and/or English); numeracy; lessons on life skills such as sexual and reproductive health and menstrual hygiene; awareness raising sessions on gender-based violence, negative consequences of early and child marriages, and human trafficking; as well as basic financial literacy, digital literacy and management skills.

2 - Activities or outputs

Activity 1: Percentage/number of crisis-affected host community and Rohingya refugee girls and boys aged 3 to 24 years old receiving education and play materials, supplies and equipment aligned with Education Sector standards and other guidelines such as INEE minimum standards. Activity 2: Percentage/number of teachers-facilitators and caregivers from the host and refugee communities trained in thematic education principles and/or pedagogy as per TPD Framework and Education Sector guidelines including COVID 19 prevention and preparedness, PSS, Crisis & Disaster Risk Reduction strategies.
