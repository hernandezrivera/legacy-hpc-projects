Project Summary

As the conflict protracts in Eastern Ukraine, it is vital to invest in the resilience of populations affected by the conflict and at risk of humanitarian disaster. With over 3 million people still in need of humanitarian aid and noting the potential negative impact from a major prolonged disruption to local infrastructures and systems, the risk of humanitarian disaster in conflict-affected areas is high.
Currently, millions of Donbas residents who are dependent on central water systems are at risk if/when critical infrastructure, e.g. the Donetsk Filter Station, is damaged. Since 2014, critical water and sanitation facilities situated close to the line of contact (LoC) have been under constant attack, disrupting access to water for millions of people on both sides of the LoC, with over 80 incidents affecting the utility supply in 2018 and more than 50 for 2019 as of September.
Damage incurred by critical infrastructure as a result of the conflict compounds the consequences of a systematic lack of investments in the public utility infrastructure pre-2014 and could trigger cascading disasters. Public infrastructure in Donbas is aging and poorly maintained and, because of the ongoing conflict, frequently damaged. Utility companies struggle to restore/repair infrastructure given persisting public debt and limited access to damaged sites as a result of the conflict, with repair works requiring windows of silence.
In addition to problems related to large networks, which require regional and/or national attention/action, municipal/communal water and wastewater systems are also in disrepair. Municipal sewage and water supply networks are dilapidated in a majority of settlements along the LoC, threatening contamination of water supply (OSCE Environmental Assessment, 2017).
Considering difficulties in undertaking adequate repairs of water supply systems or damaged power lines, local authorities and the humanitarian community have had to fill the gap in water provision caused by shell strikes and vulnerable water networks through water trucking, while communities consume water from non-potable sources. The impact of water-related risks is also compounded by state responders’ insufficient capacities and limited planning. For example, during the October 2018 water outage in Toretsk, NGOs reported that information provided by LAs was often not accurate; water provided was not of good quality nor reaching immobile people; and the water trucking capacity deployed was insufficient.
In response to this situation, ACTED will implement a water safety project to a) assess water risks in targeted communities close to the line of contact; b) develop – jointly with local authorities, communities, and public utility companies (PUCs) where relevant – water safety plans; c) provide targeted material support towards the implementation of the plans, and/or community-led projects which aim to increase the quantity or quality of water. Leveraging in-country and global capacity, and based on needs and gaps identified, ACTED ultimately aims to sustainably – through a community based approach – mitigate some of the WASH hazards that conflict-affected communities are vulnerable to and which could trigger or compound a cascading disaster.
Notably, while the activities will benefit target communities as a whole, ACTED will pay particular attention to the needs of elderly people, women, particularly in women-headed HHs, and people with disabilities/limited mobility – noting the demographic particularities of the population within 5km of the LoC: over 40% are elderly, 59% are women, and almost 15% have a disability. Project activities and outputs will be sensitive to the specific threats and vulnerabilities that are faced by people with disabilities, and mainstream consideration of these in local assessments and planning tools. The needs and vulnerabilities of IDPs, who are present in the areas targeted by ACTED, will also be considered.

1 - Needs

The majority of municipal/communal water and wastewater systems in conflict-affected areas are dilapidated or broken, noting limited access for repairs and a systematic lack of investments and maintenance. Access to and quality of water in target areas has decreased since pre-conflict, incl. because of massive leakage which reduces the provision of piped water to households (HHs). While the proportion of HHs across GCA experiencing daily water shortages decreased slightly from 2016 to 2018, over 10% of HHs within 5km of the LoC still report frequent shortages (REACH 2018). Generally, populations in settlements closest to the LoC tend to rely on decentralized water sources given distance from centralized systems, and/or dilapidation of pipelines. In rural areas close the LoC, only a third (33%) of HHs rely on piped water, followed by bottled water (11%), tube wells/boreholes (28%), dug wells (20%), water trucks (4%) and spring water (2%; REACH 2018). Almost two thirds of HHs in small urban areas (60%) rely on piped water, 17% rely on bottled water, and only 7% on tube wells/boreholes and 9% on dug wells. 2018-19 WASH assessments (arche noVa, Caritas) indicate that water quality monitoring is insufficient; authorities do not consistently test water quality across time nor location. For non-drinking purposes, HHs living in areas close to the LoC rely mostly on piped water (72% within 5km, 89% within 20km), noting recurring water shortages given damage incurred by pipelines and limited access for repairs due to the conflict. Sediment clogging in pipes reduces flow and leads to massive leakages, swamping farm fields and/or drying out water sources (2019 arche noVa needs assessment). Underground water may be contaminated because of inadequate waste disposal practices (REACH, 2017 REACH). Given a lack of government investment into infrastructure and increasing debt incurred by PUCs since the beginning of the conflict, prices for water have risen significantly and represent approximately a fifth of HH income in GCA. This incentivizes use of negative coping strategies, incl. using sub-standard water sources: conflict-affected population tend to rely on unsafe water sources and practices, increasing exposure to waterborne diseases (2019 arche noVa needs assessment; 2018-19 ACTED program data). REACH data does not suggest differences in WASH needs between women and men headed HHs. Women can however be a particular risk due to their role in the HH: they are disproportionately expected to do chores and childrearing, which increases their vulnerability and use of negative coping strategies, such as reusing dirty water for washing dishes or using soap paste to wash clothes (seen in GCA, 2019 ACTED PDM data). Projects that do not consider this may fail to benefit women; e.g. some vulnerable women in GCA struggle with cleaning/laundry as they are at work when water is delivered (arche noVa 2019 needs assessment). With low access to basic hygiene items, water cuts also affect women’s ability to address menstrual health needs. Notably, over 50% of heads of HH within 5km of the LoC are women: the project will make efforts to address their specific needs within the target communities. Impact of water/safety access also varies across age and socioeconomic groups. The elderly may have more severe needs noting that they tend to be less mobile and are more likely to have chronic illnesses or disability. Needs and vulnerabilities of IDPs, who can face more barriers to access than host communities, also need to be considered critically.. In addition to being consulted through the various needs assessments that informed the design of the project, vulnerable groups (of diverse age, gender, socioeconomic status, incl. IDPs and people with unique needs) will be consulted during project implementation to ensure appropriateness and feasibility of the intervention.

2 - Activities & outcomes

Outcome Increased water safety in target conflict-affected communities Water Risk Assessment: risks will be assessed in communities where they are known/expected to exist, looking at availability, safety, proximity and sustainability of sufficient quantity of water; need for and feasibility of water treatment, whether bulk or at household level; social, political or legal factors affecting water sources. ACTED will follow best practices for gender/age sensitive data collection. Qualitative methods will be used sensitively so women of all ages/other vulnerable groups are comfortable sharing information on perceptions, needs and priorities. Respondents will be told about and have access to ACTED’s Complaints and Response Mechanism (CRM). Enumerators will be trained on disaggregation and gender sensitive information collection; teams will be composed of men and women; FGDs with women will be moderated by women; and questionnaires will be tested for gender mainstreaming. Where relevant, data will be disaggregated by gender, age and other household/vulnerability indicators; the assessment will look at key protection concerns, including disability and gender, that need to be mainstreamed in the water safety plans and associated physical works. Water Safety Plans: Jointly with local authorities/PUCs at community level and based on the WRA, ACTED will develop WSPs to increase inclusive water safety/access in selected communities. ACTED will communicate to local authorities and PUCs involved that diverse age, gender, socioeconomic groups should be engaged. In conjunction with affected groups, WSPs will consider cumulative vulnerabilities and threats faced by elderly people, women and PwDs to ensure that they are mitigated. ACTED’s collaborative and participatory approach will encourage ownership of the process, esp. by those who will implement WSPs. Once finalized, WSPs will be handed over to the local communities and ACTED will support implementation of selected measures. Material support: ACTED will support LAs/PUCs with WSP implementation and/or community-led projects aiming to increase water quantity or quality. Location selection will be based on the WRA, done jointly with LAs/PUCs/communities, and consider protection issues and input from affected groups. Based on needs assessment, material support could comprise partial rehabilitation of water treatment stations; provision of pipes or equipment to PUCs working on water supply/access; rehabilitation of existing water sources. Joint implementation of these projects with existing water management structures will ensure that they meet immediate local needs, and involvement of communities will increase accountability towards affected populations. ACTED will ensure that men and women from different age/socioeconomic groups, incl. IDPs and PwDs, are consulted during the process. ACTED will ensure that water points are rehabilitated/repaired in well-lit and safe areas to mitigate protection concerns for women, the elderly, and PwDs. Water testing will be conducted at all sites where repairs are implemented to evaluate the impact of the intervention on beneficiaries. ACTED has established appropriate CRM across all projects to enable beneficiaries to report abuse by staff/others and provide feedback on activities.. All staff are trained on and all beneficiaries made aware of ACTED’s Code of Conduct and especially the CRM. The CRM is available to people with disabilities through SMS, Viber and email. It is managed by an independent AME team (dedicated staff). Complaints are managed according to specific SOPs. ACTED aims to close the feedback loop within 15 days to clearly and rapidly address concerns voiced by beneficiaries. ACTED also facilitates exchanges through social workers and community leaders. As the project focuses on areas close the LoC , it is expected that beneficiaries will consist of a majority of women (57%) and elderly people (33%), PwDs (15%), and IDPs (4%).
