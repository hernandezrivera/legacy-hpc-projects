Project Summary

UNICEF aims to advance equitable access to quality learning for the most disadvantaged
children, adolescents and youth in Syria. UNICEF’s priority areas are as follows:
a) Scaling up equitable access to education
b) Enhancing the quality of education services
c) Strengthening education systems
With over 2m children out of school, UNICEF will focus on both increasing access to the
hardest to reach especially the displaced, those across lines of control in the midst of the
ongoing crisis and ensuring safe learning environments for those returning to newly
accessible areas and at risk dropping out. Within this framework UNICEF will continue to
advocate for greater humanitarian access and seek to reach the most marginalized
children. Priority vulnerable groups will entail those who are displaced and out of school
and who are in the communities that host them. Geographical priority will be given to
areas with high severity, including newly accessible, militarily encircled, besieged and hard
to reach areas. Furthermore, accountability to affected populations, including community engagement and community systems strengthening will be a critical marker of interventions.UNICEF will reinforce coordination within Damascus, Amman and
Gaziantep hubs in a Whole of Syria approach.
As these vulnerable groups face multiple barriers to schooling, it is critical to strengthen a
knowledge/evidence base and address their barriers in a cross sectorial way. UNICEF will
increase synergies with Social/Child Protection, Adolescent Development and
Participation (ADAP), Communication for Development (C4D), and Water, Sanitation and
Hygiene (WASH).
UNICEF will respond to immediate humanitarian needs as investments for the future. It
will strengthen partnership with authorities, NGOs, communities and schools in view of
resilience building and early recovery. Furthermore, UNICEF will coordinate with other UN
agencies to leverage resources in jointly selected communities and schools. UNICEF leads
the education sector coordination in all the WoS hubs including the sub hubs of Aleppo,
Homs, Tartous, Hassakeh, Idleb, Hama, Raqqa, Dar’a, Quneitra and Deir ez Zor. The

Needs

The protracted crisis in Syria, now in its eighth year, has forced an estimated 2.1 million, over one third of children, out-of-school and has put a further 1.3 million children at risk of dropping out. Over 5.8 million school-aged children (including more than 100,000 Palestine refugee children) and over 120,000 teachers are in need of education assistance inside Syria, 61 per cent of whom are in acute and immediate need. Hostilities have resulted in over one in three schools being damaged, destroyed, no longer accessible or occupied for shelter and other purposes. Schools are reported as the most common building type used for IDP sites in Syria, accounting for an estimated 32 per cent of collective centres. Due to a shortage of functional classrooms, classes are overcrowded and over one million children required to attend double-shift schools. Teaching capacity remains overstretched. Over 140,000 teachers and education personnel are no longer in their teaching posts. This shortage, as well as irregular and under payment of qualifiedteachers also limits the quality and provision of education. Children who are out of school face increased protection risks including child marriage and the worst forms of child labour. More than 40 per cent of the out-of-school children in Syria are between 15 and 17 years of age and have differentiated learning, skills development and livelihoods needs. The escalation of violence has led to increasing war related psychological trauma for children, their families and education personnel. Exposure to violence decreasesattendance, leads to drop out, and affects learning outcomes by limiting concentration. Insome areas, over half of children with a disability have an unmet need for education, and over two thirds require specialized health services which are not available in their area. The changes in control in central and south Syria in early and mid-2018 as well as increasing hostilities in northwest Syria in September 2018 caused new waves of civilian displacement into Rural Damascus, Idleb Aleppo, and to Northeast Syria. Existing education capacity in these areas was already limited prior to recent displacement influx. Schools in IDP/returnee receiving communities have limited absorption capacity for newly arriving students while in most camps education services are deeply insufficient or nonexistent. In public schools in locations in Rural Damascus, Queneiitra and Daraa, classroom sizes reached 150 students per teacher in September 2018 with an average of 48:1 pupil teacher ratio. However, the shift of control has opened accredited education opportunities to school-age children in South and Central Syria that require durable education services. A 2018 assessment of 226 camps in Aleppo and Idleb governorates found that 73 per cent of camps and settlements have no education services at all, showing no improvement from 2017. The education system is fragmented and overburdened with multiple curricula taught. This has significant repercussions on the provision and certification of learning. Protection risks have been observed during official examination periods for students crossing active frontlines to sit for official exams.

Implementation of activities

UNICEF will focus on scaling up access to quality education and enhancing education system through implementation of education activities guided by WoS Education Response Plan. The education activities will be implemented in prioritized areas with high severity in 14 governorates in Syria. UNICEF will make use its established implementation system to carry out education activities. UNICEF will deliver education through partnership with the Ministry of Education, civil society organizations (CSOs), national and international NGOs. The partnership uses different modalities of implementation including Partnership Cooperation Agreement (PCA) with implementing partners, Long Term Agreement (LTA) with suppliersand contractors. Furthermore, the implementation of activities will include community engagement and mobilization, particularly for Back to Learning (BtL). This will be particularly pertinent for supporting out of school and returnee children, including those in hard to reach and newly accessible areas. As such, proposed activities will include: 1. Enhancing the community engagement to complement the focus on education service delivery2. Support introduction of life skills platforms in classes, schools and community settings as an entry point for social cohesion, and as a means to support Back to Learning efforts.3. Support evidence generation to analyze root-causation of OOSC and to support the documentation of best practices.4. Re-enforcing the capacity building efforts with a communication for social and behavioral change component for key staff from ministry and partners empowering them for optimal BTL planning and implementation.

Protection Risk Analysis

The education assessment indicated that a key barrier to education access is lack of safety and security. The Monitoring and Reporting Mechanism on Grave Violation against Children’s (SCR 1612, 2005) verified 56 attacks against education and education personnel in the first half of 2018. Compared to 2017, the number of verified attacks on schools for only the first half of 2018 is more than 65% of the total number of the previous year, showing an increased trend. As populations move into increasingly unexploded ordnance (UXO) contaminated areas, the presence of UXOs prevents children from going to school or makes it dangerous for children and school personnel to do so. All forms of attacks on schools or learning spaces remain a significant protection risk. Efforts are needed to ensure that deconfliction is done and is effective. As the population in north west Syria (NWS) and north east Syria (NES) are pushed into increasingly contaminated areas, more cross-sectoral and cross-cutting engagements are needed to sensitize the population on prevention and reporting. The threat of military offensive also necessitates adequately resourced and practiced safety and security planning for schools, including awareness sessions on UXOs for both children and adults, deconfliction and school safety and evacuation plans. Changes in the security situation can also lead to increased risks including displacement and restrictions of movement for the population and local partners. Poverty and displacement push vulnerable families to negative coping mechanisms including child labour and child marriage. The vulnerability of out of school children in such circumstances puts children at substantially higher risk of being exposed to exploitation, abuse and rights violations.Mitigating measures• Partners will take into account risks to children, students, teachers and education personnel when planning and designing their projects and work towards minimizing risks through consultative process with Syrian national and local authorities as well as communities.• Key messages for back-to-learning (BTL) campaigns will take into account the concerns of parents and other community members.• Ensure projects in insecure locations have security plans for their staff and the projects beneficiaries have sufficient resources to enact those plans. Commit partners to ensure a minimum duty of care to their staff including a right of refusal.• Ensure that staff members, teachers and education personnel are trained in safety and security, including mine risk education and deconfliction.• Ensure relevant authorities/groups are aware of/ engaged with the project and commit to ensuring basic safety/access for its implementation.• Ensure flexible context-informed approaches and mechanisms to deliver the project outputs/outcomes, so that the projects activities remain relevant and can be implemented in a changing context.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Key risks and mitigation measures:1. At the operational level, several education management and governance related risks undermining the delivery of quality education services, such as lack of qualified teachers and education personnel, teacher absenteeism, and the impossibility to deliver education supplies due to access constraints. Education partners might also face security-related challenges at a field level, whilst coordinating with education authorities due to unclear governance structures, active armed opposition groups and politicization of the education sector;Mitigation Measures: UNICEF is now working much more on systems strengthening and institutional building measures to improve the delivery of education services. Increased advocacy, coordination and planning with local partners will be further enhanced.2. The protection related risks to children accessing school are still diverse and overwhelming ranging from security, school safety due to unexploded ordinates, poverty, discrimination and exclusion of children based on political affiliations as well as those with disability, culture related gender based violence. Mitigation measures: UNICEF will continue the adoption of flexible learning mechanisms and a diversified range of protective learning spaces to more safely and effectively deliver education interventions. The comprehensive school approach and other multi-sectoral measures will ensure the provision of a holistic package while integrating a community engagement component to improve access;3. The impacts of a constantly changing security and political situation means efforts to address school improvements in newly accessible areas may be reversed at any moment further exacerbating the circumstances of marginalized groups including vulnerable children. Mitigation measures: UNICEF will continue and further increase to conduct advocacy and seek improved access through community-based approaches and dialogues with other all concerned authorities to improving access for these groups. Preparedness and contingency plan will be enhanced. Key Assumptions:1. Changes in both the security and political situation since mid-2018 which have seen reductions in military operations, the opening up areas for resumption of education will be maintained in 2019 and beyond.2. Continuous good will and political support as well as flexibility which has enabled the programme to reach the hard to reach will be maintained. 3. The current level of donor funding for the sector and hence UNICEF interventions will be maintained.4. The current projections of both the PIN and severity scale will not drastically change in the coming months.
