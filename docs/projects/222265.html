Project Summary

UNICEF's initiative aims to deliver safe, gender-responsive, and high-quality child protection services to safeguard the most vulnerable children in South Sudan from the devastating impacts of natural disasters, conflict, and poverty. The program seeks to establish a comprehensive and sustainable child protection system across the country by providing life-saving emergency response services and mitigation programs. Target regions for the intervention align with the Priority 1 and 2 locations identified in the Humanitarian Needs Response Plan (HNRP) through inter-sectoral analysis.
UNICEF is committed to ensuring that critical child protection services are accessible in communities affected by violence, flooding, and other natural disasters. The initiative prioritizes children at high risk, including those without caregivers, child-headed households, children with disabilities, survivors of rape and sexual violence, children associated with armed forces or groups, children living on the streets, child laborers, and those trafficked for marriage, labor, or exploitation. Additionally, recognizing the ongoing risks posed by explosive ordnance in conflict-affected areas, UNICEF will integrate risk education into its programming to protect children from injury and raise awareness about the dangers of unexploded ordnance.
To achieve these goals, UNICEF will establish partnerships with local organizations to ensure that trained professionals provide rapid mental health and psychosocial support. This approach aligns with UNICEF's localization strategy, which focuses on empowering local partners to deliver quality services. Each supported social worker will manage cases of severely affected children, adhering to internationally recognized standards.
The social workforce supporting this initiative includes senior social workers responsible for managing cases involving severe child rights violations such as rape, sexual violence, incarceration, orphanhood, or disabilities that hinder access to essential services. A number of social workers have been seconded to the Ministry of Gender, Child, and Social Welfare to strengthen institutional capacity. Case workers focus on managing less severe but still critical cases, providing intensive support to prevent further abuse, neglect, or deprivation. Para-social workers play a vital role in reintegrating children into their communities, identifying children at risk of abuse or neglect, building supportive relationships with children lacking stable care, and facilitating structured mental health and psychosocial support programs.
As the provider of last resort for child protection in South Sudan, UNICEF's interventions are guided by two main approaches. The first is complex and sensitive case management. UNICEF is committed to ensuring no child is left behind and is mandated by the international community to serve as the provider of last resort. This means that when partner organizations identify cases, they bear the duty of care for those children. However, if partners cannot find appropriate solutions, they may escalate cases to UNICEF for guidance and support. For cases involving extreme complexity or sensitivity, UNICEF may take on direct case management. This occurs rarely, as the focus remains on building the capacity of government and local non-governmental organizations to manage such cases effectively. UNICEF’s primary role is to strengthen South Sudan's child protection system by developing a workforce of highly skilled, ethical, and professional case workers within local organizations and government institutions.
The second approach involves creating a referral chain to address serious child protection threats. By 2025, UNICEF aims to establish a system where cases are identified at the community level by para-social workers, who will provide initial care and support. 1. Needs

In 2025, South Sudan will experience extensive humanitarian needs amid substantial aid funding reductions. Approximately 9 million people, including 4.9 million children, 2.2 million women, and 1.4 million individuals with disabilities, will need humanitarian assistance. The country will continue to experience a protracted child protection crisis, particularly in conflict-affected and underserved areas across the Upper Nile, Bahr el Ghazal, Unity, and Jonglei States. Key child protection issues include family separation, recruitment into armed groups, physical and sexual violence, exploitation, and psychosocial distress. Children in urban areas resort to exploitation and enrollment into gangs as an extreme survival coping mechanism. Children with disabilities and survivors of gender-based violence (GBV), particularly girls, are disproportionately at risk, with limited access to essential services such as health, psychosocial support, and legal aid.Adding to these challenges, explosive ordnance contamination continues to pose a grave threat to children and their communities in South Sudan. Decades of conflict have left vast areas littered with landmines, unexploded ordnance (UXO), and other remnants of war, particularly in regions already struggling with high humanitarian needs. Children are often the most vulnerable, exposed to these dangers while playing, walking to school, or helping with daily chores. Explosive ordnance contamination not only endangers lives but also restricts access to farmland, schools, and essential services, exacerbating poverty and displacement. Risk education will be critical in raising awareness and equipping children and caregivers with the knowledge to avoid explosive hazards.With a target of 300,000 boys, girls, and their caregivers, UNICEF’s Child Protection (CP) Section will focus on enhancing linkages between child protection in emergencies, youth and adolescent programs, gender-based violence response, and systems strengthening to address the evolving needs of children. Case management for survivors will be central to the program, implemented through 930 social workers (438 women) supervised by 31 implementing partners. Collaboration with Juba University, the Ministry of Gender, Child, and Social Welfare, and other key stakeholders will contribute to continuous improvement in the skills of the social workforce, ensuring sustainability and shifting strategies from a data-driven focus on unaccompanied and separated children (UASC) to building empathetic relationships with survivors of complex and multiple abuses, such as child marriage, child labor, and neglect.Case management processes need to be reimagined to address broader child protection issues in South Sudan. Currently, the majority of cases documented in case management plans and CPIMS+ involve UASC, with nearly 50% of the total child protection caseload since 2011 falling into this category. This narrow focus has resulted in an insufficient number of cases addressing children with disabilities, children living on the streets, children in conflict with the law, child laborers, and other vulnerable groups. Supervisory practices also require improvement, as many supervisors do not engage regularly with case workers, follow up on actions, monitor outcomes, or provide mentorship and coaching. Routine "case conversations," which are vital for discussing challenges, celebrating successes, and improving practices, are inconsistent across regions.Additionally, referrals are frequently treated as case closures, with no further follow-up to ensure that the referred child’s needs have been addressed. Strengthening referral systems and monitoring practices will be essential to ensuring that children are better protected and supported after referrals. The program will advice on norms and standards, facilitation of dialogue and cooperation, and research and development focused on innovative EORE solutions.1,000 of people reached through messaging on prevention and access to services for PSEA: the program will look at the four pillars of PSEA-Management and Coordination, Community Engagement and Support, Prevention, and Response. The program will provide a comprehensive framework for incorporating SEA prevention and response throughout each organization.120,000 Women, girls, and boys accessing gender-based violence risk mitigation, prevention and/or response interventions:UNICEF will scale up GBV response, prevention, and risk mitigation programming in line with its Core Commitments for Children (CCC) under three key interlinked outputs. 1) Women, girls, and boys have improved access to specialized GBV case management and PSS.UNICEF will provide technical support, training, and capacity building to provide quality comprehensive GBV response services, including GBV case management and PSS, and enhance partners' expertise as GBV first responders. GBV partners will also work with other service providers to improve GBV referral pathways and coordination. In locations impacted by the Sudan crisis, GBV emergency response interventions will be scaled up. 2) Communities are targeted with social norms interventions to address harmful norms and practices. “Communities Care” is UNICEF’s flagship programme to address the social norms, root causes, and drivers of GBV. It has two components: (1) to create an environment in which survivors can obtain holistic, compassionate care and support from quality survivor-centered services and (2) to catalyse community-led action to transform harmful norms and beliefs that underpin GBV. UNICEF will provide technical support including trainings, on the job mentoring, learning forums and regular review and adaptations based on lessons learned. 3) Capacity of Nutrition and WASH partners to implement GBV risk mitigation measures are strengthened.Evidence from the three-year study on the effectiveness of GBV risk mitigation within nutrition programming in South Sudan demonstrated promising outcomes such as increased referrals to/from GBV and Nutrition services and improved safety and access of women and girls to nutrition services. UNICEF will provide technical support to partners to implement the GBV Risk Mitigation Programmatic Package. This includes GBV risk identification through safety audits and consultations, training staff on GBV, enhancing community outreach to improve effective, timely, and confidential two-way referrals with other sectors.
