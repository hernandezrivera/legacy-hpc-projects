Project Summary

" "" DRC proposes the project focusing camps 8E, 8W, 10, and 12, on shelter assistance and community engagement activities. For shelter assistance, the focus will be targeting 2,673 households for SUM support in Camps 8E, 8W and 10, and an additional providing 1,400 households with TDK and technical support in Camp 8W as a gap in TDK distribution has been identified in F Block, as shelter interventions by the private partner in this block do not include TDK provision.
Through the IOM common pipeline partnership arrangement, DRC will receive the shelter materials, while DRC will be responsible for independent funding and executing the project's operations including direct staff cost, warehousing, managing Distribution Points, and other shared costs. DRC will continue to deploy the Shelter technical coordinator position for the SCCCM Sector to contribute to sector outputs as a partner. The SUM package will meet sector standards, with CFWs selected based on DRC camp profiling and scoring tools, prioritizing vulnerable individuals and matching those with specific needs to suitable assignments. A cash-based approach will support shelter for 50 host community households, supplemented by 29 man-days of labor and carpentry. SUM assistance will employ a CFWs model, with selected assignments tailored to beneficiaries’ specific needs, aiming to boost community ownership and shelter-building skills. DRC will develop prioritization criteria for the distribution of SUM in consultation with Shelter Focal and SMS community groups of different AGD, and abilities. Porter support and/or construction labor assistance will be provided to households with special shelter needs based on assessments of the household's capacity to carry shelter materials from the distribution point to their shelter and use the materials as intended. DRC will involve diverse community groups in SMS activities, with the groups designing and implementing communication strategies for TDK use. The DMUs will assist in TDK installation and disaster preparedness as part of DRR efforts. DRC will work with local representatives to establish communication channels and select beneficiaries through community consultation. Eligible beneficiaries will receive cash-based assistance, allowing them to make informed choices about tailored shelter improvements, repairs, or upgrades. Throughout the project, DRC will conduct construction monitoring, facilitating open dialogues to refine project outcomes.
DRC will cover co-fund for existing IOM-funded SMS activities focused on empowering youth who face sociocultural, safety, and educational barriers, excluding them from decision-making. By using a Human-Centered Design (HCD) approach, the project seeks to enable youth-driven solutions and increase self-reliance by improving access to economic opportunities, education, and marketable skills. This initiative will support youth-led activities to address cultural factors and build resilience through community-led micro-projects. DRC will also engage the wider community regardless of AGD—including the Disability Inclusion Support Committee (DISC)—through regular open meetings to discuss general concerns, gather feedback, and share issues in camp coordination and protection platforms for advocacy. Through Radio Listening Group Sessions for women and girls, DRC will disseminate information on GBV risks, case referrals, early marriage prevention and other life savings information using RLG content. The script aims to counter intimate partner violence, emphasizing the positive roles of women and the impact of religion and tradition. DRC will also support AGD groups across four camps with awareness and capacity-building on life-saving topics and protection. Activities will be coordinated with camp authorities and key focal points. The MEAL team will monitor progress and conduct PAM, aiming to assess the effectiveness, relevance, and outcomes of the shelter interventions after implementation.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

"The 2024 Shelter Performance Standard Assessment by the SCCCM Sector found that 71% of respondents received shelter assistance last year, while 29% did not. Additionally, 42% of shelters reported no rainwater intrusion, while 58% indicated that rainwater did enter. Also, 85% of shelters failed to meet the Desired Performance Standards for treated bamboo, and 70% showed visible signs of infestation. Furthermore, ad-hoc private partners who offer shelter support to households without implementing a proper care and maintenance plan leave these households in a precarious position after just one year of shelter construction. The latest SMS service monitoring report indicates that in Camp 10 Blocks B, C, and D, approximately 302 shelters reconstructed by a private partner in 2022 and 2023 have not received any SUM assistance. In Camp 8E, 1672 shelters in Blocks A & C need upgrades following a major fire in March 2021 but have not received any shelter materials since then. Additionally, in Camp 8W, 699 shelters in Block F built in August 2023 will likely require assistance in 2025 due to rapid deterioration from extreme conditions, including flooding and high winds. Structurally compromised shelters primarily impact women, the elderly, and people with disabilities who spend most of their time at home due to cultural constraints and movement barriers in the camps. The IOM/ACAPS “Our Thoughts” study points out that damaged or inadequate shelters force women and girls to “make the difficult decision of whether to leave for their health and comfort or whether to remain inside to maintain their dignity”. This was evident in the report of Shelter Performance Standard Report of 2024, only 23% of shelters had fully or partially tied down the roofs according to the sector guidance, with a minimum of six anchor points properly fixed to the shelter and ground or with at least four anchor points properly fixed to the shelter and ground. 77% of shelters did not meet the standard with less than four anchor points fixed to the shelter and ground. TDK helps reinforce and secure the shelters, ensuring they can withstand strong winds, heavy rainfall, and other environmental challenges commonly faced in the region. The influx and environmental pressures heighten the demand for resilient host community housing. The JMSNA 2023 shows 51% of Host community households in kutcha and 35% in semi-pucca shelters —made of temporary and semi-permanent materials, increasing cyclone vulnerability; 60% already report damages or issues.Approximately 22% of the Rohingya population are youth aged 15-24, with a significant proportion being female, indicating a notable representation among youth girls. A December 2023 MSF documentary titled “Rohingya Youth Trapped in Violence and Despair in Cox's Bazar” highlights that youth girls encounter substantial barriers to community engagement due to cultural norms, safety concerns, and restricted access to education and leadership roles. They are frequently excluded from decision-making processes and have few opportunities to express their needs. This marginalization is worsened by gender-based violence and restrictive social norms, limiting their community involvement and increasing vulnerability. As the SMS lead agency, DRC will enhance community engagement by promoting youth girls and women’s groups to empower and support their meaningful participation in camp life. The IOM Bangladesh Appeal 2024: Rohingya Humanitarian Crisis, report highlights significant unmet needs in food security, shelter materials, sanitation, and livelihoods. Statelessness and legal barriers restrict the Rohingya's movement and ability to maintain a basic standard of living. SMS enhances community engagement by establishing feedback mechanisms that include refugee voices in decision-making. It will also support CLPs to empower youth and address their involvement in negative activities by providing constructive opportunities "

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

Protection is integrated into all DRC programs and DRC will involve refugees and host communities in decision-making for shelter interventions, ensuring their input to identify protection risks and solutions. This includes engaging the DISC and other AGD groups in CLP design, implementation, and monitoring to strengthen community ownership and accountability. DRC will also empower youth through skills-building and livelihood activities, especially supporting girls by fostering youth-driven initiatives that address social exclusion and mitigate GVB risks through HCD. DRC collaborates with Shelter Focal and community groups to set inclusive prioritization criteria, focusing on individuals with specific needs, such as elderly persons, those with disabilities, female-headed households, and children, and ensures porter or labor assistance for those unable to transport materials independently. To mitigate protection risks in cash-for-work programs, DRC will explain worker selection criteria to the community beforehand, obtain informed consent before capturing personal data, and follow DRC data protection protocols. Cash-based assistance for host communities empowers vulnerable households to make decisions that enhance dignity and ownership. DRC SMS will work with local authorities, CICs, and landowners, in coordination with HLP actors, to reduce eviction risks, supporting conflict mitigation and social cohesion. DRC will provide orientation sessions for female CFWs, covering work expectations, safety precautions, and job responsibilities to increase comfort, confidence, and awareness of their rights. Each DRC implementation area has a designated PSEA focal point and a code of conduct response mechanism. The 11 existing CFRM desks and regular mobile CFRM sessions by SMS, targeting women and hard-to-reach individuals, will enable the community to report shelter safety issues or GBV risks

2.3 - Age & Disability

As the SMS agency for the proposed location, DRC conducts regular service monitoring and assessments to identify specific needs, protection concerns, and vulnerabilities within the refugee population, consulting with the community and considering gender, age, and diversity factors. DRC’s service monitoring tools identify gaps in shelter interventions and prioritize the needs of vulnerable households. Age and gender-disaggregated data is recorded in DRC’s CFW database, linked to an interactive dashboard for easy analysis. DRC aims to engage a culturally appropriate number of participants from diverse genders and ages, assigning them workspaces and tasks that align with their capacities. Work conditions will be explained to the community before recruiting cash-for-work participants to encourage women to apply. Households with People with Disabilities will be provided porter/labor support to carry materials and reconstruct their shelters. DRC will conduct Radio Listening Group sessions for women and girls that address GBV risks, promote resilience, and provide culturally sensitive preventive strategies for elderly and disabled listeners. DRC will hold regular open meetings to address safety concerns of vulnerable groups, with accessible feedback and decision-making mechanisms for those with mobility or communication challenges. Across four camps, DRC will support AGD groups through awareness and capacity-building on protection and life-saving topics. Supported by DMUs, DRC will host sessions on safe relocation, emergency centers, and preparedness for the elderly and PWD. An Accessibility Audit, conducted with a Disability Specialized Organization, will enhance service accessibility across age and gender groups. Beneficiaries will be involved in each planning and implementation stage to identify needs, vulnerabilities, and priorities. Mobile CFRM will reach elderly individuals and persons with disabilities, allowing them to express their concerns and needs

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

DRC will develop prioritization criteria for the distribution of SUM in consultation with SMS community groups of different ages, genders, and abilities. CFWs selection prioritizing vulnerable individuals identified through DRC camp profiling and scoring tools and individuals with specific needs will receive suitable assignments in tailored work environments. DRC SMS community groups of different ages, genders, and abilities will be engaged in the development of tie-down kit communication strategies catering to the needs of each group. DRC will conduct post-distribution and post-construction monitoring of shelter activities completed at the beginning and end of this project. Results will be shared through gender-segregated open dialogues with the community to validate findings and collect recommendations for project improvement. Proposed open meetings between service providers and the community will facilitate regular updates on services and gather feedback for program improvement. DRC will utilize various accessible channels and methodologies to ensure all AGD populations clearly understand the shared information. Through the existing established CFRM, the beneficiaries will have the chance to provide feedback or any complaints. All complaints will be treated confidentially and addressed promptly and appropriately, to close the feedback loop. The CFRM safeguards beneficiary personal details via data protection principles. DRC MEAL conducts an annual survey with CFW participants to assess whether cash incentives effectively meet their financial needs, evaluate the adequacy of work conditions and training, and gauge participants' satisfaction with management. All workers are informed about DRC’s CoC Response Mechanism as part of their induction. All DRC staff and CFW participants undergo an annual code of conduct and PSEA training. DRC has a PSEA focal point assigned to each of its implementation areas and maintains a code of conduct response mechanism

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

DRC is committed to preventing and responding to PSEA through a comprehensive policy framework. Each implementation area has an assigned PSEA focal point, who plays a crucial role in upholding the organization's code of conduct and response mechanisms. DRC ensures that persons of concern and stakeholders are informed about the PSEA reporting mechanisms, including the contact information for PSEA focal points. This information is disseminated through DRC’s CFRM awareness sessions and the DRC SMS community engagement platform, enabling stakeholders to provide feedback or raise concerns. All DRC staff and volunteers are required to complete annual training on the CoC and PSEA to reinforce the organization's commitment to safeguarding. To further enhance awareness, DRC conducts Radio Listening Group sessions tailored to women and girls, focusing on PSEA risks, prevention strategies, and case referrals. These sessions are designed with cultural sensitivity in mind, ensuring that messaging aligns with community values and norms. Additionally, DRC has established Community Groups that will receive training and orientation on the PSEA mechanism. DRC collaborates closely with protection focal points to strengthen referral pathways and ensure accountability in responding to reported incidents. Regular assessments of the risks of sexual exploitation and abuse within camps and during project implementation are conducted by PSEA Focal. This includes identifying vulnerable groups and potential abusers, and adapting project activities to mitigate risks. Tailored interventions are implemented to ensure that activities do not inadvertently place beneficiaries in vulnerable situations

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Under this project, DRC will provide TDK assistance to all households in Camps 8E and 8W. DMUs supervised by DRC SMS, will support households in developing skills for TDK installation, raising awareness on its benefits, and ensuring proper installation as part of ongoing DRR initiatives. The TDKs play a critical role in reducing the risk of shelter damage or destruction during extreme weather events, helping to protect refugees’ lives and possessions. DRC will prioritize environmentally friendly materials, such as treated bamboo and locally sourced resources, to improve shelter durability while minimizing the ecological impact. To promote environmental stewardship, DRC will engage youth in implementing CLPs such as reforestation initiatives and camp cleaning campaigns. For such initiatives, DRC will closely collaborate with EEN. Furthermore, AGD groups will participate in awareness-raising sessions on DRR and the Early Warning System to enhance preparedness. As the SMS agency, DRC will conduct risk mapping to identify high-risk households and areas within the camps, focusing on environmental hazards like flooding and landslides. As part of the existing SMS CWC activities, DRC will incorporate content addressing the long-term impacts of climate change, specifically focusing on its effects in the Rohingya camps. DRC will closely collaborate with SD and DRR partners to mitigate these risks effectively

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

DRC leads EPRP at the camp level in the proposed location, leveraging its role as the SMS agency. This proposed project will be implemented through DRC’s existing SMS team, which has substantial experience conducting regular risk assessments to identify potential risks in camps. The team will collaborate closely with relevant stakeholders to inform mitigation and preparedness strategies, addressing both shelter and community engagement needs. Under EPR measures, the DMUs will support households in applying skills for TDK installation, raising awareness about its importance, and ensuring correct installation to reduce vulnerability during emergencies. Radio Listener Group sessions will serve as a vital channel for disseminating EPR information, helping prepare the community effectively. Following the IOM AoR SoP for Emergency CFWs, DRC will enlist potential CFW participants who will be available during emergencies and train them in critical EPR skills such as emergency relocation and debris removal. Community groups will participate in regular training and emergency response drills, with a focus on youth, women’s groups, and other vulnerable populations. These sessions will emphasize safe shelter practices, emergency response skills, and the use of established reporting mechanisms. As the EPRP lead at the camp level, DRC will maintain an EPRP checklist and Business Continuity Plan to ensure the ongoing ability to respond to emergencies. In its capacity as a member of the EPRP Working Group, DRC will work closely with different stakeholders to prevent duplication of efforts and promote efficient coordination. DRC’s EPR efforts will provide age- and gender-responsive emergency preparedness and response measures tailored to the significant natural and human-made hazards affecting the camps and surrounding host communities, in alignment with the Multi-Hazard Response Plan 2022

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

DRC will mobilize youth groups within the proposed camps, working closely with established community committees to enhance youth well-being, empowerment, and resilience, thereby fostering meaningful participation. DRC will train these youth groups in the Human-Centered Design (HCD) process, engaging them in identifying youth-led initiatives to address the specific challenges of camp life. Through these initiatives, youth will contribute to reducing protection and disaster risks in the camps by implementing community-driven micro-projects. This collaboration between DRC SMS and the youth groups underscores the critical role of community participation and inclusion in humanitarian efforts. The HCD initiatives will be scaled to reach a broader youth community within the camps, empowering marginalized youth and adolescents to become social innovators and entrepreneurs, thereby promoting sustainable solutions. The existing youth groups manage a platform called “Social Window for Talent,” in DRC-managed camps, where youth identify talents within the community and create linkages between these talents and DRC’s youth engagement activities. The Social Window for Talent platform will be incorporated into Human-Centered Design initiatives to help youth apply their skills to solve community challenges, fostering an environment where youth-led problem-solving drives positive change

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

This project aims to empower youth resilience through HCD, fostering ownership in decision-making and facilitating youth-led micro-projects that address cultural, safety, and economic challenges within the camps. HCD initiatives will specifically empower young women and girls by promoting leadership, vocational skills, and decision-making abilities, thereby strengthening individual resilience and enhancing community capacity through inclusive engagement. RLG Drama sessions will showcase positive roles for women and incorporate cultural themes, empowering them to advocate for their rights. Distributing TDK and providing training on their usage will help reinforce shelters against natural hazards. Disaster preparedness training sessions will further bolster community resilience. By employing a cash-based approach for shelter assistance in host community, DRC will support shelter improvements, allowing individuals to make choices that prioritize their safety and comfort

4.2 Do project activities in the host community link to development initiatives, and/or align with development priorities in the Cox’s Bazar district? If yes, briefly describe.If yes, briefly describe.

The project’s focus on providing resilient housing addresses the urgent need for improved shelter among host communities affected by the influx of Rohingya refugees and environmental challenges. This aligns with the district's priorities to enhance resilience to climate change and natural disasters, which are critical in the context of Cox’s Bazar, an area prone to extreme weather events. The project not only addresses the immediate shelter needs but also contributes to the broader economic resilience of the host community. By integrating livelihood opportunities into the shelter assistance, the initiative supports the district’s goals of fostering economic development and reducing poverty, particularly among vulnerable groups
