Needs

In the Gaza Strip, the recurrent outbreaks of violent conflicts and the ongoing blockade continue to pose a serious threat to life, liberty and security of all Gaza population as follows the Clusters and OCHA assessments. Armed conflict, military incursions and the resulting damage and destruction of facilities and property disrupted productive sector with major industries and private sector companies employing less than 40%of the labor force they employed prior to 2014 (OCHA “Gaza: Two Years After” 2016). It adds to already precarious situation where since 2006, the economy of Gaza has been in constant decline reaching in 2017 unemployment rate of 53.7% by the end of the second quarter of 2018 (PCBS August 2018), reported by the World Bank as one of the highest in the world. As unemployment in the Gaza Strip remains twice as high as that in the West Bank, it makes the local population highly dependent on international aid and assistance (1.3 million people in Gaza are in need of humanitarian assistance; more than a million people require assistance to access basic services, including health care, water and sanitation (OCHA “Gaza: Two Years After” 2016); around 60% of Gazans are food insecure, nearly 80% receives some form of international aid, mainly food assistance (UNCT “Leave No One Behind” 2016)). Unemployment among Gaza youth (15-29) is particularly critical as it reaches 61.2% (PCBS August 2018). In addition to low labor demand, women also continue to face challenges to join the labor force as the female participation rate in Gaza is 19.7%, with an unemployment rate of 71.5% (PCBS August 2017). Altogether women are far more vulnerable given the humanitarian conditions across Gaza, where unequal access to resources and assets triggers their food insecurity (UNCT “Leave No One Behind” 2016, FSS GAM 2019) while about 9% of households in Gaza are female headed (PCBS July 2017) including 800 women widowed following the 2014 war (meaning the loss of the main breadwinner, restricted ability to access humanitarian assistance and property rights) (HNO 2016). Female headed households face higher and increasing food insecurity (46% FSS GAM 2019). Additionally, women, girls and elderly women do not benefit equally from services and humanitarian response and continue to be a key priority groups, while children from poorer HH are more prone to school dropout, child labor (boys), child marriage (girls), violence (Protection GAM 2019, HNO 2019).During ongoing SYFS work in cash for work, cash for internship and income generating interventions the reoccurring recommendation from the youth and women beneficiaries, and unskilled and low skilled workers is to provide similar interventions to meet the increasing basic needs of impoverished and otherwise vulnerable and marginalized families, especially in times of current USAID cuts of funding for such projects and UNRWA cuts in services and employment. During SYFS work the communities in different parts of the Gaza Strip asked SYFS to act as intermediary towards the donors to fund interventions like the one presented below that will serve 150 workers with cash for work intervention and 150 youth graduates (22-29 years old) with cash for internship, as well as 100 women-headed and other food insecure households with income generating activities. At least 50% of targeted beneficiaries are women with special consideration given to women-headed households. Potential activities will include sewing and other activities acceptable by women. Potential activities includes sewing, carpentry (cash for work), graduates in IT and social sciences (cash for internship), sewing, home garden, small green houses, breeding rabbits, sheep, cows etc. and other activities, especially those acceptable by women (IGA). All sectors have been identified based on needs identified through SYFS current existing records in internal database of beneficiaries in need of such support.

Activities

The potential beneficiaries will be identified based on SYFS recent projects beneficiary waiting lists (internal database), hardship cases nominated by the MOSD, UNRWA, and beneficiaries identified during the field work of SYFS community engagement and additionally coordinated with this entities, as well as with CPWG in relevant cases. Following the identification of potential beneficiaries, verification and home visits will be conducted to determine eligibility for the project assistance and specific protection requirements. Then, beneficiaries will be ranked and the most vulnerable will be selected. The selection criteria will include: (A)Household food insecurity (B)Household characteristics (number of individuals in households, PWD in the family, elderly persons in the family, pregnant and lactating women, single women, women headed households, large families with children, other health conditions); (C)Economic situation (no income/low income per household member, vulnerable according to UNRWA and MOSD database); (D)Beneficiaries who have not received similar assistance/unemployed for 6 months or longer; (E) readiness to devote full time to the allocated job/internship/IGA. In addition, youth graduates will have to graduate within the past two years and be unemployed ever since to be considered for the selection. The project benefits from multi-year planning to expand the outreach to the most vulnerable beneficiaries in the subsequent years.Activity 1: Identification and verification of beneficiaries and private sector actors for CfW, CfI (including matching) and IGA. Participatory approach will involve the representatives of the communities, both women and men. Through the field staff, the target communities will be involved in sharing the vulnerability criteria for selecting the beneficiaries of the action. Women and youth representatives will be part of the consultation in order to identify special needs from these groups of beneficiaries. In this stage, project staff will also assess special needs related to disabilities among the beneficiaries and will discuss with the communities the best way to adapt the project activities in order to address these issues.Output 1: 315 beneficiaries and their households (at least 50%women; in addition also 50%youth 22-29 years old) selected and matched with employers. Activity 2: Cash for work for 3 months for unskilled and low skilled workers (potential sectors include sewing, carpentry-sectors identified based on needs identified through SYFS current existing records in internal database of beneficiaries in need of such support).Output 2: 120 workers and their households (at least 50%women) regain access to livelihoods. Activity 3: Cash for internship for 3months for youth graduates 22-29 years old (potential sectors include graduates in IT and social sciences-sectors have been identified based on needs identified through SYFS current existing records in internal database of beneficiaries in need of such support).Output 3: 120 youth graduates and their households (22-29 years old, 50%women) gain professional experience and access to livelihoods.Activity 4: Provide intensive capacity building in IGA followed by beneficiaries’ applications with specific ideas and budgets (modules including basic life skills, basics of marketing and financial skills for IGA).Output 4: 75 beneficiaries (at least 50%women) participated in the intensive capacity building.Activity 5: Select and support (financially and with technical expertise) IGA.Output 5: 52 beneficiaries and their households (at least 50%women) selected and supported (potential sectors include sewing, home garden (small greenhouses) (an area of 500m2 and water tank of 1,000 liters capacity) and/or breeding sheep (3sheep+270kilos of fodder), especially those acceptable by women, sectors have been identified based on needs identified through SYFS current records in internal database of beneficiaries in need of such support).

Indicators

Indicator 1.1: # of beneficiaries, disaggregated by sex, age and governorate, selected for CfW and CfI and matched with employers and IGA (target 1.1: 315 individuals; at least 50% women; in addition also 50% youth 22-29 years old). Indicator 2.1: # of beneficiaries and their households, disaggregated by sex, age and governorate, received cash for work support (target 2.1: 120 individuals and their households; at least 50% women). Indicator 2.2: % of CfW beneficiaries and their households, disaggregated by sex, age and governorate, reporting improved access to livelihoods (target 2.2: 80% of beneficiaries and their households). Indicator 3.1: # of beneficiaries and their households, disaggregated by sex, age and governorate, received cash for internship support (target 2.1: 120 individuals 22-29 years old and their households; at least 50% women). Indicator 3.2: % of CfI beneficiaries and their households, disaggregated by sex, age and governorate, reporting gaining professional experience and improved access to livelihoods (target 3.2: 80% of beneficiaries and their households).Indicator 4.1: # of beneficiaries, disaggregated by sex, age and governorate, provided with intensive capacity building in IGA (target 4.1: 75 individuals; at least 50% women). Indicator 4.2: % of beneficiaries, disaggregated by sex, age and governorate, reporting improved knowledge about IGA (target 4.2: 75% of beneficiaries). Indicator 5.1: # of beneficiaries and their households, disaggregated by sex, age and governorate, received IGA support (target 5.1: 52 individuals and their households; at least 50% women).Indicator 5.2: % of IGA beneficiaries and their households, disaggregated by sex, age and governorate, reporting improved access to livelihoods (target 5.2: 75% of beneficiaries and their households).Potential adoption of indicators such as CSI under the guidance of the FSS, upon availability of resources and consolidation of good practices.SYFS uses an integrated, systematic approach to monitoring and evaluation: identifying, capturing, evaluating, managing and sharing all program knowledge through periodic tracking of outputs and outcomes and dissemination of regular reports. In the starting phase of the project the baseline data will be collected against which progress will be tracked, as well as specify data sources and collection methods and schedules, explain limitations and quality protocols, and describe evaluation methods. Indicators will be quantitative measures that can be tracked and standardized. The project staff will also employ qualitative methods as appropriate in order to capture the impact of the project. Sex and age disaggregated data collection includes review and analysis of project documents, structured and semi structured surveys, focus groups, interviews with beneficiaries and relevant stakeholders (women, men, girls, boys), on site visits and observation. Ongoing feedback from beneficiaries is regularly reviewed during weekly staff meetings to address any arising issues, including protection, to assure the accountability to the affected and targeted population. Following the baseline, activity/output data are collected monthly, entered into monitoring database, internally assessed against relevant benchmarks and compiled into reports used to inform project management and donor. At the end of the project external evaluation is conducted to explore the extent to which results are achieved and the underlying reasons. It assesses the achievements of the project, as well as implementation process and management. It is also used to identify best practices and lessons learned for future relevant programming. Findings from such evaluation are shared with donor and relevant stakeholders.
