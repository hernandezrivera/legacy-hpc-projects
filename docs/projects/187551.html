Project Summary

SC’s child protection intervention is: 1) Children and adolescents have increased resilience and access to violence prevention and response services; 2) Caregivers, community members and other sector staff have strengthened capacity to protect children/adolescents, and support their healthy development and resilience. SC’s intervention aims to improve the safety and wellbeing of girls and boys through individual case management for the most at-risk, including UASC, children with disabilities, child survivors, as well as children affected by armed conflict and armed groups (CAAFAG). Case management is the entry point to support the needs of UASC, including Syrian, Iraqis and third county nationals, and FTR is initiated by caseworkers in coordination with relevant protection actors. For children without parental care and in line with interagency SOPs, SC trains foster families, supports existing informal care arrangements, and identifies alternative forms of care when foster families are not available, such as supported independent living or ICCs, as a last resort. Case management is also a vital service for CAAFAG and an area of focus for SC to ensure that released or excluded children access the holistic services and individual support for reintegration. CVA for child protection through case management is being piloted in community settings. Training, coaching and supervision for caseworkers is a key part of delivering quality case management, and the Steps to Protect rollout includes a focus on supervision, as well as on feedback and accountability.

SC reaches children and adolescents through inclusive age and gender-sensitive group activities to foster psychosocial support, stress processing and resilience. In the absence of safe spaces in both camps and communities, a range of static, mobile and integrated child-friendly spaces (CFSs) offer group activities designed for creative expression, to develop protection knowledge and life skills, as well as strengthen an environment of trust. These spaces provide an entry point for community engagement as a hub for protection-related information and referrals, such as for children involved in child labor. Based on assessed needs and in consultation with children and caregivers, SC + partners will deliver structured PSS curricula. A targeted effort will be made to engage children with disabilities.

For the trusted and responsible adults in children’s lives–caregivers, teachers, community members and service providers, SC builds their knowledge and skills to communicate with children, identify risks facing and take action to increase children’s safety. SC will continue its rollout of the Parenting without Violence (PwV) Common Approach, with an focus on caregiver self-care and stress management alongside positive parenting practices. SC will offer sessions for mothers of young children and incorporate a focus on early childhood nutrition (delivered in tandem with nutrition colleagues) as well as outreach to caregivers of children with disabilities. PwV encourages promotion of positive parenting messages and gender equality through community structures, such as Child Protection Committees and Parent/Teacher Associations (PTAs). These structures have been supported previously and will be sustained in the proposed period. Community members and other sector staff will be targeted with capacity building on safe identification and referral, basic MHPSS including PFA, GBV and PSEA, as well as CPMS. Efforts will be made to build capacity of SC staff and partners to include children and caregivers with disabilities. Community members, including children, will identify the risks and develop awareness raising initiatives. To mitigate the risk of GBV, SC will conduct targeted GBV awareness sessions with adolescents and caregivers using an adapted GBV prevention toolkit to improve knowledge on root causes and consequences of GBV and accessing available GBV response services.

4 - Needs

NES hosts 1.9 million out of 13.4 million people in need across Syria (HNO2021). Pull factors created by humanitarian assistance in IDP camps are rising, at the same time continued service provision to camp-based populations remain critical. Equally important are needs of IDPs, returnees, and host communities in urban settings who have limited access to basic services. Early marriage, family separation and child labor were reported widely as negative coping mechanisms (MSNA 2021 KI). Specialized services and PSS were cited as key protection needs (REACH NES HSOS, Sep 2021). Prevention and response to child recruitment is needed given widespread gaps in services for CAAFAG (NES CP AoR 2021). In October 2021, 2.8 million IDPs reside in NWS, of whom 1.7 million people live in displacement sites (OCHA NWS and RAATA Situation Update No. 32). There are profound safety risks for children in contested areas as well as in camps and informal settlements; yet IDPs remain trapped due to limited prospects for durable solutions and lack of services (HNO2022). The situation, compounded by a range of behavioral drivers, has caused communities to rely on GBV/early marriage and child labor that disproportionately hurt adolescents (HNO2022; SC NWS Child Labour Assessment, 2021). More than half of HH report at least one member with psychological distress, with higher prevalence among girls and women (MSNA2021 HH survey). PwD face more barriers to accessing services + social exclusion (HNO2022).

5 - Implementation of activities

SCI activities in NES will be implemented by SC staff as well as partners; in NWS activities are partner-led; all are supported by a CP/MHPSS Specialist. Quality implementation will be supported by technical guidance and capacity building by CP, MHPSS and Gender & Inclusion TA drawing upon SC’s global and regional expertise and interagency standards. For example, SC is delivering the Steps to Protect Common Approach for its CM staff and partners, which is SC’s endorsed approach to CPCM and includes provision of in-kind emergency funds. CVA approaches will be piloted in tandem with CM to address risks caused by economic drivers; the CVA for CP MEAL Toolkit will guide risk mitigation in design and close monitoring, including effects on child wellbeing. Detailed implementation and monitoring plans are developed with field teams at the outset. Monthly review meetings with partners ensure plans are in place, communication channels are open, and performance is regularly evaluated. Monthly activities reporting is documented by field staff, and staff assigned to sub-cluster/WG reporting processes contribute to 4Ws, CM/CPIMS+, etc. Structured supervision is ongoing and contextualized quality benchmarks used for monitoring. Data is verified in IPTTs to track progress vis-à-vis targets. MEAL is strengthening child-friendly feedback mechanisms, such as FGDs and child participation activities to increase participation, inclusion and listen to those who are least often heard.

9 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk 1: Risk that existing traditional practices and community structures conflict with child protection principles (e.g. child rights messaging perceived as a threat). Likelihood: high. Impact: high. Mitigation: Train key staff as resources to act as trainers/facilitators; Regular communication with communities. Risk 2: Risk of increasing violence in the home and community for girls and boys receiving specialized services. Likelihood: high. Impact: high. Mitigation: Includ self-care strategies in programs and HR policies; training on ‘do no harm’; ensure support of relevant authorities to provision of specialized services. Risk 3: Inappropriate response to individual cases in line with case management guidelines which can lead to further harm to the child, due to lack of trained staff working with children. Likelihood: medium. Impact: medium. Mitigation: Code of Conduct and Child Safeguarding Policies, supported by refresher trainings. Risk 4: Risk to the wellbeing of child protection staff due to exposure to violence (e.g. secondary or vicarious trauma). Likelihood: high, Impact: high. Mitigation: Include Self-care strategies in programs and HR policies; establish peer support mechanisms. Risk 5: Risk to physical safety of SC staff if perceived to interfere in family-based concerns. Likelihood: high, Impact: High. Mitigation: Continue involvement and coordination with relevant authorities and stakeholders in place; Seek informed consent of beneficiaries.


10 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

Risks: Women and girls not able to access services or participate in income generating activities due to sexual harassment; exploitation and abuse by aid workers; female heads of HHs and adolescent girls in particular risk of sexual exploitation in exchange to services (Voices from Syria, 2022); risk of not reporting SEA for fear of retaliation; lack of awareness on reporting channels; stigma and shame and fear of losing on the services making it difficult for SC to hold staff/partners involved accountable. Mitigations: SC has a Child Safe Guarding policy which includes PSEA. To mitigate the risk of PSEA during the assistance delivery, the following will be included: mandatory training and regular refreshers on Child Safeguarding, Safe programming and PSEA policy to SC and partner staff; working closely with the WoS PSEA TF to strengthen feedback, reporting and response mechanisms for SEA; conducting community awareness and sensitization sessions on PSEA with emphasis on reporting and ensuring safety of survivors through referrals; regular participatory safety audit to identify protection and safety concerns arising as a result of project implementation and developing action plans to mitigate identified risks collaboratively with the community; including PSEA messages and topics in all IEC materials and training sessions conducted under the project; ensuring that centrality of protection is embedded in all projects to mitigate risks and prevent potential harm.


11 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Key risks and assumptions identified and mitigation measures include: A volatile security environment and escalation in conflict, such as recent incidents in Idleb and Al-Hasakeh, which may prohibit movement of staff and limit access by children and families in need to services. SC regularly monitors contextual developments and employs security focal points to advise on and guide staff movements. SC staff complete mandatory security trainings and context-specific safety and security management plans are regular reviewed and update. SC actively engages in advocacy efforts at multiple levels to highlight risk to civilians, particularly children, and advocate for sustained humanitarian access. Risk of continued COVID-19 restrictions may delay or hamper project delivery. Mitigation includes adapting programming modalities (reduced group size, remote, etc.), use of PPE by all SC and partner staff; incorporating COVID-19 awareness and health safety messages in all activities with children and families. The deepening economic crisis in Syria is leading to decreases in purchasing power for communities and complicating budgeting process for humanitarian actors. Mitigation measures include diverse money transfer channels and continuing to deliver our CVA in foreign currencies (USD) so beneficiaries do not lose part of their assistance due to currency devaluations. Engagement with donors on budgeting flexibility as well as reviewing currency fluctuations and tracking market trends.
