Project Summary

The project will support the provision of comprehensive GBV services, including case management and Psycho-social support for GBV survivors, for IDPs in and out of camps, returnees and vulnerable host communities in Ninewa (with a specific focus on West Mosul). Mitigation of GBV risk and prevention of further violence will also be included through a community approach involving extensively men and boys.
The capacity of GBV service providers will be strengthened in order to ensure that the access to GBV service provision is safe, confidential and timely.
The project will support static women’s centers inside PHCs in Ninewa Governorate, where GBV-related services and activities will be offered. The spaces are already operative since 2017 and will continue to be supported, while the new spaces will be equipped to ensure safety and confidentiality and comfortable provision of case management and PSS activities. Basic child-friendly spaces inside the centers will be in place in order to facilitate the participation of women with children.

Needs

UPP carried out a GBV rapid assessment (FGDs, KIIs, interviews, safety audits) in different areas inside Mosul during October and November 2018. Results has been combined with background information, Iraq Gender Profile 2018, MCNA 2018 and GBVIMS data collected during 2018 in four women centers. Results has been combined with WRO assessment in Salamiyah 2 camp. Maslawi society is ruled by a pervasive patriarchal system, which grants men power and control over women and supports unequal power relationships, access and control over resources for women and men. SGBV is extensive but underreported due to heavy gender norms and social stigmatization combined with inadequate response mechanisms.Psychological abuse is the most prevalent type of GBV (41% of reported cases), followed by physical abuse (40%) and denial of resources, opportunities, services (13%). Very often, violence is associated with mental health problems, mainly depression (57%) and anxiety (26%). Most of the cases come under domestic violence (90% of the cases). 64% of women reported the husband is the perpetrator, followed by other family member (17%) and primary caregiver (13%). Domestic violence has been indicated has occurring very often or often by all respondents of KIIs that reported violence at home as the most significant safety and security concern faced by adult women, while it represents the second most significant concern for adolescent girls, following forced marriage. The main reasons for under-reporting are the risk of being identified as survivors (first reason given by all respondents) and the lack of confidential treatment from GBV service providers (63.6% of the respondents). 84% of cases received in the last 6 months has been reported after more than one year from the incident. Total absence of services in the past years, normalization of violence and low levels of information and knowledge about services contribute to underreporting. When it comes to physical or sexual violence reporting levels are even lower: it is more socially acceptable to conceal and hide the crime than it is to seek help and justice for the survivor. Honor killing and shotgun wedding are extremely common and socially accepted as responses to sexual violence. Lack of information and understanding of available services are barriers to access essential services. Among the 71% of respondents who confirmed the presence of health services for women in the area, only 36% then has been able to mention at least one service. The limited access to information about family planning and reproductive health leads young girls to have large families, even though they are unwilling or unprepared. Most of the health facilities assessed do not have trained staff, supplies and protocols in place (for example, CMR, emergency contraception, treatment for STIs and PEP kits). It emerged also that health providers’ bad attitude towards women and girls lead them not to approach health facilities, unless extremely necessary. Conversely, among women there is a high level of trust in NGOs, mainly because of privacy and confidentiality. Women and girls of families perceived as associated to extremist groups face discrimination and abuse and are ostracized by others who hold them responsible for the actions of their family members. As a result, they often do not access vital services, children are out-of-school and family members often present with depression and anxiety. Male survivors of GBV face stigmatization too. Masculinity models prevent them from seeking help, while the feeling of being unable to fulfill their cultural expectations and their roles incoming providers lead to increased frustration and anger, thus violence. Respondents say women and girl survivors seek help from a family member (90%), friend (81%), and humanitarian actors (81%), while police and health providers are considered untrustworthy. Also, the lack of female staff prevents women to approach police. Widows, divorced and unmarried women has been identified as the most exposed to violence, followed by adolescent girls. The latter face high risk of forced marriage and denial of education opportunities. When it comes to access to information and services on sexual and reproductive health, adolescents are entirely excluded. Many basic forms of violence (beating, insulting…) are perceived as normal behaviors by women. Respondents showed a high level of normalization and legitimization of GBV within survivors, families, perpetrators and the community in general.

Activities

The project will support static women’s centers, where GBV-related services and activities will be offered. Spaces will be equipped to ensure safety and confidentiality and comfortable provision of PSS activities. Child-friendly spaces inside the centers will be in place in order to facilitate the participation of women with children. 1. Conduction of continuous GBV assessments: a. Focus group discussions and key informant interview;b. Adolescent girls’ assessment;c. Service mapping exercise;d. GBV situational analysis; 2. Provision of awareness raising and community outreach activities:a. Door-to-door dissemination of information on available GBV-related services (including information on referral pathway for survivors);b. Awareness sessions for women, girls, men and boys on domestic violence, reporting, sexual violence and family planning and other relevant topics;c. Mass information campaign: development and dissemination of key messages (prevention messaging, where to report, physical and mental health consequences of sexual violence and other forms of GBV, how to access care, victim blaming/rejection/isolation, availability of services, etc.) addressed to women, girls, men and boys;d. Prevention campaign: International Women’s Day and 16 Days of activism. 3. Provision of Individual and group psychosocial support: a. Specialized GBV case management (level 3 of the MHPSS pyramid);b. Individual specialized psychological counselling (level 3);c. PFA and group counselling (level 2); d. Resilience and adolescent girls’ programs (based on AG toolkit for Iraq) (level 2);e. referrals system to specialized psychiatric services (level 4). 4. Coaching, mentoring and supervision sessions on GBV prevention and response of staff working with GBV service providers: Coaching and mentoring of GBV service providers who directly care for survivors (social workers, psychologists, outreach workers, caseworkers, psychiatrists, GBV team leaders, doctors, nurses): GBV outreach methodologies, GBV basic concepts, GBV risk mitigation, Caring for child survivors, GBV legal framework, PFA and Case management and others. Coaching and mentoring of MH specialists and health workers who directly care for GBV survivors on MH protocols (MHGap, resilience groups I DEAL, Self Help Plus). Coaching and mentoring of health personnel on Clinical Management of Rape. 5. Provision or recreational activities for women and girls.

Protection Risk Analysis

Protection Risks beneficiaries or humanitarians may be exposed to through the project implementation: 1- Security risks: IEDs, VBIEDs, mines, deterioration of the general security situation in Ninewa.2- Personal Safety risks in project locations3- Road Safety risks Protection risks for target beneficiaries in the project locations: - Exposure to violence- Traditional harmful practices- Underreporting and no access to quality services

Mitigation Measures

Mitigation measures: 1- Security and Safety risks: All UPP staff accessing the project locations in Ninewa Governorate is trained in VEPT (Violent Environment Awerness Training) and in mine/IED awareness. All UPP security manager and security Focal Points assess daily the security situation in the project location and decide whether activities can be implemented regularly or if any contingency plan must be activated.2- Personal Safety risks in project locations: All project locations are equipped with first aid kits and a First Aid focal point specifically trained in advanced first aid and first aid management is appointed in each UPP field office. 3- Road Safety risks: all UPP drivers are regularly trained in Drivers Safety and Security and their performance and respect of the rules is monitored by both the fleet manager and the security focal points. The project will, on the other hand, contribute to mitigate and prevent protection risks for the population target by the project, combining preventive and responsive activities. - Exposure to violence: the project will directly contribute to the provision of essential psychosocial services for GBV survivors, supporting survivors in healing through quality care, and improving the capacity of the local community support system. GBV case management and MHPSS services targeted on survivors of violence will be strengthen and supplemented by the promotion of integrated aftercare, working with a range of partners, including legal assistance and medical care. Local capacities in offering support and quality services will be enhanced through specific training on GBV-related protocols. - Traditional harmful practices: mistreatment of widows and divorced women, as well as forced marriages, including shotgun marriages, and honor killings emerged as serious risks for women and girls. The project will promote behavior change and encourage opposition to violence against women, raising awareness and building knowledge. On one hand, it is essential to challenge the normalization of violence in the community working directly with women, and on the other hand it is extremely important to engage continuously men and boys in prevention. In this respect, the work on strengthening prevention and mitigation of GBV, engaging also men and boys, will contribute to the slow process of influencing and changing social norms that perpetrate harmful practices and condone violence. A continuous assessment process will allow identifying both services that are still missing and potential risks for women and girls. On this side, advocacy with local authorities and the cluster system will be conducted. - Underreporting and no access to quality services: many survivors do not disclose violence due to fear of repercussions, social stigma, rejection from partners/families and service providers’ bad attitudes. This represent a high risk for GBV survivors that are prevented from access to life-saving services, especially in case of sexual violence. The project will promote reporting, raising awareness among beneficiaries, and information campaigns will embrace information on services availability, including dissemination of referral pathways. Moreover, the project will promote training for GBV services providers (medical staff, health workers,…) on GBV core concepts, confidentiality and caring for survivors, in order to facilitate care and safe access to services for survivors based on the survivor-centered approach.

How is your project accountable to affected population

Information about the program will be provided to the affected population through daily outreach activities. Informative materials (flayers and posters) containing details of services provided and contacts for each center (address, map and hotline number), approved by UPP communication experts, will be translated into Arabic and distributed in strategic places (e.g. clinics, shops…). A locked complain box will be present in each center and beneficiaries will be invited to submit complaints. Feedback questioner on services will be also available in the centers. Field staff will be instructed to collect complaints and suggestions, in person or via hotline, from beneficiaries and report to the management. Moreover, UPP complain mechanism (hotline, email and boxes) is available in each UPP office in order to allow stakeholders other-than-direct-beneficiaries to submit complaints and suggestions. Partners complain mechanism are available as well. Feedbacks will be used to improve the quality of the activities and put into place corrective measures, if needed. Focused group discussions with women and girls will be conducted to involve more beneficiaries into the implementation of the project and the adaptation of activities in the light of lesson learned. Learnings will be documented and part of the monitoring and evaluation plan of the intervention. UPP and partners' staff will be trained on PSEA, Anti-fraud and Anti-corruption policies, and UPP Standards of Conduct and will be closely monitored during the implementation of the project.
