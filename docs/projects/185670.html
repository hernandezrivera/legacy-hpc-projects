Project Summary

The project goals are to ensure that displacement-affected Rohingya and host community adolescents and youth can claim their right to continued learning and education in an inclusive and protective environment leading to increased resilience, individual and community wellbeing, social cohesion, and economic progress, and enhanced empowerment and agency for all.
Starting with Youth need assessment with ACAPS at the beginning of the project to identify adolescent and youth (15 to 24 years) needs, services will be designed, implemented and monitored, to ensure access to learning for female learners, learners with disabilities, and youth at protection risks including LGBTI community.
NRC will work to deliver access to relevant and quality learning through personal, social, and emotional development opportunities along with livelihood opportunities for Rohingya and host communities. Basic skills including foundational literacy and numeracy and technology-based digital literacy (through BSF partnership), a stand-alone tailored life skills programme with transferable 21st-century skills, PSS and work skills including labor skills training, short and long vocational training will also be designed and delivered. Rohingya refugee learners will have learning opportunities using existing LCFA resources and content development for alternative learning pathways. Services will include engagement through community projects, sports, and work for social cohesion and a disability-inclusive society, focusing on females, learners with disabilities, and LGBTI community.
For host community learners, remedial classes, conditional cash support, supporting governance structures, assets provisions, and provisions for improving wellbeing to take steps to close engorged learning gap that due to COVID 19 pandemic. Collaboration with UNO, Headteachers, parents, and existing committees through meetings, workshops, and training, along with student-led community projects to ensure social cohesion.
Through safe and inclusive programming, a safe space for learners to learn, connect and share experiences will be provided at both camp and host community. Temporary community-based learning centers, a learning center with gender-segregated WASH facilities and water sources, inclusive features, solar power for ventilation and lighting, boundaries, etc will be rehabilitated/constructed.
In all possible camp settings, inclusiveness measures including physical measures, offline learning for learners with disabilities especially at hard to reach areas as well as training of the learning facilitators will be provided, and partnership with CDD will support to provide appropriate services for learners with disabilities and structured referral pathways for needed services.
Using Teacher professional development framework, teacher and facilitators training will be provided with the focus of female teachers' capacity building, to contribute to closing the gap of female participation.
Capacity building on inclusive and gender-responsive education practices, crisis-sensitive planning, management, and coordination for quality provision will be ensured in collaboration with other actors as well as the government. Curricula/guidance developed throughout the project will be shared with the sector, partners, and government.
Community education committee women, parents of the learners, influential members of the community, and facilitators will be assembled. Host community schools’ committees will also be included. The committees will be key gateways to discuss the learning process, feedback, sensitization of inclusive education initiatives, and promote the return to school/learning centers post COVID-19. Included services will be Better Learning Programme (BLP) psychosocial support, recreation activities, and temporary learning classes, delivering life-saving messages, and conducting community outreach and engagement. Mobile teams will be enabled to distribute essential materials.

1 - Identified Needs

By the fifth year of the humanitarian response, the response has faced COVID 19 pandemic, and from end of September 2021, was able to operate under reduced restrictions from COVID guidelines. Different modalities were used for providing basic assistance during this time. Although progress were made, Rohingya refugees remain at perilous situation for having uncertain future, by being at the bottom tire of climate change impacts and disaster risks. Continuous challenges remain for engorged learning gaps, as well as gender based violence, inadequate WASH facilities, minimal education opportunities and restricted from participating in livelihood activities, meaning they remain highly aid dependent. Access to quality, inclusive, diverse and age appropriate education for both Rohingya and host community children and youth have been limited. According to 2022 JRP PiN, there are 534,245 children, adolescent and youth age 3 to 24 in need of support. During COVID, door to door learning services were only options that was provided by different education organizations. The Government of Bangladesh does not allow refugees to enrol in formal education or partake in livelihoods activities outside of the camps. The efforts to provide education services to refugees have been constrained by a number of factors, including a lack of space for facilities in camps that are already heavily congested, the lack of qualified teaching personnel specially females and the governments constraints pre and during COVID. Based on education sector gap analysis, the Education Sector partners currently provide services through several learning centres and home-based learning centres spread throughout the camps. Services are available primarily to children aged 3-14, with minimal educational opportunities currently available to adolescents and youth aged 15 and over. According to J-MSNA 2021, that it is commonly reported that expected challenges when sending children back to learning facilities are - Risk of infection with COVID 19 on the way or at learning facility, Lack of qualified teaching staff, Children have fallen too far behind. As reasons for not sending children back were mentioned largely age related, not having been enrolled pre COVID, and not considering education important. For host community, it is mentioned in the report that most commonly reported expected challenges when sending children back to schools as Lack of money to pay for fees or other education related expenses, Risk of infection with COVID 19 on the way or at school, Children have fallen too far behind. While most education actors work in camps, the host community have been left with little assistance. During the JRP consultation meetings with the authorities, they indicated the gap in response to the high drop-out rate among adolescent and youth due to income generation activities, early marriage, and parents’ disinterest in education, lack of alternative modalities which accommodates access to livelihood opportunities. Across Bangladesh, the impacts of climate change and environmental degradation contribute to more frequent extreme weather events and severe disasters. This year, the humanitarian context was also impacted by massive fire in Noyapara RC camp, and in camp 8E, 9 and 10 in separate occasions. The humanitarian emergency worsened at the onset of the monsoon season, with heavy rain, high winds, flooding and landslides damaging not only the refugees’ shelters but a reality for millions living along Bangladesh’s coast to displacement and forced migration.
