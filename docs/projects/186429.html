Project Summary

TWB/CLEAR Global will provide language services and communication support across sectors for more effective communication with communities, with a particular focus on the development of a common multilingual feedback mechanism. The project's primary target for 2022 is that 20% of the affected population report receiving information in a preferred language other than Hausa. TWB/CLEAR Global also aims to ensure that, by the end of 2022, there is consensus on a incremental approach to a sustainable and effective inter-agency accountability mechanism, with some initial activities undertaken.

1 - Needs

The information needs of affected populations in northeast Nigeria are evident: populations require clear, prioritised information in languages that enable them to make the best decisions for themselves and their communities. Without clear, trusted, timely and actionable information, every other aspect of the humanitarian response is compromised.Language barriers continue to hamper humanitarian communication with vulnerable people in northeast Nigeria The 2018, 2020 and 2021 Multi-Sector Needs Assessments (MSNA) conducted by REACH, data from IOM’s Displacement Tracking Matrix, and focused research by Translators without Borders highlight critical gaps. Communicating in Hausa alone is not enough. The 2021 MSNA data shows that Hausa is the primary language for a minority of people surveyed across the BAY states (34%), although it is the main language of humanitarian communication. Internally displaced people speak more than 30 different primary languages; among them, Kanuri, Fulfulde, Bura, Shuwa Arabic, and Marghi are the most common. Humanitarian information provision, data collection and feedback mechanisms must cater to a wider range of languages spoken in their targeted area. Both language and format matter. In Borno, 59% of respondents in Borno State said they prefer to receive information in a language other than Hausa. Low literacy levels also mean that some people―particularly women—will not understand any written information. Clear, accurate content and mostly verbal mother tongue communication are critical to reach everyone, including the most vulnerable and less literate groups. Widespread communication gaps compromise accountability. A reliance on spoken Hausa and written English (including for suggestion boxes) makes it difficult for all but the most educated and native Hausa speakers to engage in real dialogue with humanitarians. Young women and older people in particular are not confident that their feedback is appropriately received and addressed. In addition, people are not comfortable discussing misconduct by aid workers and other sensitive issues in person. The result is less effective, inclusive, and transparent community feedback mechanisms. Community members report engagement and mutual trust would be improved if they could communicate in local languages, including via remote communication channels (e.g. hotlines, chatbots). Women, in particular, do not trust that their voices are heard. Based on TWB’s AAP Assessment (2021-forthcoming), we found that young women and older people in particular were not confident that their feedback was appropriately received and addressed via CFMs such as suggestion boxes. Our research found that people prefer face-to-face communications to report complaints and feedback. But community members reported that engagement and mutual trust would be improved if they could communicate in local languages, including via remote communication channels. Hotlines were seen as good, but not quick enough; people wanted information in real-time.

2 - Activities

TWB will lead efforts to define and contribute to building an information/communications ecosystem that complements current efforts.1. Language services for two-way communication* Translate: translation, including in audio format, in nine languages of the affected communities. * Communication training: sector-specific language awareness and humanitarian interpretation training to field workers and volunteers* Visual and pictorial support: graphics and design, ensuring that people at all literacy levels have access to clear, useful information.* Plain language editing: plain language editing and training to help partners develop jargon-free, clear messages appropriate for their target audience. 2. Define and Contribute to an Information/Communications Ecosystem for more effective CwC/AAP TWB will support the AAP/CE WG members to define what a collective feedback mechanism could look like, taking an incremental approach toward building confidence in a collective system. A foundational principle will be strengthening the role of Nigerian organizations and community leaders as information and feedback focal points in the community. TWB will research the current ecosystem and identify gaps. Trusted communications channels will then be provided with regularly updated information and channels with which to feedback complaints and ask questions/pass on concerns coming from people. For those who prefer anonymity or who do not trust or cannot access other sources, the project goes further by providing access to language enabled information kiosks/tools where they can ask questions and get feedback when they want it. Information and feedback from all channels will be consolidated into a dashboard that will become more sophisticated as confidence builds.* Research on the use of and trust in communications/information channels in Borno State, including language- and communication-related obstacles to access. The research will focus on existing channels (radio, local leaders, aid workers, Nigerian organizations, community leaders, and other two-way communication mechanisms) and explore potential for new channels. The findings will include community feedback about what has and hasn’t worked. The report’s recommendations will identify gaps and will outline the way forward for the design of a holistic communications ecosystem.* Strengthen trusted information and communications channels through more robust, regular interactions. Based on the research, TWB will strengthen already trusted information channels through weekly call-in radio programs on popular stations and/or stations that focus on non-Hausa speakers; development and dissemination of key messages to community leaders, local NGOs/CSOs, initially based on sector priorities, and moving to responding to feedback from radio programs and local leaders; training of local leaders in AAP and AAP tools; development and dissemination of key information; consolidating dashboarding of issues and feedback. We will report back to communities on the issues and responses. * Provide information directly to IDPs and host communities, making appropriate use of technology. Based on the research above, TWB will develop a range of multilingual communications tools (free SMS/USSD chatbots WhatsApp/Messenger bots, information kiosks - depending on connectivity) to ensure that affected people can ask questions and get answers quickly and easily in their own language. Over time, as the systems improve, the tools will also become voice enabled (possibly not until 2023). These tools will be openly available and TWB will conduct training and awareness sessions to set expectations and ensure that, at a minimum, community leaders, aid workers, and government officials can access and use them. Questions will be collected by a “newsroom” that will be responsible for getting responses to questions/concerns and ensuring the bots/kiosks are regularly updated.

8.6 - Please describe briefly the modalities your project will put in place to ensure protection mainstreaming (particularly referring to the above questions).

The links between language and ethnicity require careful attention to the potential implications of gathering and sharing information on the languages people speak. Conflict sensitivity and protection risk will be an explicit consideration in TWB’s research and mapping activities, as well as in the provision of language services, with a risk analysis to be conducted in collaboration with protection sector partners and others across the response. Training on the basics of interpreting and language awareness for humanitarian protection staff and volunteers will include an explicit focus on both gender- and conflict-sensitivity. TWB will consult with protection sector partners and draw on research findings to develop these aspects of both training programs for the context of northeast Nigeria.

9.5 - How will your project ensure feedback is collected from the most vulnerable community members?

Through TWB’s text and audio mechanisms, we are able to create the same information for all nine language groups that we serve. We place a particular emphasis on making information, communication and messaging available – and in a language that is well-understood – to those who are less mobile, visually impaired, illiterate, very young or elderly. During community consultations and frequent monitoring activities, TWB will reach out to vulnerable members of the community for specific feedback and information to ensure their concerns are incorporated into all relevant activities.
