Project Summary

HI inclusive education projects expertise in working across three distinct levels (community and family level, school and services level and national and policy level). Maintaining standards based on the lessons learned and experiences from its vast array of inclusive education projects, HI develops and standardizes various components of inclusive education services.
Ensuring that no children is left behind, HI targets children with a range of different disabilities to support their access to education by applying a multi-sectorial approach and strong links with other sectors such as rehabilitation, psychosocial support, child protection and many others.
Support for both formal enrolment and home-based learning sessions with Individual Education Plans (IEP) are provided to the beneficiaries in collaboration with their caregivers, teachers and volunteers. In addition, assistive devices, therapeutic materials, learning and recreational materials are prescribed to the children based on their individual specialized needs identified via technical assessments. Building on the interventions in 2021, in 2022, HI will continue to implement its Inclusive Education projects through: 1. the provision of Individualized Education Action Plans to 760 children with disabilities (gender balanced representation), 2. Meanwhile working on diminishing the social stigma, communication and environmental barriers through awareness sessions (on gender norms, barriers due to intersectional attributes) and accessibility modification at school/LCs and community levels, along with the provision of assistive and mobility devices.
Also, in 2022, HI plans to train 120 mid to senior level education Implementing Partners (IPs) staffs to become master trainer on Inclusive education. Off them 50% of them must be female while persons with disabilities will be given priority for the training. These master trainers will cascade knowledge to facilitator/ teachers to improve and promote inclusive teaching learning environment in LC/CFs in their program, more awareness and sensitization to accept and support inclusive education, enrolling children with disabilities (50% of them will be girls with disabilities) by partners in their own learning centers to create more ownership and responsibility among teachers and staffs.
A thorough barriers and facilitators assessment will lead to joint action plan with the IPs of Education sector which will be regularly supported in implementation and followed up. Number of tools, modules and tip sheets will be developed which can be shared with the education sector for their endorsement and improve the inclusive education ecosystem. The partnership will apply a twin-track approach of implementation modalities of disability specific services and inclusive education services. That is, focus on the specific needs of children with disabilities as well as a focus on mainstreaming services will be applied, so they are fully inclusive. The approach will ensure that existing structural gaps and individual needs of children with disabilities are addressed in programming, while at the same time community, environmental and socio-economic barriers to inclusive education are identified and considered.
HI puts in intensive efforts to recruit gender and disability inclusive workforce (including among volunteers), targets men and women as caregivers to improve understanding on needs and barriers of girls and boys with disabilities in accessing education and awareness on gender norms. Moreover, HI will particularly priorities the girls with disabilities for their inclusion in the education facilities. Among the target beneficiaries 50% will be girls with disabilities. Moreover, HI will offer some life skills training as well as “Basic Education/” to the girls and boys with disabilities (aged 5-14) in the camp context. This will support help them in their empowerment and better representation.

1 - Identified Needs

More than 10,00,000 Rohingya refugees have settled in the refugee camps of Ukhiya and Teknaf upazilas of the Cox’s Bazar district in Bangladesh fleeing violence in Myanmar’s Rakhine State. Around 52% of the total refugee population are children with 1% of the refugee households having a person with disability in the family. The population continues to live in overcrowded camps vulnerable to landslides and flash-flooding with heavy dependence on humanitarian assistance to cover their basic needs three-years after the mass influx began in August 2017. This dependency on humanitarian assistance is particularly higher for person with disabilities as they are more vulnerable to exploitation, abuse and violence than persons without disabilities and much less likely to access any facility-based services. Particularly for educational facilities, children with disabilities are less likely to attend learning centers than children without disabilities (for both age groups 3-5 and 6-14). Learning centers in the camps have a number of barriers which are preventing children with disabilities from attending. Physical barriers exist due to the inaccessible terrain and a lack of ramps or accessible toilets. They also face attitudinal barriers from staff, parents or other children who may not believe children with disabilities should attend mainstream education. This was evident from the 2021 Reach Education Needs Assessment which reported participants experiencing substantial physical access constraints for children with disabilities (only 14 per cent of assessed centers were wheelchair accessible), as well as worrying levels of verbal and physical abuse from peers at learning centers. In addition, the needs assessment also reports that only 26 per cent of surveyed staff received training in supporting children with disabilities. This further confirms that environmental barriers such as the learning environment are also prevalent since teachers are not trained in inclusive education pedagogy while learning centers are rarely equipped with accessible and inclusive learning materials. This gap in low uptake in enrollment for children with disabilities and the need to equip all educations facilities in the camps to become as inclusive and accessible require substantial and immediate efforts. The 2021 REACH-PWG Age & Disability assessment report noted that overall, 12% of individuals were identified as persons with disabilities. It also states that among younger age groups, significantly lower proportions of children with disabilities than children without disabilities were found to have been enrolled in formal and informal learning centres before their closure due to the COVID-19 outbreak (in March 2020). Overall, 65% of children with disabilities aged 5 to 9 had reportedly attended temporary learning centres (TLCs) for at least 4 days a week as compared to 88% of children without disabilities in the same age group. The proportions of children reportedly not having completed any education were higher among children with disabilities relative to children without disabilities, and among boys relative to girls with disabilities. These results potentially indicate a trend of persons with disabilities being enrolled into education later than persons without disabilities, rather than not being enrolled at all. At the same time, persons with disabilities may potentially take longer or be slightly less likely to complete their education compared to persons without disabilities. Hence, HI takes the role of technical partner to education implementing agencies, education sector to build their capacity on identification, enrolment and retention of children with disabilities & inclusive plans and strategy. HI also take role of direct implementing partner of education sector to target children with moderate and severe impairment with more focus on gender and other intersectionality e.g. impairment groups etc.
