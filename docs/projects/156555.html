Project Summary

In this intervention, Shafak, in line with education cluster’s objectives and in coordination with involved authorities as well as targeted communities, will focus on achieving two specific goals:
-scaling up access to formal and non-formal education for Syrian crisis-affected children
-enhancing the quality of formal and non-formal education within a protective environment
Access strategies will include small-scale rehabilitation of damaged schools and establishment of other safe temporary/alternative learning spaces through a gender-sensitive and disability-friendly approach. It is critical to improve the access to quality education services for IDPs in camps and within communities and ensure that these services are sufficiently linked to Child Protection interventions including the integration of mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) and other specialized protection services. It is increasingly critical to expand access to psychosocial support and recreational activities for vulnerable children, particularly in areas of high severity of need.
Shafak will support formal education in 20 schools targeting approximately 6400 students (3300 boys and 3100 girls) where this intervention will minimize the risk of students dropping out, where monthly incentives to teachers will be provided, as they don’t have any alternative fund in addition to provision of education materials for students and teachers and providing schools with necessary operational costs (including heating for winter).
As education is one of the very basic children’s rights Shafak is planning to provide access to education for children with disabilities by providing transportation services and improve school environment by provide targeted assistance within the schools in order to encourage these children to continue their education and integrate them with their peers.
Non-formal education opportunities and pathways back to inclusive education at age appropriate levels are increasingly required to address the complex learning needs of children. Thus, non-formal education will target school girls and boys (aged 8-12 and gender disaggregated) to fill in existing needs, where 10 Non-Formal Education centres will be supported targeting approximately 7600 children (3800 boys/3800 girls, 8-12 years old) to provide literacy and numeracy along with PSS sessions; the PSS activities will be selected from I-Deal curriculum; the Curriculum used for Non-Formal Activities will be Curriculum B, that combines 2 academic years in one or, the Self Learning Programme (SLP).
Children and youth accessing formal and non-formal education are not experiencing quality teaching, and there are growing concerns that the toll of the crisis in its many forms is preventing children from attaining foundational literacy and numeracy skills as well as those skills relevant to cognitive, social and economic empowerment. Hence, teachers will be equipped with the skills in non-formal education modalities, child-centred pedagogy, and positive classroom management to cope with overcrowded classrooms, address and accommodate host community, displaced and returnee children within their classes.
Stakeholders in the project implementation site, including children, adolescents and older persons, including PwDs and their opinions are taken into account while developing the project design, implementation of activities so that they have better access to humanitarian services and information in accordance to their specific situation.
The project will make sure that children engaging in child labour or recruited for military purposes, or living with older or disabled caregivers or adolescent girls at risk of early or forced marriage are given special attention and have their own space to participate in the project activities and are properly referred as per Protection cluster guidelines.

Needs

The protracted crisis in Syria, now in its eighth year, has forced 2.1 million, over one third of children, out-of-school and has put a further 1.3 million children at risk of dropping out. Over 5.8 million school-aged children and about 121,972 education personnel are in need of education assistance inside Syria, 61 per cent of whom are in acute and immediate need. (WoS Education Severity Needs Analysis, 2018)The provision of education services inside Syria is limited. Hostilities have resulted in over one in three schools being damaged, destroyed, no longer accessible or occupied for shelter and other purposes (WoS Education Needs Assessment, 2018)Due to a shortage of functional classrooms, classes are overcrowded and over one million children required to attend double-shift schools. (Education Management Information System data, Ministry of Education, 2017-18 academic year)Idleb governorate is the largest in terms of numbers of schools, students and teaching staff in the north of Syria, where the number of students in schools in all stages of education 414 thousand students, distributed over 14,450 classrooms and a school division … While the number of teaching staff distributed to schools in the province of Idlib, compared to 24200 people, where the males accounted for about 48.35% or 11700, while the number of female 51.65% equivalent to 12500. (RCG, Education Sector in northern Syria, 2019)An extreme shortage and under payment of qualified teachers also limits the quality and provision of education. Children who are out of school face increased protection risks including child marriage and the worst forms of child labor. (EDUCATION HNO Narrative_21_01_19_v2)The ability of schools to absorb more IDPs into the education system is very limited. Some schools operate in double shifts (morning and afternoon classes) to accommodate the large influx of displaced children. This reduces the teaching learning hours per day, adversely affecting learning outcomes for IDPs and host community residents alike. As the conflict continues, effective teaching becomes increasingly challenging due to mixed-aged, mixed-ability, over-crowded and under resourced classrooms. (EDUCATION HNO Narrative_21_01_19_v2)In addition, having been exposed to the psychological trauma of hostilities, displacement and family upheaval generated by the crisis, many children and teachers are affected by psychological trauma. A 2018 survey across multiple governorates indicate 1 in 8 children per classroom have psychosocial support needs requiring specialized interventions for effective teaching and learning, affecting the learning not only of the individual students affected, but also other pupils and teaching staff. (EDUCATION HNO Narrative_21_01_19_v2)The barriers to accessing quality and relevant learning for children inside Syria are complex and include a wide range of protection and socio-economic issues, extending beyond the education sector. These complicating factors include the prevalence of child labour, child marriage, lack of identity and education attainment documentation, lack of safety both at home and in school, the cumulative psychosocial effects of the protracted crisis and extensive displacement. In some areas, over half of children with a disability have an unmet need for education, and over two thirds require specialized health services which are not available in their area. (Physical Rehabilitation Working Group, 2018)After years of crisis, the number of school aged children who have been out of school for more than five years is on the rise resulting in complex learning needs. More than 40 per cent of the out-of-school children in Syria are between 15 and 17 years of age and have differentiated learning, skills development and livelihoods needs (No Lost Generation Initiative, 2018).

Implementation of activities

The intervention is designed to provide quality education services to Syrian conflict-affected children who most vulnerable inside Syria, by proposing activities within areas with high density of IDPs and HCs. To do so, Shafak will make sure to involve stakeholders, including children, adolescents and older persons, including PwDs and their opinions in the implementation plan and that their voices are heard and taken into account while specific needs assessments are conducted and while implementing activities, so that they have better access to humanitarian services and information in accordance to their specific situation. These consultations include focus group discussions with participation of different target beneficiaries, including stakeholders such as teachers, school management, local authorities, and students themselves (with age and gender taken in consideration). Through these FGD, Shafak looks to identify particular needs as highlighted by the various groups and look at addressing these needs in the project design and implementation phases as possible. Other crosscutting issues such as GBV protection, child protection from violence, exploitation and abuse, and rights of vulnerable groups will be mainstreamed in the project.Shafak will start with validating the needs in the target schools through field surveys to reassess the needs. By these surveys, Shafak will identify the exact schools’ needs, children (girls and boys) and teachers (females and males) numbers in the proposed schools. This will offer an opportunity to know the number of schools, children and teachers available for contributing in the project.Shafak will conduct the procurement though open and transparent tender process as per Shafak procurement policy and procedures. The rehabilitation of schools and WASH facilities will be implemented by local contractors selected through open tender processes and supervision of Shafak technical WASH team to make sure the process is done in line with WASH cluster standards. The same will be implemented for schools’ furniture distribution. After each distribution round, the MEAL teams will do PDM for the kits’ distribution. Moreover, Shafak will organize community awareness sessions and back-to-school campaigns in the communities surrounding the schools and community education stakeholders will be engaged in the process in order to facilitate the communication among all education stakeholders in the targeted communities. In addition to achievement exams, ASER exams will be conducted for all students as a baseline study in math and Arabic. The same exam will be conducted at the end of intervention in order to measure the learning outcomes on students.Safety and security measures will be implemented on all schools and orientation sessions on these measures will be introduced to the schools’ staff, making sure that safety plans are in place and has been well delivered and drilled by the school staff. Shafak will also consider the safety of children & teachers as priority by distributing fire extinguishers & first aid kits for schools.Moreover, the project will make sure that children engaging in child labour or recruited for military purposes, or living with older or disabled caregivers or adolescent girls at risk of early or forced marriage are given special attention and have their own space to participate in the project activities and are properly referred as per Shafak referral mechanism and protection sector guidelines.Shafak will conduct field monitoring visits for all schools supported in this project. The M&E senior officer will be responsible for supervising the projects’ activities and will communicate and lead M&E field team, so will oversee project monitoring and evaluation activities and manage M&E field staff. The results obtained through monitoring will be presented and discussed with stakeholders in the field, which includes beneficiaries in a transparent manner.

Protection Risk Analysis

Risk (1): Sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA), including harassment, against women and girls by distributors and male beneficiaries while queuing and before/after distributionsRisk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure:- recruit male and female volunteers who are respected in the community and independent of armed groups. No children to be employed (under 18 years old)- make sure staff are already trained on Do No Harm, protection from SEA (PSEA), and child safeguarding; and sign and adhere to code of conduct that respects and uphold the rights of beneficiaries and PSEA- set up communication and complaints mechanisms or desks that are clearly marked; face interviews with beneficiaries, including women and children Risk (2):Tension between host community and IDPs (who were evacuated)Risk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure:- clearly communicate selection criteria to the community, community leaders and beneficiaries prior to the announcement of the activity.- harmonize the assistance delivered as per Cluster instructions and guidelines.- coordinate with local council in the targeted area as well as being active in field coordination Risk (3): Difficult access for women, unaccompanied children, elderly, people with disabilities (PWD)Risk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure:- Register all adult women, unaccompanied children, elderly, people with disabilities as main recipients of Kits distributed- During post -distribution follow-up, understand how women, children, and other groups accessed the distributed goods and their feedback on the process and materials Risk (4): Child safety incidents in school due to bullying and overburdened teachersRisk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure:- Training of partners and local teams on Child safety, development of schools’ specific child safety policies; establishment of complaint mechanisms in school;- Security/safety training for organization, students and school staff. Risk (5): Protection risks of mixing different age groups in the same centre/classRisk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure:- Create separate times of attendance for each age group; Risk (6): Resistance to gender mainstreaming from male leadership structures.Risk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure:- Programme activities which engage women will be endorsed by community and religious leaders to gain support from male community members to reduce the likelihood of resistance to these activities. Risk (7): children being out of school due to unavailability of school furniture upon completion of school rehabilitation and construction of prefabricated classrooms and the risk of not having enough WASH facilities for boys and girls. This might cause lack of privacy to the children.Risk Management Strategy/Mitigating Measure:- Include school furniture in school rehabilitation plan or sign an MoU with authorities to ensure provision of school furniture by them upon completion of school rehabilitation; - Work in collaboration with WASH section who will establish WASH facilities in schools.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1. Risk: Target areas are inaccessible or insecure because of advance of armed groups and/or aerial shelling, or that staff or beneficiaries may be injured, killed or kidnapped by armed groups during implementation of activitiesMitigation: - Monitoring of the security situation; extensive acceptance measures to sustain good relations with communities for continuous understanding of local context and to facilitate unimpeded access to target areas; coordination for programmatic contingency (shifting resources/funds to other areas) - Minimal transfer of cash through staff to reduce exposure. 2.Risk: borders are temporarily closed, or humanitarian access restricted, between Syria and its neighbouring countries because of national regulations, of advances by GoS towards neighbouring countries' borders.Mitigation: - ongoing advocacy with relevant authorities for humanitarian access to vulnerable populations in Syria. Dividing stock of commodities between Syria and neighbouring countries in anticipation of risk; prioritizing local procurements (inside Syria); 3.Risk: Existing traditional practices and community structures conflict with child protection principles (e.g. corporal punishment, child rights messaging is perceived as a threat, etc.). Mitigation: - thorough contextual analysis to be undertaken and ensure safe access as part of the project design; - teachers are trained on positive discipline techniques as part of their basic training and sign a code of conduct in the school; - teachers and classrooms are regularly monitored; a child safe-guarding focal point is identified in the school; - Parent-Teacher Committees are consulted on any reports from their homes of children being mistreated.4. Risk: There is a risk of discrimination leading to certain minority groups or communities to be excluded from the education opportunities. Mitigation: - Local Council and Education Directorate are involved in the selection of teachers and registration of students at the schools; - Focus Group Discussions are held with different community groups and leaders; - conducting community outreach activities such as back-to-school campaigns; - children and youth are engaged in recreational and social cohesion activities within the community.5. Risk: Insufficient safety and health standards at schools. Mitigation: - planning and on-going communication with local education authorities, community leaders, schools, etc; - comprehensive school rehabilitation including installation of handrails, rehabilitation of wash facilities, fixing of electrical systems, and other interventions to ensure the health and safety of children and teachers. 6.Risk: Risk of children being out of school due to unavailability of school furniture upon rehabilitation. Mitigation: - comprehensive needs assessment of schools to ensure that where furniture is unavailable, Shafak will procure and install the needed items.
