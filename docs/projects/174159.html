Project Summary

ROHI intends to continue education activities at the camp schools with more activities geared towards preventing the spread of the coronavirus. Children will be mobilized through awareness activities to attend classes in the existing camp TLS. Classes will be conducted through different timings of up to 2 shifts with only few children in attendance in order to maintain the culture of social distancing. Through community participation, ROHI will engage adolescents in each camp who will be trained on COVID-19/hygeine practices and self learning approaches enabling them to follow up of children as part of blended self learning (part of sessions in TLSs and part in children’s tents/huts). On other fron, the adolescents will lead the awareness campaigns on best hygiene practices and COVID-19 related messages in IDP camps. Since small group approaches will be employed in IDP camps to provide opportunities to as many children as possible, adolescents will also take part in the tutoring sessions with the children in the camps through UNICEF’s blended self learning model and thereafter follow up their assigned group of children to their houses to ensure engagement wile they are away of the TLSs as part of the self learning model, children will be provided with instructional materials in reading and numeracy. The adolescents will be trained also to provide coaching activities for the children outside the school premises. Digital learning platforms will be set up in each of the camps to provide additional learning through the two shifts.
ROHI will partner with the child protection sector to leverage the community volunteers/social workers who will provide PSS activities in the child friendly spaces to give PSS opportunities to all children accessing TLSs and as part of multi-sectoral approach. The children who participate in the education activities will also be engaged in the CFS. In order not to disrupt on-going classes in TLSs, CP community volunteers will be accompanying groups of children from TLSs to child friendly spaces to ensure complementarity of CP and Education services.
The Education in Emergencies Working Group through the Strategic Advisory Group has developed and published a COVID19 strategic response plan, however needs are vast, and an adequate response requires support from partners. In order to support the response plan, ROHI has developed a program which outlines 3 main objectives:
• Prevent the spread of COVID-19 and mitigate risks of SEA among learners, teachers, parents and School Based Management Commitees (SBMC).
• Provide continuous education support programs through self learning and digital learning platforms using trained adolescents with the integration of pychosocial activities.
• Establishment of community participation through adolescents, teachers and SBMC to create continuous awareness of COVID19 and respond to WASH gaps in camp schools.

1 - Needs

The impact of the crisis on the education system has been severe, leaving generations of children without opportunities to learn and even more vulnerable. With the COVID-19 pandemic, the education system globally has been impacted greatly. Schools across Nigeria closed on 19th March, 2020 to prevent the spread of COVID-19, forcing 36 million enrolled students out of school. They join an estimated 13.2 million primary school-aged children who were out of school before the pandemic hit (UBEC 2019 personnel audit data). For many girls, this temporary hiatus to their education risks becoming permanent. While this disruption to education and the expected reduction in global growth have far-reaching effects for all, their impact will be particularly detrimental to the most disadvantaged students and their families, especially in conflict affected communities. The educational consequences of COVID-19 will last beyond the period of school closures and will be particularly severe for conflict affected girls. Girls are more at risk than boys of dropping out of school altogether following school closures and that women and girls are more vulnerable to the worst effects of the current pandemic. Drawing on data from the Ebola epidemic in Sierra Leone, we estimate that approximately 20 million more secondary school-aged girls could be out of school after the crisis has passed , if dropouts increase by the same rate. Longer-term, poorer countries may struggle to provide sufficient financing for education, especially to support schools, teachers and students to fight re-emergence of the virus and stay safe from indirect effects of further outbreaks.The Federal Ministry of Education (FME) and other Education Ministries and Boards at the various States in Nigeria have flagged up the distance learning approach through radios, television and the internet. In North East Nigeria, poverty has been a great barrier preventing children most especially girls from accessing education through digital media. At various States, the State Ministry of Education and the State Universal Basic Education Boards have produced and aired radio and television lessons across the country.
