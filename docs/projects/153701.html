Project Summary

The protracted conflict in South Sudan with accompanying mass displacement continues to place a high toll on IDPs in POCs, IDP camps and on affected host communities. OCHA estimates that 6.1 million people or 47% of the estimated population of the country in 2018 are affected. The protection cluster estimates that of the 6.1 million, 2,825,242 people are in need of protection services, 56.4% being women, 61% children and 2% elderly. HI estimates Persons with disabilities including mental health issues affected by conflict and displacement to be in the range 5-7% of the whole caseload.
In 2019, HI will target 33,200 persons (19,700 females and 13,500 male (from about 5,533HH) in Juba county in Central State, Mayendit county in greater Unity state and Yambio & Tambura counties in Western Equatoria state for general protection and GBV (Gender Based Violence) services. At least 23,200 beneficiaries will benefit from general protection services and 10,000 from GBV services. Four key interventions will be implemented: psychosocial support with disabilities rehabilitation; life skills and IGA training and support for survivors of GBV; dispute resolution/peacebuilding among communities affected by conflict; and comprehensive case management for GBV survivors. All actions will mainstream protection awareness raising and GBV prevention messaging. In view of the new peace agreement initiatives, there are high hopes of reconciliation and peace process, HI anticipates to engage the communities in IDP camps and those in the host communities in peace-building and conflict mitigation to promote peaceful coexistence among communities in target locations. The prospects of peace come with an increased likelihood of returnees. HI will therefore preposition to be able to provide services to refugees and IDPs returning to their homes or being re-integrated in communities. Displacement impacts more seriously on persons with disabilities, and HI will single them out to ensure they have equal access to humanitarian services. The challenge of idle youth including those with disabilities in camps is overarching; idle youth are a ticking bomb, and HI will aim to harness their energy by organizing them in groups, training them in IGA and vocational skills and linking them to procurement opportunities within HI and other agencies for them to compete for supply of basic products and materials such as T-shirts, scarfs, banners and simple assistive devices (walking sticks and canes, reusable sanitary pads, tricycles, crutches, etc).
Activities will target IDPs in POCs and IDP camps or informal settlements, members of host communities in villages and returnees. These actions contribute towards realization of South Sudan HRP 2019 objectives 1, 2 and 3, and Protection Cluster objectives 1, 2, 3 of 2019. The interventions target children in POCs, IDP camps, schools and in host communities, in a static environment, but with flexibility to relocate as per population movements..
These actions will be undertaken in or around locations where HI already has a static presence. Where access constraints are low to medium, HI will implement activities directly, where constraints are high, HI will work through Local NGOs (including Disabled Peoples Organizations), employing staff locally and equipping them with desired training. HI currently has field offices in all the targeted states, or will have established bases by first quarter of 2019 using existing funding on MHPSS and disability rehabilitation programs. Where possible, HI will conduct a targeting assessment in collaboration with other humanitarian actors, local authorities and interest groups to avoid duplication and overlapping of resources and services. HI’s intervention in the 4 counties is estimated to cost USD 1,000,000 (USD 30.12 per capita, over a period of 12 months), with USD 700,000 towards general protection actions and USD 300,000 towards SGBV activities.

Needs

Despite the significant efforts of humanitarian agencies, gaps in practical humanitarian assistance still remain substantial, rated ‘high’ by OCHA in 2018 at 60% for Education, 54% for Health, and 40% each for Protection and Water, Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH). HI will focus on protection and gender-based violence (GBV) interventions that would address intermediate needs of survivors, engage state and traditional authorities and support the long-term empowerment of women and girls rather than just on practical needs. According to the 2019 South Sudan Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP), protection remains the top concern, with pervasive human rights violations, especially gender-based violence (GBV) compounding the mental and psychosocial well-being of millions of south Sudanese. Violence and rights violations have become a persistent reality for women, girls and boys, with rape and other types of GBV largely going unreported. According to an IOM report of November 2017, “in 1 of 2 of households, a woman or girl have experienced GBV in the last 12 months,” and UNICEF estimates that at least 19,000 boys of school going age have been conscripted as child soldiers by various armed groups since 2013. The most recent humanitarian bulletin estimates up to 2.4 million children, mostly girls, not receiving an education in South Sudan. Girl enrolment in upper primary classes (5-8) is very low, as many adolescent girls (estimated by SNV in Eastern Equatoria in a 2014 study at 63%) are ignorant of safe menstrual hygiene management, and also cannot afford cannot afford sanitary pads during their menses, hence they drop out of school due to shame during menstruation and are married off early. Recent studies by agencies including Humanity & Inclusion (HI), Oxfam, and Care International reveal that displacement and resulting displacements seriously impacts persons with disabilities, who constitute approximately 7%-10% of the population in South Sudan, even more direly than the general population, as their suffering is compounded by stigma and exclusion from decision making and access to basic services. The more vulnerable IDPs, especially the elderly and persons with disabilities find it difficult to go to the riverside to fetch water and thus being supported by the community. However they lack water purification tabs that are not provided by the community. Despite humanitarian efforts made so far, there is critical need to integrate material support such as dignity kits to promote impact of rehabilitation and PSS services. At least 67% of IDPs live in counties described by OCHA as having ‘medium’ and ‘high access constraints’. States with greatest access constraints are ranked as Western Bahr el Ghazal, Western Equatoria, Upper Nile and Unity. In POC camps where movement is restricted 24 hrs, the toll on IPD mental health is quite high and psychosocial support a priority service. Through aligning its actions to the South Sudan HRP strategy for 2019 and Protection Objectives 2019, HI will seek to reduce vulnerability and increase resilience of 33,200 persons in four counties by targeting 9700 persons in Juba POC and IDP camps, 11,500 persons in Mayendit, 7,000 in Tambura, 5,000 in Yambio. A total of 23,200 will benefit from general protection and 10,000 from GBV servicesHI has worked in South Sudan since 2006 to support the most vulnerable populations, including girls, boys, women and men with disabilities. HI is a leading agency for Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (IMHPSS) and disability rehabilitation, with operations in Western Equatoria and Unity among other regions of the country. Drawing on HI’s experiences in protection programming in POCs and IDP camps in South Sudan, the proposed project will address the unmet sexual and reproductive health rights (SRHR) and needs of target population, particularly singling out the needs of those with disabilities.

Briefly describe the mechanisms

Protection Risk Analysis?

Yes

Briefly describe how

HI has recently (in September-October 2018) carried out a desk gender based analysis of the targeted locations. Recent studies and reports by various agencies operating in South Sudan do guide this preliminary GBA. Reports like Amnesty International’s (2017) Do Not Remain Silent provide horrific accounts of brutal rape, beatings, psychological terror, and even killings in greater Unity and Western Equatoria among other regions, perpetrated by armed groups during the renewed fighting of 2016-2017. These studies indicate a serious, and mostly deleterious, disruption of traditional gender norms and societal practices by conflict and displacement, with either gender assuming new roles. Traditionally, South Sudanese households are patrilineal, with men dominating leadership and decision-making roles, as well as having the say on land and other productive resources, while women hold reproductive and caregiving roles, including fetching firewood and water and cooking for the family. Among men and young male adolescents, engagement in conflict (either current or prior) and violence are linked and deeply rooted. In a society entirely unaccustomed to same-sex relations, boys and men have been raped and left with permanent psychological scars. The erosion of men’s capacity to economically support their families increases pressure (and consequently violence) on them. Besides cultural norms, families have cited extreme poverty as a driving factor in the decision to marry off young girls (for dowry) or for women to engage in sex for food. Recognizing the deep roots of such practices, HI will draw on its experience of community engagement to accompany and empower women and initiate community-awareness and women leadership confidence and peace building campaigns through local women’s groups.

Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Barriers have been identified above. HI will work with various beneficiaries to address key identified protection gaps, namely: barriers to children (especially poor girls, boys and those living with disabilities) to accessing education, stigma, inequitable access to humanitarians services by survivors of GBV and women’s’ and girls’ rights violations.Disability Risk analysis Disability risk analysis includes assessment to identify barriers to persons with various types of impairments (physical deformity, hearing impairment, vision impairments, cognitive and intellectual impairment). Major risks concerns persons with disability which will be integrated in the project response plan include;• Barrier to access to services of daily living, focusing on environmental barriers and mobility of persons with disability. These includes, access to food distribution centres and social amenities including WASH, school, health and markets. Accompaniment by caregivers or social workers to service distribution points shall be promoted.• Risk of social exclusion, bullying and discrimination: This will be catered for through protection and gender equality training and awareness to caregivers, neighbours and in schools.• Risk of being left behind in decision making: advocacy and creating structures for inclusion of persons with disabilities and actively promoting active participation of persons with disabilities in leadership and decision making platforms –every interest group or representative forum shall include a person(s) with disabilities.• Minimize the risk of impact during emergency, evacuation or impromptu relocation: Build a database of persons with disabilities and where they live so that they can be reached urgently in times of crisis .

Addresses inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

The project will adopt a gender-equality and disability inclusion lens in all aspects and stages of the proposed work, seeking to identify, describe, and strengthen community capacity to address gender disparities and harmful social norms that contribute to sexual and gender-based violence. In line with HRP GAM guidelines, the project will strengthen and support greater access to comprehensive GBV services (including psychosocial and mental health) for all, with a particular emphasis on the needs of women and girls.

Project activities address inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

The activities will address inequalities and protection threats as girls will have greater access to menstrual hygiene knowledge and will be able to attend school and go about their lives with greater ease. All those receiving rehabilitation will be enabled to have greater mobility, thereby reducing their dependence on others, increasing their self-esteem, and have greater access to the services they need, thereby assisting them to have protection from harm during service provision. MHPSS activities are designed to increase the resilience of persons from the effects of the conflict to live better lives with greater self-worth and reduce community tensions.

Does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

The project measures (through periodic satisfaction surveys and through "exit interviews" when a beneficiary has no longer need of HI services - for example rehabilitation when they have reached their mobility goal or their MHPSS problem has been alleviated) access of beneficiaries involvement in community social and economic activities, the beneficiary’s accessibility to other community services e.g., WASH, Health services, food distribution points, access to current news and information and perception in decision making. Rehabilitation services, Psychosocial participation in sport and other community event, adequate standard of living, education and work employment opportunities. Activities will be designed and implemented aligned to a survivor-centered approach , with activities informed by the International Agency Standing Committee (IASC) guidelines for integrating Gender-Based Violence intervention in Humanitarian Action. Do No Harm practices will be observed and beneficiaries sensitized and facilitated through dialogue on community-based mitigation measures on environmental hazards that accompany massive displacement and camp settlements. HI staff and staff of partners will be trained in Gender-Based Analysis and the mainstreaming of strategic gender rights in project design and implementation. Given the delicate security access issues in the two states, HI will mainstream Security Risk management in its project implementation
