Project Summary

The economic collapse and significant reduction in government social spending have plunged additional segments of the population into humanitarian need. MSNA 2022 data indicates that the income gap continues to widen, with average household expenditure now exceeding income by 60%, compared to 49% in 2021.
The recent record-high level of inflation and energy prices mean that the 2023/2024 winter season will inflict further hardship on families who live in extreme poverty and face social and economic exclusion, such as families caring for children with disabilities (CWD) and female-headed households (FHH). These families will be unable to purchase essential items such as fuel for heating and winter clothes for children. UNICEF PDM surveys show that families caring for CWD fall on average 40% below the MEB. FHH in urban and peri-urban slums live in extreme poverty, with most unable to meet their basic needs; they systematically face greater economic vulnerability than male-headed families. Evidence collected by UNICEF indicate that fuel for heating is a massive burden on families’ budget during winter, representing 10% of families’ monthly expenditure. As a result, most families use negative coping mechanisms.
To respond to this situation, UNICEF is implementing a Cash for Winter Programme, the main objective of which is to support highly vulnerable families in urban and peri-urban areas respond to the essential needs of their children and help them cope with the growing socio-economic hardship during winter months. The cash assistance will be essential for these families to purchase fuel and gas for heating and cooking, as well as other essential items such as warm clothes.
The programme has two components: (i) three rounds of cash transfers to vulnerable families with children during wintertime (one round every two months between October 2023 and February 2024). Each family receives US$ 60 per round, or a total of US$ 180 during the winter season. The amount is informed by the Cash Working Group recommended value for cash for winter NFIs in 2022 and might be updated in 2023 based on the CWG recommendations; (ii) referral mechanism where the most vulnerable children and their families are accompanied and referred to social services in the communities. This referral component is critical to help families respond to the needs of their children, beyond the cash transfer, thus contributing to strengthen their resilience.
UNICEF 2021-2022 PDM surveys indicated that more than 93% of families reported that food was among the top 3 items they spent cash assistance on, followed by heating fuel (50%), medical expenses and rent (31% each), debt repayment (25%), clothing (19%), education (13%) and toiletries/diapers (10%).

In 2023, the project aims to reach 47,000 families (235,000 people, including 117,500 children) with cash assistance and referral services in urban and peri-urban slums in 9 governorates.
Building on the partnerships established in 2020 with a large network of national NGOs, UNICEF will identify vulnerable families through three targeting methods: (i) geographical targeting, focusing on areas of highest winter severity index (2,3,4) as identified in the NFIs sector winter strategy; (ii) categorical targeting: families with CWD; FHH in urban and peri-urban slums where housing infrastructure has been destroyed and where there is high level of displacement; families with a member with a chronic illness; and families with orphaned children
The programme strategy is in line with the NFI sector result framework 2023. UNICEF closely coordinates with the NFIs sector and the CWG at national and sub-national levels
Accountability to beneficiaries’ is ensured through three main channels: (i) redress and grievance mechanisms; (ii) PDM Surveys; (iii) on-site monitoring.
The project targets 47,000 families in 2023: (i) 17% IDPs, 36% host communities, 47% returnees; (ii) 48% females, 52% males; (iii) 40% of families of CWD

3 - Needs

According to MSNA (2022), average household expenditure (SYP 855,499) exceeded income (SYP 533,514) by 60%, compared to 49% in 2021. Needs are particularly acute in urban and peri-urban slums where families, especially FHH, families with orphaned children and IDP families are disproportionately affected, leaving them unable to meet the basic needs of their children during winter. As per the 2022 HNO returnees (12%), IDPs in collective centres (19%) and IDPs out of sites (9%) have the lowest ability to meet their basic needs, with females and people with disabilities the most affected. Over 70% of HH say their ability to meet basic needs is insufficient. Needs are even more acute during wintertime or prior to the school year. UNICEF PDM surveys of Cash for Winter interventions in 2021-2022 echo MSNA/HNO 2022 findings indicating greater vulnerability among FHH: 10% rely on charity, remittances/ borrowing, compared to 2% among male-headed households. FHH were three times more likely than male-headed ones to be in lower income categories. UNICEF cash assistance in 2022/2023 is informed by lessons learned from 2021/2022 winter response, which indicated that when compared to the average families’ monthly expenditure, the amount of cash assistance was significant (40%). However, when compared to expenditures over the course of winter, it was only 10%. UNICEF increased from one to three rounds of transfers to have significant impact on children and their families during winter.

4 - Implementation of activities

The programme is implemented by national NGOs responsible for the identification, registration and verification of beneficiaries, evaluation of their eligibility, and provision of referral services. To ensure the integrity of the programme, there is a division of roles whereby UNICEF uses the services of a third-party financial service provider (FSP) to transfer the cash grants, while national NGO partners implement the referral component. The main activities are:1- Training of field monitors and orientation of NGO partners on the programme’s objectives and criteria.2- Communication campaigns in the selected areas with clear messages on eligibility and duration of the programme.3- Identification and registration of beneficiaries through partners’ existing registries, complemented by door-to-door outreach and home visits.4- Verification of the family composition, children-specific characteristics and needs. 5- UNICEF field offices conduct regular assessments of the targeted locations to ensure access to markets.6- Provision of three rounds of cash transfer (one every two months) through FSPs. 7- Referral services to families through the duration of the cash assistance. It is the provision of social services to the most vulnerable families and strengthens their linkage with the official humanitarian actors and governmental entities.8- Monitoring activities, including PDM, randomized verification of beneficiaries, and centralized grievance and redress mechanisms.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk #1: Social tensions generated by lack of clarity on eligibility criteria, or harassment towards beneficiaries receiving the cash transfer. Likelihood (high)Mitigation measures: (i) clear communication strategy on eligibility criteria and selection modalities; (ii) strict verification mechanisms; (iii) continuous on-site monitoring; (iv) complaint and response mechanism (v) rigorous monitoring schemes; (vi) procedure for data protection developed by the IP; (vii) Field monitors ensure that eligible children are not excluded.Risk #2: Abuse of Authority by distributions actors (GBV, discrimination, monetary coercion)Likelihood (Medium)Mitigation measures: (i) pre-screening of IPs and close monitoring of the implementation; (ii) IPs are required to develop internal procedures to identify, track and respond to possible cases of abuse, exploitation or gender-based violence; and (iii) UNICEF systematically train its IPs on prevention and Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse, as well as abuse of authority and conflict of interests. Risk #3: Protection risks related to walking in a long and unsecured distance between the targeted locations and the FSP outletsLikelihood (Medium)Mitigation measures: (i) coordination with the FSPs to expand their outlets to nearby locations to the targeted beneficiaries, (ii) coordination with the FSPs and IPs for a clear distribution plan and prior notification to beneficiaries, (iii) increase the duration of cash delivery to a week


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

UNICEF has its PSEA policy in place and ensures establishing mechanisms to prevent and respond to sexual exploitation and abuse. In addition, UNICEF as co-lead agency for the inter-agency network (ICN) on PSEA, will continue to support and work closely with other agencies aiming at enhancing the portfolio of protection from sexual exploitation and abuse. The main goal of the ICN is to enhance the networking for the benefit of people at risk, and ensure access to complaint mechanisms and specialized services for victims. Therefore, continuous capacity building for humanitarian workers on zero tolerance for SEA and awareness-raising for the community on rights to humanitarian aid free of any exchange in a dignified way is integrated in project activities. Consequently, as per-condition for contracting implementing partners, PSEA assurance and capacity building plans are guaranteed. This includes providing technical support in the fields of Child Safeguarding, GBV, Code of Conduct and PSEA.


9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Assumption: The overall socio-economic situation in the country continues to deteriorate, increasing the needs of targeted populations. Risk #1: Delays in obtaining official approvals can delay implementation. likelihood (Medium) Mitigation measures #1: (i) development of standardized agreements with the IPs across targeted governorates to ensure speedy review; (ii) establish good communication channels with MoSAL officials to ensure all challenges are shared and addressed in a timely manner. Risk #2: Fraud.likelihood (Medium)Mitigation measures #2: (i) Systematic communication by SMS with beneficiaries (ii) monitoring of each payment by FSP. Risk #3: High level of inflation limit the effectiveness of the cash component. likelihood (High) Mitigation measures #3: (i) negotiate with the Central Bank a preferential exchange rate; (ii) adjust transfer values on inflation. Risk #4: Delay in the process of cash distribution to beneficiaries due to the limited absorption capacity of the FSPs. likelihood (High) Mitigation measures #4: Expansion of the number of agreements with FSPs as feasible. Risk #5: Difficulty to obtain data on eligible families as per the targeting criteria. Likelihood (high) Mitigation measures# 5: Conduct door-to-door visits by field monitors of IPs in the targeted geographical locations.
