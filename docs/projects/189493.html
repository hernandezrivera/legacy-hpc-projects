1 - Needs

South Sudan - faces multiple overlapping and mutually reinforcing crises including conflict and intercommunal violence, population displacement, influx of refugees, economic downturn, low levels of agricultural productivity, food insecurity, climate change and associated hydro-meteorological hazards, transboundary pests and diseases, invasion of desert locust and the COVID-19 pandemic. These crises have had huge human and economic costs with immediate and long-lasting consequences for livelihoods and food and nutrition security. The protracted crisis, and now climate-related shocks, have continued to drive humanitarian needs in South Sudan, which have undermined the development gains achieved since independence and worsened the humanitarian situation, which remains dire. The country is facing its highest levels of food insecurity and malnutrition since independence 10 years ago.According to the IPC-compatible food security analysis conducted in 2021/22 by the Food Security and Livelihoods Cluster for the 2022 Humanitarian Needs Overview, an unprecedented 8.0 million South Sudanese are likely to face severe acute food insecurity at the peak of the 2022 lean season (May-July). This is an 11 percent increase when compared to the 7.2 million who were projected to face severe acute food insecurity in the lean season of 2021. The counties of greatest concern are Jonglei, Lakes, Unity and Warrap, with Fangak County in Jonglei State. The main drivers of food insecurity in the rural areas of the country are climatic, conflict, and economic shocks on top of poor or lack of access to basic services. In 2021, civil insecurity impacted on the already fragile situation of vulnerable, poor and food insecure people in both rural and urban settings. Inter-communal violence, cattle raiding, revenge killings, and armed conflict left many dead, caused displacement and exacerbated the dire humanitarian situation. Agricultural-dependent livelihoods are disrupted, further compounding the existing shocks and stressors, with the likelihood of reducing the 2022 agricultural production. In 2021, excessive rainfall, river overflows, and accumulation of residual soil moisture triggered for the third consecutive year widespread flooding which affected more than 835 000 people. More than two-thirds of the flood-affected areas are now facing the risk of hunger as food prices have recorded a 15 percent increase since August 2021. The FAO assessments show that an estimated 65 177 hectares of land under cereals were damaged, and about 37 624 tonnes of cereals were lost in 2021 in the flood-affected areas. Farm level production and productivity has consistently remained low, with an average farm size of 1.7 hectares, and cereal yield levels have remained low at about 800kg/hectare. This is attributed to limited extension services, high cost of labor, resulting in high dependence on hand tools, poor agronomic practices, poor post-harvest management, and poor soil, water, and pest and disease management skills. These limiting production and post-harvest factors are compounded by extreme and variable weather events. According to FAO, national gross cereal production in 2021 stood at 1 049 000 metric tons, which was 4 percent lower than the 1 093 000 metric tons produced in 2020. With a projected population of 12.4 million, the 2022 estimated cereal deficit of 541 000 metric tons is the highest ever since the crisis started in 2013. The decline in production is mainly due to the significant flood-related crop losses reported in Jonglei, Warrap, Lakes, Unity and Upper Nile states. For the livestock sector, the cumulative effect of the 2019 and 2020 floods exacerbated the situation in 2021. The 2021 floods affected an estimated 10.1 million animals across the various states, and resulted in 795 000 animal deaths especially of small ruminants (sheep and goats) which are typically owned by poorer and more vulnerable households.

5 - Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA)
5.1. Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA)?

Yes

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

Cash and voucher is preferred because they offer multiple benefits to the local economy and the recipients of the assistance. Cash or voucher are a dignified form of assistance (where the conditions are favourable). They place the decision making power in the hands of the recipients to identify and meet their own priority needs. The recipients of the assistance have choice over type and quality; therefore, their decisions are valued and influence the agro dealers/input providers to endeavor to deliver quality at competitive prices, in a bid to attract more buyers. The beneficiary has control, which is lost with in-kind support. Moreover, resources are directly injected into the local communities, enabling them to access goods/services from the local markets, therefore creating demand, which in turn stimulates supply. These boost the small and local traders and markets, improving their functionality therefore contributing to boosting the local economy.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

Cash interventions are informed by different sources of information on market functionality. The Joint Market Monitoring Initiative (JMMI) created by the Cash Working Group in south Sudan conducts markets assessments across the country on prices and market indicators to guide cash and voucher programming. The FAO’s Crop and Livestock Market Information System (CLiMIS) provides real time analyzed data and information on agricultural and livestock products to aid understanding of market performance and planning of relevant interventions. FAO also carries out rapid market assessments in intervention locations prior to guide implementation. Additionally, given the nature of FAO cash interventions, (with the objective of supporting increased/improved production/productivity), these market assessments are complemented with broader assessments including the CFSAM and SSSA which do have a specifically relationship to the agricultural markets, ensuring a holistic process of analyzing market functionality.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

FAO is committed to providing a high quality, accountable and dignified response, which takes account of the needs and capacities of women, men and vulnerable groups. Together with the international aid and humanitarian community, FAO has adopted the seven (7) Commitments on Accountability to Affected Populations, which means that FAO meaningfully engages affected people at every key stage of the project cycle (assessment, design, implementation and monitoring). These commitments are: * Supporting staff to acquire the understanding, knowledge and competencies that will enable them to meet FAO’s commitments. * Communities receiving assistance should receive information about the services and support available in local languages. Means of communication are appropriate and tailored to local circumstances including communicating the targeting criteria and the available feedback mechanism. Ensure information on inputs during the seed fair and distribution is provided to beneficiaries in local languages. *Ensure the Channels are able to capture project based information and offer response to close the feedback loop * Ensure there is a feedback desk at every distribution point and feedback is recorded clearly on the template.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

FAO has a toll free number (515) for general grievances and 882 for SEA complaints, AAP assistants based in all field offices to whom the vulnerable members can share feedback in local languages.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

All Implementing partners (IP) are regulated under a Letter of agreement with FAO that include information on PSEA and training on PSEA and corruption specifically. FAO also supports PSEA and anticorruption training/awareness at cluster level. FAO AAP assistants and M&E staff monitor such aspects closely.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

FAO is formally signed-up and committed to the IASC Commitments on Accountability to Affected People (AAP). These aim to produce quality humanitarian and development programs by maximizing the dignified and active involvement of communities in all aspects of programs that affect their lives mostly on how power imbalance can be reduced. FAOSS has functional feedback mechanisms including suggestion boxes, feedback desks, toll free hotline numbers (515 for general grievances and 882 for SEA complaints), AAP focal points that are trained and employed by FAO in each field office. The various options for feedback and complaints are expected to facilitate access to the system to ensure the most vulnerable feel safe to speak up. FAO’s project designs and implementation makes use of community committees whose membership comprises of r people with disabilities, women and youth representatives, among others, to participate and engage in the project. FAO is a member of the UN PSEA Task force which supports Community Based Complaints Mechanism (CBCM), which also comprises of national and international NGOs.

12 - Contribution to Collective Outcomes
12.1. Does your project contribute to any of the three Collective Outcomes, i.e. Improved Food security; Enhanced support for prevention and response to needs of survivors of Gender-Based Violence; and Flood displacement?

Yes

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

FAO’s ground level intervention is classified as direct and through implementing partners. FAO’s direct implementation involves FAO staff who are always introducing prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV as FAO already has a dedicated team dealing with the issue. In addition, FAO always attaches these requirements as part of the letter of agreement and the accountability to affected population (AAP) and gender team closely follows the implementation. Hence, FAO contributes to the rolling out and implementation of GBV related preventions, risk mitigation and response.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

FAO is currently providing DRR support in Jonglei, Upper Nile and Northern Bahr El Ghazal. This community level service includes flood prevention and mitigation measures which provides information early enough for households to move to highland areas and move their valuable assets and animals to safe areas. In addition, some of the infrastructure works (including dykes) will prevent households to divert flash floods and overflows. Furthermore, FAO provides short maturing crop and vegetable seeds and fishing kits for households to grow through flood recession or doing it after the flood incidents. These interventions, therefore, allows households not to migrate remote areas and in some cases allows them to prevent displacement and hence contributes to the reduction of flood displacement.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

Yes, by providing agricultural inputs (seeds, tools, vaccination and animal health treatment and fishing kits) and capacity building (training on best agricultural practices), beneficiaries (IPC phase 3 and above) will be able to produce their own food. According to FAO analysis, the intervention helps to allow households to produce crop production, which is sufficient at least for about 5 to 6 months of households needs. The vaccination and animal health interventions helps households to improve the productive capacity of animals and hence increases households’ nutrition and can increase animal sells to supplement their income sources. The fishing kits and related trainings have always been an important contributor increasing the purchasing capacity households, especially of areas living along the riverine areas. All these interventions, therefore, significantly contributes to the reduction in the number of people in extreme food insecurity categories (IPC phase 4 and 5) by 2023, especially at the peak of the lean season
