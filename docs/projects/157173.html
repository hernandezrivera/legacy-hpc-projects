Project Summary

From 01 January through 31 December 2019 and in partnership with Lead Partner INGO, we will continue our currently running EuropeAid-funded Education programme under which we currently supports four schools in the Ariha sub-district of Idleb governorate, assisting 1,515 students, 97 teachers/school staff (including 4 psychosocial (PSS) facilitators) over the course of this grant.
We are proposing to support an additional six schools for a total of ten schools under a formal education component of this programme in addition to 16 Self Learning Program (SLP) temporary learning spaces in four camps under a non-formal education component, expanding it educational activities into the HNO 2019’s highly prioritized geographic areas of Saraqab and Darkosh sub-districts of Ariha, Idleb (in addition to the current coverage of Ariha sub-district); Madiq Castle in Hama and Afrin, Jandairis, Haritan and Zarbah in rural Aleppo.
Structured around our holistic model for education during times of crisis and recovery, the proposed program has three primary objectives: (1) to improve academic teaching and learning; (2) to promote student wellbeing and protection; and (3) to strengthen educational access and resources. Comprising both formal and non-formal education components, the program will reach a total of 5920 children (3163 males, 2757 females) — of which 35 percent are adolescents and youth — as well as 291 teachers / school staff (166 males and 125 females) and 640 caretakers (256 males and 384 females).
Under the formal education component - via the ten schools - we aim to reach a total of 4000 children (2196 males, 1804 females) as well as 209 teachers (119 males and 90 female) and 400 caretakers (150 males and 250 females), noting that all students who receive formal education under this project will also have access to the non-formal education component. Under the non-formal education component - 16 Self-Learning Program (SLP) temporary learning spaces in 4 camps, we aim to prepare 1920 out-of-school children (967 male, 953 female) for integration into formal primary schools after one year of study, including 16 teachers (8 males and 8 females). Note: expansion on both components’ activities can be found under the Implementation of Activities section of this proposal.
Academic teaching and learning is coupled with program components focused on student safety and wellbeing. School personnel receive training in child protection and psychosocial care, and all students participate in weekly, age-appropriate PSS sessions led by dedicated facilitators. These activities aim to promote positive social-emotional development among children, and include a youth Life Skills curriculum for older students. Further, each school has a Protection Facilitator who is responsible for monitoring protection mainstreaming measures, such as a case management and referral system for students requiring external support. School personnel are also trained on emergency preparedness and response, and develop plans to deal with threats like attacks on or near schools.
In light of economic barriers that require some students to work rather than attend school, the proposed intervention also includes a supplemental livelihoods pilot component. This pilot will target the most vulnerable children and families who rely on child labor as an economic coping mechanism by offering conditional cash-vouchers or food baskets to offset the financial losses incurred when transitioning children from work to school.

Needs

The war in Syria has devastated educational access and opportunity across the country. After eight years of conflict, more than 2.1 million children are out-of-school, one-third of all school buildings have been damaged or destroyed, and 120,000 qualified teachers have been lost due to injury, insecurity, or death. For children who have managed to continue their education, 5.8 million students still require educational assistance, ranging from basic school supplies to highly targeted academic, psychosocial and protection-related support. If these and other needs go unmet, there is significant concern that additional students will be forced out of the classroom, including the estimated 1.2 million children currently believed to be at risk of dropping out (HRP 2019). Such needs are especially acute in Northern Syria, including in the program’s proposed target areas of Jandairis, Afrin, Haritan and Zarbah in Aleppo Governorate, Rural Madiq Castle in Hama and Ariha, Saraqab and Darkosh sub-districts of Idleb governorate— which the HNO classifies as having ‘severe’ or ‘critical’ educational needs. In addition to research and analysis provided by UN, INGO and cluster actors, our understanding of the challenges facing students and teachers reflects findings from 12 educational needs assessments conducted in and around the target areas during 2018. In Rural Aleppo, for example, assessment data confirms that the primary barriers to accessing and providing quality formal education are: the dearth of safe, useable school facilities, especially during winter months; the challenge of training and compensating teachers, resulting in a high turnover of unqualified and/or volunteer instructors; and unmet school supply needs, which undermines the quality of instruction and exacerbates the pervasive challenge of managing large class sizes and multi-level cohorts. For children and their families, these limitations are compounded by threats to students’ physical safety and psychosocial wellbeing. This includes peer-to-peer abuse and corporal punishment inside schools, as well as issues of harassment and attack when traveling to and from school sites. Many female adolescents and youth also miss out on education as a result of family pressures to assume domestic and caretaking responsibilities, while young males often abandon schooling to earn money and contribute to household livelihoods (Our Organization, 2018). In the most extreme cases, these gendered barriers to education include early marriage, which can involve girls as young as 6 or 7, as well as the worst and most damaging forms of child labor (HRP 2019). Broadly speaking, ongoing exposure to violence and exploitation has resulted in high levels of “toxic stress”, with psychological trauma now common among young Syrians (Save the Children 2017). Internal displacement has further destabilised schools and education systems in the north, with many overcrowded schools unable to register newly arriving students despite running double-shifts. In camp settings, the education situation is especially dire: out of the 331 camps currently located in Idleb, Aleppo, Hama and Ar-Raqqa, only 62 contain schools or learning spaces, many of which are struggling to meet basic standards for safe, quality education provision (ACU 2018). With reference to age as a barrier to quality education, the disproportionate focus, especially among humanitarian organisations, on the educational needs of primary-level students has resulted in a lack of attention paid to older adolescents and youth. In general, there is little access to quality secondary education in the target areas, as well as few opportunities for youth ‘hard’ and ‘soft’ skill development. This has led many young people to feel aimless, disillusioned and forgotten about, with some relying on negative coping mechanisms, such as violence or other potentially harmful activities.

Implementation of activities

With regards to the formal education component, the program will support ten schools through a range of academic activities. To begin, school sites are assessed for infrastructural issues, and light rehabilitation works are carried out where necessary. This includes outfitting schools for winter weather, and ensuring WASH facilities meet health and gender-sensitive standards. Next, after teachers are trained on pedagogy for student-centred and mixed-level classrooms, schools offer a core set of academic classes in Arabic, English, math and social sciences that follow recognised curriculums. While the majority of our sites cater to primary students, about one-third of the schools also include secondary students and, more advanced subjects. Throughout the program, we provide teachers with monthly stipends, as well as structured opportunities for professional development and feedback from on-the-ground education officers. Our non-formal education programs include many of the same activities found at our formal school sites, though they are designed to be more modular and flexible in response to the unique needs of children living in informal tented settlements. Based on the cluster’s Self Learning Program (SLP) and approach, the proposed intervention also includes 16 SLP temporary learning spaces in 4 camps, which will aim to prepare 1920 out-of-school children (967 male, 953 female) for integration into formal primary schools after one year of study. Prior to engaging children in SLP activities, we will assess current learning levels of each child (including literacy and numeracy) using placement test tools. While SLP sites offer students fewer weekly contact hours, the program trains children and their caretakers to follow a self-guided curriculum for home-based study. For this reason, SLP components include a larger number of family awareness sessions and community outreach activities (such as home visits). Like our formal programs, however, SLP facilitators are trained in academic teaching, child protection and psychosocial care, and students participate in weekly PSS activities at Child Friendly Spaces. Gender will be considered throughout the range of program activities, including in rehabilitation of WASH facilities, school personnel training (with modules on gender in PSS and child protection), and community outreach activities (parent awareness sessions on addressing gendered barriers to access, such as early marriage and child labour). Further, our dual focus on both primary and secondary students means that our programs support age and level-appropriate quality instruction, especially for older adolescents and youth. Here too, teacher training on managing mixed-level cohorts means that older students attending lower-level grades will received targeted support and attention from educators when possible. All education activities are monitored, verified and/or assessed through a variety of tools and practices. Our education team in each area includes a dedicated MEAL officer, who oversees key monitoring and reporting activities during weekly site visits. This includes teacher and classroom observations (which also feed into our broader approach to educator professional development) as well as checking and verifying school management practices around student/teacher attendance, school spending, and facility upkeep. Student learning in Arabic, English and Math is also assessed using a contextualised version of the ASER exam, as are improvements in children’s psychosocial wellbeing using the SDQ tool. These and other mechanisms, including student focus group discussions and parent interviews, are used to gather both qualitative and quantitative baseline and endline data, and to assess the overall quality and impact of our programs against our targets, indicators and objectives.

Protection Risk Analysis

— Attacks on and around schools, including airstrikes, shelling or ground offensives, resulting in psychosocial trauma, physical injury or death to students, teachers and program staff (HIGH): We will closely monitor the security situation in target areas, and establish emergency response plans, threat notification systems, and sheltering or evacuation procedures for program sites. School personnel will also be trained in first aid and school safety, and schools will be outfitted with health and safety kits. If threats persist, schools will be closed and potentially relocated, relying on alternative modalities for service delivery (including home schooling) during closure periods. — Child safety when traveling to and from school, including harassment, assault and conflict-related aerial or ground attack (MEDIUM): In instances where there is forewarning, a notification system will be established to inform students and their families about security threats and/or school closures. More generally, a Protection facilitator at each school will create and maintain a ‘Walking School Bus’ program to ensure students travel to and from school together, and with adults when possible. First aid and emergency response trainings will also prepare school personnel to respond to student injury if necessary. A child friendly feedback mechanism will be established which will allow for these issues to be raised and discussed with children and teachers. — School-related child protection concerns, such as bullying from peers, corporal punishment from teachers or gender-based harassment or abuse (MEDIUM): Child-protection considerations are mainstreamed into all relevant program activities. With regards to student-to-student abuse, our PSS and life skills classes teach children and youth about the importance of positive and respectful behaviour with peers. Teachers also receive child protection training that covers identifying and managing bullying and harassment, as well as training on non-violent skills for classroom and behaviour management. To further protect students, all program staff sign and agree to uphold child safeguarding standards regarding the appropriate treatment of the children in our care. A child friendly feedback mechanism will be established which will allow for these issues to be raised and discussed with children and teachers. — Community tensions resulting from perceived inequalities related to selecting beneficiaries, hiring staff, and procuring project supplies or services (MEDIUM): All project activities and procedures will be based on transparent, needs-focused criteria for engaging with local communities and markets. Beneficiaries for the cash component, for example, will be selected in consultation with a school-advisory board using vulnerability criteria to identify the most in-need families in each community. Similarly, teachers will be chosen and paid in line with education directorate policies regarding fairly hiring and compensating school personnel. Further, procurement for school supply and rehabilitation services will follow a bidding and contracting process to prohibit partiality or nepotism. — Theft or assault during or as a result of cash payments and supply distribution (LOW-MEDIUM): Payment of salaries, distribution of cash vouchers and provision of student kits all carry the potential of attracting unwanted, harmful attention for recipients. We mitigate associated risks by establishing payment and distribution protocol to monitor and safeguard staff and beneficiaries when receiving cash or in-kind items.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

— Changes regarding the security situation in the target areas, including the deterioration of safety and mobility conditions, as well as the displacement of staff and/or beneficiaries, in ways that hinder or prohibit program access and provision (MEDIUM-HIGH): We closely monitor the security situation in Northern Syria, and establish emergency response and contingency plans to deal with changes in the stability and access. These plans include relocating program sites if necessary, as well as using alternative modalities of service delivery, such as home schooling. — Increased internal displacement, both into and out of the target areas (MEDIUM-HIGH): Our monitoring of conflict dynamics includes close attention to migration flows in Northern Syria. In instances where we are unable to meet the educational needs of IDPs arriving to our catchment areas, head teachers and school Protection Facilitators will attempt to link children and their families with other schools in the community. Conversely, when conflict factors cause students and teachers at our schools to flee, we rely on contingency planning and coordination with local authorities to fill personnel gaps and continue service delivery, if safe to do so. — Border closures and delays in transporting project goods and supplies (MEDIUM): If supply lines are disrupted due to border closures, we rely on alternative channels for procurement in local markets, where possible. As a contingency measure, we also divide program supplies among warehouses in Syria. During closure periods, we also undertake advocacy with relevant parties to appeal for resumed humanitarian access and entry. — Instability of local markets (MEDIUM): The high fluctuation of currency and market prices inside Syria can affect the exchange value of program goods and salaries. We monitor exchange rates and market dynamics to anticipate and plan for such changes, and adapt budgeting and planning accordingly. — Interference of armed, political or religious groups and/or individuals seeking to co-opt program activities for influence or exploitation (MEDIUM): We are committed to the core humanitarian principles of neutrality, impartiality and independence. As it relates to the influence or infringement of violent, political or religious groups, all program staff are briefed on and agree to uphold these principals, as well as the Do No Harm approach. We also build relationships with local authorities and community leaders to generate understanding and support for the humanitarian nature of our work. Further, students, staff and community members are encouraged to use the program complaints mechanism to report concerns regarding individuals or groups who may be working to undermine these core values.
