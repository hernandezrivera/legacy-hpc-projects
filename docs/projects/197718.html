Project Summary

NRC's project will contribute to the Food Security Sector’s (FSS) HRP Objectives 1, 2, and 3.
When relevant and feasible, NRC will seek to have integrated/complementary responses that promote synergies with Multi-purpose Cash Assistance (MPCA) and early recovery and livelihoods assistance, such as linking lifesaving and food assistance interventions with livelihoods/early recovery initiatives and other interventions aimed at creating employment and income generation opportunities, and strengthening target communities’ self-reliance and resilience.
NRC will implement the following interventions:
FSS Objective 1: 1.1. NRC will provide the assessed food-insecure people with monthly food assistance, to enable them to scale-up and sustain minimum food consumption needs. Depending on needs and available (funding) resources, assistance will be provided for a minimum of 8 and up to 12 consecutive months.
1.2. In case of mass displacements caused by sudden escalation, NRC will provide emergency response to crisis-affected, vulnerable people, with short- term food assistance. This may be supplemented by MPCA as lifesaving and/or life-sustaining assistance intended for the most vulnerable to enable them to meet general basic needs, ensuring no overlap between the recipients of short-term food assistance and that of MPCA.
Wherever markets are functional, accessible, and of sufficient capacity, NRC will prioritize unconditional cash as the most appropriate, cost-effective, and most-preferred modality. If needed, NRC will use vouchers or in-kind modalities. Both interventions will contribute to the reduced application of negative coping strategies and, to ensure better utilization of the assistance and increase its impact in promoting FS outcomes, will be accompanied by information campaigns about the importance of dietary diversity and nutritious dietary choices.
FSS Objective 2: 2.1. Local/home-based food production support will be in-kind and target specific types of agricultural/food production activities, such as horticulture/greenhouse vegetables, dairy farming, poultry, cereals, legumes, etc.
2.2. Cash for agricultural inputs – NRC will use labelled cash as a modality for this intervention, which will be applied wherever feasible, and for a variety of agricultural/food production activities.
Regardless of the modality applied, through these interventions the selected small-scale subsistence farmers will benefit from access to and utilization of the inputs they need to rehabilitate or sustain their production, such as fodder, seeds/seedlings, fertilizer, animal vitamins or medicines, equipment (irrigation, greenhouses), and/or tools. Both interventions will be aimed at enabling affected households to protect and build their productive assets, and improve their self-reliance and resilience prospects. To ensure greater impact, effectiveness, and sustainability for beneficiaries, distributions will be preceded by technical training (incl. on climate-smart agriculture), and followed by provision of field advisory assistance.
To facilitate the transition of displacement-affected populations from humanitarian assistance to self-reliance and sustainable livelihoods, NRC will use market-based approaches to support economic recovery of households and facilitate their access to demand-driven Income Generation (IG) and employment through:
2.3. Small business grants (conditional cash/in-kind) followed-up by coaching/advisory support, to support long-term IG and (self)-employment.
2.4. Cash for Work (CFW) activities as a means to improve access to short-term employment and IG.
2.5. Vocational, business, and soft skills training, and apprenticeship programmes.
FSS Objective 3: NRC will also use the CFW modality to improve communities' resilience and capacity to sustain households’ livelihoods. Depending on priorities identified, this may include rehabilitation of grain silos, water/irrigation systems, access roads to fields, etc.

3 - Needs

In September 2022, the national average price of a WFP standard reference food basket reached SYP 329,526, up 40 % since the onset of the crisis in Ukraine – a four-fold increase in two years (2023 Syria HNO Food Security Assessment). According to early results of assessments (FSA and FSLA) conducted by WFP and the FSS, an estimated 15 million people will be food insecure in 2023 (12.1 million currently, of which 1.3 million are severely food insecure, and 1.8 million at risk of food insecurity). Around 6.7 million people are displaced within Syria, with 4.8 million people relying on monthly food assistance. Accordingly, approximately 50% of Syrians now spend more than 75% of their expenditure on food. As a result of converging crises, the socio-economic situation in Syria worsened further in 2022, especially impacting the most vulnerable. Subsequently, 52% of households report having had to reduce overall food consumption. In the absence of full-time employment, the unemployed resort to purely remunerated, yet hardly available daily jobs. As a result, 84% of households report that their income is insufficient to meet basic needs. Due to the further deteriorated food security situation in Syria, largely caused by the lack of income, underperforming agriculture seasons and increased food process, the share of population applying crises-coping mechanisms reached 50% of the total population.

7 - Protection Risk Analysis

(1): Corruption at the level of beneficiary selection, including exclusion of specific people or communities. Mitigation Measures: 1) Complaints/Feedback mechanisms in place and accessible to beneficiaries. 2) Transparent and participatory approach used; Various outreach modalities utilised and own selection criteria is used. 3) Post-distribution monitoring includes questions about exchange of aid for services or money. 4) NRC compliance standards and procedures. 5) staff awareness on anti-corruption policies. (2): Requirement of beneficiary vetting by some donors pose a number of protection risks for beneficiaries. Mitigation measures: 1) Institutional policies in place to guarantee confidentiality of beneficiary data. No beneficiary vetting accepted during implementation. 2) Programmatic tools and processes designed in a manner which guarantees impartial selection of beneficiaries. (3): Centralised, large-scale distributions might create protection- and security-related risks for beneficiaries and staff/partners. Also, they may not be physically accessible for people affected by impaired mobility or those unable to leave the house. Mitigation Measures: 1) Prioritisation of household-level distributions where feasible and appropriate. 2) Where centralised distribution sites are required, selection will include risk assessment and alternative distribution methods. 3) Distributions will be organized in a way that reduces the number of people gathering and queuing.


8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

Potential SEA risks are mainly identified during the beneficiary selection process, the distribution of assistance and while accessing services. NRC communicates clearly with communities on the expected assistance modalities as well as confidential complain and feedback channels available to them and makes sure gender balanced teams register and deliver assistance to communities whenever possible. During post-delivery monitoring (e.g. PDMs), NRC inquires if people were asked to exchange anything for aid or service. In order to reduce perceptions of power, NRC asks in PDMs who, according to the recipients of aid, provided the assistance they received, enabling identification of possible areas of concern. All NRC staff and non-staff are bound by NRC's Code of Conduct, protection from sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA), and child safeguarding (CSG) policies. Several sessions take place with staff to raise awareness on PSEA and CSG, in addition to sessions for contractors. All NRC teams have to complete mandatory online PSEA and safeguarding essentials e-courses as part of their induction. NRC's Complaints and Feedback Mechanism (CFM) is open and sensitive to SEA and CSG reports, which are managed by a dedicated team in Head Office with support from regional and country office teams who regularly updates guidance to emerging needs and trends to tailor management and support to cases. Efforts in 2021 to strengthen child-friendly CFM started and are expected to conclude in 2022.


9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1. NRC staff are injured, killed, or suffer psychological trauma due to conflict: Mitigation: Security briefings, HEAT and HEIST trainings, briefings, more support from UNMAS.2. Fraud/corruption/bribery. Mitigation: enforcing NRC's policy of zero tolerance through strengthening of procurement and operating procedures, promote the "whistle-blowing" policy, corruption risk mapping, ensure partners implement anti-corruption measures. 3. NRC is unable to timely operate due to the impact of internationals sanctions. Mitigation: request for derogation, looking for different banking channels, advocacy.4. NRC is unable to timely implement due to delays in approvals from Government/local authorities. Mitigation: Advocacy plan in place, set the lead time for approval submission to be 2 weeks with a close follow.5. NRC is unable to timely deliver the planned activities due to rapid inflation and change of prices. Mitigation: review budget and targets on a quarterly basis, communication with the donors, find more convenient payment terms to suppliers.6. Donor policies, including overly restrictive redlines and perceptions, affect the ability to implement and scale-up programs. Mitigation: Engaging with donor, Joint advocacy efforts with DINGOs, Intensify the negotiations with local partner and MOUs, Long terms programs and reconstructions, media c-overage in private and public advocacy.7. COVID-19/Cholera outbreak and associated Restrictions: SOPs for safe implementation.
