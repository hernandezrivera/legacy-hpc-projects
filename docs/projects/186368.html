Project Summary

Street Child will work to improve access to quality, protective education for out-of-school children, quality of teaching and learning for formal schoolchildren to address acute learning and retention barriers for 60,000 conflict-affected children in the BAY states of northeast Nigeria. The project, while education-focused, will also seek to address critical child protection issues that also serve as barriers to retention and learning – teachers will be trained on PSS, child protection, and child-centered pedagogies to contribute toward schools as protective environments as well as Street Child’s goal of children being safe, in school, and learning and the Federal Government’s Safe School Declaration. Street Child will work closely with communities and with local partners and stakeholders to build the capacity of those working in education in the region, improving sustainability and contributing to long-term systems strengthening.
Within the scope of this project, the following LGAs have been identified for intervention:
Adamawa LGAs: Madagali, Michika, Mubi North, Mubi South
Borno LGAs: MMC, Jere, Gwoza, Damboa, Monguno, Biu, Hawul, Mobbar, Dikwa, Konduga
Yobe LGAs: Gujba, Gulani

1 - Needs

The conflict in northeast Nigeria has been characterised by attacks on education and the targeting of schools, teachers, and students by armed groups. This has led to an overwhelming of an already weak school system, with schools unable to absorb displaced children looking to continue education, while other families keep their children at home in order to protect them from infamous mass kidnaps which have taken place in the region. The strained system is failing children – learning outcomes, including foundational literacy and numeracy and numeracy are low, while drop-out rates are high. The 2021 HRP estimated that approximately 1.3 million children were in need of education support, while 20,000 teachers required capacity building and training in improved teaching and learning approaches. Factors limiting access to education in Northeast Nigeria have affected both boys and girls, but access to quality education has tended to be even more limited for girls than for boys, and adolescent girls are amongst the most vulnerable to dropping out of school. While access to quality, safe education is limited for both boys and girls in northeast Nigeria, the limiting factors are more pronounced for girls than boys – adolescent girls are among the most vulnerable constituencies for dropping out of school, often due to protection risk events such as early marriage or pregnancy as well as families only being able to afford a limited number of children in education. Children with Disabilities (CwD) experience considerable barriers to enrolling in school at all, with retention and learning indicators also notably low – a Street Child consultation found that 57% of CwD are out-of-school children. Risk factors for CwD are social stigmatisation, lack of specialised trained teachers, lack of inclusive facilities and learning aids, and high costs of education and specialised materials. There is a need to better integrate and respond to the barriers that particularly vulnerable groups, including girls and CwD, face in accessing quality learning opportunities in safe and protective learning environments. Recognising the key role education plays in providing immediate physical, psychosocial and cognitive protection in emergency contexts, there is a need to build safe and protective learning spaces. Building the capacity of teachers on pedagogical skills, psychosocial skills and child protection knowledge is crucial to ensure that education is protective and builds life-savings knowledge on topics such as health, hygiene and GBV (gender-based violence). At the same time, given that the low educational outcomes in NE Nigeria pre-date the current crisis, there is also a need to ensure that the support provided contributes to strengthening the system as a whole and in the long-term. In addition to formal education northeast Nigeria has a large Islamic/Quranic school sector (IQE), which focuses on rote learning and reciting of Islamic texts such as the Quran. There have been attempts by Federal and State governments to introduce modern subjects into these schools, so that students can attain basic literacy and numeracy, but these have seen limited success due to ideological resistance of Islamic teachers. However, this education modality is important due to its prevalence (up to 80% of all schools in some northern states are IQE) and the number of poor and girl learners who study there. Many communities and families prefer IQE to the formal education sector, but also have expressed interest in integrating some non-religious education into the IQE system.

2 - Activities

Street Child will provide Education in Emergencies support to a total of 150,000 (80,000 Formal; 35,000 Non-formal; 35,000 IQE) children in Borno, Yobe and Adamawa states. 2,500 (1700 formal; 400 non-formal; 400 IQE) teachers will be involved in the project with 60% of them female. Out of school children will be placed in temporary learning spaces (TLSs) supervised by Community Volunteer Teachers (CVTs). CVTs will be trained and receive an allowance to facilitate non-formal classes, including training on PSS and child protection. Where necessary, new semi-permanent TLSs will be constructed in line with best practices prioritising children's safety and security, including safe locations, perimeter fences and the availability of first aid kits in line with the Safe Schools Declaration. Construction and renovations will also aim to meet the accessibility needs of CwD. The implementation of the national EiE curriculum, which Street Child has worked on, will be scaled-up and used as the accelerated learning curriculum in our TLCs. The learning progress of children attending Temporary Learning Spaces TLCs will be regularly assessed, with the aim of mainstreaming children into the formal system to allow them to continue their education. Street Child will work closely with the local and state education authorities to facilitate this mainstreaming. Similarly, Street Child will work with the education authorities to achieve official accreditation of CVTs through the Federal Teacher Scheme, allowing these community members to become government teachers and be included on the government payroll for sustainability. To support the strengthening of the formal education system and improve learning outcomes in formal schools for all children, Street Child will train government primary school teachers on pedagogical skills and psychosocial skills. This will include training teachers to implement the innovative Teaching at the Right Level (TaRL) approach, with the aim of remediating lack of skills in foundational literacy and numeracy. Formal schools will also be supported with teaching and learning materials and adaptation of school structures (including WASH facilities) to be more inclusive, and the training of teachers on inclusive, child-friendly pedagogical skills. This will empower teachers to be able to meet the differing learning and support needs of different children, particularly with the aim of ensuring the needs of CwD and girls in accessing education and quality learning are met. Community Education Committees (CECs) or School-Based Management Committees (SBMCs), with a mandate to manage and maintain TLS, will be established or strengthened – building sustainability within the community, as well as supporting entry and acceptance. Street Child will provide committee members with comprehensive training on children’s rights and protection mechanisms, inclusion, school management and disaster risk reduction. Basic child protection prevention, identification and response principles will also be taught to teachers, teacher mentors, CEC/SMBC members and other school personnel in line with Street Child’s commitment to integrated education and child protection programming. Schools and TLSs will also be supported to develop clear, comprehensive safeguarding mechanisms that facilitate childrens’ safety in school.

7.1.d - In which LGA are you planning to undertake Nexus activities?

Street Child's approach to EiE bridges the humanitarian-development nexus, providing immediate protection whilst also contributing to resilience-building and system strengthening. A key element that Street Child will work on is collaborating with relevant state authorities to ensure that the learning children and teachers gain in TLSs and other non-formal settings during the current crisis is validated and recognized, allowing these non-formal teachers and learners to be mainstreamed into the formal education system. Nexus activities will therefore be undertaken in all targeted LGAs: Gujba, Gulani, Mubi, Michika, Madagali, MMC, Jere, Gwoza, Damboa, Monguno, Biu, Hawul, Mobbar, Dikwa and Konduga.

8.6 - Please describe briefly the modalities your project will put in place to ensure protection mainstreaming (particularly referring to the above questions).

The prioritisation of protection, safety, dignity, accountability and minimising unintended negative impacts ensure that the communities where Street Child works can access meaningful, dignified support. Street Child is committed to centring and mainstreaming protection throughout its activities and projects, and has pre-emptively identified potential protection risks within the project as well as taking active steps to integrate mitigation and management strategies. Protection reporting, response and prevention mechanisms are integrated into the design, and protection mainstreaming indicators are included within the monitoring and evaluation framework. At the beneficiary selection stage, Street Child will use tested vulnerability assessment tools and clear selection process to ensure that beneficiaries are selected based on need and through predetermined and clearly communicated criteria of vulnerability. In order to ensure participation and minimise inter-group tensions, targets have been set to include different communities (IDP, host community and returnee) as well as both men and women. Community leaders and members will be informed of the selection process and criteria during the inception stages, and continuous sensitisation of the selection criteria will be used to ensure that possible intra- and inter-group tensions are minimised. Sensitisation on these topics through regular community visits and feedback sessions has proven successful during past projects, where dissatisfaction about not being included in the project has been reduced by clearly explaining the aims of the project and how beneficiaries were selected. As part of our commitment to accountability and protection, Street Child commits to work towards the elimination of exploitation and abuse in the communities where we work and in our engagement with vulnerable populations. Street Child has a clear safeguarding policy in place which applies to all personnel and affiliates, including implementing partners and community members who are affiliated to our work. All Street Child staff, partner staff, and project-affiliated community members such as volunteers and committee members receive comprehensive training of the Street Child safeguarding policy. This comprehensive approach, which is also intended to be rolled out to vendors, ensures that all actors within SC projects are operating under the same rules and procedures and creates a harmonised safeguarding environment. In addition to training key community members on the Street Child safeguarding policy, wider community outreach and sensitisation is carried out via hosting of community forums and meetings, or use of visual aids including posters and murals which offer accessible and culturally relevant and appropriate key messages to empower community members on understanding and reporting concerns. Street Child is also a member of the PSEA Working Group and the focal person representing the organisation is also the safeguarding focal person and is involved in this project. Any incident report will be also shared with the WG who request regular reports.

9.5 - How will your project ensure feedback is collected from the most vulnerable community members?

Proven feedback mechanisms, which are already widely used and understood by stakeholders and beneficiaries in northeast Nigeria, will be utilised at all stages of the project cycle. At the planning stage, a needs assessment will be conducted in targeted communities and utilise focus group discussions (FGDs) to collect direct feedback from a diverse range of stakeholder constituencies, including gender, age, disability, and other group markers. FGDs will take place at both baseline and endline assessment, for community members to give feedback on the project life cycle and to allow SC to measure community satisfaction. In all target communities, a dedicated complaints mechanism officer (CRM) will be identified and made responsible for collection of feedback and coordinating the sharing and actioning of feedback between Street Child, partners, and community members. The CRM officer will hold weekly helpdesk sessions in each community for any beneficiaries who are unable or unwilling to use other feedback mechanisms. A complaints register, featuring details of complaint and actions taken, will be maintained. Outside of individual feedback, Street Child or project partner staff will attend weekly coordination meetings with community coalitions and structures, for instance CBCPCs with an equal representation of women and men, to collect feedback and share progress relating to specific child protection concerns. For community members who want to give feedback anonymously, anonymous complaint boxes will be placed in each community and checked weekly. A toll-free line will also be advertised where community members can call at any time to provide feedback or share urgent concerns, for instance acute safeguarding concerns. All feedback received will be fed into review sessions and management decisions, allowing activities to be adapted to respond where appropriate in line with Street Child's commitment to accountability and evidence-based learning.
