Project Summary

The project, "Assistance For the Rohingya Refugees (RRs) and Host Communities (HCs) in Bangladesh," will foster safe and resilient living conditions for FDMN (Rohingya) and host community members in Ukhiya, Cox’s Bazar, throughout 2025. It aims to improve shelter and non-food item (NFI) access, reducing physical and protection-related risks.
Shelter interventions will include constructing 300 Mid-Term Shelters (MTS) and repairing 50 shelters in Camp 1 East for FDMN residents, as well as building 15 new shelters for host community members in Jalia Palong. Activities will follow Shelter/NFI sector guidelines, prioritizing accessibility and resilience, such as drainage spaces and durable pathways. Shelters will include features like ramps, wider doors, and gender-sensitive spaces, such as lockable partitions. These efforts aim to address mobility challenges, reduce exposure to hazards, and enhance inclusivity.
NFI initiatives will focus on essential needs for safety and warmth. To improve nighttime security, 2,000 solar lights will be distributed, prioritizing women-led households. Winter support will include distributing blankets to 1,500 Rohingya and 800 host beneficiaries, as well as hoodies for 600 Rohingya children. For high-risk groups, such as young children and the elderly, tailored items like ergonomic mats and warm blankets will reduce health vulnerabilities. Beneficiary selection will involve community leaders, local authorities, and UNHCR to ensure transparency and prioritize the most vulnerable.
The project’s gender and inclusion strategy will promote equitable participation of men and women across activities. Gender-segregated lines on distribution days will ensure beneficiary comfort, while chairs will be provided for the elderly and persons with disabilities. Women volunteers from the community will work alongside men to facilitate group sessions, ensuring messages are effectively conveyed. Local residents, including elders, persons with disabilities, and youth, will contribute to mapping, site selection, and risk assessment. Gender balance will be maintained with a 50:50 ratio in tasks and equal pay. Collaboration with the 4W dashboard and other actors will help identify and prioritize vulnerable shelters.
Protection is central to the project, with measures to safeguard against vulnerabilities. Strategic shelter placement will reduce hazard exposure, and child labor prevention will be enforced in all activities. PSEA guidelines will be adhered to through staff training, collaboration with PSEA networks, and workshops. Women and girls at heightened risk or survivors of GBV will be connected to appropriate support services in partnership with protection agencies.
Community engagement will remain integral to the project, ensuring beneficiary voices guide planning and execution. IEC materials tailored for different literacy levels and a Complaints and Response Mechanism (CRM) will facilitate communication. Women-led committees will address community safety, and feedback channels—including notes, calls, SMS, and face-to-face interactions—will ensure transparency and accountability. These efforts will create a safe, inclusive, and resilient living environment for all beneficiaries.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

In the October 2023 J-MSNA report, distinct disparities in priority needs emerged between host community households and refugee households. Host community households exhibited a higher variance in priority needs, while refugee households tended to converge. Nevertheless, both groups consistently identified food and shelter materials/upgrades as crucial priorities. Breaking down the first priority needs, 14% of host community and 9% of FDMN expressed the need for shelter materials/upgrades. In the second priority needs category, 12% of host community and 28% of FDMN highlighted shelter materials/upgrades. Regarding humanitarian assistance, 3% of host community households and a significantly higher 32% of FDMN households reported receiving various types of assistance in the 12 months before data collection. Shelter types significantly impact living conditions. In the host community, 51% have ""Jhuprie"" houses constructed with earth, bamboo, wood, and CGI sheets or thatch, while 41% of FDMN households have ""Kutcha"" shelters made of branches, bags, tarpaulin, jute, etc. Among FDMN households not improving their shelters (41%), the majority cited a lack of humanitarian support (60%) or insufficient support (34%). Commonly, refugee households in camps 20 (59%), 18 (58%), 8W (56%), and 4 (55%) reported these reasons for not improving their shelters. For the 44% of host community households not improving their shelters, reasons included not being needed (45%) or lacking funds (43%). Safety concerns were evident, with 15% of the host community feeling unsafe walking alone at night, contrasting with a more pronounced sentiment among the FDMN community at 42%. Some households mentioned paying for guards at night as an expenditure. Top reasons for shelters not being fully functional for sleeping included rainwater leaking in the sleeping area for both host community (87%) and FDMN (69%). Insufficient space was also notable (42%) among FDMN households. Concerning cooking facilities, 93% of FDMN households reported rainwater leaking in the kitchen area, compared to 66% in the host community. Lack of access to cooking facilities was reported by 34% of FDMN households. In terms of electricity, 90% of FDMN households reported issues with intermittent and insufficient electricity, while 66% of the host community faced the same issue. Additionally, 35% of the host community reported having no electricity and no solar lamp. Despite challenges, 54% of host community households expressed a need for solar lamps/panels, and 43% of FDMN households had similar needs. Regarding lighting sources, the majority of host community households used the electrical grid (95%), while FDMN households primarily used a solar home system (69%), followed by solar-powered lanterns or flashlights (11%), and electricity, including solar mini-grids (9%). Both population groups faced challenges with their shelters, with 72% of refugees and 60% of the host community reporting issues. Significantly, 56% of the host community and 59% of refugees reported having taken steps to improve their shelters over the 12 months preceding data collection. However, there were concerns about communication and feedback mechanisms within the refugee population. Only half of refugee households were reportedly aware of a feedback and complaint mechanism. Among those who made complaints, two-thirds reported that no action was taken in response, highlighting potential gaps in the feedback and response system within the refugee community. This underscores the importance of improving communication channels and addressing concerns promptly to enhance the effectiveness of humanitarian assistance.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

The shelters we provide are meticulously designed for structural safety, utilizing materials resistant to rapid deterioration. Situated on sites shielded from disasters, these shelters are thoughtfully located for easy accessibility to water points, local markets, and essential services. Our commitment to safety, dignity, and harm prevention is paramount in our approach. We prioritize inclusivity by ensuring free access for all age groups in shelter construction, implementation, and monitoring. Engaging with beneficiaries is at the heart of our strategy, involving them in need assessments, site development, lightweight works, and shelter construction wherever feasible. For individuals with disabilities (PWD), our engagement extends to identifying and mapping potential risks and environmental disasters. Through collaborative efforts, we strive to create shelters that not only provide physical protection but also empower and cater to the unique needs of each beneficiary.

2.3 - Age & Disability

The project ensures the meaningful inclusion of persons with disabilities (PWD) and diverse age groups in all activities, targeting at least 10% of beneficiaries as PWD. Shelters will be designed with accessibility features such as ramps, wider doorways, and firm, stable pathways to accommodate individuals with mobility challenges. Strategic site locations will ensure proximity to essential services like water points, local markets, playgrounds, and child-friendly spaces. To achieve disability inclusion, we will conduct need assessments in collaboration with PWD and their caregivers to identify barriers and tailor interventions. These assessments will inform shelter design and site development, ensuring accessibility and safety. PWD will be engaged in lightweight works and construction activities suitable to their abilities, enabling their active participation. Focus group discussions (FGDs) will be conducted to address unique needs and provide insights into risk mitigation for all age groups. Special attention will be given to gender considerations. Beneficiary selection will prioritize vulnerable groups such as women-headed households, widows, elder women, single mothers, and families with disabled members. Gender-sensitive FGDs and one-on-one sessions will address the unique challenges of women and girls, particularly those with disabilities, ensuring their active involvement in project design, implementation, and monitoring. Staff will receive training on age and disability inclusion, focusing on safeguarding principles. Visual reminders and orientation sessions will reinforce adherence to child safeguarding policies and protection measures. By adopting these measures, the project aligns with the JRP Age and Disability guidelines to create an inclusive, accessible, and safe environment for all beneficiaries.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

We will establish a robust Complaints and Feedback Mechanism (CFM) to address concerns and gather valuable feedback from beneficiaries. Our dedicated CFM personnel will be actively present at project sites, facilitating direct communication through face-to-face interactions, phone calls, SMS, and written correspondence with project staff and designated CFRM/Monitoring Focal Points.A comprehensive need assessment will be conducted in consultation with beneficiaries prior to the project design, ensuring that their needs and priorities are at the core of the initiative. GUSS will then disseminate an active information-sharing plan through its health post in Camp 1 (E) and an information booth at the RRRC Office. Detailed selection criteria for beneficiaries will also be shared transparently to promote clarity and trust. Regular communication with the community on actions taken in response to concerns gathered through the CFRM will be a key focus, fostering a continuous feedback loop. Additionally, GUSS will hold monthly evaluation consultations and feedback sessions with beneficiaries to assess program progress and incorporate lessons learned into future activities. A register will be maintained throughout. Throughout the project life cycle, the participation of affected individuals will be ensured, adhering to inclusive principles in design, implementation, and monitoring. Beneficiaries will be engaged in need assessments, shelter construction, site development, and various project stages, supported by satisfaction surveys to drive continuous improvement. Information disclosure will follow GUSS’s transparent Information Disclosure Policy, with IEC materials providing clear details of the assistance being offered. By implementing these measures, GUSS will ensure accountability and inclusivity while maintaining a high standard of program quality.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

Global Unnayan Seba Sangstha (GUSS) integrates Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) as a core principle across all project activities, ensuring a secure and respectful environment for stakeholders. Guided by our Gender and PSEA Policies, we implement targeted measures to prevent and address SEA risks while fostering accountability and community trust. Mandatory PSEA training will be conducted for all staff, volunteers, implementing partners (IPs), vendors, and third parties involved in the project. These sessions will cover prevention strategies, reporting mechanisms, and appropriate responses to SEA incidents. In addition, PSEA awareness sessions for beneficiaries will provide accessible information about SEA risks, their rights, and available support systems, creating a knowledgeable and vigilant community. Community consultations will focus on Complaint Feedback and Response Mechanisms (CFRM), SEA risk identification, and preferred communication channels for reporting concerns. The feedback collected will inform project-specific PSEA strategies, ensuring they are responsive to the needs and contexts of the community. We will develop and distribute Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) materials on PSEA, using visually clear and culturally sensitive content to ensure accessibility for all, including vulnerable groups. These materials will emphasize recognizing, reporting, and preventing SEA risks and will be widely disseminated across project locations. Robust and confidential reporting mechanisms will include suggestion boxes, hotline numbers, and face-to-face interactions with trained focal points to empower beneficiaries to safely report incidents. These measures will ensure accountability and build community trust.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Yes, our project is committed to environmental sustainability and climate action at its core. We ensure zero harm to the environment by prioritizing the preservation of trees, plants, and the natural landscape. Our project includes a comprehensive impact assessment, factoring in social, cultural, and environmental aspects to guide sustainable practices. To strengthen resilience against natural disasters, we use high-quality, sustainable materials such as treated bamboo, tarpaulin, and metal footings in construction. Site development activities will reduce vulnerability to landslides, earthquakes, and other natural hazards.Aligned with a robust Environment Policy, our project promotes practices like high daylight consumption, resource recycling, discouraging deforestation, reducing CO2 emissions, and fostering environmental awareness. We adhere to the Energy and Environment Technical Working Group (EETWG) Strategy for Climate Action in Cox’s Bazar (2023-2025), particularly complementing Objective 2.1: "The natural land and water resource base within the camps is maintained post-restoration, and new site development and shelter works adopt nature-based and sustainable solutions." Additionally, we will coordinate with EETWG to ensure that our solar lamp NFI distributions align with technical specifications for environmental sustainability. We are also committed to sourcing shelter and NFI materials locally as much as possible, further supporting the local economy and reducing transportation-related carbon emissions. Through these measures, GUSS will uphold its commitment to climate action, sustainable development, and environmental protection throughout the project lifecycle.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

The organization supports affected families during emergencies, such as cyclones, flash floods, or floods, by providing essential aid including food, hygiene supplies, and non-food items (NFIs) both during and after disasters. If an emergency occurs while a project is underway, the organization will extend support to those impacted, provided funding is available. Additionally, GUSS responds to extreme weather events, such as cold waves, by distributing warm blankets and clothing to those in need.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

GUSS will actively engage youth (15-24 years old) as volunteers in certain project activities, providing them with meaningful opportunities to contribute to their communities. Youth volunteers will play a key role in conducting awareness sessions on safety, hygiene, and shelter usage, tailored to the needs of their peers and families. The older individuals in this age bracket will assist in shelter construction tasks, such as assembling parts or preparing materials. Additionally, youth will help organize and manage queues during NFI distribution, ensuring smooth and orderly processes while assisting elderly and disabled beneficiaries as needed.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

Our projects will support self-reliance and resilience by actively involving community members as paid volunteers. These volunteers will help identify beneficiaries and play key roles in core project activities like distribution, maintaining order, and even assisting with shelter construction and repairs. By hiring directly from the community, we not only ensure the smooth implementation of the project but also empower refugees with job skills and a sense of ownership, thereby strengthening their resilience and fostering a collaborative environment.

4.2 Do project activities in the host community link to development initiatives, and/or align with development priorities in the Cox’s Bazar district? If yes, briefly describe.If yes, briefly describe.

By constructing new shelters and providing essential NFIs for the host community, we aim to strengthen local infrastructure and support vulnerable populations, enhancing overall resilience. Our distribution of solar lights and warm clothing also addresses energy needs and basic living conditions, which are priorities in local development frameworks and contribute to sustainable community growth in Ukhiya.
