Project Summary

With an estimated 54% of the population in crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity, amongst whom an estimated 1.7m people are facing Emergency (IPC Phase 4), high levels of food insecurity continue to persist in South Sudan during the 2019 lean season as compared to the same period in 2018. Persistent acute food insecurity is attributed to unusually high food prices, localized conflicts, climate shocks, slow recovery from prolonged asset depletion, limited access to markets and high number of returnees from Sudan and Ethiopia, putting additional pressure on available resources and livelihoods.
Between January and April 2020, 14 counties are projected to be in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity: Jonglei State is expected to have the highest number of people (1.18 million) estimated to face Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity, followed by former Upper Nile State (765,000 persons). Lack of roads and basic infrastructure for accessing essential services and commuting as well as continued insecurity related to inter-communal conflicts, cattle raiding, child kidnapping, particularly in Nasir, Ulang, Maiwut and Longechuk Counties of Upper Nile State, have significantly disrupted access to markets and fishing. While the operational environment in targeted areas remains challenging, life-saving support in key sectors is critical for internally displaced persons (IDP), refugee, returnees and host communities. RI’s proposed program, “Food assistance, production and strengthening ability shocks” aims to deliver lifesaving humanitarian assistance through provision of food assistance that meets short term hunger gaps while helping households build longer term food security and resilience against future shocks and stresses; prioritizing cash in areas with functional markets and in-kind transfers where cash programming is not appropriate. The proposed program is expected to enable the most vulnerable to address immediate food needs and reduce negative coping mechanisms, thus addressing protection concerns while preventing malnutrition and further depletion of productive assets. A multi-sectoral model will be applied, integrating livelihoods, nutrition sensitive food security and dietary diversity and resilience building interventions while addressing hygiene and safe sanitation and mainstreaming protection. Further, the proposed program aims to contribute to Food Security and Livelihoods cluster objectives - CO1, CO2, and CO3 that are aligned with the SDG 2 “End Hunger - achieve food security and improved nutrition, and promote sustainable agriculture.”
The program will target vulnerable populations, especially women, girls, elderly, disabled, PLW, children U5, HIV & the chronically sick amongst IDPs, refugee, returnees or host communities. RI is well placed to implement the above program as it has been operating in the location since 9 years, having established a strong working relationship with local authorities, built trust with communities, delivered inclusive and impactful programs that mainstream gender and protection, aiming to providing quality services amongst the most vulnerable and marginalized populations. RI is an active member of national and county level coordination mechanisms and ensures community participation in all phases of the program cycle. The project will enable RI to strengthen its emergency food security and livelihoods activities to deliver integrated emergency assistance to vulnerable populations while strengthening synergies with its health and WASH activities being implemented in the proposed target areas. WFP supported GFD in Longechuk, TSFP and IFP support in Maiwut and Nasir will further help RI address food security and nutrition concerns amongst vulnerable groups. RI’s integrated approach-health, nutrition and WASH, supported by OFDA, BPRM, UNHCR, UNICEF, WFP and FAO, will strengthen synergies the impact of the proposed project in the same locations.

1 - Needs

The September 2019 IPC report, Longechuk, Maiwut and Ulang were in the emergency phase 4 (for both food security and nutrition) and are projected to remain. Based on a number of reports and assessments, including RI’s food security and recent baseline assessments, SMART surveys, observations and consultations with the community and local authorities, the identified needs include restricted food access to limited livelihoods options amongst IDPs, refugee, returnees and host community. Overall, an estimated 655,000 people, representing 47.8 percent of the population in the former Upper Nile state will be in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) and Emergency (IPC Phase 4) during the harvest period. Greater Upper Nile hosts 60% of all IDPs in South Sudan, with Ulang County alone hosting close to 10,000 IDPs (outh Sudan Humanitarian Needs Overview 2019). Women and children are disproportionally affected by the crisis, representing 85% of the IDP population. With many having reported negative coping strategies, including selling their assets and reducing daily meal consumption per person, IDPs, refugee and returnees are particularly vulnerable, In Maban, refugees rely heavily on humanitarian agencies for provision of life-saving and basic services. Fragile relations with host communities results in precarious security which is exacerbated by hostile local government authorities. Ethno-political tensions remain high among refugees owing to violent inter-ethnic and tribal clashes in May 2017, which resulted in segregation of refugee camps along ethnic lines which further complicates the operational environment. Cohesion amongst refugees and host communities in Maban is weak and often tense, particularly concerning land use, natural resources, firewood collection, and access to social services, family disputes, and overstretched absorption capacity of conflict- affected communities. Malnutrition rates are high, being attributed to high levels of food insecurity, poor infant and young child feeding practices as well as high morbidity due to a weak health system in the country. The June 2018 SMART survey conducted in Maiwut County revealed a GAM rate of 10% (classified as serious) aggravated by poor health and food security, SAM rate as 2.4%, measles vaccination coverage among children 9 to 59 months of age at only 7.2%, vitamin A supplement coverage at 5.7%, and deworming at 3.4% – resulting in high morbidity rate in a county whereas malaria is endemic. GAM rates are expected to increase in the event of the above contributing factors not being addressed. In Nasir, the GAM rate is 17.2% and SAM rate is 3.3% (classified by WHO as emergency). In Ulang, the GAM rate is 14.5% and SAM is 6.1% - exceeding the WHO threshold of 2.5%. In Maban, nutrition situation remains severe with a GAM rate of 10.2%, and SAM at 2.8% also above 2.5% WHO’s emergency threshold; exclusive breastfeeding rate of 31.9% in the Maban host community. Although the SMART Survey and IPC show a minor improvement of overall acute malnutrition rates as compared to the same period last year, current rates are still above WHO thresholds. With conflict continuing, the key drivers of food insecurity, including high food prices, restrictions on safe movements, and below average agricultural sector labor opportunities are likely to continue impacting food security outcomes. Poor households will likely face further food consumption gaps as they exhaust coping strategies and livelihoods disruptions continue. The recent heavy rains coupled with flood across targeted counties have inundated shelters, water sources, crops, and essential service infrastructure crucial productive assets and livestock replacements. The recent floods in Maban have rendered many roads impassable, isolating communities, loss of productive assets and cultivated land, restricting people’s access to markets and humanitarian assistance, contaminating water and impacting people’s health and nutrition

6.6 - Will beneficiaries' rights be legally recognised?

Based on the sphere and protection standards the beneficiaries rights to humanitarian assistance will be observed during the whole project cycle. The proposed project incorporates community feedback and complaint mechanisms, including: complaint desks at distribution sites, anonymous and non-anonymous reporting mechanisms to a focal person at each of RI's field offices, post distribution monitoring (PDM), Ad-hoc supervisory and beneficiary feedback meetings conducted by senior monitoring staff. PSEA is mainstreamed across RI's policies and programming. Populations will be informed of SEA impacts and levels of reporting mechanisms, anonymous and non-anonymous through AAP messaging. In RI, SEA is considered to be gross misconduct and calls for immediate termination of staff.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

RI engages with communities through the program cycle, especially through its awareness building activities, specialized health and nutrition consultations, house visits and household level interactions. RI also conducts regular meetings with key stakeholders and wider community that are not necessarily its direct beneficiaries, informal women’s groups, water user committees, farmers/livestock breeder groups, project management committees, at distribution sites for livelihoods kits and food, and through training. Community elders, leaders, key representatives, especially women and youth, are consulted on a regular basis for feedback on programming, quality of services and staff performance. Coordination meetings with national and international NGOs at Juba and county level permit RI to keep abreast of local updates, have a deeper insight into community needs, challenges and negative coping mechanisms so that these are addressed through programming and advocacy and help communities live safe and dignified lives. The project is founded on various reports and assessments, including RI’s food security assessments, SMART surveys, observations and consultations, both with men and women, RI identified widespread needs regarding access to food, and limited economic opportunities and livelihoods, among IDPs, returnees and vulnerable host community members. Needs identified during the baseline assessment and consultation with vulnerable population has informed this project design.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

This approach will promote inclusive and equitable policies, structures and services, particularly for women, youth and other marginalized groups. Community structures such as farmers groups, and VSLA groups will continue to learn from one another and seek assistance from local technical extension. 6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

The interventions are aligned to the overall humanitarian needs of the vulnerable population. To ensure triangulation and designing of appropriate interventions, RI conducted literature reviews and consulted local authorities in targeted counties. Equally important, the proposed RI Food Security and livelihoods project is aligned with the South Sudan National objective and South Sudan Food Security and livelihoods Cluster 2020 Strategic objectives and will directly contribute to the 2020 South Sudan Humanitarian Response Plan. The integrated approach will address high levels of malnutrition (SAM and AMN) among the children and their families. Malnutrition, negatively impacts household productivity and can reduce incomes directly through healthcare-related expenses. As gender based violence is pervasive, the project ensure that gender issues are addressed through mainstreaming gender equality across age groups.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

The protection environment is volatile and not sufficiently stable or safe, especially for girls, women and children as well as people with disabilities. Safety risks are expected from the distance for distribution points that potentially expose children and women to violence and insecurity from people and wild animals. All these risks will be addressed through working with all relevant stakeholders to identify central distribution areas and ensuring that protection, safety and security measures are put in place prior to distribution. All project interventions will be inclusive for differently vulnerable populations especially women, girls, men & boys, elderly, disabled, PLW, children U5, HIV & the chronically sick staying in either IDPs, refugee, returnees or host communities. RI will mainstream protection by supporting integrity, dignity, and safety for all targeted populations. Women and children will be prioritized and seek counsel during the implementation process. Protection will be supported through women and youth consultations and representatives, targeting the most vulnerable, and support rights-based and participatory approaches. Awareness building exercise on protection concerns will be conducted before the needs assessment so that communities make informed decisions on location of project intervention. RI developed community feedback mechanism whereby protection issues will be collected and facilities, services and assistance will be used to mitigate and improve the protection environment.

8.18 - Please indicate how the risks selected above will be mitigated.

Key marginalized stakeholders such as women, youth, elders, informal groups will be specifically consulted, and their awareness raised in order to hold informed consultations and bring out concerns. Targeting criteria, beneficiary numbers, expected norms of engaging, define outputs per modality and benefits will clear. The roles and of each stakeholder will be developed during inception meeting and adhered to during project implementation, monitoring and evaluation that will enhance learning and improvement among all concerned. The project will address ‘Do no Harm’ to ensure that interventions do not contribute to intra and inter-communal tensions by affected population. The project considers specific protection needs of all population groups and the modality to address them. Rights awareness messaging will be disseminated prior to all distributions, trainings and through PMCs. Referral pathways for SGBV victims and survivors will be established and utilized for case management. Self-reported cases of SBGV will be referred to local protection partners for care and case management. A security lens will be applied to targeting of communities and households in order to mitigate any potential conflict related to program exclusion. Every RI office has an appointed security focal point who advises, develops, and updates local security procedures for the protection of staff and beneficiaries. Also, RI receive information regarding the security situation through the UNDSS and the International NGO Security Forum. RI will use these resources to keep its staff, beneficiaries and assets safe. RI utilizes community mobilizers as well as mobile teams to reach the most vulnerable where RI may not be able to reach. RI regularly engages in RRMs to reach IDPs and returnees - where static services may be inaccessible. Moreover, in all operational field sites RI maintains means of movement including boats, motorbikes, donkey carts, and vehicles to reach all targeted beneficiaries

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

Community based protection is an essential component of RI’s human security work. Human security recognizes that vulnerable people are exposed to a constant series of threats and risks and focuses on need to retain a minimum quality of life defined as freedom from fear, freedom from want and freedom to take action on one’s own behalf. RI acknowledges the importance of these rights; all its programs are designed to empower the poor, vulnerable and excluded to exercise their rights and acquire a voice, to emphasize the need for protection and respect and assist the most vulnerable by increasing their safety with dignity, by developing local prevention and response strategies and organizing their resources to reduce exposure to harm. For example; RI will go directly to the communities to deliver the vouchers and where necessary; female staff will deliver vouchers and provide community sensitization and outreach to female beneficiaries; where required, the disabled and elderly will be assisted to use the vouchers/cash. RI will engage with community leaders at each step and will invite women’s open participation to voice their views and concerns in case of dissatisfaction. The proposed project incorporates community feedback and complaint mechanisms, including: complaint desks at distribution sites, anonymous and non-anonymous reporting mechanisms to a focal person at each of RI's field offices, post distribution monitoring (PDM), Ad-hoc supervisory and beneficiary feedback meetings conducted by senior monitoring staff.
