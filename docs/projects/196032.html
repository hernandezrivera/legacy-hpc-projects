Project Summary

UNICEF education will support access to inclusive early childhood development, pre-primary, primary and secondary education, and skills development opportunities for 244,247 children (51% girls) refugee crisis affected children, and adolescents aged 3-18 years, including 200,067 in the camps and 44,180 in Teknaf and Ukhyia upazilas of Cox’s Bazar District. UNICEF will work with partners to improve enrolment, retention and inclusive learning environment for girls, boys, (with about 2 per cent of the targeted population being children with disabilities). UNICEF in partnership with HI will ensure that inclusion and disability mainstreaming is done across all partners as well as capacitating teachers and communities on how to identify and support children with disabilities and assistive devices provided for. The project will address gender issues by ensuring that girls have better access to learning centers through the girls only sessions, especially from grade 6 onwards where normally girls drop out as they reach puberty.
In the camps, around 2,800 learning centres will be supported by UNICEF to provide access to progressive learning in safe and protective environment. The capacity of teachers, technical officers and programme officers will be built to enable them to effectively support the delivery of improved learning for 184,320 (50% girls) children aged 3-18 years using the Myanmar curriculum for KG, grades 1,2, 6, 7, 8 and 9. While the Learning Competency Framework and Approach (LCFA) level 3 will end in June 2023 resulting in grade 3, 4 and 5 using the Myanmar curriculum & grade 10. Participation in an accelerated learning programme (ALP) will only be available for children who are significantly too old to be in Myanmar Curriculum grade classes, with the intention of moving children back into different grades within the formal national Myanmar Curriculum as soon as they have made sufficient progress. ALP will be provided on a needs basis also considering the lack of classroom space and required ALP training for teachers by the curriculum providers. To ensure that children have quality education, UNICEF supports capacity building of partners and teachers/facilitators on inclusive education, basic pedagogy, subject knowledge, assessment, safeguarding & PSEA. This includes rolling out Teacher Professional Development (TPD) programmes to improve the quality of teaching and learning. To ensure social cohesion and conflict sensitive programming, UNICEF engages & trains teachers from the host community and Rohingya camps as part of the teaching force. The use of education technology solutions will enhance the delivery of education services, including community engagement and administration, as well as contributing to TPD in the camps. UNICEF will also continue to support out-of-school adolescents and youth through cross-sectoral interventions in Multi-Purpose Centres (MPCs) across the camps. Through these interventions, OOS adolescents aged 15 to 18 years will be provided with life & softskills, literacy and numeracy to build their resilience. Youth aged 19-24 also receive TVET training in partnership with the Child Protection team. These skill programmes help to deconstruct gender norms and highlight the values, experience and knowledge of females and males.
In the host communities in Teknaf and Ukhyia, UNICEF will support about 44,180 children, adolescents, and youth, including those with disabilities, to access quality primary education as well as vocational skills. A total of 142 schools with an enrollment of about 44,000 children will benefit from the School Effectiveness grants provided by UNICEF. In addition, support to host community will focus on out-of-school adolescent and youth for vocational skills training and children in non-formal education (ABAL). UNICEF also works closely with the government and ministries at a variety of level, including institutional capacity strengthening in Cox's Bazar district.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

In Rohingya refugee camps, girls’ access to education remains low, especially for adolescent girls due to prevalent socio-cultural attitudes and norms which are linked to puberty and menstruation. Only 3% of girls aged 15 to 18 reported having attended learning centers (LCs) compared to 28% of boys of the same age group. Access to education worsened during the pandemic, leading to two years of unprecedented changes in learning access and delivery of education (J-MSNA 2020). All learning centers in camps and in host communities, as measure to curb the spread of COVID-19, were closed from March 2020 to September 2021, resulting in lost learning time and seriously disrupting children's education. There is a need to maximise classroom and community facility spaces, to ensure access to education for all children and to motivate skilled individuals to rejoin or enter teaching, providing them with the capacity building, skills and renumeration they need to do this. Lack of learning materials, especially new Myanmar Curriculum Textbooks, Teachers’ Guides, Student's workbooks, and teacher’s guides for the Accelerated Learning Program for the Overaged learner and guidance from teachers are key challenges in learning continuity. In the Rohingya refugee camps there has been some success with recruiting higher percentages of female teachers, with 56 per cent of teachers being female. However, most female teachers are Bangladeshis from the host community, with female Rohingya teachers comprising only 25 per cent of the total teaching force. This presents a challenge for the roll out of the Myanmar curriculum (see below), with greater numbers of female teachers with sufficient Myanmar language skills needing to be recruited and trained to create a more protective environment for girls. Opportunities to participate in quality education and complete primary and secondary cycles provides children with improved futures including in socio-economic indicators, livelihood options and greater resilence. Increasing the quality of education and the engagement & involvment of parents is critical to increase demand and supply. Protection trends for adolescent girls suggest that social and religious barriers restrict the mobility of girls in public once they reach adolescence & increase the risk of early marriage. This contributes to social isolation and exclusion, low levels of foundational literacy, psychological distress and protection risks. It has been found that parents are more likely to send their girls to education facilities if there are female teachers, sex-separated classrooms and female monitors to accompany the girls to/from school. In Cox’s Bazar district is also one of the low performing districts in Bangladesh in terms of access to education and learners’ performance, with the lowest Net Enrolment Rate (NER) (91.39%) in Bangladesh . School enrolment rate of children with disabilities is concerning low (1400 students) in Cox’s Bazar. Teachers and school management committees are not equipped with right sets of skills and knowledge for inclusive education. The primary survival completion rate is between 74.10- 78.60% in Cox’s Bazar , with an overall student dropout rate of around 22%. The latest national student assessment results for Grades 3 and 5, which covered Bangla and mathematics, showed the performance of students in Cox’s Bazar was among the lowest in Bangladesh, with the combined mean score of 233 points as compared to the national average of 274 points. According to the Annual Sector Performance Report (ASPR) 2021, Cox’s Bazar also has the lowest Net Enrolment Rate (NER) (91.39%) in Bangladesh. 2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

UNICEF and partners will work to increase operational efficiency by allocating each block to one partner per technical area. Meaning there shouldn't be two providers for ECD in one block, two providers of Myanmar Curriculum in the same block or two providers for youth and adolescent programming (15-18) per block, which is delivered by UNICEF in Multi-Purpose Centres (MPCs). If possible, UNICEF will consider the capacity of the partner to manage and provide quality holistic education services from ECD, to Myanmar Curriculum to vocational skills training but most of our partners do not have this capacity and are CSO partners with UNICEF due to their expertise in certain types of education programming. UNICEF is also the only provider of formal secondary education, necessitating ongoing presence in most camps. Linked to this, UNICEF expects to move to new camps or blocks next year, as the number of secondary children and need for secondary classrooms will increase from June/July 2023 as LCFA Level III ends and children are tested and placed into Grade 6, as well as children currently in MCP transitioning into the next academic year & the start of MC Grade 10 classes. Many meetings held with UNHCR for rationalisation to agree an arrangement where one UN partner leads for different camps (whilst maintaining the same number of LCs and beneficiary reach). Priority will be given to continued and sustained quality education in the camps and host communities. For the JRP 2023, UNICEF will reach in the refugee community 25% of children aged 3, 30% of children aged 4, 66% of children aged 5-14, 25% of children aged 15-18 and 3% of young people aged 19-24. UNICEF's coverage across the camps is 66% with over 2800 learning centres in JRP 2022 which will be maintained. For an integrated approach, we will continue to work with our Child Protection team in MPCs, with shared partners to ensure protection measures and referrals across the learning centres.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection

UNICEF engages with the Education Sector, Protection Sector, Child Protection Sub-Sector, and other stakeholders, including the humanitarian and development sectors in Cox’s Bazar to ensure accessible reporting and referral mechanisms are in place to support survivor-informed response to incident notifications. UNICEF has a PSEA unit in Cox's Bazar and our partners are assessed regularly on PSEA and have action plans to strengthen children's protection (including awareness, mitigation, response and reporting). Protection mainstreaming within the education programme focuses on the 4 key elements of prioritising safety and dignity, promoting equitable and impartial access to all, ensuring accountability and strengthening participation and empowerment. A few examples include providing a secure learning environment; reducing the risk of harm to girls and female teachers; promoting equitable and impartial access to all; and ensuring affected populations know their right to education

3.2 - Gender

Strong focus on girls' education will be ensured through awareness raising and sensitization of parents and traditional leaders; scale-up of girls-only sessions for older year classes & female guardians to accompany girls' to school and remain with them in classes; gender-segregated water, sanitation and hygiene facilities and menstrual hygiene management; recruiting and capacity building for female Rohingya teachers and; developing and sharing gender-sensitive teaching and learning materials with caregivers, teachers and students. We will focus on inclusion of 3% children with disabilities, including outreach in communities to identify children with disabilities, working with the Washington Group questions to identify assistive devices to support that child's education, and continuous dialogue with the child and their parents to encourage them to enrol and attend school. Interventions as teacher training in materials development to support inclusion for children will be mainstreamed

3.3 - Age including child safeguarding

A zero-tolerance policy and approach to exploitation and is in place to protect children, adolescents and adults from sexual exploitation, abuse and sexual harassment. These policies also apply to partners who support the implementation of programmes in support of UNICEF. This includes ensuring access to incident reporting mechanisms, affording protection to individuals under whistleblower policies and referring survivors to appropriate assistance services in line with the UN Victim Assistance Protocol including MHPSS and health services. At each partner, CSP focal persons are nominated and trained to provide support and respond effectively. At the LC level all teachers, children, community members and volunteers have access to different reporting mechanisms to report concerns (like phone hotline, community (teacher) focal points, NGO focal points, feedback boxes). UNICEF has dedicated PSEA and Safeguarding Unit, as well as an education team focal points to support partners

3.4 - Diversity

UNICEF supports and promotes diversity in all our programmes and among our staff. The UNICEF is committed to including everyone, irrespective of their gender identity, nationality, ethnicity, age, dis/ability, sexual orientation, religion, socio-economic background or any other personal characteristic. This includes in the selection of teachers where both male and female from Rohingya and Bangladesh community are given equal opportunities for engagement and in the composition of Parent Groups in the camps. UNICEF also works with Humanity & Inclusion (Handicap Interntional) to support children with disabilities in the camps, including with their access, participation and retention in learning.

3.5 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

UNICEF’s work in the context of the Rohingya response, as with all emergency contexts, is informed by the Core Commitments to Children in Humanitarian Action, Accountability to Affected Population (AAP) and Building Back Better principles. To ensure AAP, UNICEF has an active feedback and complaints mechanisms at each of LC where suggestion/feedback boxes are used by children, teachers and community members. For transparency and accountability, the boxes are opened by both teachers and community representatives together. A free hotline number is also publicly displayed at each LC. UNICEF conducts regular field visits and 3rd party monitoring to ensure the quality-of-service delivery and AAP while analyses of feedback from communities is also done. Further, we conduct focus group discussions with children, and caregivers to understand issues affecting children, particularly those with disabilities and incorporate findings into programming.

3.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Yes. UNICEF and partners promote and ensure emergency and disaster preparedness in the camps through training community and parent support groups as well as teachers and children on DRR. This includes the creation of community led DRR plans in learning centres which are functional, sustainable and locally owned. This in turn will reduce the impact of the disasters such as fires and floods which are common in the Rohingya camps. Within the education team of UNICEF, there is a construction engineer who ensures LCs are built from sustainable and durable materials which withstand the impacts of climate change as much as possible. UNICEF also leads the DRR working group within the education sector.
