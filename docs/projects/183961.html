Project Summary

Sudan is currently estimated to host more than one million refugees and asylum-seekers from Chad,
the Central African Republic, the Democratic Republic of Congo. Eritrea, Ethiopia, Somalia, South
Sudan, Syria, and Yemen.1 Most of the refugee population is currently living outside of official camps
in remote and poorly developed locations with limited access to goods and services. Meanwhile,
those residing in camp settlements are provided with modest assistance, which may not meet the
basic needs. In 2022 ADRA aims at continuing in its efforts to provide multi-sectoral and integrated assistance with an increased attention to durable solutions and building community resilience. ADRA will continue its efforts in targeting refugees in Sudan focusing in three main locations, Khartoum, White Nile and Blue Nile. The project aims at providing at improving the well-being for refugee populations in Sudan through integrated and tailored response as well as increasing advocacy efforts with government and donors for long-term solutions. The project will target over 120,000 refugees with tailored and multi-sector assistance.
White Nile currently holds the largest number of South Sudanese Refugees in all Sudan, with 276, 070 Refugees across the camps and over 87,000 out of camp refugees out of which 55% are females and 44% children. The situation in White Nile poses a challenge as the needs are diverse and range from protracted to emergency with the new arrivals. ADRA’s refugee response in White Nile State will encompass multi-purpose cash assistance, that will be tied closely with protection response food security and livelihood assistance to both in camp and out of camp refugees. The project will improve WASH in the camps through the rehabilitation and construction of water points, as well as sanitation facilities, both communal and at household level. In addition to that ADRA will increase tailored awareness messages for refugees on hygiene and sanitation, as well as provide NFI to the most vulnerable households and new arrivals. As part of ADRA’s Global Education strategy ADRA’s out of camp assessment has highlighted the high educational needs for out of camp refugees. The project will aim at providing EiE training and assistance to out of camp refugees in Kosti, Rabak, Aljabaleen and Alsalam.
In Blue Nile, ADRA also aims at a multi-sector response to the Ethiopian Refugees in Wad Almahi camp. This will address the Shelter, WASH, FSL and protection needs in the camp.
As a long term actor in Khartoum, especially for development programing and in order to address the heightened needs of urban refugee population, ADRA will design tailored approaches to meet the specific needs of the different refugee populations. The project will priorities protection as well as multipurpose cash assistance for refugees in urban populations. The project will target the most Jabal Awlya, Um bada and East Nile refugee populations. A do no harm’s approach will be applied, where ADRA’s current development programing will also target the host populations in those areas.

1 - Needs

As of Sept 2021, Sudan hosts an estimated 1,111,1329 refugees and asylum-seekers from the Central African Republic, Chad, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Somalia, South Sudan, Syria, Yemen, among others. The main refugee-hosting states are Khartoum, White Nile, Kassala, South and East Darfur, as well as West and South Kordofan. South Sudanese refugees are the largest refugee population, with a recorded number of 786,524 refugees living across all states. Most of the current overall refugee population (70%) are living outside of official camps. Out-of-camp settlements include large collective self-settlements, communities that are integrated with the host community, and urban areas. Many out-of-camp settlements are in remote and underdeveloped areas where resources, infrastructure, and basic services are extremely limited. In the camps, the assistance provided is modest and, in some cases, does not meet minimum living standards. Over 268,335 of them are hosted in White Nile State (UNCHR Dec. 2020 & Jan. 2021). 55 % of the South Sudanese Refugee (SSR) families are led by females and 1 % are led by children. 2 % of the registered population is living with disabilities and close to 12,000 children are currently unaccompanied and separated from their family (UNHCR Dec. 2020). The current conditions in South Sudan are not conducive for large-scale voluntary, safe, informed, dignified nor sustainable return. As in other parts of the continent, prices for food and basic commodities are rising, posing a potentially devastating threat to millions of refugees who are already struggling due to precariously unstable sources of income, with most of them depending on hand outs or seasonal daily labor opportunities. As a result of these challenges, many refugees are resorting to negative coping mechanisms, such as reducing meal portions, generate debt to buy food and selling off household assets to provide for their families’ most acute needs. More than 80% of refugees in South Sudan are estimated to be resorting to such measures. In some cases, refugees are resorting to begging, transactional sex, or early or forced marriages to be able to afford food In the refugee camps in While Nile State and surrounding host communities, families have been hit hard by the absence of long-term socio-economic opportunities, coupled with a country-wide economic crisis. It is currently preventing those most vulnerable from rebuilding their lives, meeting their basic needs, and strengthening community-based protection. 95 % of the surveyed 3000 households in Khor-Alwarel, Al-Jameya and Alkashafa stated that the COVID-19 pandemic has caused a loss of income. 39 % reported a loss in income between 25 – 50 % while 25 % lost from 50 – 100 % of their income. Only 68 families (2%) currently had breadwinners working enabling their families to supplement the limited aid that is currently being delivered for SSRs in the three camps. 65 % of families have a monthly income of less than 1000 SDG. Out of the 3000 surveyed families more than 83,66 % were not able to reach an acceptable food consumption score (FCS). 58,78% of families had a poor FCS. It must be noted that at the time of data collection only 180 families reported to have already received their monthly food distribution (provided by WFP) which can to some extend explain the high level of food insecurity reported. However, out of the 180 families who did receive their food packages, only 4 of them (2%) reached an acceptable FCS indicating that families are resorting to negative coping strategies to cover their most basic needs. According to the MSNA Dec. 2020 80 % (84 % for refugees in particular) of the 884 households surveyed struggled to meet their basic needs in the 30 days prior to data collection, a result that correlates with approximately 90 % of SSRs in the three camps in White Nile State responding that they are unable to meet their basic needs.

2 - Activities/Outputs

To address the needs of the diverse and acute needs of refugees ADRA will tailor it’s assistance towards new and protracted refugees, as well as urban (out of camp) and in camp refugees. The following project activities and outputs are anticipated.FSL: 1- Refugee population (urban and camp) receive non-conditional multi-purpose Cash assistance. ADRA will provide a minimum expenditure basket. 2- Providing vocational training and community saving schemes for urban refugees. 3- Provision of livelihood and vocational training to urban and in camp refugees. Literacy and Education: 1- Rehabilitation of schools, and provision of school supplies to in camp and out of camp refugee populations. 2- Literacy circles for urban refugee females. 3. Training of teachers in EiE. WASH: 1- Drilling of boar holes. 2- Provision of clean and safe water to refugee populations through rehabilitation of hand pumps and tap stands. 3- Installation of household level latrines. 4- Cash for work sanitation activities for cleaning of existing shared sanitation facilities. 5- Provision of hygiene information. 6- Provision of hygiene NFI. Shelter: 1- Emergency Shelter for incoming refugees. 2- NFI assistance to refugees. Protection, GBV & Child protection: 1- Community based protection groups for referral and community-based PSS. 2- PSS counselling. 3- Training and capacity building for service providers. 4- Increased advocacy and awareness raising. 5- Multi-purpose cash assistance and IPA.
