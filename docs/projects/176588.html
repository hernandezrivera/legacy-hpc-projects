Project Summary

The project aims to strengthen community and rights-based protection response for IDPs, IDP returnees and vulnerable persons within their host communities. The project will focus on providing physical and legal protection through advocacy, timely protection monitoring, life-saving protection response based on identified needs and solutions. The project will address Housing, Land and Property (HLP) rights issues through the provision of legal assistance, facilitate issuance of property and identity documents, and work with mediation mechanisms to support HLP related dispute resolution. Community-based networks will be utilized as part of the protection response to foster community buy in and ownership of protection. The project will use evidence-based planning to expand outreach and develop the capacity of displaced communities to cope, recover, contribute to and ultimately achieve solutions. Out of 1.6 million IDPs, 475,000 IDPs and IDP returnees will be supported with protection interventions and assistance in seven (7) states and an administrative areas. Recognising the protection concerns linked to food insecurity, the Agency activities also include a scaling-up of activities in South Sudan’s highly food insecure and prioritised locations, i.e. Pibor County, Akobo, Aweil South, Tonj East, Tonj North, Tonj South. There will be continuous advocacy and support in the area of law and policy with relevant government bodies such as the Ministries of Justice Home Affairs, Humanitarian Affairs and Disaster Management, and the Relief and Rehabilitation Commission (RRC) to ensure the centrality of protection, enact the “Protection and Assistance to Internally Displaced Persons Act 2018” (IDP) Bill, National Framework on Returns, and various HLP initiatives. The relevant line ministries will also receive capacity building and technical assistance. Building on UNHCR’s existing operational presence in Bentiu Bor, Juba, Malakal, Wau, Yambio, Yei, the Agency will continue to advance on the 2020 geographic expansion of IDP activities by leveraging both static and mobile presence through 15 partners to Central Equatoria, Unity, Upper Nile, Joinglei, Western Bahr El Ghazal, Northern Bahr El Ghazal, Lakes, Western Equatoira, Eastern Equatoris and Warrap. Response and monitoring will cover 19 identified priority counties and Pibor Administrative Area, including remote or hard to-reach areas, in full support to the Protection Cluster Strategy for mobile assessments and response. Partners comprise of both national and international organizations, with selection undertaken on the basis of their pre-existing presence, access to populations of concern and capacity to carry out static and remote protection monitoring and response. While not all proposed activity locations fall under the severity 3, 4 or 5 in terms of food insecurity, recognizing the fluidly and unpredictable nature of sub-national violence in South Sudan, UNHCR’s protection by presence approach requires presence in areas affected by conflict as well as areas of potential return in light of increased momentum in terms of solutions
Resources and advocacy will focus primarily on protection response and solutions, either durable or transitional (where the former is not yet possible), including assessments on needs, risks, and intentions and feasibility of durable solutions, while also strengthening community-based structures, supporting HLP initiatives, promotion of livelihoods and peaceful coexistence. Improving coordination and quality of assessments and monitoring will be a key component. A mobile tool was introduced in late 2019 that has enhanced data collection and triangulation of information through timely collection and analysis capabilities, which will further improve information products, leading to a more fitting risk-mitigation approach, reduced response time; while underpinning protection mainstreaming across other operational interventions, including of other Clusters.

1 - Needs

South Sudan continues to be characterized as a protection crisis since the last outbreak of large-scale armed violence in 2016. In 2020, despite progress through the establishment of a new transitional government, slow implementation of the revitalized peace agreement, as well as the outbreak of sub-national organized violence and communal clashes throughout the country demonstrated that peace in South Sudan remains fragile. More than 60% of IDPs are women and children; the most vulnerable among the displaced. Effects of displacement are exacerbated by severe food insecurity, resulting not only from climate shocks but also aggravated by the effects of prolonged conflict, which has also compromised access to land, water, limited livelihood options and contributed to the overall deterioration of local economies. Persons with Specific Needs (PSN) face heightened protection risks due to their individual circumstances owning to discrimination, their identity, or other factors that prevent them from fully enjoying their rights or accessing services they need. The onset of the COVID-19 pandemic and its related restrictions on movement, worsened an already fragile economic situation. In areas such as Upper Nile, Jonglei and Unity, unprecedented floods from 2019 to 2020 have left affected communities increasingly vulnerable given loss of home, agricultural lands and livelihoods. Realization of HLP rights for the people of South Sudan remains a growing key protection concern across the country, particularly with IDPs returning to find their properties) occupied. Access to formal and traditional/customary legal systems to resolve HLP related disputes are often difficult for IDPs to navigate without assistance. At the same time, IDPs continue to face additional protection risks, including threat to physical safety, serious human rights abuses including sexual and gender based abuse, limited freedom of movement and forced recruitment (including of children), and family separation. With a significant number of IDPs living in remote, isolated and hard-to-reach areas, mobile response capacity continues to be a priority, complemented by regular protection monitoring in the deep field. With the formation of the Transitional Unity Government in 2020 and cautious optimism that fragile peace could hold, ongoing spontaneous returns of IDPs is expected to continue increasing. This will allow for small-scale localized solutions in areas of relative stability, mostly self-initiated by IDPs. Based on the data from UNHCR’s profiling exercises conducted in 2019 and 2020 the main challenges for IDPs living in camps and urban settings is communal violence, limited livelihood options, availability of shelter and transportation home While the overall conditions across the country are not yet conducive for mass returns; pockets of stability offer opportunities for durable or transitional solutions. There is a critical need to provide up to date information to IDPs on the situation in areas of potential return, in order to support them to make a well-informed decisions on their movement, and to ensure any return is voluntary, well informed and durable. Concretely, through this project, UNHCR seeks to support women, men, boys and girls with protection concerns through case management and referral services, awareness raising, distribution of cash-based assistance in connection with safe, dignified, and voluntary return as well as provision of legal counselling.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

Information about PSEA is posted in all key areas including public areas. The project strengthens referral mechanism and ensure that necessary information including PSEA hotline is provided to the beneficiaries as needed, including referrals to established Women and Girls Friendly Spaces. Awareness raising on rights includes a component of GBV and SEA risks and mitigation. UNHCR and its partners will make efforts to ensure that the affected people are aware and informed of PSEA mitigation and available services using IEC materials. Posters carrying information about protection, ethical principles including anti-SEA posters are posted at various accessible locations such as premises of partners, distribution points, protection desks, public notice boards in the communities and UNHCR premises. UNHCR have designated PSEA focal persons in all office with static UNHCR presence, all UNHCR staff and UNHCR partners staff have signed a code of conduct with zero tolerance on PSEA and commitment to inform beneficiaries about protection against SEA.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Feedback sessions to the communities are also planned so that persons of concern, including Persons with Disabilities (PWD), illiterate, women and children, give feedback on implemented projects and are informed of the findings of the assessments. UNHCR and partners involves representatives from all target populations including individuals living with disabilities and individuals who are illiterate. Feedback is given using a language that they understand. Wider sections of the communities are involved including youth groups, community leaders, women groups and persons with disabilities to ensure adequate reach of the information. Apart from that, continuous FGDs and KIIs are organized throughout the year through the protection desks as well as mobile missions to collect qualitative data on the performance of partners and progress of the implemented activities. In addition, based on the roll-out of the Protection Monitoring Tool (PMT), UNHCR also collects the perceptions of IDPs through quantitative data collection initiatives which informs UNHCR advocacy, implementation as well as any need for review of its project whenever necessary. Particular attention is drawn to the most vulnerable groups (including PWD, Illiterate people) whose opinions are particularly assessed by specific interviews made through household visits; in case their voices are otherwise missed in group sessions.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

Crisis-affected people, including IDPs, host communities and IDP returnees are consulted at the beginning of the project, as well as throughout the project cycle. The UNHCR age, gender and diversity mainstreaming approach is used to identify the needs and priorities across all gender and age groups. The target population is also involved in periodic review meetings and joint assessments/monitoring exercises. UNHCR feedback and complaint mechanisms include suggestion boxes, established protection desks and free of charge protection hotline. The target population is also involved in periodic review meetings and joint assessments/monitoring exercises where their views and concerns are recorded. UNHCR and partners involve representatives from all target population including youth groups, community leaders, women groups and persons with disabilities. Protection information desks operated by UNHCR and partners staff are used to identify, track, document and assist affected people. Capacity building trainings in the communities integrate the affected people to be part of the beneficiary in the trainings. Equally important, the affected women and girls are rendered the opportunity to voice their concerns through different forums and GBV awareness raising sessions. The community members (IDPs, IDPs returnees and host community) are actively involved in all the program stages including implementation as well as participatory evaluation and assessments ensuring that, all distinct groups are recognized. UNHCR and its partners will ensure that the affected people are aware, informed of the objective of the project and available services through community information channels, community workers, leaders and community representatives. Posters carrying information about protection, HLP rights, ethical principles including anti-fraud posters are posted at various accessible locations such as premises of partners, distribution points, protection desks, public notice boards in the communities and UNHCR partners premises. Visibility signposts are displayed with information about the ongoing activities, at the protection information desk with contact information for further inquiry.

At least once a year, UNHCR conducts participatory assessments using the Age, Gender-Diversity Mainstreaming Methodology in all main locations where it delivers protection assistance and/or implements protection interventions. The Participatory assessments are a condition for the planning of the annual programming and designing projects. Feedback sessions to the communities are also planned so that persons of concern give feedback on implemented projects and are informed of the findings of the assessments. UNHCR and partners involves representatives from all target populations including youth groups, community leaders, women groups and persons with disabilities to ensure adequate reach of the information. Ccontinuous FGDs and KIIs are organized throughout the year through the protection desks as well as mobile missions to collect qualitative data on the performance of partners and progress of the implemented activities. In addition, based on the roll-out of the Protection Monitoring Tool (PMT), UNHCR also collects the perceptions of IDPs through quantitative data collection. Feedback from beneficiaries informs UNHCR advocacy, implementation as well as any need for review of its projects whenever necessary.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

The prevalence of GBV in South Sudan continues to be of great concern, requiring continued and dedicated efforts to ensure the protection of vulnerable persons, including women, girls, boys, persons with disabilities, when accessing humanitarian services. Not all persons have access to a mobile phone, continued protection monitoring and bolstered referral mechanisms are aimed at identification and awareness raising on PSEA and rights. UNHCR and its partners will make efforts to ensure that the affected people are aware and informed of PSEA mitigation and available services using IEC materials. Posters on protection, ethical principles, and anti-SEA are posted at various accessible locations such as premises of partners, distribution points, protection desks, public notice boards and UNHCR premises. In addition, UNHCR have designated PSEA focal persons in all offices with static UNHCR presence, all UNHCR staff and partner staff have signed a code of conduct with zero tolerance on PSEA and commitment to inform beneficiaries about protection against SEA. The isolated and rural locations whereby clashes and subsequent displacement tend to take place, adds additional hurdles in ensuring marginalized groups, persons with disabilities, older persons, women, boys and girls participate in voicing their views and needs in consultations and decision-making. In terms of mitigating measures, the response is guided by the needs identified through protection monitoring carried out directly and/or by partners prior to initiating the response. Crisis-affected people are consulted at the beginning and throughout the project cycle. The UNHCR age, gender and diversity mainstreaming approach is used to identify the needs and priorities across all gender and age groups. A key focus includes the outreach to vulnerable community members for targeted and tailored support, as well as ensure referral services are functioning for engaging specialist support for example in the area of child protection, nutrition and health. UNHCR is aware of the enormous challenges that can face persons of concern in coming forward with complaints, including misappropriation of aid and misuse of power. This also includes the difficulties survivors of sexual exploitation or abuse face in coming forward, and of the deep professional and personal considerations, and even fears, that may prevent them from doing so. UNHCR is striving to address concerns by ensuring that reporting mechanisms are known, accessible and trusted, and that victims who report sexual misconduct feel safe and protected. UNHCR has a survivor-centered approach that places the rights and protection of victims, survivors and persons directly affected at the centre of our actions. Actions include improving the experience of recourse, providing better medical and psycho-social support, strengthening our policies, and developing additional protective measures for those who have experienced or witnessed such abuses. This goes beyond UNHCR, and is extended also to partner staff with regular trainings, capacity building and awareness raising ongoing. As communities return to areas of origin/habitual residencies they face risks of finding their land/properties occupied by strangers. As such house/land/property disputes are expected to keep on surfacing particularly in areas of returns. To mitigate the risks, preparations for humanitarian supported returns includes a HLP assessment to ensure that returning families have access to land and property, their arrival is expected by their families and community leaders. Furthermore, UNHCR works to ensure returns are supported in returning to areas where protection partners are operating including partners dealing with HLP issues. State level solution task forces include HLP as their key component and UNHCR co -leads the task forces together with the government. Assistance for shelter considerations also ensures that property ownership and documentation are in order.
