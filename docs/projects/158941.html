Project Summary

This project targets Palestinian children directly and indirectly exposed to occupation-related violence and abuse, including settler violence, unsafe school environments, home and school demolitions, and detention and/or arrests. War Child Holland (WCH) and its partner will be targeting acutely affected children and families living in Area C (Jordan Valley and Tubas), East Jerusalem (communities outside the West Bank Barrier), and Hebron H2.
This project aims to strengthen the resilience of children, caregivers, schools, and communities in the face of chronic exposure to conflict-related violence by: (1) providing community-based child protection, psychosocial and mental health services to children and their families, (2) supporting parents, school staff, and community-based organizations with psychosocial interventions and capacity building to better take care of and respond to the needs of children in distress, (3) case management and referrals to specialized mental health, social, and educational services, including vocational and remedial education, and (4) addressing IHL and IHRL violations through advocacy initiatives targeting all duty bearers and based on documentation of grave violations.
Through the project, children will have access to structured recreational and psychosocial support (PSS) interventions provided by 6 CBOs in Area C of the Jordan Valley, East Jerusalem, and H2, and 6 schools in Area C of the Jordan Valley - supported by WCH to help children and adolescents at high risk of occupation related violence to develop positive and alternative coping strategies, conflict resolution skills, and engage in regular activities in a safe and protective environment.
Severely affected children will also have access to case management from War Child’s case workers, who in addition to providing timely and ongoing individualized support to the child and family, will identify and refer children struggling with child protection and clinical mental health issues to specialized services, in line with the national referral pathway, including the Child Protection Network (CPN). Children and families in need of specialized mental health and/or legal services will be referred to partner organizations under War Child’s current HF-funded projects, as well as external mental health and other service providers, (e.g., the MOH and PRCS), as required. There will also be a particular focus on supporting and enabling young people to re-engage with education (formal, non-formal or vocational) after conflict-related disruptions such as detention, home arrest, and injury through coordination with and referrals to protection and education cluster partners, ensuring that children receive multidisciplinary and holistic support.
Caregivers will also be offered WCH’s caregiver support intervention designed to help them manage their own stress in order to better support children, and reduce harsh disciplinary practices. Referrals for specialized mental health services will also be provided to caregivers through WCH’s already established referral network.
Capitalizing on their cohesive network of sources including local media, health workers, CBOs, and community leaders and members throughout the West Bank, partner field workers will closely monitor, document, and rapidly report grave violations against children, including child killings and injuries; settler violence; and recruitment. Strengthened monitoring and reporting of grave violations will yield thematic reports and advocacy initiatives to address protection violations and call for greater accountability on IHL and IHRL by duty bearers.

1 - Needs

This project addresses protection risks stemming from occupation-related violence and violations of IHR/IHL, and complements and fills gaps in community-based child protection and PSS interventions in Area C of the Jordan Valley, Hebron, and East Jerusalem. The gaps and needs were identified through the 2019 HNO, WCH’s 2019 MHPSS, CP and education needs assessment of children living in Area C of the Tubas governorate, and 2018 PSS and CP needs assessment for Area C of the Jericho governorate; OCHA household survey of Hebron H2; WCH’s 2018 HF project in Area C of the Jordan Valley; baseline data collected from partners, including DCIP reports on child arrest and detention; and UNICEF’s recent review of the humanitarian MHPSS needs and gaps in the State of Palestine.The targeted communities face regular incidents of settler violence, military drills, home raids, and demolition and displacement orders- which according to WCH’s assessments, are the main causes of stress and distress for children. In Area C of the Jordan Valley, MHPSS symptoms among children included fear/dread, behavioral symptoms (fighting, violence towards others, anger), and declining academic performance. In Hebron H2, according to OCHA’s household survey, 69% of households experienced settlers’ attacks or intimidation in the past 3 years, where a majority reported psychosocial distress as a main result, and 70% said their children have no access to extra curricula activities. In East Jerusalem, home demolitions, detention and house arrest, daily military raids, and restrictions to play and access places of worship have psychosocial and traumatic impacts on children. The Area C communities and schools targeted are some of the highest ranked in vulnerability according to the Vulnerability Profile Project (OCHA), Protection Vulnerability Index (West Bank Protection Consortium), and Education Cluster Vulnerable School Matrix. While the need for child protection and psychosocial support is increasing, the current care system is overstretched, unable to respond to the protection and psychosocial needs of children and their caregivers, as access to national services such as the CPN is often hampered due to movement restrictions, and little if any case management services provided by NNGOs/INGOs. The MOSD has expressed to War Child the need for support to the CPN in the field, as they only respond to high-risk cases, do not have the capacity to follow-up on low-medium risk cases, and are challenged to access the remote villages and Bedouin communities targeted under this project. Communities also highlighted the need for community and school-based recreational activities and PSS interventions focused on children’s behavioral problems (violence, anger, and bullying). The MoEHE in Area C of the Jordan Valley has also expressed the need for in and after-school PSS activities, noting that the only training school counselors have received was in PFA . Through this project, War Child will provide case management services and referrals for low and medium risk cases, which are currently not covered by the MOSD or other organizations, preventing them from becoming high-risk cases. In addition, WCH will strengthen the capacity of CBOs and schools to provide CP and PSS interventions for children and families, with focus on behavioral problems and conflict resolution. There is also a continued need for monitoring and documentation of grave violations, and for advocacy initiatives to promote respect for IHL and IHRL, as there continues to be a little adherence, and no accountability for violations. As reported in the 2018 HNO, 3706 incidents of grave violations against children were documented In the West Bank affecting 31,927 children. In addition, with the recent expulsion of TIPH from Hebron-H2 District by the Israeli authorities, there no longer is an international presence to monitor and report on the violations against children living in H2.

2 - Activities

Objective 1: Increased respect for IHL and IHRL and accountability for violations *HRP Activity 2: Monitoring and documentation Grave Violations Against Children. Capitalizing on their cohesive network of sources including local media, health workers, CBOs, and community leaders and members throughout the West Bank, protection cluster partner field workers will closely monitor, document, and rapidly report grave violations against children, including child killings and injuries; settler violence; and recruitment. The result will include thematic reports to expose the violations and advocate for their cessation. *HRP Activity 3: Advocacy initiatives that aim to address protection concerns, prevent violations or enhance accountability. Through proven and innovative advocacy strategies and tools, implementing partners work to expose violations against children, increase accountability for violations, and advance rights and protection for children. Through this project the partner will generate content for newswires, photographs, and video clips, and will conduct briefing sessions with high-ranking officials and international groups to address violations against Palestinian children. Protection Cluster Objective 2/Education Cluster Objective 2: *Protection Cluster HRP Activity 1/Education Cluster HRP Activity 3 – implemented in coordination with Protection and Education Cluster partners:•Structured recreational PSS sessions for 1440 acutely affected children aged 6-18, their siblings and classmates, using the WCH Team Up methodology implemented through partnerships with 6 CBOs in Area C of Jordan Valley, Hebron, and East Jerusalem (reaching 960 children), and 6 schools in Area C of the Jordan Valley(reaching 480 children)• Group-based psychosocial support and life skills sessions for 360 children, using the WCH DEALs methodology that aims to enhance children’s resilience by providing positive coping skills, conflict resolution skills, and enhancing autonomy, critical thinking, anger control, acceptance of others, empathy and self-esteem, including: Win-Win DEALs for adolescents between the ages of 13-18 years old, and Little Fellow intervention for children between the ages of 6-10 years old. Implemented through partnerships with 6 CBOs in Area C of Jordan Valley, Hebron, and East Jerusalem (reaching 240 children), and 6 schools in Area C of the Jordan Valley(reaching 120 children)• Training 40 community facilitators and school counselors on TeamUp and DEALs methodologies, child safeguarding, inclusion and identification and referral of children with special psychosocial and child protection needs • Case Management: WCH will coordinate and implement multi-sectoral case management for 100 affected children. The project will ensure that detected children receive integrated quality services, through appropriate safe referral to specialized services in coordination with CPWG, and in close collaboration with the CPN at governorate level. *HRP Activity 2: Child Protection workers are trained on the guidelines on mental health and psychosocial support: 20 community facilitators will be trained on the updated version the Child Protection Minimum Standards CPMS and the ISAC guidelines mental health and psychosocial support in humanitarian emergencies. *HRP Activity 3: Provision of MHPSS for adults and youth:• Caregiver psychosocial support intervention (CSI): 270 caregivers of affected children will be engaged in this MHPSS intervention to improve their wellbeing and emotional availability, strengthen their social networks and support, build coping methods for dealing with stress, anger and sadness, and identify alternatives to harsh discipline actions with children. • Training 20 community facilitators on the CSI methodology

9 - Protection mainstreaming
9.1 - Do No Harm

WCH aims at being accountable to the children and families of this project. One key measure is the implementation of WCH Child Safeguarding Policy, along with a reporting mechanism. All our staff are trained on the policy upon signing their contract, and are supported to prevent harm to children we work with. WCH also commits to support local organizations, NGOs and partners to be aware of the importance of a child safeguarding policy and supports them to develop their own. Within WCH programs, children are regularly informed, at start of any project, of what they can expect from our organization and staff, as well as being provided with clear information on details about how they can give feedback and complain if they experience or witness misconduct by representatives of WCH. Staff and partners will also be trained on Safe identification and referral, and the new updated version of the CPMS Child Protection Minimum Standard and IASC MHPSS in emergencies.This project includes case management services, and War Child ensures adherence to information management and data protection protocols that uphold the privacy and confidentiality of cases. Access to reports and case files will be kept limited to the professionals working on the cases and other professional organizations whose work require seeing these reports. War Child will ensure the confidentiality through safety measures outlined in the information sharing agreement that will be developed and signed by the project partners, and War Child will have data protection protocol starting from the beginning of the project.

9.2 - Equal and impartial access to aid

To ensure meaningful access and participation, vulnerable children and caregivers (including people with disabilities) will be identified through outreach (including home visits), awareness raising sessions in the community, structured PSS programs, then referred to case management for assessment. The case workers are based in the community where we work, making it easier to identify vulnerable children and caregivers. Following a comprehensive assessment, case workers will refer children who are under 18 years old and their caregivers to the needed internal services or external services. Access to external MHPSS services in the targeted communities are limited, therefore requiring children and their caregivers to travel to the nearest city which may be several hours away. As the travel and transportation costs may at times be a barrier to access, War Child in these instances will provide transportation support and case workers will accompany the children (including children with disabilities) to the specialized services to ensure access to care. In addition, given the overall and high need of children and families affected by conflict- related violence, selection of participants in community-based PSS activities (TeamUp, DEALs, and Caregiver Support Intervention) and case management services will be based on specific vulnerability criteria (such as being acutely affected by or at high-risk of conflict-related violence, family or community violence/abuse/neglect, disabilities, child labor, etc), which will also be well publicized through community meetings. Communities will also have the opportunity to help define the selection criteria, including adding or removing criteria. Case workers will also coordinate with the CPN officers in their governorates who oversee protection cases for children with disabilities to ensure that children with disabilities are referred to relevant PSS activities as needed and coordination will be closely done with the available services provided by Protection Cluster actors such as the YMCA and the PRCS for children with disabilities. The non-specialized activities are provided by community members themselves, trained and supported as part of the project. Activities and interventions will be further refined and adapted to meet the specific needs of the communities through engagement of CBOs and community facilitators who will regularly engage women, men, girls and boys (including persons with disabilities) in defining their needs and tailoring the community-based PSS interventions. Case workers and community facilitators will also receive an orientation training by War Child staff on inclusion principles in order to skillfully adapt PSS interventions to ensure inclusivity, including for children with disabilities. WCH strives that all programs should meet the eight criteria for meaningful participation. These criteria measure how children perceive the quality of their participation in our programs. This indicator is measured through a group exercise or survey, and the exercise introduces children to each standard for meaningful participation. Children share their opinions through an anonymous vote. The survey allows children to provide feedback on different aspects of each standard for meaningful participation.
