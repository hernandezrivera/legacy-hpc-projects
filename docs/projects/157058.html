Project Summary

This project will create new opportunities for positive mechanisms to the affected population to support their development and wellbeing through one existing community centers and the establishment of one additional community centre, as well as mobile teams that will be conducting awareness raising, and psychosocial activities. Both community centres will be open to all members of the communities, regardless of their sex, social status and age, and both IDPs and host community members could benefit from the services provided. The mobile teams that are already operating in some areas in Rural Damascus will be able to reach the areas of higher pressing needs, and an additional mobile team will be operating in the difficult-to-reach areas from the newly established centre. Therefore, International Medical Corps will provide structured psychosocial support (PSS) and non-formal education activities to children and youth aiming at building key life skills and knowledge. IMC will continue providing rehabilitative service for children with intellectual disabilities (including developmental delay), and will train their parents/caregiver on assisting their children’s psychosocial, physical and educational developments in the newly established centre in Dar’a. The Youth Empowerment Program (YEP) will also be provided to youth to enable them to become active members and agents of positive change in their community. This can also contribute to empowering families to protect children and to enhancing the resilience of the entire community.
Moreover, to help families to better understand their children facing developmental, parents/caregiver will have the opportunity to learn more about the condition of their children and practice new skills to better support their development. Though the Portage training parents learn how they can assess their children's development and offer them stimuli in each developmental stage through early childhood onwards
Adults challenged by emotional distress, and psychopathology symptoms will also be supported through the specialized group and individual sessions and whenever necessary there will be referrals.

Needs

Almost eight years after the start of the conflict, the situation in the Syrian Arab Republic remains one of the worst humanitarian crises in the world. According to the humanitarian action for children report published by UNICEF, some 11.7 million people require humanitarian assistance, including 6.12 million people who are internally displaced. Conflict continues to be the principal driver of humanitarian needs, with the civilian population in many parts of the country exposed to significant protection risks which threaten life, dignity and wellbeing on a daily basis. Children, including those unaccompanied, separated or living with older/disabled caregivers, remain particularly vulnerable. Out of the estimated 8.35 million children living in the Syrian Arab Republic, 5.6 million are in acute need due to a convergence of vulnerabilities resulting from displacement, exposure to hostilities, and limited access to basic goods and services. With 1,303,622 IDPs in Rural Damascus (39.97% out of the total population), and 24,761 of returnees where the protection severity ranks between 1 to 6 in the different sub-districts in Rural Damascus. Out of the total population in Rural Damascus, 76.5% are listed as people in need for protection based on HNO data. As for Jaramana area according to the severity scale of the protection risk analysis (PRA) the severity is 4. As for As-Sanamayn area in Dar’a, with 44,324 IDPs (24.37% out of the total population), and 3,243 returnees, the protection severity scale reaches 4 where 83.95% of the population are listed as people in need for protection based on HNO data.Sudden and often multiple displacements followed by protracted periods of time in collective shelters, have adverse effects on the mental and psychological wellbeing of Syrians residing in shelters and in small, crowded provisional housing. The need for increased psychosocial support and specialized mental health services is also evidence by the increased number of people surviving armed conflict and loss. Displacement presents also significant protection challenges, particularly for women and children, as households lose traditional socio-economic safety nets and frequently experience extremely poor living conditions. Children are often forced to drop out of school and work to provide for the family, and women and girls are extremely vulnerable to domestic violence, sexual violence, inside and outside the household, early and forced marriage, and other forms of GBV. GBV survivors are unable to access essential medical care, psychosocial support and other vital support or a reluctant to do so given the stigma and culture of silence pervasive in the country. According 2018 Syria Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO), after assessing 160 communities in Rural Damascus governorate; child labor, child recruitment, unaccompanied and separated children, family violence, and child marriage were reported as issues of concern. More specifically, ninety-four percent of the assessed communities reported child labor as an issue of concern, where the most affected population groups were adolescent boys from 15-17 years, followed by adolescent boys from 12-14 years. Fifty-five percent of the assessed communicates reported child recruitment as an issue of concern, where the most affected population groups were adolescent boys, followed by girls. Eighty-one percent of the assessed communities reported unaccompanied and separated children as an issue of concern, where the most affected population included adolescent boys, followed by adolescent girls. Seventy-eight percent of the assessed communities reported family violence as an issue of concern, where the most affected population were adolescent boys, followed by adolescent girls. Eighty-seven percent of the assessed communities reported child marriage as an issue of concern, where the most affected population were adolescent girls followed by adolescent boys.

Implementation of activities

Activity 1: Awareness session through campaigns -The awareness sessions will be designed based on the most prevalent protection risks encountered in each community and will cover areas related to Child Protection, GBV, Inclusion of people with disabilities, all these being areas in which IMC has previous success stories in training with community members in Syria. Through this project IMC will continue building capacity of women, men girls and boys in order to be actively involved in the protection of their own communities. Indicators: # of people reached through awareness raising sessions (cumulative interventions) other than legal awareness. # of middle-age and older adults reached through awareness sessions # of people reached through community-based protection services, including individual targeted assistance for persons with specific protection needs (includes PSS) (cumulative interventions)Activity 2: Community based protection services and other activities delivered through the establishment of community centers and the expansion of the existing community centre in Jaramana. Psychosocial assessments and interventions for people of concern reaching the centre or through outreach in the communities The services and activities will be delivered within the community centre in Jaramana, which will be expanded to create a space adequate for larger group activities and community lead events. Another multisectorial community centre will be established Dar’a, Sanamayen sub-district, in a nearby location to IMC PHC clinic. Greater focus will be given to MHPSS support, early childhood development and community capacity building. Provision of targeted assistance, such as psychosocial support and referrals to specialized MH or Protection services, to persons with specific needs through mobile teams, to address specific protection concerns as identified through protection monitoring/casework. IMC MHPSS/Protection Mobile teams, are operating in R. Damascus, covering for the needs of the population in Babila and Yelda. Additional mobile teams will be added to cover for areas and communities far to reach, such as Sanamaeyn, and areas characterised by displacement and returnees. The number of mobile teams and outreach volunteers will be determined based on the needs assessment and the target population in each location. Support will be provided by trained PSS counselors and case managers in the centres. Protection Risk Analysis

Activity 1. Threat: Content of message is not fully adapted to the social and cultural environment and creates misunderstanding or even tensions and has repercussions on the organization implementing the campaign Mitigation Measures: Conduct impact assessments on the reach/effect of the campaign and on the reception/ reaction by the different segments of the population in the communities. Provide adequate messages tailored to women and men needs, children and youth. Activity 2. Threat: Risk of inter or intra community tension due to service delivery locations or types of services offered if one community is served and a neighboring community not served/cannot access or if information offered is not consistent between communities as there may be perceptions of discrimination or favoritism. Mitigation Measures: 1. make use of existing tools/ mapping and coordinate at local level with other protection actors to ensure balanced coverage responding to needs; avoid overlapping or concentration of activities if possible and unless motivated by needs; consider focusing on underserved/rural areas as well as projected return areas. 2. Follow IMC Guidelines and use checklists and ensure follow-up and monitoring through complain boxes. PSEA is being addressed within all IMC staff through awareness sessions and trainings. 3. Ensure consultation with communities and local authorities on population needs, trends (including return), population dynamics in the area 4. Ensure information sharing with community members on services available 6. Work with national actors and community based groups to ensure sustainability of the intervention. Activity 3: Threat: 1. Tension in community, for perceived inequality, discrimination, favoritism, including risk for project staff, Mitigation Measure: 1. Establish adequate selection criteria mechanisms based on vulnerabilities; 2. Consult with actors on the ground through operational coordination mechanisms to establish targeting criteria that are as harmonized as possible throughout protection service providers; 3. Active participation in the protection Sector to support the harmonization of targeting criteria, while steps have already been undertaken by the major protection agencies running these programs (e.g. UNHCR Guidelines) 4. Follow Guidelines adopted by the protection actor and ensure that they are properly applied in all project locations. Activity 4: Threat: 1. Different vulnerable groups might not have the same access to services and assistance. Mitigation Measures: 1. Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Unstable security situation, which might limited the access for the supervisor to reach the locations and do direct monitoring and supervision of the implementations of the activities. Mitigation strategy; prepare a remote supervision plan to supervisor the activities accordingly. Shortage of skilled staff: based on the health department experience in the implementation area, MHPSS department has some concern regarding finding capacities in that area, like ECD trainers, PSS counselors, Educational specialists and other specialties. Mitigation strategy; provide specialized training to recruited staff and building capacity of the community in the targeted area. Difficulties of finding suitable places that comply with protection needs for the beneficiaries, also, getting the needed approvals on time. Tension in community, for perceived inequality, discrimination, favoritism, including risk for project staff. Mitigation strategy: IMC will establish adequate selection criteria mechanisms based on vulnerabilities, communicate regularly and clearly to the community about what services are available and for whom they are intended. Moreover, IMC will avoid the exclusion from assistance of persons with no identity documents within the community center
