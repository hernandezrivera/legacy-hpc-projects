Project Summary

Syrians have been exposed to extreme levels of violence and have often lost loved ones, livelihoods, and homes, which can cause significant deterioration of one’s mental health. Today many Syrians suffer from mental health and psychosocial (MHPSS) disorders, such as dysthymia and bipolar disorder, yet lack the required treatment due to the limited availability of services. Mental health disorders are increasing substantially, affecting millions of traumatized people; 1 out of 5 Syrians suffer from a mental disorder.
Moreover, the war in Syria has permanently disabled thousands of people. Conflict-related injuries, psychological stress, lack of primary health care services, and poor management of chronic conditions have severely affected people with disabilities. Specialized care and well-trained professional health workers are required in order to provide suitable services. As the conflict continues and the number of injured people rises, increasing the capacity to deliver long-term rehabilitative care is a must. Because health facilities have been targeted, Northern Syria is left with few physical rehabilitation centers. These centers are in dire need of support to operate, train and build capacity for local health workers, thereby increasing the quality of services provided. Based on the aforementioned information and situational context, there is an urgent need to implement a comprehensive program that provides life-saving MHPS and physical rehabilitation services and enhances healthcare personnel’s capacity to overcome major health care limitations in Northern Syria.
This project aims to provide life-saving services to conflict-affected populations in Northwest Syria whereas the project has 3 primary goals:
• To provide life-saving services to conflict-affected populations in Northwest Syria including health care (primary, secondary and trauma) through:
1: Provide primary health care consultations to IDPs and the host community
2: Provide trauma services, including minor and major surgeries, to IDPs and host communities
3: Provide the needed medications, supplies and equipment to maintain the hospital
• To reduce the prevalence of mental health, psychological disorders and functional limitations of people affected by the conflict in Northwest Syria
This objective will target 11 health facilities. Two Psychosocial Support Workers (PSSWs) will be hired in each facility to provide PS services within the facilities. PSSWs will also conduct outreach activities in communities to provide services to vulnerable groups unable to reach the facilities. Three team leaders will coordinate the workload for all 22 PSSWs. SAMS will strive to mainstream gender in each team. PSSWs will be trained on guidelines produced by the technical group of the health cluster, according to a methodology endorsed by WHO, in addition to other relevant training.

Needs

As a result of the war, many Syrians currently suffer from a wide range of MHPS disorders. Those inside Syria are frequently subjected to and/or ‎have witnessed torture, kidnappings, disappearances and massacres. They ‎have been targeted themselves and have seen friends and family killed by bombs and snipers. Their homes and neighborhoods have been destroyed, and they have suffered severe physical injuries. MHPS services can be key to restoring basic psychological function, supporting resilience and fostering positive ‎coping strategies for Syrians. Studies have shown that when children are exposed to “events beyond the natural boundaries of human ‎experience,” also referred to as traumatic or psychologically wounding events, they suffer from a variety of stress reactions. Manifestations include, but are not limited to, withdrawal from social contact, the development of anti-social and sometimes harmful behaviors, changes in temperament, nightmares, eating disorders and learning ‎problems. The effects of such trauma are also inter-generational. Subsequent generations will also be affected, either directly or indirectly, through this generation’s children. However, with concerted and strategic intervention, ‎stress reactions are reported to disappear over time.SAMS’s Information Management Unit (IMU) has conducted MHPS Services (MHPSS) assessments among SAMS’s supported facilities in Idleb, Hama, Homs and Aleppo governorates. A total of 346 staff were interviewed, consisting of 110 doctors, 34 midwives, 199 nurses, and 3 psychotherapists. A majority (76%) of doctors interviewed reported that they diagnose patients with mental health or psychosocial disorder on a regular basis. Notably, approximately 45% of cases seen are under 18 years old. The MHPS disorders affecting patients range from developmental disorders, bipolar disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, substance dependence, and behavior disorders. According to 46% of respondents, sufficient treatment is not provided to patients due to limited resources, training, and staff. Of the healthcare professionals interviewed, 296 have not received training on MHPS disorders, highlighting the extreme deficiency of medical professionals qualified to treat MHPSS patients. However, most staff expressed the importance of MHPS treatment and expressed willingness to participate in training on the topic. By the same token, there are approximately 2.9 million permanently disabled people in Syria and 30,000 new conflict-related trauma cases per month, resulting in thousands of permanent disabilities that require long-term physical rehabilitation care. Institutional weaknesses in providing non-emergency care treatment also contribute to the growing number of long-term disabilities. For example, untreated diabetes can result in amputations, or complications at birth can lead to cerebral palsy in a child. Despite anecdotal evidence of such situations, these cases have been difficult to track due to the collapse of the health care system in Syria and the constant displacement of people. Additionally, the evaluated communities, particularly the IDPs, face multi-dimensional healthcare challenges as a result of a series of long-term structural and crisis-oriented problems. There are several circumstances and conditions that depended on the vulnerability of tens of thousands to diverse disease groups, frequently the respiratory infectious diseases and child diarrhea. The predisposing factors incorporate the prevailing instability, conflicts, and food-insecurity and periodic power interferences. On the other hand, war-related injuries complications and heart diseases are the largest death causes for both the IDPs and the host communities.

Implementation of activities

I. Improving access to primary, secondary and trauma health care in (Ma'aret Mesrin hospital in Idleb District)Activities:1.1: Provide primary health care consultations to IDPs and the host community, this activity is provided through out-patient clinics. These clinics’ scope includes general surgery, orthopedics, cardio surgery, vascular surgery, Ear Nose and Throat (ENT), internal medicine, pediatrics and maxillofacial surgery. The two main types of out-patient needs are trauma and pediatrics. Currently, the clinics’ average consultation rate is exceeding 350 consultations (including new and follow-up) per day. 1.2: Provide trauma services, including minor and major surgeries, to IDPs and host communitiesThe main focus will be on trauma cases, including both minor and major surgeries resulting from war and non-war injuries, as well as other types of surgeries aforementioned. On average, more than 10 major surgeries take place daily at the facility. Basic laboratory and radiology services are being provided as well. 1.3: Provide the needed medications, supplies and equipment to maintain the hospitalAll medications will be provided to patients free of charge where there is a pharmacy within the hospital that is managed by a certified pharmacist. II. Reduce the prevalence of mental health and psychosocial disorders by increasing the access to MHPSS services (11 health facilities to be targeted: Saraqeb PHC And Sarmin Health Facility in Idleb District - Kurin PHC and Jabal Al-Zawiyeh Mobile clinic in Ariha District - Ossama Albaroudi PHC and Hama Mobile Clinic In Al Ma’ra District - Kafr Lucin PHC in Harim district all in rural Idleb), and ( Kafr Nbutheh PHC in As-Suqaylabiyah district in rural Hama), and (Haritan PHC, Al-Sahhara PHC, Al-Abzemo PHC in Jebel Saman in rural Aleppo) Two Psychosocial Support Workers (PSSWs) will be hired in each facility to provide PS services within the facilities. PSSWs will also conduct outreach activities in communities to provide services to vulnerable groups unable to reach the facilities. Three team leaders will coordinate the workload for all 22 PSSWs. Furthermore, doctors in the facilities will be trained on the Mental Health Gap (MHGAP) through practical experience working with patients with mental disorders in order to fill a void in current mental health services. Activities:2.1: Provide MHPSS services in 11 strategic health facilities in Northwest Syria2.2: Train general practitioners on specialized MhGAP2.3: Train the health facilities’ staff on MHGAP detection and referral processes2.4: Train PSSWs on international protocols and guidelines for addressing psychosocial concerns Project personnel for this project Personnel to be covered with the project activities include 186 staff members distributed between medical and non-medical staff including in addition to the project administrative staff in Turkey and on field the following (15 Physicians - Pharmacist – 15 Medical Technicians – 14 Nurses - 12 Paramedics -22 MHPSS Officers - 3 MHPSS Team Leaders - Staff Care Specialist - 22 Guards - Nutrition Specialist - Consultant physiotherapy- Orthosis senior technician - 5 Orthosis technicians - 5 physiotherapy technicians - Data Entry Officer - Warehouse officer - Receptionist - Cleaners - Security...)

Protection Risk Analysis

Risk: Sexual Exploitation and abuse by Facilities staff toward beneficiaries. Possibility: high. Effect: high. Mitigation measures: 1. Set PSEA Policy and effectively implement the policy in Hiring the staff and oriented them on the policy, training them, and make sure every staff signs the policy, 2. Effective complaint mechanism in place, 3. Awareness raising for beneficiaries on PSEA, 4. Clear complaints channels for beneficiaries and distribute brochures and leaflets in all places in the facilities (waiting rooms, entrance, Etc.). Risk: Sexual and Gender-Based Violence in the facilities between beneficiaries. Possibility: high. Effect: High. Mitigation measures: 1. Use or establish separated toilets for men and women, 2. Establish spacious waiting rooms to host a huge number of patient. 3. Put keys for the empty rooms if any. Risk: Facilities are inaccessible for the disabled patient and elderly persons. Possibility: moderate. Effect: High. Mitigation measures: 1. Build a ramp for disabled people at the facility entrance, 2. Make sure of the availability of logistics supplies needed for elderly persons and people with disabilities. Risk: impure or spoiled medications and supplies that could be harmful to beneficiaries. Possibility: Low. Effect: High. Mitigation measures: 1. Procure medication with longtime validity (taking into consideration the transportation time for this medication and deliver it to the facilities than to beneficiaries to be used), 2. Procure the medications from reliable sources, 3. ensure cold-chain transportation for drugs requiring low temperatures, 4. Regular monitoring of the storage conditions of the medications and the validity date.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Risk: Control by unaccepted groups/entities or GoS.Mitigation measures: SAMS will be re-running all anti-terrorism/background checks on all staff, volunteers, partners, hawala, and other vendors associated with the current award. Additional Money Transfer Verifications will take place as well where SAMS has added further anti-fraud verifications conducted by SAMS staff in Syria and Turkey before monthly disbursal of salaries to recipients. Separately, SAMS MEAL staff are continuously running their own separate spot checks, analyzing a sample of the payroll, conducting staff interviews, and verifying the salary amount received, in addition to their regular visits to the facilities, where they observe staff presence and conduct inventory checks. Risk: Attack by GoS and its allies/others. Mitigation measures: In addition to making sure to select in advance secure locations; Security assessment (site & building); Evacuation SOP; Sourcing alternative locations, in case of need for relocation. Risk: Staff/Beneficiaries Frustrated/Unhappy with the program and services providedMitigation measure: Feedback mechanisms, while established prior to January 2019, add another layer of verification and accountability for SAMS in the implementation of programs, thanks to multiple channels through which staff and beneficiaries may communicate feedback and complaints confidentially to SAMS’ MEAL team, without fear of reprisals or leakage of information. Risk: Abduction (kidnapping) Mitigation measure: Hostile Environment and Safety Management Training; Access and movement SOPs; Local networks to monitor security situation and trends; Communication plan and local acceptance; Security Briefing; Counter Abduction; Abduction Management training for staff and partners. Risk: Theft Mitigation Measures: Safety and security upgrades for SAMS facilities, including CCTVcameras and guards; Photo verification of received shipments, signed waybills, and notification if any missing items noted in HF count within 72 hours; Review of stock/inventory records during monitoring visits; Hostile Environment and Safety Management Training; Access and movement SOPs; Local networks to monitor security situation and trends; Communication plan and local acceptance; Quarterly physicalinventory of warehouses; review of monthly consumption reports; Security Briefing forstaff and partners.
