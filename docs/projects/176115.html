Project Summary

According to the IPC analysis released on December 2020, between December and March 2021 about 5.8 million people (48.3 per cent of the population) faced Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse acute food insecurity. Of this population, 1.8 million people faced Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity, with 11,000 people likely in Catastrophe (IPC Phase 5) acute food insecurity, specifically in the Pibor Administrative Area. The nutrition situation is critical, with 53 counties (68 per cent) classified as IPC Acute Malnutrition (AMN) Phase 3 and above in November 2020 to March 2021. Out of this, 29 counties are in IPC AMN Phase 4, and 24 counties in IPC AMN Phase 3. Of the counties in IPC AMN Phase 4, nearly 80 per cent are in Greater Upper Nile with the next largest proportion in Greater Bahr el Ghazal. About 1.4 million children under five years are expected to suffer from acute malnutrition in 2021, including 313,391 children suffering from severe acute malnutrition (SAM).
The cumulative effect of many years of conflict, displacement, economic meltdown, and lack of basic services perpetuates the underlying causes of malnutrition, thus limiting the impact of treatment. Acute malnutrition is attributed to persistent high food insecurity, poor maternal nutrition and poor infant and young child feeding practices, as well as high morbidity due to a weak health system, poor sanitation and lack of safe water.
In response, UNICEF nutrition experts will collaborate with actors in health and WASH to reinforce preventative activities and treat severe acute malnutrition. These activities will include securing safe water through: rehabilitation of non-functioning boreholes; distribution of water treatment kits to secure safe water; hygiene promotion through distribution of soap; growth monitoring and promotion (GMP) and early child care and development (ECCD); counselling on appropriate infant and young child feeding (IYCF) practices; promotion of kitchen gardens and cooking demonstrations to ensure normal physical and cognitive developments; malaria testing and treatment; vaccinations, micronutrient supplementation and deworming to boost immunity; and community engagement to encourage greater utilisation of available services.
To improve community participation and awareness among caregivers and other community stakeholders, UNICEF will use multiple channels to mobilise communities around key behaviours to prevent undernutrition. These channels will include home visits, community meetings, mother-to-mother support groups, outreach in markets and cattle camps, public address systems, road shows, theatre for development, and announcements in churches and mosques.
Given the current nutrition situation and in response to humanitarian needs in South Sudan, UNICEF and nutrition implementing partners plan to reach 3,726,852 people (2,588,549 children under five and 1,138,303 pregnant and lactating women). The estimated total cost of delivering this UNICEF nutrition project is US$ 54,444,733 in 2021.

1 - Needs

Ongoing treatment of acute malnutrition and preventative interventions like promotion of exclusive breastfeeding until six months, complementary feeding, hygiene, and micronutrient supplementation have contributed to prevent malnutrition in South Sudan. In collaboration with 38 implementing partners, UNICEF provides preventative and curative nutrition services for children and women. From January – December 2020, a total of 201,055 children (75 per cent of the target) suffering from SAM were treated in inpatient and outpatient therapeutic programmes. Compared to the same period to 2019, SAM admission has reduced by 19 per cent. However, other factors like localised conflict, displacement, suspension of facilities, flooding, and changes in admission criteria to SAM programmes due to COVID-19 are also associated with this reduction in SAM admission. In 2020, performance indicators of SAM treatment were within acceptable sphere standards, with a cure rate of 94.9 per cent, death rate of 0.3 and defaulter rate of 3 per cent. During the same period, counselling services for infant and young child feeding reached 1,633,085 pregnant women and caregivers of children 0-23 months. (>100% of the target). This contributed to maintaining high levels of exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) at 68.1 per cent. Despite positive improvements in exclusive breastfeeding and continued breastfeeding beyond 2 years of age, complementary feeding is still a challenge. Only 17.4 per cent of children (6-23 months) receive sufficiently diverse foods. A decrease was also noted in the percentage of children receiving food which qualifies as a minimum acceptable diet: from 7 per cent in 2019 to 4.1 per cent in 2020. This deterioration can be attributed to devaluation of the local currency, leading to limited availability of food, compounded by displacement of large population groups due to extensive flooding and inter-communal violence. In other words, food insecurity and other nutrition-sensitive factors such as water, sanitation and hygiene; child protection; schooling; maternal and child health; family planning services; social safety nets; and women’s empowerment are not well addressed. This project will also support a package of health and WASH interventions to prevent and reduce malnutrition. UNICEF will promote collective social action towards the promotion of good feeding practices focusing on maternal and child nutrition, malaria prevention, prevention of diarrhoea and hygiene promotion. This will be achieved through sustained communication, awareness creation, community engagement, community mobilisation and mass media. In summary, in collaboration with health and WASH actors, malnutrition treatment activities will be reinforced by malnutrition preventative activities. These include securing safe water through rehabilitation of non-functioning boreholes; distribution of water treatment kits to secure safe water; hygiene promotion through distribution of soap; growth monitoring and promotion (GMP) and early childhood and development (ECD) to prevent malnutrition; malaria testing and treatment; vaccinations and micronutrient supplementation to boost immunity; and community engagement to encourage better utilisation of services. UNICEF will use a multi-channel approach which includes interpersonal communication through home visits; community meetings; outreach sessions in markets and cattle camps; public address systems; roadshows; theatre for development; and announcements in mosques and churches. Given the current nutrition situation and in response to humanitarian needs in South Sudan, UNICEF and nutrition implementing partners have projected to reach 3,726,852 people (2,588,549 children under five and 1,138,303 pregnant and lactating women). The estimated total cost of delivering the UNICEF nutrition project is US$ 54,444,733 in 2021.

2 - Project activities

UNICEF and nutrition partners will implement numerous interventions and activities to respond to needs. The planned activities are:  Train health and nutrition workers on malnutrition prevention at national, state, county and community levels.  Provide targeted individual and group counselling on MIYCN to caregivers of children aged below 24 months, including men.  Support establishment of demonstration kitchen gardens at facilities and in communities.  Promote dietary diversity, food demonstration with locally available food, and cooking methods through cooking demonstrations.  Support growth monitoring and promotion at facility and community levels.  Provide micronutrient supplements, specifically vitamin A, and deworming through integration with National Immunisation Days (NID), as a standalone activity or with acute malnutrition screening. Conduct malaria testing and treatment at nutrition sites (OTP).  Distribute long lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs), WASH non-food items, and conduct hygiene promotion at OTP sites through the WASH cluster.  Train health and nutrition staff on how to provide psychosocial support to families and distribute ECD kits at nutrition sites (OTP).  Train health and nutrition workers on community management of acute malnutrition (CMAM) to improve care for children aged 6-59 months with malnutrition.  Conduct community mobilisation activities and active screening for early case detection and referral of children with SAM. Provide integrated treatment to children with SAM, both at static facilities and through community outreach.  Provide integrated care for children with SAM in OTP/Stabilization Centres in health facilities, including WASH services and ECD at nutrition sites.  Create awareness and provide gender-based violence referral services at OTP sites, focusing on women’s empowerment in community meetings. Conduct timely emergency preparedness and response. Monitor the progress and quality of nutrition interventions and services at the static nutrition facilities and outreach sites.  Provide integrated on-the-job coaching and mentorship on SAM treatment, supplies management and MIYCN. Routinely manage nutrition programme data and information.  Conduct nutrition assessments throughout the country twice yearly: FSNMS.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

likelihood (Low) and impact (high) UNICEF has a zero-tolerance policy for child safeguarding violations and sexual exploitation and abuse perpetrated by its employees, personnel, sub-contractors and implementing partners. In South Sudan, a combination of various factors (i.e., high prevalence of Sexual Gender Based Violence, conflict, the high vulnerability of beneficiaries dependent on humanitarian assistance to meet basic needs, elevated presence of humanitarian staff and related personnel, weak rule of law…) makes the country a high-risk environment for (SEA. Following the endorsement of the United Nations Protocol on Allegations of SEA Involving Implementing Partners in February 2018, UNICEF has incorporated child safeguarding and prevention of SEA standards in contractual clauses of all partnership agreements signed between UNICEF and Civil Society Organisation (CSO) partners. In addition to that, in February 2020, UNICEF South Sudan has started to roll-out UNICEF new global Procedure on Managing Risks of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse in Implementing Partnerships which includes a more stringent assessment for all CSOs partners, as foreseen by the above-mentioned UN Protocol. The preventions of sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA) assessment rates organizational capacities to prevent/ respond to SEA and covers six core standards: Policy, Management and Human Resources systems in place, Mandatory training, Reporting, Assistance and referrals for survivors and Investigations. The organizational capacity rating converts to a SEA risk rating (High, Moderate, Low); the principle being that UNICEF cannot enter in a partnership with a CSO with a High SEA risk rating. When the procedure was rolled-out in February 2020, all UNICEF partners rated at Moderate and High risk of not having systems and risk mitigation measures in place for PSEA were invited to develop an action plan to identify and address the organizational gaps identified during the assessment. In parallel, the UNICEF PSEA Toolkit was launched in to support partners to improve PSEA organizational risk mitigation measures. This comprehensive tool is used by partners to develop additional PSEA policies, procedures and systems within their respective organisation. UNICEF also held numerous workshops and one-on-one support to partners. likelihood (Low) and impact (high) Older persons, women and girls will be consulted through community leaders and lead mothers during establishment and provision of nutrition services. Particularly, lead mothers are the bridge between the women/girls and nutrition service providers. Thus, UNICEF encourages all partners to include mothers as community nutrition volunteers throughout the development of Partnership Cooperation Agreements (PCA)/Programme Documents (PDs). likelihood (Low) and impact (medium) Community Nutrition Volunteers and lead mothers will be engaged in this project and will play a key role in sharing information about the project in their respective communities, bridging the communities and nutrition service providers to bring complaints to nutrition workers and give them feedback. likelihood (Low) and impact (low) The registration books in nutrition sites are confidential.
