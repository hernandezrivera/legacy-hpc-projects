Project Summary

The IRC’s project aligns with its global vision, Country Strategic Action Plan 2025–2028, and the JRP 2025 objectives. Activities and target beneficiaries are prioritized through community consultations, protection monitoring data, client feedback initiatives, ISNA, assessment reports, insights from field teams, and a legal gaps analysis. An active member of the Protection Sector SAG, ATWG, ADWG, GDHWG, PSEA Network, and Peace and Security Working Group, monthly 5W reports, protection monitoring updates, and border monitoring updates, and participate in camp-level coordination meetings with CICs and other stakeholders.
The project will continue in Camps 1E, 1W, 15, 16, 21, and 25—where IRC has been running integrated protection programs since 2017—and in 10 unions in Ukhiya and Teknaf. Operations will expand into Camps 5, 6, 13, and 22, fostering complementarities by integrating GBV, CP, health, and livelihood interventions to offer clients a comprehensive service package under a unified approach. IRC’s IPs will carry out legal assistance, CBP, CwC, social cohesion and peace building, and community engagement activities in host communities.
The project adopts community-based, AGD-responsive approaches central to its design and interventions. These include adopting WGQs in protection and border monitoring, advocacy initiatives, two-way communication, and community engagement. Key activities involve protection analysis to uncover rights violations and access concerns for diverse groups, case management for individuals at heightened protection risks, and IPA support informed by UNHCR profiling and regular protection monitoring. Vulnerable individuals, including persons with disabilities and the elderly, will receive portable solar-powered torchlights as part of IPA support to mitigate protection threats as part of IPA and case management clients following individual protection risk assessment, vulnerability and safety assessments.
IRC will strengthen inclusive and integrated CBP structures (50% male,50% female), including gender-diverse populations and PWDs at camp level, and sub-committee at block level to handle non-criminal cases and ensure timely referrals for essential services. CBPs will conduct regular protection risk analyses to inform action plans and create awareness of environmental conservation and protection. They will also engage in peacebuilding and conflict resolution initiatives to promote coexistence between refugees and host communities through community dialogues, meetings, and recreational activities, besides block level CBPs will facilitate sports and cultural activities. IRC will develop IEC and BCC materials, while leveraging the online InfoSheba platform to amplify these efforts. Furthermore, IRC will conduct a study and initiate advocacy initiatives at both Cox’s Bazar and national levels. IRC will engage with youth networks to address community tensions through youth-led initiatives.
The project integrates climate-sensitive actions, including a multi-sector assessment on climate change and associated protection risks, disaster preparedness for monsoon and cyclone responses, and identifying negative coping mechanisms. IRC will provide legal assistance in host communities, particularly for women and adolescent girls, and deliver protection mainstreaming training to IRC staff, partners, and other sectors. These trainings will emphasize safety, dignity, accountability, meaningful access, and participation.
IRC’s CFRM ensures accountability at office and field levels. IRC conducts awareness campaigns on SEA, child safeguarding, and reporting mechanisms. Capacity-building initiatives are conducted for IRC and partner staff and volunteers. Additionally, IRC’s established 2-way communication system ensures life-saving information is effectively disseminated and responsive to community needs in both host and camp settings.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

Now in its eighth year, the protracted crisis is a protection crisis and its being compounded by climate change. The Rohingya, the world's largest stateless population, have faced decades of severe persecution, systematic discrimination, and targeted violence by successive Myanmar governments, leading to their mass displacement to Cox’s Bazar where over 1,010,298 (including new arrivals) reside (GoB/UNHCR Oct 2024). The 2023 Age and Disability Inclusion Needs Assessment highlighted that 12% of refugees have a disability. This trend is similarly reflected in the 2024 ISNA, where 11% of female and 13% of male respondents reported having at least one disability. Besides, UNHCR-GoB factsheet (September 2023) identify 3.02% persons with disabilities and 4% elderly persons. The overall camp security situation remains highly volatile, deteriorated further by the reduced presence of APBn, limited patrol, and efforts to maintain law and order in the camps (Joint protection monitoring report, Q3, 2024). The presence of armed groups has compromised the civilian and humanitarian nature of the camps with security incidents of killings, abductions, use of arms, forced recruitment and extortion being prevalent and widespread across the 33 over congested camps (ISNA, 2024). The escalation of violent in Rakhine state in Myanmar forced Rohingyas to flee in search of safety in Bangladesh (Protection Flash Update Report, #1, May 2024). The ISNA 2024 revealed theft as the most common protection concern at 51%, the presence of organized groups was the second most reported concern at 48% as refugees are scared due to increased gunshots and confrontations between organized groups and increase in abduction and kidnapping were also prevalent concerns for 38% of the households. It is worth noting that approximately one third (33%) of households reported that at least one member showed symptoms of psychological distress which increased significantly in camps 1E,1W,5, 6,13, 15,16,21, 22 and 25 at 55% and the increase linked to compromised security situations and the high likelihood to develop mental health symptoms. Those experiencing psychological distress manifest it in the form of excessive worry or feelings of being unsafe at 67%, and difficulty sleeping including insomnia and restless sleep at 53%.The most significant protection risk unearthed was crime and violence including presence of arms groups, abduction, and extorsion which increased from 48% as JMSNA 2023 to 61% ISNA, 2024 perpetrated by armed groups. The ISNA 2024 revealed that Rohingya influx poses challenges to the formal justice system, impacting its effectiveness and community trust. Investigations of cases face significant delays, and obtaining witness testimony becomes difficult, hindering the progress of justice. Mainly, lack of awareness 93%, financial constraints 85%, accessibility problems 75%, mistrust in the justice system 73% are the main reasons limited access to justice. Reasons for reluctancy included fear of victimization, costly legal procedures, lengthy legal procedures, and a lack of trust in the legal system. The camps face varied risks and vulnerabilities, with limited emergency preparedness and response capabilities. 19% of areas across 33 camps are highly susceptible to landslides, 8% of shelters are at high risk of flooding, and 97% of households are highly vulnerable to fires and cyclones. Additionally, there are significant gaps in AAP within the Rohingya response: 27.76% of individuals are unaware of how to file complaints, 43.23% have not received feedback on their concerns, and 17.36% of respondents reported dissatisfaction with the response.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

The project is firmly centered on protection mainstreaming, ensuring that humanitarian interventions prioritize the safety, dignity, and rights of affected populations. By embedding a protection-focused approach, IRC aims to prevent, reduce, and address risks and vulnerabilities throughout the Project Cycle Management (PCM). This approach involves designing and implementing activities that tackle protection risks and potential violations while addressing the structural factors that create vulnerabilities, thereby amplifying the protective benefits of humanitarian aid. A key element of the project is building the capacity of both IRC staff and external partners to adhere to protection principles, ensuring activities do no harm, promote safety and dignity, and enhance meaningful access, accountability, participation, and empowerment across all phases of the project.Protection Mainstreaming Action Plan (PMAP) will be incorporated into ongoing monitoring and evaluation, allowing for consistent assessment of activities, services, and outcomes through a protection lens. Regular PMAP reviews will track progress, adjust to emerging risks, and identify new opportunities, ensuring that the project can proactively manage unintended impacts, mitigate negative outcomes, and strengthen positive effects. Grounded in a rights-based framework, this approach ensures programming is inclusive, reliable, and of high quality, considering age, gender, disability, cultural appropriateness, and specific community needs.Therefore, IRC will use the PM Scorecard for periodic evaluations, ensuring remedial measures address any gaps identified. Community consultations and participatory risk assessments will engage diverse groups—ensuring that interventions are inclusive and meet community-specific needs. Analysis of power dynamics and cultural norms will guide interventions to promote equity and strengthen community resources, focusing on including the most vulnerable people.

Adolescents and youth will be engaged to mitigate protection risks, particularly gender-based violence, while social cohesion initiatives will create inclusive spaces for host and camp communities.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

The IRC and its implementing partners are committed to a robust AAP Framework at both global and national levels, aligned with the IASC’s AAP commitments. This approach integrates AAP principles throughout all facets of humanitarian programming, underscoring the organization’s dedication to client responsiveness, an essential organizational priority. By emphasizing the meaningful participation of people receiving aid, the IRC seeks to enhance the relevance, effectiveness, and appropriateness of its services. Fundamental to the IRC’s AAP work is its adherence to a strict Code of Conduct, a Zero-Tolerance Policy on Sexual Exploitation, Abuse, Harassment, and strong child safeguarding measures, ensuring accountability toward clients, affected communities, and all stakeholders.The IRC’s CFRM facilitates the collection, processing, and response to service-related feedback and complaints, handled by trained staff and volunteers through satisfaction surveys and other feedback mechanisms. The project’s CBP component supports community involvement in decision-making processes, with regular consultations conducted in camps to identify key issues and ensure program adjustments based on community needs. Resilience within these communities is bolstered through engagement, transparency, and IRC’s multiple channels, including feedback boxes, emails, helplines, and a two-way communication system to respond to individual and collective feedback promptly.As a proactive member of the PSEA Network, the IRC helps maintain a SEA reporting system for its staff and partners, working closely to reduce risks. Additionally, a safeguarding strategy—emphasizing child safeguarding—is in place, ensuring IRC projects and personnel avoid causing harm, while any safety concerns or misconduct cases are promptly reported and addressed by a designated focal point at each project location.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

The IRC and its implementing partners (YPSA, PULSE BD, and BNWLA) are committed to integrating PSEA into the Rohingya Refugee Response in Bangladesh by following a comprehensive framework, including the IRC’s safeguarding policy, the Core Humanitarian Standards, and the Minimum Operating Standards for PSEA (MOS-PSEA). The IRC will ensure that all staff and partners are fully informed of, and comply with, the organization’s codes of conduct and safeguarding policies. A rigorous hiring process will include screening for any history of SEA, including self-declarations by candidates. The Senior Management Team (SMT) will lead policy implementation, fostering a zero-tolerance culture with ongoing training on PSEA. Dedicated Safeguarding staff and PSEA Focal Points will work with the project team and PSEA Network to align PSEA. IRC will offer tailored, context-specific PSEA training for staff and partners, especially frontline workers, to build capacity in a “do no harm” approach and confidentiality. Community engagement is key: IRC will develop complaint and feedback mechanisms that are accessible, safe, and clear to beneficiaries at service points. A survivor-centered approach ensures survivors' safety, confidentiality, and access to support services without the obligation to formally report. Feedback mechanisms, including in-person reports, hotlines, and complaint boxes, will allow for reporting across genders and ages. IRC maintains a mandatory reporting policy for SEA involving minors under 18. As the reporting of SEA is a mandatory requirement, IRC is committed to notifying the donor and relevant stakeholders of any SEA concerns. High-quality investigations will be conducted with the intent to support survivors, hold perpetrators accountable, and strengthen prevention. Regular field visits, interviews, and focus groups will monitor implementation, share best practices, and improve program quality.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

As the climate crisis intensifies, Rohingya refugees face increasing risks that worsen their already vulnerable conditions. Limited access to Liquid Petroleum Gas (LPG) within the camps forces many refugees, especially women and children, to gather firewood for cooking. This accelerates deforestation—intensifying local climate impacts—and exposes them to significant protection risks, including harassment, violence, and exploitation, as well as health hazards like respiratory issues and burns. Inadequate lighting in camp areas, such as around shelters, latrines, and pathways, further endangers safety, raising the risk of violence, including sexual assault, and enabling the presence of armed groups, which undermines community security.In response, IRC’s Protection Program is integrating climate adaptation and mitigation strategies to address these resource and safety challenges. Addressing the LPG shortage is a priority; IRC will conduct a protection analysis to support advocacy efforts aimed at increasing sustainable energy provisions, reducing environmental degradation, and minimizing risks of violence and exploitation. IRC will also work with camp management, community volunteers, and other sectors to improve lighting in high-risk areas by advocating for solar lighting solutions that enhance safety while preserving the environment. Therefore, portable torch lights powered by solar will be provided as part of IPA and case management clients following individual protection risk assessment, vulnerability and safety assessments.Through two-way communication, IRC will disseminate climate-related information, strengthening community awareness and resilience to climate impacts. To support an environmentally sustainable approach, IRC is incorporating Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) and Disaster Risk Management (DRM) elements across operations, recognizing that climate-related vulnerabilities and protection risks are particularly severe for women, girls, and children.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

The IRC’s Protection in Emergency Response Unit is dedicated to swiftly address protection concerns during disasters like monsoons and cyclones, working closely with the protection sector and IRC’s EPR team. This unit focuses on safeguarding vulnerable populations—especially women, girls, children, and persons with disabilities—by strengthening Community-Based Protection (CBP) Committees that are vital for enhancing resilience and disaster preparedness. Comprising community members, including women, these committees are trained to identify and respond to protection risks within their communities. The IRC builds their capacity through training, multi-hazard awareness messages, and provides essential emergency response equipment and personal protective gear to support safe and effective responses during emergencies. Activities include Fire Safety Assessments and simulation drills. By integrating Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) into the Protection sector, the IRC prioritizes business continuity planning, emergency response protocols, community preparedness, capacity building, and the provision of essential equipment, ultimately enhancing the safety and well-being of vulnerable individuals. Collaboration with energy and environmental networks supports climate mitigation and adaptation, further reducing risks and promoting resilience in the face of disasters.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

IRC will engage youth by fostering their involvement in community-based protection structures (CBPs) and encouraging youth leadership in protection activities. Youth will be trained to participate in protection risk analysis, enabling them to identify community-specific risks, incidents, and trends that impact their peers. Youth-led (including women) initiatives will promote peacebuilding and conflict resolution through community dialogues and recreational activities that strengthen connections among young refugees and between refugees and host communities. By participating in climate change and disaster preparedness assessments, youth will gain valuable skills and contribute to resilience planning. The project will also offer targeted legal and protection mainstreaming training to youth, equipping them with knowledge on safety, dignity, and accountability in service delivery. The two-way communication system will support youth engagement by sharing relevant, life-saving information and providing channels for youth feedback. Additionally, the IRC’s Complaint Feedback and Response Mechanism (CFRM) offers youth a trusted avenue for reporting concerns and safeguarding their rights, encouraging active participation in protection efforts across camps and host communities.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

This IRC project fosters self-reliance among refugees by establishing inclusive community-based protection structures and strengthening local systems to manage non-criminal cases, promoting peaceful coexistence through conflict resolution, and ensuring access to essential services. By delivering protection case management, refugees can reclaim their rights, build resilience, and participate in informed decision-making. The project’s focus on environmental awareness, disaster preparedness, and gender-sensitive approaches further enables sustainable livelihoods in coordination with Livelihood Sectors.
