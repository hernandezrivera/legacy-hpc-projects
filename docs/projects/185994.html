Project Summary

The project aims at Strengthening Food Access and Building Resilience through Agricultural Support to 472,614 Vulnerable IDPs, Returnees and Host communities in Yobe and Borno States.
The project will be implemented for a duration of 12 months across 6 LGAs of Yobe and 1 LGA of Borno State through three (03) major interventions:
- The provision of E-Voucher (CBT) to 326,182 beneficiaries covering at least 70% of food basket based monthly on the prevailing MEB data that will be conducted in the first phase of the project.
- The provision of support to 326,182 beneficiaries towards accessing cooking fuel monthly cooking fuel, fuel-efficient cookstoves, and capacity building on briquetting of agricultural/organic waste through the most appropriate modalities.
- The provision of needed inputs and capacity building to strengthen resilience of crisis-affected people by re-establishing, improving and diversifying key agriculture livelihoods (including crop production livestock, forestry, and natural resources management) for 313,079 beneficiaries. 78,014 beneficiaries will receive capacity building and agriculture extension services for agriculture-based business entrepreneurship, technology transfer, value addition, processing and other cross-cutting issues including environment and protection.

1 - Needs

The ongoing conflict in north-east Nigeria, now entering its eleventh year, and the upsurge in violent attacks witnessed over the past year in the crisis-affected states of Borno, Adamawa and Yobe. According to the Nigerian 2021 Humanitarian Response Plan (February 2021), 1.92 million people are internally displaced, and 257,000 have sought refuge in neighboring Cameroon, Chad and Niger. The majority (54%) of the internally displaced people (IDPs) have found refuge in host communities. Borno State has 81% of the IDPs, of whom slightly more than half (54%) stay in IDP camps. In 2020, some 81,000 newly displaced people arrived in camps and the host communities across the BAY states. The armed conflict has no clear end in sight. The Nigerian Armed Forces’ strategy (since mid-2019) of focusing on regrouping troops into ’super camps,’ while improving security for IDPs who had gathered in sites within adjacent ‘garrison towns,’ has affected security and protection for IDPs or other civilians outside of these areas, as well as attempts to reach them with humanitarian aid. The prospects for displaced people’s safe return to their areas of origin are tenuous, though some areas of origin are conducive for return, or could be made conducive because they are relatively safe and accessible to humanitarian actors (generally more so in Yobe and Adamawa states than in Borno state). Nigerian refugees in Cameroon and Niger continue to arrive into bordering LGAs in Nigeria due to insecurity and poor living conditions in their areas of asylum. Some 6,000 Nigerian refugees crossed international borders into IDP camps in 2020, many of them (68%) into Damasak and Bama towns. The operating environment remains extremely volatile, particularly in Borno State, where all the major supply routes have become dangerous—due to risk of attacks by non-state armed groups (NSAGs), as well as from unexploded ordnance and improvised landmines. This poses a risk to civilians and, moreover, aid workers, humanitarian cargo and assets. Humanitarian hubs and aid organizations’ offices suffered regular attacks in 2021. This decade-long armed conflict in northeastern Nigeria has severely affected food production systems in this part of the country. In fact, Adamawa, Borno and Yobe being the most affected states and experiencing high level of displacement, enormous human, social and economic losses, causing high levels of acute food insecurity and malnutrition. Of the 2.2 million IDPs in the three northeastern states, more than 80% are in Borno state The influx of IDPs is placing an additional burden on host communities who already face limited access to land and other resources thus affecting food production. Vulnerable households also have less access to humanitarian services due to the inaccessibility of some areas deemed ‘no go’ amid security concerns. As communities struggle with the effects of COVID-19 and related containment measures, including income losses, trade disruptions and rising food prices, other factors such as drought, floods, pests and diseases have also led to reduced crop yields and livestock productivity. This forces the majority of affected populations to depend on humanitarian assistance for their survival. Food security assistance for Damaturu, Jakusko, Geidam, Yunusari and Kaga LGA area of intervention has the potentials of contributing to saving lives, especially as this intervention targets the most vulnerable population. By providing safe and meaningful physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food to meet their dietary needs and food preferences, it contributes to an active and healthy life (FAO).

2 - Activities

Activities or outputs Food assistance • Community participation meetings to select the most in need vulnerable households, ensuring participation of the most vulnerable groups within the target communities and equal representation of man and women • Generate beneficiary data/ distribution lists • Vendors selection through clear criteria: geographic proximity with the communities, capacity and quality of storage, quantity and variety of food items. Vendors will be required to have a bank account and a safe. • Cash based transfer to the most in need households in line with the FSSWG and the CWG harmonized guidelines • Conduct PDM and establish an effective complaints handling and feedback mechanism • Routine monitoring of protection/GBV concerns in food assistance activities to ensure safety and dignity of beneficiaries accessing assistance • Routine monitoring and community sensitization • Price monitoring/ continuous market assessments Farming kits distribution • Selection of the beneficiaries • Distribution of farming kits (seeds, fertilizers, tools) to the most food unsecured households • Training on agricultural production • Monitoring of the correct utilization of the tools and the techniques Indicators and targets. Assumptions: • Harvest prospects: Main harvests in the area will be substantially below-average following attacks and resultant population displacements in the area that have reduced the farming population and restricted farming activities. In the proposed areas, cultivation activities occur only within the territories of the LGA headquarters. Restrictions on cultivation of tall growing crops are further limiting the main harvest prospects in the area. Similarly, land access constrains, inputs such as fertilizer and IEDs will continue to restrict the level of cultivation in the area. • Household stocks: Household stock levels will increase during the main harvest, though stock levels will be below average following the anticipated reduction in the main harvest. Household stocks will deplete faster than usual, one to two months earlier than the typical lean season period and most households will resort to markets for food earlier than normal. • Market functioning levels: Due to continuing attacks and insecurity, market function is expected to remain at a maximum of 40 percent, most markets in the northeast will continue to function below the pre-conflict period through May 2020. • Sources of income: Agricultural labour opportunities during the main harvest and dry season cultivation, construction work, firewood sales, petty trading, craft making, cap knitting, and labour migration will provide limited income for the vulnerable population in the area. Income sources and wages will generally remain below average during the period. • Coping strategies: Households will continue to engage in intense labour opportunities, petty trading, and firewood sales to earn limited income and to access food. Others will solely depend on limited humanitarian assistance for food. Women will engage in domestic work in better off households and children will be encouraged to beg to earn income and food.

7.1.a - Please mention the HDP package and its goal, its other components and key actors, and your project’s interaction with them

The complexity of the current protracted humanitarian crisis has led the international community to realize that a silos approach is inefficient to respond to the needs of conflict-affected populations. As emphasized at the 2016 World Humanitarian Summit (WHS), it is of utmost urgency to mainstream humanitarian, development and peace realms into an integrated action capitalizing on their respective comparative advantages to successfully achieve the 2030 Agenda - Sustainable Development Goals. Indeed, there is worldwide growing recognition that there can be no peace without sustainable development and no sustainable development without peace. COOPI fully embraces this "New Way of Working" and, in alignment with the HDP Nexus, it is proposing a project with a cross-cutting and inter-sectoral perspective which synergically combines life-saving humanitarian assistance with long-term development activities and peace-building dialogue sessions at community level. This is based on the premise that to achieve long-lasting peaceful coexistence between IDPs, returnees and host communities, it is paramount to address immediate basic needs through a development lens which does not only ensure early recovery but aims to strengthen beneficiaries' resilience to conflict-driven and climate stressors in the long-run. As a proof, besides promoting the recovery of the main livelihoods and the rehabilitation of basic public infrastructures, COOPI simultaneously aims to provide skills-training for youth and capacity-building sessions for LGAs departments as well as to carry out peace-mitigation and conflict-management dialogue sessions at community level. By doing so, COOPI's goal complimentarily encompasses a strong contribution to social cohesion, economic development and self-reliant livelihoods through a localization approach and state system's strengthening: both of them ensuring the sustainability of the action and thus an enduring peace consolidation. To this purpose, COOPI relies on a broad variety of partners, widely ranging from LGAs departments and International Organizations to local associations and community leaders, all across the three interlinked sectors of humanitarian assistance, development and peace building

8.6 - Please describe briefly the modalities your project will put in place to ensure protection mainstreaming (particularly referring to the above questions).

Mainstream protection into humanitarian assistance programs Protectionmainstreaming ensures that human rights and their promotion are central to eachintervention, laying the foundations for reconstruction and more equitable andsustainable development. In line with the international developments within thetheoretical framework, COOPI also ensures the full participation and involvement ofwomen, men, boys, and girls. Protection is mainstreaming in the key component of thedesign and the implementation of each step of the project. COOPI will closely monitorthe respect of its application. A strong sensitization will be carried out among thebeneficiaries. They will be informed of their right, and how to report any breach.Redemptions will be conducted in locations that are within easy reach for the targetbeneficiaries and during the daytime to ensure that beneficiaries get to their homes beforedark. The planning of project days and times will be done in a manner that will notinterfere much with other community daily activities and programs targeted atchildren, pregnant and breastfeeding women, and girls. Special considerations forpregnant and breastfeeding women and girls, children, and people with specific needswill be made to ensure that priority is given to them during distributions. The staff on-site will be recognizable by wearing visibility and reflective jackets. COOPI staffinvolved in the project will be trained and sensitized on the COOPI Code of Ethics andCOOPI Nigeria Child Protection Code of Conduct. All trainees will be asked to signCOOPI’s Code of Conduct documents to ensure accountability and reporting after thecompletion of the training sessions. Beneficiaries and community leaders will besensitized on PSEA and the COOPI internal policies and Code of Conduct in addition toreporting channels such as the internal COOPI whistleblower policy via email orhelpline phone number. In each LGA Protection Officer and one CRM assistant willbe available to gather and answer the questions of the members of thecommunities. They will fill a complaint book that will record all the complaints. Atevery step of the program, beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries will be reminded that theyare free to use this platform to share information or complaints. A help and feedbackdesk and complaint boxes will also be set up at each distribution site for the peoplethat would like to remain anonymous. In order to reduce inter-communal tensions andfoster impartiality, COOPI will select beneficiaries according tovulnerability criteria only, not taking into consideration their status (Host communitiesand IDPs). Gender will be taken into account in the selection of the beneficiaries.However, the increased knowledge of the women regarding food security and nutritionwill empower them and might foster a more important role in the decision-makingamong the households. The Project Management Committees formed by COOPI in theprevious phase of the project has a strong woman representation and they willcontinue to be involved in the monitoring of the project. Gender and protection will betaken into account in the entire project implementation. The strong presence of womenin the project and their continuous protection will indulge COOPI to make sure theproject respects the safety, dignity, and integrity of the women, men, girls, andboys receiving the assistance. COOPI monitoring team will ensure that the rights of allbeneficiaries are fully respected and prevent gender oppressive stereotypes.

9.5 - How will your project ensure feedback is collected from the most vulnerable community members?

COOPI follows the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC)’s Accountability to Affected Populations Framework and the 2010 Humanitarian Accountability Partnership’s (HAP) Standard in Accountability and Quality Management to set standards/benchmarks in its accountability approach to emergencies. COOPI already has in place a Complaint Response Mechanism (CRM) with Community Feedback Assistants and Project Management Committees in alignment with guidance provided by Nutrition Sector, and donors. COOPI will promotes a comprehensive community participatory approach whereby community members are constantly involved in all the project phases from the need assessment, project design and following activities’ implementation. These include children, persons with disability, teachers, SBMC, key interest groups, nutrition committees, school management committees, protection committees, LGAs and other relevant stakeholders for all three sectors of intervention. COOPI will put in place a Compliant Response Mechanism (CRM) whereby beneficiaries can channel/express their grievances and seek redress. To this purpose, COOPI will employ dedicated Complaints Reporting Mechanism (CRM) Officers whose responsibility will be collecting and responding to complaints and feedbacks. Where the nature or the subject of the complaint exceeds his/her jurisdiction, the complaint will be referred to a designated member of COOPI’s Senior Management Team (SMT) to manage the complaint. Furthermore, all COOPI will set up complaint desks and suggestion boxes in areas that maintain anonymous usage and that are physically accessible to all during crucial activities to receive complaints and feedback from beneficiaries and concerned community members. They will also provide Hotline Telephone contacts of the focal person (CRM Officer) to community members and posted in each community. All reported cases, as well as solutions, shall be recorded for future learning.
