Project Summary

After over seven years of violence, the Syrian war remains one of the most dangerous conflicts for children. The child protection needs across Syria are immense, far outweighing the available provision of necessary services. Conflict affected girls, boys and their families are facing appalling protection, neglect and deprivation risks on a daily basis, exacerbated by conflict related stress, exclusion or separation from their families. Report shows many IDPs have been displaced up to 10 times during the past years (IRC protection monitoring report) and therefore have adopted negative coping mechanisms to sustain themselves.
The SC will provide life-saving assistance and promote a protective environment for conflict-affected children and their families through strengthening resilience and protection. Establishing or strengthening existing community-based child protection mechanism provision of specialised child protection services and through child protection capacity building and integration shall be the key focus for this project..
Best practices in systems strengthening through community-based child protection approach in North Eastand South Central Syria shall be replicated in North West Syria. Trainings, regular meetings and action planning to identify, prevent and respond to child protection issues by the Child protection committees at the community level. Structured psychosocial support activities at the child and adolescent friendly spaces Outreach sessions to ensure social inclusion of that vulnerable including children with disabilities will be delivered to communities. These sessions shall v raise awareness on violence against children, mine risk education and the prevention of family separation.
In order to strengthen children’s protective environment SC shall engage parents and caregivers in parenting without violence session and mother- toddler groups, with priority given to child mothers and vulnerable caregivers.Case management will be delivered by trained and technically supported case workers through coaching and supervision to reach vulnerable and at-risk children, including unaccompanied and separated children, child survivors of sexual and gender based violence and child labour, SC will promote and advocate for appropriate alternative care for UASC, with a focus on community-based and family-based care. Capacity building to case management teams, relevant authorities and foster parents to strengthen the quality of care. In both NE Syria and NW Syria, SC has conducted UASC assessments to review the alternative care systems and to advocate for best practices.
At the inter-agency coordination level, SC will continue coordinating with other protection actors in the implementation of CP activities and will maintain active participation in sub-cluster meetings and other relevant coordination forums including Protection Working Group in NE Syria.and camp-level case management meetings. The lead role on child protection coordination in Al Hole will soon be with SC . In NW Syria, SC remains an active member of the Child Protection Sub Cluster. At the WoS level, SC plays a key role in WoS child protection coordination, while formal meetings are not taking place. SC will lead on a number of technical initiatives, including the development of case management SOPs, Information Sharing protocols training and roll out f CPIMS+ database for NE that is due in the first quarter and on the development of alternative care guidance. In the Turkey hub, SC is on the technical review committee under the Child Protection Sub Cluster. SC also works closely with other actors to raise awareness on safe identification and referral, and in turn, also works with actors to refer children to basic services.

4 - Needs

The HNO identified that protection remains a great concern in Syria. These concerns are both varied yet inter-related. Specifically, children continue to face pervasive violence and a range of protection risks: death, injury, involvement in armed groups, torture, detention, abduction, sexual violence, early marriage and child labor. These risks affect children’s wellbeing – weakening their sense of security, purpose, control and significance as well as causing profound distress for children and caregivers. It is estimated that over 2.6 million children remain displaced. WV needs assessment found that UASC were the most vulnerable IDP group. Male suffered from deep psychosocial tensions, frequent displacement and stresses of unemployment whereas females identified girls forced child marriage, female headed-household and people with disabilities as the top three most vulnerable groups. As of November 12th, 74,381 people remain displaced in NES, including from Al-Hassakeh (48,134), Ar-Raqqa (19,471) and Aleppo (6,776) governorates. [1] In addition to the newly displaced, around 92,000 IDPs and refugees remain in Al Hol, Areesha, Mahmoudli, Newroz and Roj camps.Al-Hol alone continues to hold approximately 73,000 Iraqi refugees, Syrian IDPs and families of foreign fighters. Many of the families staying in collective shelters will be asked to relocate to IDP camps, some of which are currently being expanded or are newly (re)-opening. Currently, there are 28,000 foreign children in NES, 20,000 of whom are Iraqi children. The remaining 8,000 foreign children are from 60 other countries.Half of these foreign children are under 5 years old. There are currently foreign children living in three camps in NES: Mahmoudli camp (10 Iraqi, 25 foreign children), Al-Hol camp and Annex (19,500 Iraqi children, 7,100 foreign children), and in Roj camp (200 Iraqi, 950 foreign children). There are 68 foreign children in 3 interim care centers in NES.

5 - Implementation of activities

SC shall implemented through two modalities- directly and indirectly with partners, aims to implement: Community based CP mechanisms in targeted locations. CP Committees (CPCs) will be established or where existing, supported through training to prevent and respond to CP concerns.and adolescent groups supported to actively engage in developing child-led CP initiatives. The provision of safe and protective child and adolescent spaces to engage in structured, sustained child protection programmes, including PSS provided through static and mobile interventions, designed in consultation with children and communities. This will include the implementation of SC’s Child Resilience programme and Healing and Education through Arts (HEART) programme. Outreach to engage the most vulnerable children and adolescents in PSS and CP activities, including children with disabilities and adolescent girls The implementation of SC’s Common Approach to Parenting Without Violence, including mother-toddler groups and positive parenting groups. SC will focus on involving caregivers from particularly vulnerable and at-risk families in the programme, including young mothers and child mothers Outreach for awareness raising and the facilitation of community events to prevent and respond to CP issues, in consultation with CPCs and child clubs. CP and GBV situation monitoring conducted together with children and women through the implementation of participatory consultation and observation tools. Individual case management support to girls and boys, service providers and families, including outreach, registration, assessment, case planning, follow up, direct service provision, referrals, monitoring, case review and Individual Protection Assistance (NFIs, referrals, etc.) provided to vulnerable and children based on their case plans, and the provision of appropriate, community-based, alternative care for UASC

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk: SCI or partner staff violating SCI CSG rules resulting in harm to children All SC staff, volunteers and representatives sign the Child Safeguarding Policy and Code of Conduct, child safeguarding risk assessments are conducted for all project sites and mitigating actions and put in place, all staff are trained on the Child Safeguarding Policy and Code of Conduct, with regular refreshers, and a confidential reporting mechanism and disseminated. Risk: Affected populations and SC staff and volunteers, are placed at risk of sexual exploitation and abuse, including harassment. Training will be provided to SC staff and volunteers on SC’s Anti-Harassment Policy and Code of Conduct. SC will also continue to provide trainings on (PSEA) through SC’s PSEA focal points. Confidential reporting mechanisms will be maintained, with reports thoroughly and confidentiality investigated with follow up action plans implemented. Risk: Community tension due to service delivery locations or types of services offered in one community Continuously ensure service mapping, consultation with community on program interventions, regular communication with different communities on availability of services including host communities and returnee populations. Expansion of services to non-camp locations where needs are present, to avoid concentration of services in camps. Regular consultations with communities, FGDs, feedback boxes Risk: Inadequately trained service providers and/or low-quality specialized CP/PSS services results in doing harm or perpetuating unintended consequences to girls, boys and their caregivers availing of the services. This can include re-traumatization without referral to higher-level PSS. It can also result in traumatizing service providers themselves. With more specialized services, we will ensure clear messaging on what services are available to survivors and ensure strong support mechanisms are in place for PSS professionals and those delivering PFA

7 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

(S&S) All partner staff involved in this project are aware of the evacuation procedures and routes if needed. Vehicles for quick evacuation of staff are available, and positioned in an appropriate place. Project field teams and field offices keep in regular, agreed up contact, as per security protocols. SC and its partners have identified both the risks associated with the project and accompanying mitigation measures. Staff movements must be pre-approved and refresher security trainings regularly conducted. The Hostile Environment Awareness Training is mandatory for NES deployments; local staff are trained on Advanced Personal S&S. SC’s partners engage with relevant stakeholders and receive timely updates. SC works on a common response to unacceptable conditions. -Airstrikes SC supports partners to immediately report security incidents to be analysed and develop and implement comprehensive safety plans. -Loss of access SC and partners conduct thorough security assessments and maintain updated contingency plans. If necessary, SC will suspend programs and work to resume activities. If this is not viable, SC would assess possibilities for relocation. Work permits: Measures are in place to mitigate the Gov. of Turkey (GoT) legislative environment hindering SC’s ability to operate, I.e. using a third-party HR company to overcome work permit challenges. -Diversion of resources to terrorist/ proscribed groups In 2018, interviews were conducted to identify areas for increased risk mitigation; an action plan was developed. SC launched an initiative with OCHA to provide external training on international humanitarian law and negotiation with armed actors. Partners have their own systems of anti-terrorism vetting checks for all contracted suppliers and SC’s Syria office vets against the third-party database for procurements $750+. Disruption to cash transfer operations, SC is conducting a review of Hawala delivery to identify mitigation measures/additional suppliers.
