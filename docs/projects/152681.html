Project Summary

The UNICEF Child Protection programme provides support to the most vulnerable, conflict-affected girls, boys and women in South Sudan who are exposed to child protection risks and gender based violence. The programme includes providing case management and family tracing and reunification services, psychosocial support, reintegration interventions for children associated with armed forces and groups (CAAFAG), mine risk education (MRE) monitoring of grave child rights violations through the monitoring and reporting mechanism (MRM), gender based violence interventions, and critical, frontline emergency services, through integrated rapid response mechanisms (IRRMs). Within the programme budget $19.4m is needed for CPiE, $7.5m for GBV and $1.6m for MRE.

Needs

Children continue to be disproportionately affected by the ongoing conflict, with high levels of population displacement and family separation affecting over one million children, causing severe psychological distress and leaving children vulnerable to exploitation, abuse and neglect. Nearly 18500 children have been registered as unaccompanied, separated or missing since December 2013, with 7,378 cases remaining open. With the expansion of comprehensive case management services in South Sudan in line with the newly endorsed SOP for comprehensive case management, there is a need to upgrade and transit from the current CPIMS database to CPIMS+ and roll out the case management guidelines and coaching and supervision training for all implementing partners. Further capacity building is needed on identification and registration, family tracing and reunification, and interim care arrangements is needed to ensure the continued protection of children. Since 2014, some 2,700 incidents of grave violations against children have been verified by the UN. 19,000 children– mainly boys – are reported to have been recruited into armed forces and groups since December 2013, creating a high need for disarmament, demobilization and reintegration support. More than 3500 informally and formally released children are receiving support through a three-year community based reintegration programme and are in need of continuous support to fully mainstream them into being rehabilitated citizens. Meanwhile, girls are less likely to access school and psychosocial support (PSS) activities due to their traditional responsibilities in the home. Over a million children are estimated to be suffering from profound psychosocial distress. PSS activities in communities and schools are vital to help children deal with their experiences, and efforts are ongoing to strengthen community based support mechanisms. Child Protection - GBVGender based violence (GBV) already prevalent in South Sudan has been exacerbated by the renewed conflict since 2016. GBV reporting and help seeking behavior among survivors is relatively low due to deeply rooted social norms and cultural practices. While GBV remains under-reported due to barriers to reporting and access to services, anecdotal evidence indicates that the level of GBV is high across the country, with mostly women and girls being exposed to rape, gang rape, sexual slavery, and other forms of GBV. The full magnitude of the problem is not known. However, study conducted by protection partners in South Sudan 2017 revealed that some 65 per cent of women and girls in South Sudan have experienced physical and/or sexual violence in their lifetime, and some 51 per cent have suffered intimate partner violence (IPV). Some 33 per cent of women have experienced sexual violence from a non-partner, primarily during attacks or raids. The majority of girls and women experience sexual violence for the first time below age 18. The risk of child marriage remains constant due to conflict and the economic crisis. According to GBVIMS, between Jan-Mar 2017, about 3,585 GBV cases were recorded (a 34% increase compared to cases reported in 2016). Safety Perceptions Assessment conducted in June 2018 by UNICEF revealed that despite the availability of some basic services including WASH, the biggest barriers accessing them is that facilities are either too far or crowded. Women and girls have reported exposure to sexual assault near Protection of Civilians (PoC) sites, and face similar risks when walking long distances to collect water. The conflict has destroyed many support systems for survivors and impacted access to services; as such, there are high needs for continued support to GBV prevention, risk mitigation and response. In communities, there is a need for women and girls to have safe spaces for sharing their experiences and accessing support. Efforts also need to continue to mitigate GBV risk across all sectors and clusters.

Accountability to affected population
Does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

Yes

How does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

UNICEF and its implementing partners always provide beneficiaries with information. They plan, consult and agree with the community before beginning any new project or interventions and during their implementation, monitoring and evaluation. This will be a main criterion of selection of implementing partners for the 2019-2021 new country program of UNICEF All activities listed here are community based and many activities are community driven and involve community participation in their management. UNICEF and implementing partners have established complaint and feedback mechanisms in almost all project areas to provide information to affected communities and during 2019, one of the key focus is to expand and strengthen complaint and feedback mechanisms in all project locations.

Are messages context-specific or/and tailored?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Messages are context-specific and tailored for gender and age groups. This includes consulting community leaders, religious leaders, schools, women's groups, youth groups and children. All the messages have been presented and discussed with relevant clusters, working groups and task forces. All messages have been field tested to ensure they are culturally and context specific and have involved the affected population as well.

Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse

Yes

Briefly describe how

The CP section has recruited a dedicated PSEA specialist to build the capacity of UNICEF and partners on PSEA. CP’s PSEA work includes capacity building for UNICEF and partners’ staff on SEA, ensuring UNICEF and partners’ staff, volunteers and contractors read and sign code of conduct, establishing community based complaint mechanisms, awareness raising on PSEA and available reporting mechanisms and services among beneficiaries and responding to SEA cases.

Nature of the consultations

Yes

Briefly describe the nature of the consultations

UNICEF has conducted a detailed situation analysis through community based consultations including INGOs and NNGOs. Annual reviews, mid-year reviews and end country program review have been conducted in 2018 as preparation for the next country program which is three years 2019-2021. UNICEF's annual work plan is approved by the Government, there are also state level action plans and child protection working groups where issues raised by communities can be resolved. Community based child protection committees also provide a mechanism for consultation with communities. Focus group discussions are another means of consultation used in child protection projects.

Feedback /complaint mechanisms?

Yes

Briefly describe the mechanisms

Through its implementing partners, UNICEF establishes community feedback and complaint mechanisms for its interventions. These take the form of suggestion boxes where people can leave feedback anonymously, open meetings with the communities, through the reporting mechanisms of the community based child protection committees, community feedback logs where feedback and complaints from the community are recorded during monitoring visits. Assessments at the end of the implementing partners' project also provide opportunities for community feedback.

Community Engagement?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Monitoring and evaluation includes community engagement through focus group discussions and surveys. Joint monitoring of interventions between community members, members of community based child protection committees and project staff also ensure that the community is engaged. The community are also engaged during final project assessments.

Centrality of Protection in humanitarian action
Protection Risk Analysis?

Yes

Briefly describe how

UNICEF's programme is based on a protection risk analysis which considers the specific needs of different population groups including GBV survivors, CAAFAG and extremely vulnerable children. For instance, GBV programs conduct safety audits prior and during programmes and other CP programmes conduct detailed CP and general situation and protection analysis prior to all protection related humanitarian actions within child protection.

Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

The UNICEF programme identifies and addresses barriers to access. The implementing partners adopt gender strategies to ensure that girls and women equally participate in interventions. The programme addresses the specific needs of children released from armed forces and groups to ensure they are able to participate in reintegration interventions as they are often stigmatized by the community. The programme seeks to identify unaccompanied and separated children and other extremely vulnerable children to ensure that they do not face barriers to accessing the services delivered by the programme. UNICEF country office in South Sudan has expanded its security advisors from 2 officers to 6 internal experts to facilitate and assess access. Also it has recruited an Access Specialist to support creating access together with OCHA and other key actors such as WFP.

Addresses inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

All of the child protection interventions conducted as part of UNICEF's programme seek to address inequalities and protection threats. The programme includes working with CAAFAG, PSS, FTR and case management, prevention and response to GBV, MRE and reporting through the MRM. This includes addressing the following issues: grave child rights violations, sexual exploitation and abuse, neglect, deprivation and extreme poverty, gender based violence, child labour, gender inequality and harmful traditional practices, child recruitment into armed forces and groups and social exclusion.

Does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

To assess perceptions of access, UNICEF, in collaboration with partners, will conduct focus group discussions (FGDs) , key informant interviews, KAP surveys and direct observation methods and many other protection tools with community members, community and traditional leaders, local and national government authorities and other relevant key actors to better understand and as a monitoring measure of access to services and whether there has been any improvement in that area during the intervention. Monitoring and evaluation will also involve, using gender disaggregated data to measure whether there has been an equal participation of girls and women in the projects through comparison of data at the start and end of implementation.

Does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

To assess perceptions of improved safety, UNICEF, in collaboration with partners, will conduct focus group discussions (FGDs) , key informant interviews, KAP surveys and direct observation methods and many other protection tools with community members to better understand their safety and security and protection risks whether there has been any improvement in that area during the intervention. FGDs and other proposed methods are especially used in protection related humanitarian actions and will focus on the physical safety, security and protection of accessing services and other safety issues may also arise. UNICEF as a protection expert in humanitarian action, will not interview or use any information without consent of any affected population including children and women themselves. In relation to the GBV programme within the Child Protection programme safety audits are conducted and relevant actors are engaged to respond to children’s safety needs. Safety perception surveys on GBV are carried out to assess beneficiaries’ feeling of safety and community based complaint mechanisms are established to respond to beneficiaries’ complaints.

Does the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

Yes

How the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

To assess perceptions of dignity, UNICEF, in collaboration with partners, will not interview or use any information without consent of any affected population including children and women themselves. Affected population including children, youth and women will be part of planning, implementation, monitoring and evaluation of project. UNICEF also conducts perception surveys, KAP studies to better understand the population’s perception of dignity and whether there has been any improvement in that area during the intervention. UNICEF also conducts review and evaluation exercises on its goods and services such as critical CP supplies to ensure they are relevant, sustainable and serve the purpose of affected populations and their needs.
