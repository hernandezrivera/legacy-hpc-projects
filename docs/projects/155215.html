Project Summary

A clear and practicable regulatory environment is a critical prerequisite for access and a principled humanitarian response. NGOs have regularly cited to NCCI that bureaucratic lack of clarity, inconsistencies, frequent changes and overlapping jurisdictions to be among the greatest challenges facing NGOs Iraq. This results in reduced access, visa & registration challenges, delays and additional costs for NGOs in Iraq. community needs centralized regulatory guidance and support to help advocate for a positive and clarified regulatory environment.
NCCI will continue the critical work of a bureaucratic liaison unit (BLU) prioritizing NGO oriented solutions to bureaucratic impediments. BLU will work closely with government authorities and other external actors to understand new and changing regulatory policies and practices, and to communicate those to the NGO community. The BLU will work with NGOs to report and analyse the challenges being faced by NGOs and to clearly communicate those back to relevant government stakeholders. The BLU’s integration of NCCI Advocacy, knowledge management and coordination capacity help the NGO community in Iraq assure access and effectiveness through positive collaboration with government stakeholders.

Needs

NGOs will remain the primary implementers for Government plans for humanitarian, post-conflict and recovery programming. These NGOs are often welcomed by the Iraqi government at various levels, but the bureaucratic and regulatory challenges create a myriad of hurdles that make operations and access difficult. Iraq is a complex context; New and arbitrary regulations have largely resulted in a de facto requirement to navigate the long-standing cumbersome, confusing and costly regulatory environment in central Iraq. Various military and political leaders at governorate levels frequently develop new regulatory, access, security, protection, or neutrality challenges. The response and recovery has now expanded across the country so serving the highest needs areas as identified in the Humanitarian Needs Overview, NGOs must navigate overlapping GoI and KRG compliance frameworks. The result is that NGO operations such as programming, international staff recruitment, and field access are frequently interrupted. Country Directors (CD’s) cite the increasing new challenges as a major source of difficulty. Changes in procedures, new steps or requirements, duplication or arbitrary questionable reporting expectations, and other challenges are frequently launched without warning or consultation. In 2018, the largest problems were frequent changes that caused breaks in visas, but many other issues arose as well. In coordination meetings, CD’s have expressed that “with the constant visa challenges affecting out international staff, I feel like I’ve become a travel agent” (referring to the difficulty of keeping international staff in-country and working). Without more centralized support, the regulatory challenges are likely to impact humanitarian operations and severely impede the completion of the HRP objectives.

Activities

The Bureaucratic Impediments and Liaison Unit (BLU) leads the NGO forum’s engagement on government legislation and regulations affecting work of NGOs in Iraq. The BLU has developed guidance notes on 10 subjects which NGOs were known to need. However, the regulations and processes across departments change frequently, so the BLU team also responds to ad-hoc needs as well. In 2018 The BLU team worked hard to address, advocate, and inform. In many cases, the BLU team was able to point out the duplication with other ministries or help government agencies understand the impediments NGOs face, resolving or achieving a modification of the problematic policies. The following are some issues the BLU has worked on in 2018: 1. The July 2018 GOI residency 1,000,000 IQD deposit requirements required extensive advocacy with a variety of stakeholders. Eventually an exemption was granted to all NGOs expat staff.2. The GoI Residency Department changed requirements for visa activation multiple times, with the process varying by location. BLU regularly updated NGOs with each change and advocated for clarity and some small adjustments to the process. 3. The BLU provided NGOs with best practices for how to avoid GOI visa rejections for the NGOs expat staff, and agreed with the head of the residency department to request visa extension (renewal) to allow expat staff to activate their new visa directly without travel outside the country4. Negotiated with the DNGO to permit NGOs to share offices in Baghdad5. NCCI has hosted or helped facilitate meetings with government officials across ministries including GoI DNGO, JCMC, JCC, MOLSA, Tax and others. These include general coordination meetings, registration ceremonies, and ad-hoc meetings responding to challenges The following are some regulations where BLU researched, provided information and helped NGOs take a common approach to: 1. Details of how to comply with the GoI & KRG tax payment 2. The new cost of GoI visas & activations 3. New instructions from MOLSA regarding work permissions4. New MoH processes for expat staff blood test 5. New GoI DNGO changed visa forms and process 6. Updated DNGO Temporary Registration Clarifications 7. Provided registration status updates, data on visas and the impact of visa interruptions, and reporting of governorate level challenges to diplomatic missions and the UN HCTWhile many impediments arise unexpectedly, the following are some issues that the BLU already plans to address in 2019. 1. KRG Visas: The KRG Residency issued new instructions that staff who have a GoI multi-entry visa no longer need/receive KRG residency. This instruction is causing confusion and duress. Several NGOs have reported that staff with a GoI multi-entry visa in their passport were refused KRI residency. 2. GoI Visas: The BLU will continue to advocate for clarity around the renewal visa process, a faster and more streamlined visa issuance, and more 12-month visas. 3. Access: Khazer checkpoint threatens to restrict access unless NGOs submit new reports similar to requirements that have already been provided to the JCMC. The BLU is working to assure that similar or additional requirements made by local security or political actors demands for additional information are reviewed and that the NGO community can respond in a unified way. 4. Quotas: The KRG DNGO and Kirkuk governorate continue to pressure NGOs to comply with certain staff quotas. The BLU will continue to advocate for principled humanitarian policies and regulations that are inclusive to all ethnicities. 1. Work Permits: In 2018 the Ministry of Labour and Social Affairs (MoLSA) asked many NGOs to apply for work permit to their Expat staff from the ministry (at a cost of approximately $1000 for each foreign employee per year). BLU is working with the DNGO and the MOLSA minister in order to get an exemption for the NGOs expat staff.2. Tax and customs: BLU will continue working on the recent changes by the National Operations Centre (?) to help NGOs stay compliant with tax, customs, transport and the movement of goods and cargo from the KRI into GOI areas. BLU expects this process to continue changing in the near future and will support accordingly. To address these, the BLU will continue to support positive NGO-Government mutual understanding, improve the NGO understanding of regulations and processes, and to support advocacy for solutions to regulatory impediments. The intended result is a regulatory environment conducive to NGOs effectively delivering assistance to people in need. Protection Risk Analysis

This project is a coordination and advocacy project with minimal field presence and minimal physical security risks. The primary area of risk is political pushback from advocacy initiatives. NCCI has experienced positive relationships with most government agencies but it is possible for some collective advocacy around sensitive issues to be perceived by government agencies as uncollaborative or adversarial, straining the working relationship.

Mitigation Measures

This will be mitigated by careful selection of priorities for advocacy, reiterating a shared purpose with government actors and using evidence to support claims. Finally, the project also supports the efforts of other actors (UN, Foreign Missions etc.) to advocate on behalf of the NGO community.

How is your project accountable to affected population

In this project, the direct beneficiaries are the NGOs, who in turn serve the affected populations. NCCI is a member-led organization and therefore is managed by our beneficiaries. NCCI’s leadership and major decisions are driven by the membership NGOs, and the BLU team reports to those CDs in monthly CD’s meetings. Regulatory and bureaucratic impediments are a standing topic discussed in all CD meetings, and are often the number one issue of concern to CDs. These CD’s have voting power via the general assembly, and they also select the executive board from among themselves. This executive board meets every three weeks to review all NCCI performance and to guide strategy. In these meetings, the BLU, relationships with the government, BLU management, and specific approaches to individual priority issues (i.g. visas, registration, taxes etc.) are regularly reviewed. Finally, NCCI is an inclusive organization and we welcome feedback from all levels. This includes frequent ad-hoc meetings with member organizations and co-participation in various shared leadership or working groups which provide avenues for feedback from both partners and beneficiaries.
