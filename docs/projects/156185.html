Project Summary

The proposed project aims to contribute in reduction in morbidity and mortality among the
conflict affected IDPs and host communities in the targeted areas of Idleb, Aleppo and Hama
and in North-western Syria. A total of 400,000 Individuals (men, women, boys and girls) will receive health care through 70 Health facilities. The main project components include:
• Drugs, disposables, dialysis kits provision to 56 PHC, 4 Mobile Emergency unit (MEU)
and 10 dialysis centres.
• Stipend support to 150 health workers and running cost to 10 targeted facilities.
• Capacity building (training) for 100 health workers and facility management on health information management, protection & inclusion, nursing and midwifery.
Also the project will support 2 ambulances in one facility with operational cost. These ambulances will be used for referral services.
Finally out of the targeted facilities, 5 will be supported with solar panel to overcome the problem of electricity breakdown.
The proposed project will provide support to those people most in need of life-saving and
life-sustaining assistance. Specifically, the project will prioritize assistance to people with
acute and immediate humanitarian health needs, many of whom have suffered trauma
injuries or otherwise lack access to basic life-saving medical-care for both acute and
chronic conditions (such as renal failures) that renders them particularly at-risk.

Needs

According to Humanitarian Need Overview 2019, The Syria crisis has impacted the health delivery system across Syria with some regions solely dependent on humanitarian assistance for the provision health services. The number of people who are in need of health assistance in Syria is estimated to be 13.2 million. Females make up 72 per cent of the PiN. Vulnerable groups include 5.2 million women of reproductive age, 2.2 million children under 5 years old, and 2.3 million people aged over 50, as at this age vulnerability to non-communicable diseases becomes highest. Syria is home to at least 3 million disabled people, and 7 million IDPs. Across Syria health-related needs continue to be driven and exacerbated by insufficient access to healthcare due to a combination of damage and destruction of health facilities, insufficient human resources, and imposed access constraints. Attacks and resource constraints impact the functionality of health facilities. September 2018 Health Resource Availability Monitoring System (HeRAMS) data indicated that across Syria 46 per cent of health facilities were either non-functional or partially functional, with 167 facilities completely destroyed, and a further 419 facilities partially destroyed and in need of rehabilitation. Pregnant adolescent girls and their babies are at high risk of medical complications and death. They constitute a substantial high-risk group in Syria, with early marriage reported to take place in 57 per cent of assessed communities , and pregnant girls under 18 representing about 12 to 15 per cent of the deliveries taking place in health facilities. Both health providers and community representatives highlighted the limited availability of clinical management of rape (CMR) as a services gap across Syria. Localized outbreaks of measles, acute bloody diarrhea and typhoid fever were detected mainly due to limited access to clean water and sub optimal hygiene conditions. Non communicable diseases remain among the most prevalent health issues in Syria. WHO estimates that approximately 12 per cent of the population have diabetes and 20 per cent hypertension . Untreated non-communicable diseases lead to serious complications such as renal failure and disability due to stroke and amputations. Continuous professional development of existing health professionals was limited over the past seven years, which affected the quality of health services provided. Hospital-acquired infections were reported across Syria, as well as antibiotic-resistant infections. The health facilities supported by Islamic Relief previously, also mentioned similar health needs. 16 out of 22 assessed pediatric and women’s health hospitals highlighted the needs for medicine and consumable. These medicine and consumables like sutures for C-Sections are often used in lifesaving obstetric operations for women. Similarly, for pediatric care, there is extensive need of antibiotic syrups to fight increasingly complicated infections. For men, the need for medicine used to treat disorders of the prostate gland as well as ware related injuries are needed. For the elderly, the treatment of hypertension (high blood pressure) has been reported as lacking. Other non-communicable diseases treatment such as dialysis is also a concern. People with disability (PWD) are facing serious challenges to access health facilities since many of these facilities are not structurally designed to mainstream PWDs. Also non availability of appropriate vehicles and ambulances is another big challenge faced by PWDs to access health care. People most at risk include people living with chronic diseases; children who are not vaccinated; pregnant women and neonates who have no access to life-saving obstetric and neonatal care, girls and women with poor access to essential reproductive health care; those in need of mental health and psycho social support and; survivors of trauma, including the war wounded.

Implementation of activities

Access to primary health care: The project will support 56 PHC in targeted areas throughan essential package of Primary Health Services to cover health care needs of vulnerablepopulations including obstetric, trauma, internal and family medicine, reproductive healthservices and child health care. These 56 PHC will receive essential drugs and disposables. 150 health workers from these facilities will receive the monthly stipend and 10 facilities will receive the running cost. Also 5 facilities will receive solar panel. Mobile Emergency units (MEU): 4 MEU will receive drugs and disposables. In coordination with healthdirectorates, these MEU will be strategically placed to cover the areas with high catchmentpopulation (IDP and host) and with no access to healthcare services. These MEU will alsoserve as mobile surgery units to provide onsite surgery support. Dialysis centre: The project will support 10 dialysis centres with dialysis kits to conduct regular hemodialysissessions. they will also be provided with stipend and running cost. Capacity building: 100 health workers (30 men and 70 women) will receive training on include health information management (3 training), protection & inclusion (3 training), nursing (3 training) and midwifery (3 training) . The capacity building component will help to enhance the basic skills and knowledge of the health workers. Pre and post test will be conducted to gauge the improvement in knowledge levels of the participants. After the trainings, IR will make follow up visits to facilities to ensure that the training knowledge is practically translated such as age and gender disaggregated data collection and protection and inclusion measures taken. Referral services: IR will also support operational cost of 2 ambulances of the targeted one hospital. These ambulances will ensure to create a strong referral pathway to secondary and territory health care. These ambulances will bridge the access gap between vulnerable population (including females, pregnant and lactating women, people suffering from chronic illness, children, elderly, people with disability) and the health service providers.

Protection Risk Analysis

Some of the identified protection risks and mitigation measures that IR will apply include the following:I. Violence against Health Facilities and Healthcare Providers: Data on attacks against healthcare collected through Surveillance System for Attacks on Health Care (SSA) during the period of January - September 2018 documented 134 attacks across Syria (47 attacks in Idleb and 10 in Hama), exceeding the incidents reported throughout the year 2017 (122 incidents). Although the rate of attacks against healthcare declined significantly over the course of 2018, violence is still a main issue facing healthcare providers. Mitigation Measure: IR will ensure continued reporting on attacks against healthcare, and enlisting support of the humanitarian leadership (cluster) in their efforts to highlight protection challenges facing health workers. II. Gender Based Violence: Health services are often the first, and in many occasions the only point of contact for GBV survivors seeking assistance, or coming for health care for GBV related emotional or physical conditions, including injuries, without necessarily disclose the abuse due to shame or fear. In order to facilitate the required care, survivors must have safe access to health facilities, and it is critical that health providers are equipped to offer quality health services.Mitigation Measure:  Identification of safe referral pathways to ensure comprehensive management of GBV cases, in a manner guarantees safety, confidentiality, and privacy of the survivor.  Provision of drugs and disposables required for the treatment of GBV cases.  Facilitate Presence of female staff (considering female for stipend provision) in targeted health facilities to increase access of women and girls to health services III. Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse: During the course of their work, aid providers come into contact with vulnerable people. Humanitarian workers have access to goods and services that put them in a position of power over the community. Unfortunately, some humanitarian workers have used this imbalance of power to exploit and abuse vulnerable members of the community. It is important to strive and commit to preventing sexual exploitation and abuse both on organizational and programmatic levels.Mitigation Measure:  Emplacement of effective complaint and feedback mechanism.  Effective recruitment and performance management of staff.  Provide information to communities and people affected by crisis about IR, the principles it adheres to, how it expects its staff to behave, the project it is implementing and what it intends to deliver. IV. Provision of Healthcare for Vulnerable Groups and Equitable Access to Health Services: Health cluster identified five groups have been identified as the most vulnerable due to their inherent status that can be exacerbated by the ongoing crisis, 1) children under 5 (11%), 2) women of reproductive age (26%), 3) people living with disability PWD (15%), 4) over 50 years of age (12%), 5) internally displaced personnel IDPs and returnees (36%).Mitigation Measure:  Inclusion of vulnerable groups needs throughout the program cycle (analysis, design, implementation, and monitoring) through gender, age, dis-aggregated analysis of beneficiaries with identification of risk factors impacting service provision.  Addressing the specific needs of vulnerable groups (trauma, dialysis, GBV referrals)  Encouraging that health service providers ensure accessibility to services by vulnerable groups (physical accessibility, dis-aggregated areas for women and girls, etc.)

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

some of the risks and their mitigation measures identified are as follows: 1. Qualified health staff leaving area.Mitigation Measure: IR will ensure rigorous coordination with the health directorates in order to find alternatives for the health staff. 2. Disease outbreak.Mitigation Measure: IR will keep regular coordination with the targeted health facilities, health directorates and other organisations involved in the prevention of infectious outbreaks. Many staff supported by the previous project has received training in the depiction and referral of infected patients as well as information pathways. All these arrangements will ensure the early identification of any such diseases trends/outbreaks and relevant steps will be taken accordingly to mitigate this risk.3. Taxes imposed by non-state armed groups at check points inside Syria or at the border crossing. Mitigation Measure: IR remains vigilant of any such check points. Thereby to avoid these check points IR use alternative routes for the movement of project deliverable. Additionally, while delivering medicines and disposables to the targeted facility, IR asks the hospital staff to accompany the trucks moving supplies from IR warehouse to the targeted facility to avoid any such check points. For the border crossing, IR Syria signs agreement with the identified supplier whose responsibility is to deliver the items to IR Syria warehouse. IR remain in contact with the suppliers to identify any such issues (tax) on border crossing. If this happen, IR can explore to use alternate border crossing (Babul Salamah) which will slightly increase the transportation cost. 4. Delays in getting permission for red listed items. Mitigation Measure: IR team is fully aware of the red listed medical items.in case if these items are required, IR will coordinate with the targeted health facility (which has the capacity and the system emplaced to use such items like Anesthesia drugs). The quantities required will be determined and IR will coordinate with the Health Directorate to allow these drugs procurement. Simultaneously based on this need, IR will start the procurement process earlier so that the relevant permissions from Turkish authorities are secured in time to allow the border crossing and delivery inside Syria.5. Groups under UN security council sanction list may financially benefit from the project supplies passing through Syria-Turkey border crossing.Mitigation Measure: IR is implementing the similar project in same areas for over three years. The health facilities receive support directly from IR without involvement of any intermediaries. At the project initiation all the relevant stakeholders (Health Directorate, Health facility etc) are informed of the project activities. Further IR Syria strong monitoring and project controls and documentation ensure that there are no external influences or diversion of support.
