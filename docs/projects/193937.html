Project Summary

The aim of the intervention is to achieve long-term structural improvement in the human rights situation in the West Bank by monitoring the systematic violation of human rights in the West Bank, documenting and
disseminating accurate and up-to-date information regarding such violations, and by applying public and legal pressure to end them. Yesh Din works both on the individual level, legally representing and conducting advocacy on behalf of Palestinian individuals and communities, and on the systemic level through the output of data on the lack of accountability, failure of Israeli law enforcement systems, and the filing of legal actions on principled issues.
Program objectives:
1. Accountability of Israeli civilians and Israeli Security Forces Personnel (ISFP) in the West Bank is upheld in line with international law;
2. Palestinian men, women and communities are strengthened to exercise their rights over resources in the West Bank;
3. Israeli policies of dispossession from resources and control over them in the West Bank are exposed and challenged.
Program Areas:
Yesh Din currently operates within three program areas, all of which have been developed following our assessment of the needs of our beneficiaries and target population, and in accordance with our organizational strategy:
1. Law Enforcement on Settler Violence
2. Accountability of Israeli Security Forces Personnel
3. Access to Lands
In order to ensure equal participation and access to our services, Yesh Din employs female and male Palestinian researchers who make contact with our beneficiaries throughout the West Bank. Our female field researchers are dispatched to incidents involving Palestinian women and children, in order to ensure their ability to document offenses committed against them, obtain legal representation, and to initiate public advocacy on their behalf, if desired. Yesh Din has special provisions for the safeguarding of Palestinian women, children and persons with disabilities, involving obtaining consent from legal guardians, ensuring that these groups are always in the company of other women or trusted family members or legal guardians. We also collected data disaggregated by gender and disability, which helps us to assess to what degree these groups are being impacted by offenses, and also to assess the need for additional provisions to be created to support them. We always consider the perspective of gender, age, and disabilities when launching new programs or activities in order to promote equal access for all. Past gender mainstreaming processes alerted us to the importance of hiring female Palestinian field researchers to contact female beneficiaries, and the number of testimonies we collect from women has greatly increased as a result. We have also undertaken programs, such as on Forcible Home Invasions of the Israeli military into Palestinian homes, in which the data we collected from Palestinian women was vital and resulted in powerful advocacy which resulted in a change in military policy.
Yesh Din will ensure that this program does not duplicated our ongoing projects with NRC and ACPP.

1 - Needs

PROGRAM AREA 1: LAW ENFORCEMENT ON SETTLER VIOLENCE (LESV)Ideologically motivated offenses committed by Israelis against Palestinians, known as settler violence, is a near-daily occurrence in the West Bank and one which Yesh Din has monitored steadfastly since 2005. Yesh Din has monitored 1,445 police investigations into ideologically motivated offenses since 2005 and can therefore attest to the degree to which Israel fails to comply with its obligation under international law to protect Palestinians. 92% of all investigative files monitored by Yesh Din were closed by the police with no indictment filed, and 82% were closed in circumstances attesting to the failure of the police, mainly to gather evidence and identify suspects.Furthermore, data provided by the police to Yesh Din in 2020 revealed that the chance that Israeli law enforcement agencies will file an indictment against an Israeli who harmed a non-Palestinian person (19%) (Israeli security personnel and others) is six times higher than the chance of filing an indictment against an Israeli who harmed a Palestinian (3%).(https://www.yesh-din.org/en/data-sheet-december-2021-law-enforcement-on-israeli-civilians-in-the-west-bank-settler-violence/)PROGRAM AREA 2: ACCOUNTABILITY OF ISRAELI SECURITY FORCES PERSONNEL (ISFP)The nature of Israel’s military occupation of the oPt provides the foundation for widespread offenses to be committed by Israeli security forces against Palestinian civilians. Yesh Din has documented these offenses since 2007, with the most common incidents consisting of unwarranted or disproportionate violence such as shootings resulting in injury or death and beatings, damage to private property, looting, improper confiscations, and a variety of violations that are likely to occur when responding to confrontations between settlers and Palestinians or during the course of forcible home invasions. In more recent years, Yesh Din has also monitored and challenged offenses committed by the Israeli military during offenses against Gaza.Yesh Din assists Palestinian victims of offenses committed by Israeli security forces personnel (ISFP) to file complaints to the appropriate investigative bodies, usually the Military Police Criminal Investigations Division (MPCID) and provides legal representation throughout subsequent legal and administrative procedures. Through this work over the past years, Yesh Din has acquired in-depth knowledge concerning hundreds of investigations into offenses committed by ISFP and has identified systemic failures in the military administrative and judicial procedures that stand in the way of access to justice for Palestinians. With this project, Yesh Din will strive for the introduction of International Humanitarian Law (IHL) and International Criminal Law (ICL) concepts into Israeli domestic law which the Government of Israel (GOI) has claimed to be implementing based on the Turkel Commission's recommendations.(https://s3.eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/files.yesh-din.org/%D7%93%D7%A3+%D7%A0%D7%AA%D7%95%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%9D+%D7%9E%D7%A6%D7%97+2022/YeshDin+-+Data+2.22+-+English_01.pdf).PROGRAM AREA 3: ACCESS TO LANDSSince 2010, Yesh Din's Access to Lands Project has aimed to return Palestinian landowners to their land and to challenge measures and efforts to take over Palestinian land via settlement expansion, which dispossesses Palestinians and destroys the territorial contiguity necessary for a Palestinian state or for any just resolution. -The local Palestinian population has a need to document human rights violations committed against them by settlers and Israeli security forces, for their voices to be amplified on a global stage. -The local Palestinian population has a need for the systematic collection of documentation in support of pursuit of legal remedy in incidents in which police and military police complaints are filed.-The local Palestinian population has a need for strengthened awareness of the legal rights to which they are entitled and how to access relevant resources; this need is compounded for Palestinian women.-Civil society working on public and legal advocacy need access to police and military records of investigations and cases in order to monitor their effectiveness. -Local and international organizations, activists, lawyers, journalists, academics and individuals working on issues relating to Israel and the West Bank need reliable sources of information on settler violence and related policies.-The local Palestinian population is in need of facilitated access to police stations and legal representation vis-à-vis Israeli authorities and courts.-Palestinians need civil society to act as a watch-dog to promote accountability and effectiveness of Israeli law enforcement.-Local and international duty-bearers, organizations, journalists and activists have a need to receive reliable and accurate data concerning incidents of settler violence in real-time in order to initiate protection interventions.-Israeli law-makers and duty-bearers have a need for synthesized data from past to present on the extent of law enforcement and state complicity in settler violence in order to push for policy change. The needs of Palestinian women and people with disabilities to receive access to justice and legal representation are compounded even more acutely. Cultural norms often require that Palestinian women not interact excessively or unaccompanied with men outside of their families, creating a need for female field researchers who are able to contact Palestinian women to strengthen their knowledge of their legal rights, and to accompany them to police stations and otherwise facilitate access to justice. Persons with disabilities are at times even more vulnerable to offenses and require additional assistance in accessing justice. Yesh Din has policies in place to meet these needs and also collects data disaggregated by gender and disability.

1. Collecting testimonyTestimonies will be collected from rights-holders and eye-witnesses of human rights violations by the field researcher, who will translate from Arabic into Hebrew so that the testimony can be entered into Yesh Din's online database. Our field team will also record the cases in the GIS database by type of incident and location and disaggregated by gender for research purposes. Our female field researchers will collect testimonies from women and ensure that women affected are aware of legal services and are represented. 2. Accompanying rights-holders to file complaints and to courtsIn cases of settler violence: Yesh Din field researchers and/or volunteers will accompany Palestinian rights-holders to file complaintswith the appropriate authority (usually JS District Police) as unaccompanied Palestinians encounter great difficulty in accessing police stations and filing complaints. Palestinians are met with uncooperative behavior from police officers, are regularly made to wait long hours to be received, and in many cases there is no Arabic to Hebrew translator. Further, most Israeli police stations in the West bank are locatedinside settlements, a fact which for Palestinians usually presents a physical and psychological obstacle to filing a police complaint. Aside from facilitating access to justice, Yesh Din’s field team and volunteers observe and relay to our legal team the manner of treatment of our beneficiary by law enforcement authorities, which constitutes part of our monitoring activities. In cases of ISFP offenses: In cases where Border Police and Police are suspected of offences against Palestinians, Yesh Din volunteers and Arabic-speaking field liaisons accompany the complainants to file complaints with the Police Investigative Unit (PIU), which has representatives stationed at Israeli DCOs in the West Bank. In cases of complaints against IDF soldiers, Yesh Din must file the complaint on behalf of the rights-holder, since the Military Investigative Police (MIP) has no offices or stations in the West Bank, and Palestinians have no way of filing the complaint themselves. When Palestinians are finally called to give their testimony, in most cases meetings at checkpoints are arranged, facilitated by Yesh Din field staff and volunteers.3. 8 - Participation (Community Engagement)
8.1 - Project needs assessment

No

If not checked, please explain why

Our field researchers meet daily with beneficiaries and are acutely aware of their needs. The field team reflects these needs and constraints when designing and adapting programs. Yesh Din holds between 40-70 information sessions and between 3-5 workshops designed especially for women per year, serving several hundred beneficiaries (between 500-800 individuals). During these information sessions, our field researchers provide information on legal rights and discuss the services provided by Yesh Din, as well as other services available to address a variety of needs that may arise from these beneficiaries. These sessions and workshops serve as a prime opportunity to conduct needs assessments from the communities we serve. We facilitate discussions regarding the central obstacles and violations faced in specific communities, be it surges in settler violence or military invasions, or new outpost construction, land declarations or other forms of land takeover. The conclusions of these discussions are documented by our field researchers and communicated back to our staff via staff and department meetings. The feedback we receive from beneficiaries informs all of our program areas, and is almost always behind the design and launching of new projects, were are specifically designed to meet the needs we ascertain from this feedback. Our project on forcible home invasions by the Israeli military was a direct result of information received from beneficiaries regarding the prevalence of these incidents and the wide array of rights violations and human rights offenses that occur as a result. Additionally, Yesh Din is very responsive to the fact that many Palestinians do not want to file complaints with the Israeli authorities, due to lack of faith in the Israeli law enforcement system to bring them justice, as well as a fear of retribution from Israeli authorities if they file a complaint. While we still encourage rights-holders to document their offenses, we do not encourage them to file complaints if they are not comfortable doing so, but merely inform them that we can assist them if they desire. This continuing trends in which Palestinians are increasingly reluctant to file complaints has informed Yesh Din's new strategy (2022-2024), in which we have elected to slightly reduce the number of representation in individual cases of offenses, in favor of devoting more of our resources to filing principled legal actions and increasing our ability to conduct advocacy, both on the local and international levels. This shift is directly in response to feedback we receive from West Bank residents over the years. With this said, with the increase in settler violence, there is still a significant percent of the population that does choose to pursue justice using any avenue available to them, including by filing complaints with Israeli authorities, and so we continue to provide this service. Finally, our new focus on combating the spread of Israeli shepherding outposts is also a result of direct feedback we receive from beneficiaries, as well as analysis of the data we collect, which indicated that incidents of violence are increasing stemming from this new style of outpost, and that Palestinians are being dispossessed from their land at astronomical rates because of these outposts. Indeed, every strategic choice by Yesh Din is informed from the needs expressed by our beneficiaries daily.

Feedback from beneficiaries will be used to inform our strategy and implementation of activities. It will be discussed in staff meetings, managerial meetings and meetings with our board of directors. Yesh Din is currently in the process of launching our external complaints mechanism, which will be in place by 2022. We have hired a female Palestinian with a social work background to serve as our external complaints coordinator. She will receive a company phone and email address, and will be available to receive feedback and complaints from beneficiaries through these avenues. Having a complaints coordinator who is external to Yesh Din ensures greater accountability and eases the process of filing a complaint for beneficiaries.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

Yesh Din has a code of conduct that includes principles relating to 'Do No Harm'. All staff must sign the code of conduct and receives a training on it. In addition, legal and public advocacy actions are carefully considered for the potential negative impacts they may result in for Palestinian individuals and society (such as negative legal precedents being set), as well as the potential benefits they may achieve. Beneficiaries are made fully aware of potential harmful impacts in such situations and their feedback greatly informs the ultimate decision. If Yesh Din's assessment indicates that a legal proceeding is likely to ultimately negatively impact the relevant individuals or communities, we will not pursue it. Additionally, Yesh Din is acutely aware that, for our beneficiaries, reliving a violent offense committed against them by providing testimony or otherwise discussing it is a potentially traumatic event. We never encourage or pressure our beneficiaries to discuss something they are not comfortable with, and this is part of the reason that we aim to minimize collecting testimonies from children, in order to minimize any further impact on the child. Testimonies are collected from children only if the child chooses to do so, with the consent and accompaniment of their legal guardian, and only if their testimony is vital to the viability of the case. We similarly protect persons with disability by ensuring their consent, reminding them that they may stop whenever they like, and ensuring they are in the company of a trusted family member or guardian when providing testimony or filing a complaint. Yesh Din refrains from taking photos of children or vulnerable adults, and only does so with consent and if the photograph is vital to the case. Yesh Din also is careful to inform beneficiaries of any risks that may be associated with filing complaints with Israeli authorities. We have received many reports that beneficiaries had their work permits to Israel revoked after they filed complaints, especially complaints against Israeli soldiers or police officers. Ultimately, it is up to the beneficiary to choose whether or not to file a complaint, but we make sure to inform them of potential ramifications. In our advocacy work, Yesh Din is careful to always obtain consent before publishing stories or photographs concerning specific beneficiaries, and we always consider potential negative impacts before publishing anything concerning our beneficiaries. We are careful never to publish identifying information about beneficiaries, and we also work on a secure server, which stores all personal information about our beneficiaries. Finally, it is important to apply Do No Harm principles to our staff as well as beneficiaries. In times of heightened tension and violent incidents in the West Bank, our field researchers sometimes halt their field visits in order to not put themselves in danger. During these times, we collect testimonies and documentation over the phone, and once tensions are eased, we return to meet with the beneficiaries in person.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

Yesh Din works mainly with Palestinians living in villages and encampments in close proximity to Israeli settlements and outposts. The communities we serve are made aware of the resources we provide through information sessions Yesh Din holds, as well as by word of mouth. Yesh Din has five Palestinian field researchers, two of whom are women, and this greatly facilitates providing legal rights awareness and access to justice for Palestinian women. Our policies provide that, whenever possible, our female field researchers will respond to incidents involving Palestinian women, and that we always offer female beneficiaries the opportunity to be contacted by a female field researcher. We also have regulations in place regarding work with children and vulnerable adults, to ensure their safeguarding. Gender mainstreaming is implemented during engagement with Palestinian communities with the help of our male and female field researchers. Yesh Din works primarily by responding to calls we receive from beneficiaries who have suffered from offenses committed against them. If the central male figure in the family contacts Yesh Din, upon meeting them, our field researchers aim to collect as many testimonies as possible regarding the incident, and make special efforts to collect testimonies from women in the family, if they are relevant witnesses. While we do not actively seek to collect testimonies form children, as part of a Do-No-Harm principle, if children are the victim of the crime or crucial witnesses, we have special provisions in place to allow our safe interaction with them. We often find that persons with disabilities are disproportionately affected by human rights offenses, and we seek to collect testimonies from them and actively facilitate their access to justice, as well as conduct advocacy on their behalves to promote the rights of persons with disabilities in a wider scope. When collecting testimonies from persons with disabilities (as with any beneficiaries), we actively apply do-no-harm principles, requiring our staff to assess, together with the individual or legal guardian, any potential negative impacts that may result from participation in our programs, and proceed accordingly. Yesh Din never pressures anyone to provide testimony or to file complaints, and minimizes contact with children and especially vulnerable persons who may be impacted negatively by reliving traumatic experiences. We are accountable to all beneficiaries through our code of conduct and confidentiality agreements, and we provide an avenue for beneficiaries to provide complaints or feedback to an external party. In order to promote awareness of our organization and the services we offer, we regularly hold information sessions, which are mainly attended by men, and workshops for women, in communities throughout the West Bank. As we found throughout the years that Palestinian women were not in large attendance in our regular information sessions, we decided to begin holding workshops specifically for women, coordinated in conjunction with local women's organizations. This ensures that women, and women's organizations, throughout the West Bank are aware that they have options for legal redress and know who to contact for assistance.
