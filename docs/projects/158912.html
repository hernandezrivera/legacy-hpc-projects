Project Summary

This project aims to provide a protective presence for Palestinians living under occupation in the West Bank, including East Jerusalem, and Gaza, through monitoring, documenting and advocacy to end violations of human rights and International Humanitarian Law (IHL) inherent to Israel's occupation, and promote accountability for these violations. The project focuses primarily on four broad issues:
First, the absence of accountability for the killing and injury of Palestinians, including children, by Israeli security forces, throughout the oPt. B’Tselem will analyze the impact of the Israeli open-fire policy, the culture of impunity and the virtual absence of consequences for the use of excessive force against Palestinians. In 2018, Israeli security forces killed 290 Palestinians, including 55 minors. Of the casualties, 254 were killed in the Gaza Strip, 34 in the West Bank (including East Jerusalem) and two within Israel. An analysis of the incidents in which Palestinians were killed in 2018 reveals that most were the result of the reckless open-fire policy implemented by Israel’s security forces, authorized by the government and the top military command, and backed by the judicial system This policy includes, among other things, shooting to kill incidents defined as “attacks”, giving unlawful orders that permit live fire at unarmed demonstrators by the Gaza-Israel fence, and bombarding densely populated areas within Gaza.
B’Tselem will monitor and document killings and injuries of Palestinians by Israeli fire, and analyze any “investigations” into security force misconduct made by Israeli authorities. One focus will be the ongoing Return March in the Gaza Strip. During these protests, Israeli forces use extensive live fire against demonstrators, in a way that is both unlawful and immoral. As a result of this open-fire policy, 190 demonstrators have been killed – 65% of all Palestinians killed by Israeli forces this year.
A second focus will be bombing populated areas in the Gaza Strip, which inevitably leads to death, injury and destruction. A third focus will be unjustified killings of Palestinians in other areas and circumstances, for example, in al-‘Esawiyah, or in demonstrations around the West Bank.
Second, B’Tselem will expose the threat of demolitions and forcible transfer of communities in Area C, East Jerusalem and Hebron, and the heightened risk to these communities posed by the extreme political reality in Israel and the absence of an international response. Areas that are likely to impacted by the Israeli policy of gradually transferring Palestinian communities are Silwan and Wadi Yasul in East Jerusalem, Hebron, the Jordan Valley, the Maale Adumim area and the South Hebron Hills, though the exact locations will be dictated by Israeli policies and can’t necessarily be known in advance.
Thirdly, we will monitor, document and examine the impact of the Israeli security force violence and state support for settler violence against Palestinian lives and property around the West Bank, including East Jerusalem. This will include monitoring, documenting and publishing violations, and analyzing state support for the actions of settlers as a deliberate strategy for territorial expansion. This strategy includes not only the failure to enforce the law, but also providing support, security and sometimes assisting settlers who terrorize Palestinians.
Fourth, we will expose the occupation routine of restriction of movement, frequent home raids, arrests and interrogations of minors. This will include restrictions of movement, in east Jerusalem, in Hebron and all around the West Bank.

1 - Needs

In light of the reality on the ground for decades, it is highly unlikely that Israel will change its policies, and the implications of these policies are set to continue or worsen. The political reality in Israel, where rightwing politicians and the settlement lobby have unprecedented power, coupled with the lack of a clear and public commitment to this issue by the US administration, has increased the risk to communities. Israel has increasingly avoided accountability for serious violations of human rights of Palestinians. Based on past experience, there are slim chances of anyone being held accountable for security forces killing Palestinians under circumstances that did not justify the use of lethal means. Another avenue in which Israel avoids accountability is the enactment of legislation that denies Palestinians harmed by illegal acts of Israeli security forces almost any possibility to file compensation suits in Israeli courts. The most realistic – or perhaps even optimist – scenario for the coming year is the continuation of Israel’s current policies: In the West Bank – seizure of land, expropriation of its resources, and displacement of the Palestinians; in the Gaza Strip – remote control of the residents while abandoning any responsibility for the disastrous outcomes of this policy. In the West Bank, Israel’s conduct indicates that it does not view the occupation as a temporary situation. Instead, it treats the West Bank as if it were part of its sovereign territory: grabbing land, exploiting natural resources for its own needs and building permanent settlements, while demolishing structures primarily in farming and shepherding communities in Area C, home to thousands of Palestinians. These Israeli policies constitute a forcible transfer, directly: through demolition of homes, or indirectly: by creating a coercive environment that affects protected Palestinians who are protected persons in an occupied territory. East Jerusalem was annexed by Israel in breach of international law. Despite the annexation, Israel treats Palestinian residents of the city as unwanted immigrants and systematically applies policies designed to drive them away from their homes and city, such as strict bans on residential construction, stringent policies with regards to family reunification, and institutional, systemic discrimination practiced in municipal and state budgeting. Additionally, the authorities cooperate with settler organizations to remove Palestinians from their homes to insert settlers in their place. In 2018, Israeli security forces killed 290 Palestinians, including 55 minors. Of the casualties, 254 were killed in the Gaza Strip, 34 in the West Bank (including East Jerusalem) and two within Israel. These incidents are a direct result of Israel’s reckless open-fire policy, authorized by the government and the top military command, and backed by the judicial system. This was made possible by an open fire policy that permits shooting to kill in instances defined as “incidents of assault” and a trigger-happy approach to demonstrations or stone throwing. The project aims are based on two key axes: Firstly, efforts to erode the image that Israel seeks to promote a clean and lawful occupation. Secondly, intensified attention to the routine of occupation, in an attempt to make this more real and less transparent, so as to make daily occupation a component of public discourse. B'Tselem will investigate unique problems women face on the domestic front due to the occupation, for example, lack of water for cooking and cleaning; caring for young children in an unstable environment; and girls dropping out of school due to movement restriction issues.

2 - Activities

1) Monitoring and Documentation: B'Tselem monitors and documents the reality on the ground utilizing its presence throughout the oPt, with 11 field researchers and 140 volunteers. Field research: B'Tselem’s 11 field researchers monitor and document human rights violations around the oPT. 3 researchers work in the Gaza Strip, 2 in the Hebron area, and one in east Jerusalem (covering the E-1 area and eastward), the South Hebron Hills, The Jordan Valley, and the Ramallah, Jenin, and Nablus areas. The researchers gather testimonies from victims and eyewitnesses to human rights violations, photo and video documentation, medical records, information from Palestinian DCO’s, and more. The West Bank field researchers work with volunteer Palestinian videographers, coordinating their training and equipping, and collecting footage that they film. The field researchers also play a critical role in gathering evidence to verify filmed human rights violations. This verification process distinguishes B'Tselem's video camera project from other groups that post video footage of human rights violations. B’Tselem has a staff of three field researchers in the Gaza Strip, one woman, Olfat al-Kurd, and two men, Khaled al-'Azayzeh, and Muhammad Sabah, who monitor and document the impact of Israeli policies in the Gaza Strip. B’Tselem’s field research everywhere, including the Gaza Strip, is done directly by our staff, independently of the de facto government in the Gaza Strip. Their sources of information are testimonies provided by victims or witnesses to violations, photographs and videos they take, or are collected from others, medical records, and more. They transfer the documentation they collect to our data coordination team in Jerusalem via digital means, including email, mobile phone, Dropbox, and fax. Data coordination: the information from the ground will be analyzed and vetted by B'Tselem's data coordinators in Jerusalem, who will cross check with other sources of information, such as media reports and official Israeli records, prepare the information for publication, and catalog data, disaggregated by gender and age, in B'Tselem's database. Camera distribution project: B’Tselem’s 140 video volunteers live in areas of high conflict where they document human rights violations by Israeli security forces and settlers. B’Tselem provides them with cameras, training and support. The project assists participants to become human rights defenders who work to protect themselves and claim their own rights through documentation of their reality. B’Tselem provides exceptionally skilled and motivated volunteers with one-on-one advanced training. The video project is empowering for women. Since the project’s inception in 2007, women have filmed some of its most powerful video footage. Women volunteers have reported a greater sense of agency, an enhanced feeling of protection provided by the video camera, and a feeling of deterrence against greater violence against them thanks to their participation in the video project. 2) Public Outreach: leveraging this information, B’Tselem will use a variety of public outreach strategies aimed to engage the public: B'Tselem will post short research reports on protection concerns on its trilingual website, was viewed by 512 K in 2018, which will also be distributed in newsletters sent to 35 K people, and on our social networks, which include Twitter, Facebook pages in Hebrew and English, and YouTube, and host nearly 300 K members. B’Tselem will issue tri lingual press releases to 2,200 journalists and members of the international community, lead field visits, give media interviews and write op-eds. 3) International Advocacy: B'Tselem will provide information and analysis to the international community, produce and disseminate advocacy materials, and conduct briefings and field visits. International advocacy work targets diplomats stationed in the region, and foreign decision-makers, and UN agencies, and civil society.

Other affected populations include the residents of the oPt, and audiences in Israel and abroad who are exposed through the project's activities to the violations of human rights of Palestinians, with the aim of raising awareness and increasing accountability.

8.6.c - How will feedback be used?

B’Tselem views monitoring and evaluation as a necessary component, and we monitor all our projects internally to assure that they are implemented, including gathering feedback from 11 field researchers and 140 volunteers on the ground. The project is monitored on an ongoing basis, through daily contact between B’Tselem staff working on the project in Gaza, the West Bank and Jerusalem, in which information, testimonies, data, pictures, video and documents are shared. West Bank and Jerusalem-based staff talk and meet on a regular basis in field visits, while contact between the Gaza staff and the West Bank and Jerusalem-based staff is conducted by digital means, daily phone calls, video conversations, and text messages. Materials are sent between Gaza, WB and Jerusalem by email, fax, DropBox and WhatsApp. This daily exchange allows information gathered by the field researchers to be communicated to our field research director and data coordination team for prioritization, cross-referencing and vetting. B’Tselem staff will also meet on a regular basis to share information from the field, provide feedback, and modify priorities as necessary. Monitoring meetings will take place in periodic staff meetings in Beit Jala and Beit Sahur. We will hear from each of the field researchers, who will update the Jerusalem staff on developments on the ground, so that informed decisions can be made about priorities, and work plans can be modified to meet the changing reality. The Gaza staff will participate in these meetings via video conversation when technological conditions allow. B’Tselem will gather feedback from our volunteers, as well beneficiary communities, facilitated by our Palestinian field researchers. We plan to hold evaluation meetings and focus groups with the volunteers in the video project to assess personal impact of participation, if the volunteers needs are met, if training is sufficient, if the legal protection is adequate, to adjust implementation as needed.

9 - Protection mainstreaming
9.1 - Do No Harm

B'Tselem monitors and documents the reality on the ground utilizing its extensive presence throughout the West Bank, including, in addition to field researchers, 140 local video volunteers, many of them women, who provide local information, enhancing community engagement in B'Tselem's work. This network of volunteers and communities informs B'Tselem's work, provides access and insights and enhances familiarity with communities at risk, and with victims and eyewitnesses of human rights violations. B'Tselem carefully considers its impact on communities on the ground and operates according to a 'no-harm' approach in which the safety and dignity of targeted communities is considered above all. Our staff and volunteers are trained to always prioritize their own safety above documentation needs and to withdraw from any threatening or dangerous situation. Our field researchers and volunteers are instructed to never intervene in an unfolding confrontation between Palestinians and Israeli security forces or settlers, only to document the events. They do not attempt to stop demolitions, for example, only to document them. B'Tselem works according to a human rights approach in which the Palestinian population participates in monitoring and documenting violations of its own human rights. Since B’Tselem employs a Human Rights Based Approach in which participation is integral, the beneficiaries, as right holders, are involved in project design, implementation and evaluation, by providing information and testimonies on the violation of their rights, testimonies, documentation, photographs and video evidence. We make every effort to be accountable to the Palestinian population. B’Tselem takes our commitment to the Palestinian population seriously, since as an organization heavily based on field research, we could not exist without the gracious cooperation of Palestinian society. Our field researchers are local members of the communities in which they monitor and document violations. The field researchers, together with the Palestinian staff of the video department, coordinate the video volunteers training, monitoring and documentation activities. They represent B’Tselem towards their local communities in the course of their field work. B’Tselem’s ability to obtain testimonies and video documentation of violations from the field depends on maintaining relations with the volunteers and their communities, and their continued cooperation. Our commitment to our volunteers is expressed through the ongoing provision of training and mentoring, legal support and representation in cases when our volunteers are detained, arrested, have their homes raided or their equipment is confiscated.

9.2 - Equal and impartial access to aid

B'Tselem does not provide humanitarian or legal aid, but we provide protective presence to vulnerable communities, monitor, and document and inform the Israeli, Palestinian, and international community about the violations of their rights under occupation. The monitoring, documentation, analysis and public exposure work that we do is delivered in an equal and impartial manner, meaning that the decisions about which of the countless violations the Palestinian public living in the oPt are subjected to B’Tselem will work on are made according to strictly professional considerations. The issues and violations are prioritized according to our ongoing analysis of the changing situation on the ground, and we prioritize according to the severity of the violation, the uniqueness and innovation of our analysis, our own expertise, and human resource considerations. The decision to publish a short research report on a specific incident or violation depends on the quality of the documentation B’Tselem obtains and the availability of reliable witness testimony.
