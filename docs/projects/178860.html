Project Summary

Sustaining and expanding essential, quality health services at primary care levels remains critical to addressing health needs of affected populations. Protracted crisis has left just 64% of hospitals and 52% of primary health care centers (PHCs) fully functional. Up to 70% of health workers have left Syria and many others have been injured or killed. 37% of the affected population require reproductive, maternal, neonatal and child health services, and at least 15% require mental health and psychosocial services with infrastructure and referral systems inadequate to cope with these needs. The health system is unable to respond to the high trauma, mental health and rehabilitation needs. This is causing physical hardship and mental trauma which in turn complicate women’s, fetal and infants’ health in pregnancy and increases risks during delivery. Overall, there is a lack of available, qualified and trained staff, with limited financial means to support supervision and referral services, and community-based interventions relating to MHPSS, child and maternal health and nutrition. Access constitutes a distinctive barrier to health care in areas with high severity scores, including those that recently witnessed changes of control like North East, North West, and parts of Southern Syria. The health system in Damascus and Aleppo is overburdened with hosting the largest internal displacements from within Syria, and by acting as the main reference point for referrals.
AAH proposes a two-pronged approach to meet the priority needs from the 2020 HRP focusing on immediate lifesaving support together with sustainability efforts, integrating health systems strengthening and infrastructure provision of primary care and essential public health functions, including NCD, nutrition, MHPSS and GBV while identifying appropriate referral modalities to ensure local capacity can meet the different needs of women, men, girls and boys. Vulnerability criteria is based on highest severity, needs assessment, (including prioritization of IDPs, returnees, population hosting displaced persons, and people with socioeconomic hardships), contextual volatility and strategic presence of AAH. Monitoring and beneficiary data will be disaggregated by sex, age and marginalized groups, including disabilities. To increase sustained access to health services, AAH will identify emergency small-scale rehabilitations to reinstate safe and dignified condition of services. The intervention will be completed with the provision of basic furniture, medical supplies and equipment. PHC staff will be trained on integrating primary health care services (including MHPSS, nutrition, NCD, and GBV) with activated supervisory visits to ensure adequacy of implementation. Community Health Workers will also be trained to integrate and deliver MHPSS, GBV, nutrition and NCD prevention at the primary healthcare centers, community and household levels within the selected health districts. The project will intervene at multiple levels within the targeted health districts, including primary static and mobile healthcare facilities. A comprehensive MEAL plan is designated to support collection and tracking of sex and age disaggregated data in line with proposed health interventions and is undertaken directly by MEAL in collaboration with health and nutrition department. Monitoring methodologies include needs assessments, baseline and end line, and activity monitoring. Systematic collection of information on gender roles and responsibilities, gender relations and decision-making dynamics, access to and control of resources, and social norms will be done. Qualitative interviews and satisfaction surveys will complement monitoring activities to examine the project’s effect on social dynamics in communities and pathways of change. Focus group discussions and key informant interviews with stakeholders, such as health facility workers and beneficiaries, at key points and midway through program cycle.

4 - Needs

Needs are exacerbated by insufficient access to healthcare due to damage and destruction of health facilities, insufficient human resources, and access constraints. 64% of hospitals and 52% of PHCs are considered fully functional, but inadequate to meet needs. 37% of affected population require reproductive, maternal, neonatal and child health services, and at least 15% require MHPSS. There is lack of available, qualified and trained staff with limited financial means to support supervision and referral services, and community-based interventions relating to mental health and psychosocial support, child and maternal health and nutrition. Up to 70% of health workers have left Syria and many others have been injured or killed. The infrastructure and referral system are inadequate to cope with the needs. Sustaining basic essential health services remains a key need. Access constitutes a distinctive barrier to health care in areas with high severity scores, including those that recently witnessed changes of control like North East, North West, and parts of Southern Syria. The health system in Damascus and Aleppo is overburdened with hosting the largest internal displacements from within Syria, and by acting as the main reference point for referrals. AAH assessments revealed an increased vulnerability to disease outbreaks, especially in North East Syria, a serious scarcity of resources and negligible presence of health actors in key areas of intervention covering a large catchment population, due to the volatile security situation, complicated approvals, and access issues. It is critical to empower people and communities, work together, use evidence and feedback loops to continuously improve performance.

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

Risk: attacks on nutrition facilities. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: de-confliction mechanism for health and nutrition facilities. Reporting attacks against health and nutrition facilities. Advocacy with all parties. Risk: gender inequality and GBV. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: train IYCF counsellors on how to engage with GBV survivors and provide information in an ethical, safe and confidential manner. Ensure referral mechanisms to specialized GBV support, health care, MHPSS, and CP. Ensure locations, times and methods of nutrition services are safe and accessible for women and other at-risk groups. Risk: children are over-exposed to the risk of harm by nutrition personnel, organization staff or related entities. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: Child Safeguarding polices and systems in place, monitored and corrective action taken. Safe, clean and sex segregated bathroom facilities should be provided. Risk: aid workers or associated people use their power or influence to sexually exploit or abuse. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: PSEA policies in place, practiced and monitored. Polices address risks at organizational and program level. The organization’s staff and communities have access to safe/effective reporting systems. Risk: not equal provision of and access to nutrition services for vulnerable groups. Likelihood: high. Impact: high. Mitigation: analyze and disaggregate all data by sex, age and disability. Implementation of nutrition services in line with global and nutrition sector guidance, including improved quality reporting and monitoring. Review informed consent procedures. Ensure that complaints and feedback mechanisms are accessible and friendly.

7 - Does the project include a PSEA strategy ?

Yes

7.1 - Please list any risks related to sexual exploitation and abuse that have been identified during the project design phase and any risk mitigation and prevention measures that have been adopted and built into the project

AAH recognizes the right of everyone to be treated with dignity and respect, and protected from sexual exploitation and abuse. AAH policy on PSEA complies with the IASC six principles relating to sexual exploitation and abuse. Implementation of humanitarian aid activities can sometimes have the unforeseen impact of creating unequal power balances within the AAH team and for recipients of aid; the organization is committed to mitigating against such risks. Sexual exploitation and abuse can do great harm to people affecting integrity and trust; AAH prohibits such exploitation by affirming a zero-tolerance approach. Disregard of the PSEA polices is regarded as gross misconduct, which could result in summary dismissal. AAH reserves the right to refer such cases to the relevant legal authorities in the abuser’s country of origin and in the host country. All AAH staff will always treat people with dignity and respect; undertake awareness raising of the PSEA policy and report any instances of a breach of this policy to the prescribed channels. All employees and representatives of AAH will not engage in sexually exploitative behavior or linking sexual favor with the receipt of aid; engage in any sexual activity with a child under 18 years of age; or being involved with any forms of prostitution or payment for sex. AAH will investigate allegations of sexual exploitation and abuse in a timely, professional, and confidential manner.

8 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Assumptions: security situation allows safe response; access is granted; partners willing to collaborate on timely manner; availability of suppliers and items, prices stable; no major displacements occur. Risk: security situation unstable and/or worsens, AAH has no access; additional administrative procedures imposed on D-INGOs, and restrictions on access to beneﬁciaries; lengthy processes to obtain approvals. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: AAH coordinates with local authorities, Ministries, and stakeholders to monitor the situation, to maintain access to target areas and beneﬁciaries, to develop relationships and mitigate restrictions. Security rules and plans regularly reviewed. Re-planning of activities to recover delays. Advocacy and coordination with the humanitarian community. Risk: devaluation of USD against SYP; International bank transfers not ﬂuid. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: changes in the value of the Syrian currency will be monitored and communicated to donors and options discussed. Risk: aid diversion, financial fraud, and corruption. Likelihood: medium. Impact: high. Mitigation: strict policies and procedures in place for procurement, financial management and risk management; internal controls set up. Monthly monitoring of financial spending. Risk: markets and services not functional for deliveries; procurement of equipment/materials delayed. Likelihood: low. Impact: medium. Mitigation: diversify procurement. Launch framework agreements or tenders for medium time frames. Risk: any kind of abuse (sexual, power, etc.). Likelihood: low. Impact: medium. Mitigation: compulsory trainings on organizational ethical policies. AAH mission has a policy for PSEA and a focal point.
