Objectives

Holistically upgrade the settlement areas and their immediate surroundings, through community-participatory, evidence-based and mid-term planning, construction and maintenance works to develop safe, dignified and environmentally sustainable living conditions.

Needs

Almost 850,000 Rohingya have crossed from Rakhine State (Myanmar) into Cox’s Bazar District (Bangladesh) since August 2017. About 700,000 have spontaneously settled in the Kutupalong-Balukhali ‘mega camp’ in Ukhia upazila while over 140,000 have settled in a number of camps in Teknaf Upazila. Over one year on, living conditions across the camps in Cox’s Bazar District have significantly improved, mostly as a result of the humanitarian response by a considerable number of national and international agencies operating under the overall leadership of the Bangladeshi authorities. Among other priorities, significant resources have been allocated to the improvement of physical access and mobility across the camps, as well as to the implementation of disaster risk reduction measures to mitigate the effect of weather events during monsoon and cyclone seasons. Nevertheless, the so far life-saving nature of the response has shown visible limitations as to the displaced population’s ability to play any meaningful role in the planning and implementation of humanitarian activities directly affecting their daily lives. Hence, as the Rohingya crisis becomes protracted and the response transitions from first-line emergency to the post-emergency phase, heightened attention should be directed to expanding refugee’s participation as a prerequisite for enhanced sustainability. In the mid/long term, improved participation is meant to translate into the progressive transfer of responsibility for the existing and future camp facilities and infrastructure to the displaced populations, which should in its turn maximize community empowerment and reduce dependency on humanitarian. As the authorities and humanitarian actors embrace a settlement approach and prepare for a large-scale reconfiguration of the camps as part of the debate around the Macro Site Development Plan (MSDP), the need for refugees’ participation and inclusion in the planning and development of small-scale site infrastructure (SSSI) stands out as one of the most pressing and yet neglected priorities. Facilitating communities not only to directly undertake the operation and maintenance (O&M) of their community infrastructure assets, but also to influence decisions which will affect the physical environment they live in is an essential step to foster ownership and self-reliance. As part of this community empowerment process, ensuring that groups traditionally under-represented in displacement settings, such as women, elders and youth, get to play a more prominent role is equally important.

Activities or outputs

Currently most Site Management and Site Development (SMSD) agencies undertake O&M through agency interventions, which in the long term is considered not to be sustainable. NRC proposes to trial the formation and empowerment of separate male and female Site Development Community Committees (SDCC) at block level trained on and engaged in SSSI-focused community-based planning. SDCC will be relatively small (10-14 members) to enable fast consultation and decision-making, as well as ease follow-up by the NRC SMS team. Members will represent the various sub-blocks within the block (2 male SDCC members/sub-block and 2 female SDCC members/sub-block). Should such a model be effective, the scope and focus of these committees could over time be expanded to progressively include other areas, including broader community governance issues. NRC will target block-level SDCCs with an intensive community capacity-building programme relying on a mixed engagement methodology. SDCC members will participate in a series of trainings and workshops through which they are supposed to familiarize themselves with the various stages of community-based interventions, including community risk assessment, prioritisation, community action planning and community-driven implementation. These will develop participants’ ability to autonomously but consultatively identify priority problems and home-grown solutions to them. Subsequently, they will translate concepts and methodologies learned during the training into practice, by participating in the development and operationalisation of block development plans, with NRC support. To foster ownership and promote self-reliance, SDCCs will also be allocated small project-based cash grants (max. 5,000 USD/project, up to a total amount of 15,000 USD/block) for the implementation of some of the works included in the block development plans. Although expenditure will be centralized and incurred directly by NRC, as long as they are eligible under the agreed upon community project framework, SDCC members will collectively have decision-making authority over funding allocation. A standard list of SSSI works with unit costs will be provided by NRC for guidance. Each SDCC will be equipped with tools and some "starter" materials to assist with maintenance of community infrastructure. The formation of separate male and female SDCCs is meant to ensure that women can take part in community consultations and influence community prioritization without being inhibited. Furthermore, since the SMSD Sector has on multiple occasions acknowledged that there have been challenges in ensuring adequate female inclusion in cash-for-work initiatives, the formation of gender-separated committees shall also contribute to increase female participation in the actual implementation of SSSI construction. Throughout the project, SDCCs are expected to provide valuable insight into barriers to female participation in site development cash-for-work activities and recommendations for improving participation. It is anticipated that viable recommendations will be implemented as a priority early in the project to increase female participation in cash-for-work initiatives and provide further livelihood opportunities.
