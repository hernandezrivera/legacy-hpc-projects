Project Summary

Jonglei has a GAM above 15% & Pibor 21.6%. The severity of needs in both Nyirol & Akobo is Extreme & Pibor as Catastrophe & FSL Cluster’s IPC-compatible AFI analysis for January- July 2022 classified Pibor, Uror, Mayendit & Akobo as “Extreme”. The SAM & MAM Burdens of Akobo, Nyirol & Pibor are 24,648, 16,394 & 26,502 respectively. In terms of DD, more than 8 in 10 HHs had inadequate food consumption in Jonglei. Jonglei (50%) had the largest proportion of HHs with inadequate diets (0 to 2 food groups) in South Sudan. Food access challenges were widespread in several counties including Akobo (69 %), Nyirol (76 %) & Pibor (81%) among others where most HHs consumed less than 3 food groups (FSNMS Round 25). Leading cause of morbidity is malaria then ARI & AWD with morbidity rates expected to increase proportionally. CRADA shall integrate H&N education messages at triage, testing & treating of malaria & EPI activities in all sites, LLITNs distribution to AM children, HH-based treatment of children under 5 with AWD & using ORS & Zinc as a conjugate treatment regimen. Not only that, WASH integration shall ensure hygiene promotion sessions on hygiene, Aqua tab education & distribution at HH level & sites, Jerrycan, bucket & soap distribution to SAM children under 5 for hygiene promotion. Installation of hand washing facilities & Construction of pit latrine.
CRADA seeks to implement Nutrition in Akobo, Nyirol & Pibor Counties in Jonglei State to prevent & treat malnutrition in children 6-59 months & PLWs with a budget of 220,631 USD. Activities to be derived from cluster objectives of increasing equitable access & utilization of quality preventative & lifesaving nutrition services for early detection & treatment of AM for girls & boys under 5, adolescents & PLW affected by AM & increase equitable access to nutrition sensitive interventions from health, WASH, FSL, Education & Protection sectors through enhanced coordination & integrated programming targeting vulnerable groups in prioritized locations and finally, enhance nutrition situation & program monitoring, analysis & utilization of nutrition information in prioritized locations by the end of 2022.
We are active in Akobo West, Nyirol & in Pibor doing Health, FSL & protection respectively. The project aims to target in; Akobo (58,388) = 2038 SAM cases (999 M, 1039 F), 4011 MAM TSFP cases (1966 M, 2045 F), 5943 MAM TSFP PLWs (3030 P, 2913 L), 18350 Vit A (8992 M, 9358 F), 16991 Deworming (8326 M, 8665 F), 9055 MIYCN (3055 P, 3000 L, 2000 Adolescents, 1000 M) & 2000 BSFP 6-23 months (800 M, 1200 F)
Nyirol (54,364) = 2057 SAM cases (1008 M, 1049 F), 3500 MAM TSFP cases (1715 M, 1785 F), 5066 MAM TSFP PLWs (2102 P, 2926 L), 17283 Vit A (8469 M, 8814 F), 16002 Deworming (7841 M, 8161 F), 8363 MIYCN (3608 P, 3610 L, 845 Adolescents, 300 M), 2093 BSFP 6-23 months (896 M, 1197 F)
Pibor (61,567) = 2452 SAM cases (1202 M, 1250 F), 4500 MAM TSFP cases (2205 M, 2295 F), 5828 MAM TSFP PLWs (2731 P, 3097 L), 18456 Vit A (9044 M, 9412 F), 17089 Deworming (8374 M, 8715 F), 8202 MIYCN (3082 P, 3184 L, 1036 Adolescents, 900 M), 2200 BSFP 6-23 months (1000 M, 1200 F), 2840 BSFP PLW (1440 P, 1400 L)
With a focus on the first 1000 days, communities shall access recommended key messages regarding MIYCN for optimal health to increase knowledge base thus attitude change towards MIYCN practices hence recommended behavior adopted. The community shall be able to practice good nutrition using the balanced diet & DD knowledge, kitchen gardening skills thus a reduction in AM cases. With WASH integration, good hygiene practices shall be adopted & the community freed from WASH related illnesses. With FSL partners on board, communities shall be taught sustainable & flood resilient agriculture to achieve good nutrition outcomes with distribution of seeds & farm implements. OTP & TSFP shall reduce malnutrition related mortality & morbidity thus offer the child a fresh start in life upon recovery from the condition.

1 - Needs

In 2022, an estimated that more than 2 million people in South Sudan will be at risk of acute malnutrition, including 302,078 children (148,018 boys and 154,060 girls) with SAM, 1,039,214 children (509,215 boys and 529,999 girls) with MAM, and 675,548 pregnant and lactating women with acute malnutrition (AM-PLW). In addition, 50,638 (15.4 per cent) out of a total of 328,610 refugees in South Sudan will need treatment for acute malnutrition, including 8,388 SAM children, 26,092 MAM children and 16,158 AM-PLW. There is a slight overall increase of 100,000 people (5.2 per cent) in 2022 compared to 1.9 million people who needed treatment for acute malnutrition in 2021. FSNMS+ Round 27 and SMART Surveys results were used to project the 2022 people in need where extrapolation of GAM/SAM was used since no primary data collection was conducted. This is more than half the population of children under five and pregnant and lactating women in South Sudan. According to IPC ACUTE FOOD INSECURITY & ACUTE MALNUTRITION ANALYSI (January-July, 2022) indicated that 6.83 are in crisis situation with 2.37 in IPC4, of which 55,000 are categorised un IPC5. The FSNMS+ round 27 results, 26 counties in six states (Jonglei, Unity, Warrap, Upper Nile, Northern Bahr el Ghazal, and Eastern Equatoria) have Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) prevalence above the emergency threshold of 15 percent (very high acute malnutrition level- WHO classification). The most affected counties are Pibor, Mayendit, Leer, Canal/Pigi among others with GAM of 21.6 per cent, 23.1 per cent and 20.5 per cent respectively. The high level of food insecurity and malnutrition are compounded by new shocks including COVID-19, localized conflicts and floods that affect large parts of the country especially in Jonglei, Upper Nile, Unity, Upper Nile and Western Equatoria states. Barriers to accessing nutrition services included insecurity, flooding, distance and poor road conditions. The HNO 2022 classifies both Nyirol, Uror, and Akobo as Extreme and Pibor as Catastrophe in terms of severity of needs. Using the FSL Cluster’s IPC-compatible acute food insecurity analysis for the HNO, November 2021 - December 2022, the four counties of Pibor, Uror, Akobo and Nyirol were all classified as “Extreme”. Given the cross linkage between food security and nutrition with it as one of the three underlying causes of malnutrition, one can only wonder how much burden there is in these three counties. The SAM & MAM Burdens for the three counties of Akobo, Nyirol & Pibor are 24,648, 16,394 & 26,502 respectively. In terms of dietary diversity, more than 8 in 10 households had inadequate food consumption in Central Equatorial and Jonglei. Within the 10 states, Jonglei (50 percent) had the largest proportion of households with inadequate diets (0 to 2 food groups). Food access challenges were widespread in several counties including Terekeka (76 percent), Kapoeta East (55 percent), Akobo (69 percent), Fangak (88 percent), Nyirol (76 percent), Pibor (81 percent) and Tonj North where most households consumed less than 3 food groups (FSNMS Round 25). The key drivers of acute malnutrition are multifaceted, including food insecurity, sub-optimal feeding practices, inadequate sanitation conditions and hygiene practices and high prevalence of disease. The leading cause of morbidity is malaria, followed by acute respiratory infections and acute watery diarrhoea. In 2022, morbidity rates are expected to increase proportionally for each of these causes as they did in 2021, compared to 2020. Barriers to access health and nutrition services exist driven by insecurity, flooding, long distance to sites, and poor road conditions. Additionally, communities reported challenges such as long waiting times, limited supplies and lack of facilities in remote locations adding to mothers/ caregiver’s workload. (HNO 2022).

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

We will hold a meeting with community leaders and various key stakeholders and community members who we will brief them about the projects objectives and get any questions from them as well. We will then form Deep community Reporting committees (DCRC) that will have grass root leaders i.e., chiefs and headmen who will identify community members who are not able to access nutrition services due to many reasons such as access, insecurity and flooding. We will have suggestion boxes at all our project meetings and trainings and a hotline number for beneficiaries to provide information on SEA, fraud and corruption. All complaints received from our beneficiaries will be investigated and solutions provided within 48 hours of the report, in the event it involves a CRADA staff, an incident report will be required to be filled within 12 hours of the incident and proper disciplinary action will be followed. All our staff and suppliers will have to sign and adhere to a strict code of conduct. Furthermore, during admission into the nutrition program and on distribution days, beneficiaries shall be informed of their entitlement in terms of what they are getting, duration, quantity & usage. Not only that but also, prior to their admission into the program, beneficiaries shall have been told on the reasons for admission and the admission criteria. The MtMSGs are key in disseminating messages to their fellow women and community members; it is such women who shall be actively used especially during community mobilization sessions such as mass awareness campaigns, community outreaches and during distribution days. We owe it to the community we serve to ensure the people we employ to serve them are professionals with enough skills and experience so as to provide the best services possible.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

CRADA will conduct a training of the community leaders, youth leaders, women leaders on our zero-tolerance policy on SEA, we will, the training will mainly focus on equipping them with the knowledge of how they can identify a victim of sexual abuse and exploitation and the reporting channels they can use to report all forms of SEA. This training will be done using CRADA’s own resources. CRADA will also use its own resources to print PSEA materials with reporting hotline and distribute the print outs, banners and posters to all nutrition centers, to local government offices and in market places. CRADA will also raise awareness of PSEA at water points, markets, and cattle camps using public address To add to that CRADA staff STRICTLY abide by the six core principles that seek to protect beneficiaries and CRADA staff too from all forms of SEA. The principles are as follows. 1. Sexual exploitation and abuse by CRADA workers constitute acts of gross misconduct and are therefore grounds for termination of employment. 2. Sexual activity with children (persons under the age of 18) is prohibited regardless of the age of majority or age of consent locally. Mistaken belief regarding the age of a child is not a defense.3. Exchange of money, employment, goods or services for sex including sexual favors or other forms of humiliating, degrading or exploitative behavior is prohibited. This includes exchange of assistance that is due to beneficiaries.4. Sexual relationships between CRADA workers and beneficiaries are strongly discouraged since they are based on inherently unequal power dynamics. Such relationships undermine the credibility and integrity of humanitarian aid worker. 5. Where a CRADA staff develops concerns or suspicions regarding sexual abuse or exploitation by a fellow staff whether in the same agency or not, he or she must report such concerns via established CRADA’s reporting mechanisms. 6. All CRADA’s staff are obliged to create and maintain an environment which prevents sexual exploitation and abuse and promotes the implementation of their code of conduct. Managers at all levels have particular responsibilities to support and develop systems which maintain this environment. As such, information on PSEA shall be provided by the Gender & Protection Officer whose main role is to act as the PSEA Focal Point for the project. The Officer is to ensure that staff are trained on PSEA and the community also gets to know about this. Posters & visual aids (IEC) materials shall be pinned at the sites for use. Through community dialogues and focused group discussions, the community shall be able to discuss issues around PSEA with concrete examples. The Annual 16 Days of Activism shall be used as an opportunity to pass out PSEA messages to the community through role plays, dramas & songs. The community shall be made aware of the PSEA Hotline Number for CRADA +211919067218 and email psea@crada.africa

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Beneficiaries know what they want, they just don’t have the means of getting them. We shall understand the beneficiaries’ needs through stakeholder consultative meetings, focused group discussions, and community dialogues. The organization shall use chiefs & sultans as part of the volunteers program. These people best understands their community and have a say in whatever enters their area. Feedback on project implementation shall be received and analyzed. Communities are diverse and not everything works everywhere. Based on this, the project shall be contextualized and well adapted to the community needs as analyzed from the feedback. For example, CNVs shall be chosen based on community reference. Casual laborers & security guards shall be selected based on their character and morality as indicated by the community.The Community Mobilizer shall be tasked with collecting community feedback and ensuring that all nutrition sites have a complaints and feedback mechanism fully equipped with a desk, file, cabinet and the person to receive the complaints. All feedback got from the community shall be treated with utmost importance and used to inform and adapt the project design so as to meet their needs.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

The project does not directly contribute to Collective Outcome 3 but rather helps to mitigate it's effects on the affected population. This is because the project teaches kitchen gardening of which flood victims can still be able to practice sack mount vegetable gardening so as to enjoy dietary diversity and adequacy.

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

This project has some GBV activities which shall help to mainstream and integrate GBV into the whole project design. The project is set to recruit Gender and Protection Officers whose roles shall be setting up of clear and safe referral pathways for GBV survivors. Through this, the project shall contribute to Collective Outcome 2.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

This project has both a curative and preventive component. The preventive component shall through the MtMSGs under MIYCN aim at changing the local beliefs and attitudes towards breastfeeding practices and dietary diversity. This same component shall ensure the establishment of kitchen gardens for demonstration purposes to demonstrate to households that a nutritionally adequate diet is possible irrespective of the circumstances.
