Project Summary

NRC’s project will contribute to Food Security (FS) sector HRP objectives 1, 2 and 3. NRC will implement its FS activities through a combination of direct and partner-led modalities, depending on context and access. The NRC Food Security project seeks to build resilience by providing a pathway from acute food needs to greater self-reliance and livelihood recovery, with a focus on the restoration of agricultural livelihoods related to food production and commercialization.
Geographic areas will be targeted based on the severity and scale of needs, inter-agency coordination at hub-level (including the Cash Working Group), and access.
NRC will provide Food Security assistance to vulnerable conflict and displacement-affected populations, including IDPs, refugees, members of host communities, IDP and refugee returnees, and non-displaced households suffering the effects of the conflict. All Food security interventions will seek to prioritize the most vulnerable households among the populations engaged for a given activity. Where appropriate, multi-sectoral vulnerability scoring techniques will be used that integrate vulnerability indicators related to food security, coping mechanisms, socio-economic status, shelter conditions, legal rights, and education. However, some activities will also target households who are less immediately vulnerable, as a way of engaging more holistically with communities and increasing resilience to shocks more systematically. As an overarching principle of engagement, the LFS strategy will focus on assisting adult family members, in acknowledgement that they are usually the prime breadwinners for their families.
FS Objective 1:
Cash Based Assistance: NRC views food security from a basic needs perspective that sees food as a prominent element among the larger basket of basic needs sought by vulnerable and insecure households. NRC will use an unrestricted cash modality to empower households to define and attain their own specific needs whenever appropriate.
Ready to Eat Foods: In cases where markets are not functional or accessible, assistance will be provided through in-kind food. Ready to eat food rations will be distributed in settings where facilities for cooking and preparing food are not available (e.g. camps).
Information and Behaviour Change: As with all material support, a minimal level of knowledge about how to prepare and consume food items provided will accompany all in-kind food assistance. Assistance will be accompanied by information about the importance of dietary diversity and nutritious dietary choices.
FS Objective 2:
Home-Based Food Production: the distribution of a home-based food production kit, accompanied by training in food production techniques. The composition of the kits will be defined in each area through participatory processes to ensure that the items provided are appropriate to the needs, interests, and capacities of the target groups they are intended to serve. In parallel, materials and training for food preservation will be provided as a way to help smooth consumption across seasons.
Income Generating Activities: This focuses on value-added food processing and commercialization as a step towards enabling the restoration of a sustainable livelihood strategy. Importantly, these activities can be accomplished at home, making them more accessible for women whose freedom to participate in activities outside the home may be challenged by cultural norms, protection concerns, and competing responsibilities. The assistance will be determined in consultation with communities, and accompanied by participatory training sessions that increase the likelihood of successful commercialization.
FS Objective 3:
System Strengthening: NRC will identify critical needs in productive infrastructure and invest in their construction or rehabilitation.

Needs

Despite security improvements in many areas of Syria during 2018, vulnerable and conflict affected households continue to experience high levels of food and livelihood insecurity. As of Feb 2019, 6.5 million people across Syria were estimated to be food insecure, and a further 2.5 million individuals are at risk of food insecurity.(2019 HNO - draft)Food insecurity in Syria is mainly driven by a lack of economic access to food. Approximately 40% of households are estimated to spend more than 65% of their income on food (2019 HNO – draft). Trends like hyperinflation, high food prices, and difficulty earning income have left many Syrian households unable to afford enough food to cover their needs. These gaps in economic access manifest in irregular food consumption patterns, a lack of dietary diversity, and a heavy reliance on coping strategies. In October 2018, WFP and FAO reported that half of all households surveyed reported buying food on credit. Reliance on this coping strategy was even more widespread among vulnerable households, particularly those that are IDP (60%), female-headed (57%), and rural (54%)(CFSAM, 2018).Populations who experience some of the most severe food insecurity are those who lack physical access to food and markets, including those in isolated camps and informal IDP settlements. Pockets of acute food insecurity are also prevalent in the remaining hard to reach areas of Idleb, Raqqa, Deir Ez-Zor, Dara’a, Quinetra, Hama, and Rural Damascus Governorates (UN OCHA July 2018). Highly vulnerable categories such as women-headed households, the elderly, and people with disabilities face particular challenges physically accessing food, often stemming from protection risks, such as threats to physical safety, or because of a lack of mobility. Evidence collected by NRC shows that more than 97% of Syrian households who received multipurpose cash in one project reported food as their top expenditure category(NRC PDMs,2017 – 2018).Exacerbating concerns around economic and physical access to food are the challenges faced by the agricultural sector, which serves as an important source of food and income for around a quarter of the population. According to ILO, it contributes around 60% of the national GDP, up from 18% in 2011 (CFSAM, 2018). Nonetheless, all sectors of the agricultural economy continued to decline in early 2019. Some of the main drivers were limitations in the supply and affordability of essential inputs such as seeds, fertilizers, pesticides, equipment, fodder, veterinary treatment, and fuel. The low availability of agricultural inputs is further aggravated by international sanctions, inflation, and the war economy. Contamination of lands, damage to infrastructure, and loss of productive assets also have a negative impact on production.Significantly, in 2017-18 these factors of the conflict combined with severe drought and erratic rainfall patterns to create a devastating impact on Syria’s 2018 cereal harvest. This is evidenced by a 29-year low in wheat production, and a 10-year low in barley production (FAO 2018). The economic consequences this historically low production are faced particularly by the more vulnerable category of rain-fed wheat farmers, who harvested only 38% of their planted crop area. In the hardest-hit Hasakeh region, rain-fed wheat farmers harvested only 6 percent of the planted area, and yielded less than 3% of the previous year’s harvest (CFSAM, 2018). These losses will not only have a major negative impact on agricultural incomes in 2018, but will make accessing seed for the 2018-19 wheat planting cycle much more challenging as a result. Similarly, the scarcity of barley will make it harder for sheep and goat herding households to maintain their flocks, which in the previous years had finally stabilized at historically low levels. In 2018, sheep populations were estimated to have declined by 46% since the start of the conflict, cattle by 41%, and poultry by 56% (CFSAM, 2018

Protection Risk Analysis

1. Risks related to physical security at points of distribution. Crowding can provide cover for harassment of women and increase risks of trampling or jostling. This will be addressed by taking measures to ensure that distributions are carried out in a calm, orderly manner, through identifying risks and mitigation measures in addition to using technology based solutions to ensure efficiency2. Sexual Exploitation and Abuse is another common risk. NRC will engage in PSEA Networks, sensitise feedback mechanisms to receive allegations, raise awareness of staff and partners on prevention through assessing and mitigating against risks 3. There is a risk of increasing nutritional vulnerabilities for selected groups if food provided does not meet minimum recommended macro and micro nutrient daily intake allowances. NRC will design food baskets with sensitivity to nutritional needs, and through the distribution of supplemental (fresh) foods to households with members in vulnerable categories4. For cash based assistance, one critical protection risks is negative impact on local markets. NRC will engage in market assessment and price monitoring to ensure that any such effects are within acceptable margins5. Cash programs can contribute to negative intra-household power dynamics. NRC will consult with women and men to understand their concerns and suggestions to reduce risks6. Exclusion is a risk across all interventions. NRC will mitigate this risk where possible by engaging in comprehensive vulnerability assessment scoring approaches to ensure that selection processes capture multiple dimensions of vulnerability. Where this is not possible due to timing and/or context, NRC will work with local communities to develop beneficiary lists in a participatory manner. NRC will conduct outreach and independent registration where beneficiary lists are derived from lists developed by administrative councils, verifying beneficiaries through household visits using technology solutions to substitute for lack of documentation. Feedback mechanisms will strengthen learning and development of more accurate selection and vulnerability criteria7. In livelihoods projects, NRC will actively monitor the risk of child labour by engaging only with adult family members for training and assistance kits. NRC will find opportunities to educate project beneficiaries about acceptable engagement of children on farms and in household tasks, stressing that at a minimum, children’s contributions should not interfere with education and other developmental priorities, and should never expose them to physical harm.8. The programme will reduce the risk of creating aid dependencies by overlapping assistance in a way that provides a pathway towards self-reliance.9. Activities will consider risks linked to limited freedom of movement by selecting venues that are local to the populations involved, using field or on-location based spaces for activities wherever possible10. Increased risk of animal disease will be managed by providing only vaccinated animals, by accompanying animal assets with routine vaccination support and procuring appropriate fodder11. For livelihoods activities involving land, LFS activities will involve Information, Counselling and Legal Assistance colleagues in conducting due diligence on housing land and property rights, and bring specialised agencies to train teams on mine risk education in areas where this is a known concern.12. Gender, age and diversity will be promoted through flexible responses that are adaptive to the differing needs of men, women and Persons with Disabilities. Trainings and services will aim to facilitate meaningful access to different groups12. Environmental risks will be mitigated through programming modalities that seek to minimize harmful impacts, promote climate change adaptation, and educate project beneficiaries on natural resource management and conservation based production practices

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

1. Escalation of violence and loss of safety and security for staff, partners or beneficiaries: NRC has dedicated Risk and Safety staff at different levels who monitor security change triggers and maintain communication with local and regional analysts. All offices maintain updated vulnerability assessments, risk tolerance statements and security plans for all operational locations. All NRC staff receive security and safety training2. Changes in territorial control. NRC will utilise available information to strengthen understanding of dynamics and nuances across different areas of operation to inform decision-making and maintain neutrality 3. Large-scale return of refugees and neighbouring states push for large-scale return of refugees: Collaboration and coordination with other NRC countries in the region. Development of contingency plans and adapting programmes as well as engaging in advocacy efforts4. Corruption: Enforcing NRC’s policies through the continuous developing and adherence to internal and donor regulations, supported by a dedicated Compliance units at various levels5. Inability or delays in acquiring permissions to operate. NRC will maintain regular and transparent communication with all stakeholders during all stages of the project to continue to build trust and create dialogue to facilitate access6. Interference by armed actors: NRC will continue to highlight the humanitarian nature of assistance and aim to strictly engage with civilian actors to coordinate and facilitate access. Attempts to interfere by armed actors will be reported and plans to engage will be discussed. NRC will utilise existing analyses to maintain a conflict and context sensitive approach and continue engaging with peers through coordination to share good practice and discuss challenges related to interference in humanitarian assistance 7. Lack of community acceptance: NRC will ensure that communities are consulted across different stages. Through recruitment and capacity building of local populations where appropriate and clear selection criteria. Maintaining transparency and opening feedback channels that contribute to effectiveness. 8. Conscription or kidnapping of NRC and LHPs staff. NRC will engage in advocacy efforts to protect humanitarian workers and put in place processes that strengthen security. NRC will coordinate closely with relevant actors to understand any contextual shifts that may amplify risks. Teams will be trained on personal safety and working in high risk environments. Decision making processes will be informed by an understanding of risks and feasible mitigation measures.9. Recruitment and retention of qualified staff in areas of operation: NRC will work to widely advertise available positions, vet and build the capacity of staff. A variety of introductory and technical training opportunities will be provided where available, in addition to promoting peer learning and exchanging of experiences.
