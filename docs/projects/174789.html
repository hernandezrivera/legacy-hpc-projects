Project Summary

PASERREL II will expand upon PASERREL I’s WASH support to ensure there is sufficient access to water, sanitation and hygiene items. Management and behavioral practices need to appropriately use all the above, to address the described gaps and WASH needs in Mopti, with special attention to be paid to combating the spread of COVID-19 and other communicable diseases. Sector will include a range of work on sanitation (emergency latrines for displaced households, plus rehabilitations for host community households), water point rehabilitation, hygiene promotion and provision of hygiene items. Target cercles are Bankass, Koro, Djenne, Bandiagara and Mopti.

1 - Contexte

Water, sanitation, and hygiene surfaced as issues of continued and significant concern in the recent PASERREL baseline, and one that takes on even more importance due to the spread of COVID-19. More than two-thirds of households surveyed in Mopti indicated that their water needs are not fulfilled, and more than one-third of households report accessing water from a non-potable source for reasons that include a lack of access, and insufficient financial resources. The lack of access to water exacerbates other issues, such as the nutritional status of vulnerable people (children, pregnant and/or lactating women), as malnutrition is linked to access to water. Additionally, as water availability decreases, people - often women, girls, and boys - must travel longer distances in search of water, resulting in more exposure to violence and an additional barrier to education for children and youth. As conflict and displacement persist, water needs may continue to increase due to pressure on existing water points as new households arrive, as well as the lack of maintenance as technical services are less readily available. This lack of access to water, especially clean water for hand washing and hygiene at the community level, has been flagged as a major constraint in UNOCHA’s first SitRep on Mali’s COVID-19 preparation and response as they reduce the ability of households to apply preventative hygiene practices. Mercy Corps Mali also recently conducted a Knowledge, Attitudes and Practice (KAP) study through the USAID-FFP funded program Food and Reconciliation in Mopti (FRM), the programmatic counterpart to PASERREL I. Just 24% of respondents had washed their hands with soap or ash at least 3 of 5 key times over the course of two days. This has significant implications for the spread of COVID-19 and merits an increased programmatic focus. The baseline study also gathered updated information on access to and use of latrines in terms of hygiene and sanitation. One-third of households do not have access to a latrine, and one-quarter of households report practicing open defecation. This highlights the need for sanitation infrastructure as well as Social and Behavior Change Communication activities (SBCC). Despite 46% of respondents reporting access to a working latrine in the recent KAP, 41% said they had last defecated in a latrine, enforcing the need to simultaneously address access and behavior change. Access to water and sanitation is also a source of potential conflict within communities, particularly between those from host communities and IDPs. As an incident report from Bankass explains, even in communities that are coexisting peacefully, access to water could damage the existing peace, especially if and when the number of IDPs increases. Furthermore, lack of access to water, environmental degradation, and climate change exacerbate individuals’, families’, and communities’ vulnerability to shocks and reduce their resilience and adaptive capacity. The need for sanitation infrastructure was underlined by the finding that 55% of households surveyed practiced open defecation, higher than the rate among the general population in Mopti in 2015 (37.5%). Observations by Mercy Corps’ field staff report that availability of latrines varies greatly from IDP site to site, with the overall trend being towards insufficient coverage for the growing number of households. Latrines had been frequently constructed in locations that lacked privacy or were in plain sight of the camp. Even among those households living with host families, latrines were often of poor construction or near full, insufficient to deal with the increasing influx of displaced households. In relation to water use, in some sites the insufficiency in the number of water points was evident in the occurrence of conflict among residents waiting for water. Mercy Corps’ prior household survey found that 34.2% of households were consuming less than 15L of water per person per day (Sphere standard), and 14.4% less than 10L per person per day. The process of fetching water could be time consuming, taking at least a half hour for about 45% of households (time to travel to water point, wait for water and return), a chore usually carried out by women and girls. RRM reports consistently find that a majority of households in sites of displacement obtained their drinking water from unprotected wells. The treatment of water is uncommon, practiced by only 9% of households according to our survey. According to recent RRM reports, most households who treat their water use aquatabs or bleach solution. Households often lack sealed water storage (between 73% and 83% in RRM assessments ).In response, PASERREL II will expand upon PASERREL I’s WASH support to ensure there is sufficient access to water, sanitation and hygiene items.
