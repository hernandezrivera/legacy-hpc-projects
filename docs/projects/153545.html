Project Summary

The proposed project will be implemented in Uror and Twic East counties of Jonglei state, Aweil East and Centre counties of Northern Bar el Ghazal, Mundri East county of Western Equatoria and Juba in Central Equatoria. The needs across all four states have continued to escalate throughout 2018, with poor harvests and prolonged conflict resulting in depleted stocks. Uror is predicted to remain at Crisis levels (IPC 3) in January –March 2019, Twic East and Aweil East are predicted to reach IPC 4 levels in the same period and Aweil Centre is IPC 3 whereas Mundri East and Juba are expected to reach IPC IPC 3 IPC 2 and IPC 3 respectively. Continued armed conflict and insecurity has disrupted livelihoods activities, and severely limited market functionality. The resultant food insecurity has lead to communities turning to negative coping strategies including sharing and borrowing food and foraging for wild fruits and roots (Tearfund post-harvest SMART Survey 2017, Tearfund Rapid Assessment Aweil, 2018). The project will Provide agricultural inputs and training to vulnerable households to increase household food production, Provide training on pest control management techniques to reduce crop losses, provide fishing inputs and training where appropriate to vulnerable households to improve food security; and provide direct distribution of food rations and Cash to the most vulnerable communities.

Needs

In January 2018, 5.3 million people in South Sudan were estimated to be experiencing Crisis and Emergency (IPC Phases 3 and 4) acute food insecurity, a 40% increase compared with the same time last year. The most recent WFP Food Security and Nutrition Monitoring Survey (FSNMS 19, Dec 2017) states that South Sudanese households are facing the worst food security situation since 2010, The situation has been exacerbated by climatic shocks, an extended dry season, lack of effective rainfall and households’ inability to access livelihoods inputs, reducing household resilience to food insecurity. Uror: The Uror baseline KAP survey conducted in January 2018 found that agricultural production is limited, despite the majority of households relying on their own production as their main source of food (68.2%). In the week preceding the survey the other main sources of food for households were work for food (11%), gifts (7%) and purchases from the market (6.2%). There were indications of negative coping strategies being employed even in the post-harvest season as 1.8% of households reported borrowing food and 3.2% relied on gathering wild foods. Twic East: In Twic East, Tearfund’s baseline KAP survey, conducted in December 2017, demonstrated a much lower rate of agricultural production in Twic East. Of the households surveyed 41.88% did not cultivate any land at all, and of the 58.12% that did cultivate land, 90.6% of them only cultivated 2 or fewer feddans. Consequently, 35.1% of households’ harvest lasted less than one month, while over three quarters (75.96%) of households produced a yield which lasted three months or fewer, indicating that the vast majority of households will face significant food insecurity throughout the year. Aweil East and Centre: The IPC outlook for January to March 2019 predicts populations in Aweil East County to be facing “Emergency” levels of food insecurity and Aweil Centre to be facing ‘Crisis levels’. With regards to FSL, the main driver of food insecurity is low productivity as a result of climate change and influx of populations stretching resources. 62% of people are indeed reporting their main source of food being through direct purchase but not having the purchasing power makes them reliable on humanitarian assistance. PDMs conducted for Aweil East in August 2018 shows critical gaps (i.e. 39% gap for crop seeds, 61% for vegetable seeds; 54% for tools). Mundri East: In Greater Mundri, access to and availability of food is severely limited due to prolonged conflict, economic instability, consequent disruption of livelihoods activities and poor 2017 harvests, which were exhausted well before the onset of the lean season. In SEM’s Greater Mundri Food Security Needs Assessment (February 2018), 83% of respondents confirmed they are food insecure, skipping meals or eating one meal a day. Community members indicated inadequate intake of food, increased malnutrition, lowered resistance to disease and increased morbidity and mortality. An interagency rapid assessment (March 2018) also found 63% of households had less than a week’s food stock at the time of the assessment, the majority of households reported receiving only 1 month’s food assistance since August 2017 and rates of food sharing were very high between IDPs and host communities. Juba: Continued insecurity and armed conflict have interrupted livelihood activities, severely limited market functionality and access, and caused mass displacements. Combined with an economic crisis that has increased food prices, devalued the local currency and prompted and hyperinflation, this has severely diminished household purchasing power . In Juba, people are facing Crisis (IPC Level 3) and the situation is expected to remain constant up to March 2018. This is leading to high rates of malnutrition; FSNMS data shows GAM rates of 14.6% (6.3-30.4%, 95% C.I.) in Juba county.

Activities

The project will undertake the following activities: Unconditional In-kind food assistance: Food distributions to 5,205 HHs (31,228 indiv) across Mundri East, Aweil East and Centre and JubaUnconditional cash & voucher based transfers with food security/ nutrition objectives : Unconditional cash and vouchers for the purchase of food will be distributed to 1562 HHs (9372 indiv) across Aweil East, Centre and Juba. Emergency food production: main season agriculture seeds & tool distribution; fish, vegetable & crop kits. 18,530 farmer HHs (111,178 indiv) as well as 21,404 fishers (128,422 indiv) will be trained on Crop and vegetable production, post harvest practices, storage and value addition. Each HH will receive either a crop/vegetable kit, or a fishing kit across Uror, Twic East and Aweil East/Centre. Support to seed fairs using locally sourced seed; seed security & multiplication strengthening: 13,300 HHs (79,800 indiv) will be targeted for training on seed multiplication initiatives, using the lead farmer approach across Uror, Twic East and Aweil East/CentreAs part of Tearfund’s risk mitigation approach, a comprehensive analysis will be conducted and periodically updated in all the project sites. Tearfund takes into account seasonal risks such as access during the rainy season and the security risks associated with the dry season. In order to mitigate this, items will be prepositioned where possible, and procurement will be done in the dry season. Appropriate measures will be taken to secure warehouses. Conversely, environmental risks such as disease outbreak/crop interventions have also been considered. Tearfund will work closely with FAO and other NGOs to ensure timely detection and reporting to the appropriate focal points. Additional emphasis will also be placed on diversifying the inputs provided in order to mitigate the risk of crop failure. Tearfund’s integrated approach to programming also adds a layer of security, as Tearfund in present in the operational areas, carrying out WASH and Nutrition interventions, as well as working through local partners. This has greatly enhanced the level of community acceptance.

Does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

Yes

How does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

At the beginning of each intervention, Tearfund provides the whole community with information about the project, including beneficiary targeting, locations, feedback mechanisms and expectations of Tearfund staff and volunteers. This is frequently re-iterated through community discussions and focus group discussions. Targeted communities are also made aware of the timing of key project activities and the locations. Feedback on these decisions is welcomed at the project design stage and throughout. The information is further disseminated by Tearfund's network of trained community volunteers, who are embedded within each village.

Are messages context-specific or/and tailored?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Culturally appropriate learning materials will be promoted throughout the intervention. The project will print all related materials in the local languages. Where literacy rates are low, the Tearfund will adjust community accountability mechanisms accordingly. In such situations, beneficiary verification methods for the cash based interventions will also be bio metric rather than code based.

Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse

Yes

Briefly describe how

Protection risks are identified and assessed before the start of the project, and will be continually monitored throughout implementation. Beneficiaries will also be sensitized on confidential feedback mechanisms and Tearfund staff are trained on how to make referrals to appropriate protection actors when possible. Tearfund will also coordinate with other protection actors in the intervention areas to ensure that there are always active referral pathways to the appropriate service providers. Furthermore, Tearfund staff are contractually bound to adhere to the internal PSEA policy, as outlined in the Tearfund Code of Conduct. This is promoted through briefing and awareness of all staff at induction as well as underpinning processes to respond to any potential breaches. Each new staff member is briefed in country - expatriate staff by the HR Manager; national support staff in the local language by the Assistant HR Manager; national field staff in the local language by the HR Officer. All staff are provided with the following documents (included below; all are also available in the national language): ▪ a copy of the personal conduct policy▪ a form which is signed agreeing to adhere to that policy▪ a child protection self-declaration form Staff are also briefed on the Staff Handbook, which reiterates these documents. A management checklist form for HR processes audits that this process has been followed (normally used by the HR Manager to monitor processes across the programme). All Tearfund employees on UK based contracts are signatories to the Personal Conduct Policy and Whistleblowing policy. All those on national contracts are signatories of the National Staff Code of Conduct individually.Responses to potential breaches are undertaken through direct intervention by line managers when a breach is identified, or through routing via the Grievance or Whistleblowing policies.

Nature of the consultations

Yes

Briefly describe the nature of the consultations

Tearfund is committed to ensuring the affected populations actively participate in project activities. Tearfund has found that in the past, marginalized groups may fear being involved in community discussions. Therefore Tearfund actively encourages and insists on the involvement of all sectors of the community , including IDPs and returnees. Separate consultations are conducted for men and women, in consideration of cultural or social pressures that women may face when trying to express themselves. Tearfund frequently conducts focus group discussions and key informant interviews, in addition to periodic monitoring assessments. Specifically with regards to the proposed project, Tearfund will seek to consult communities on the specific types of inputs they prefer, including types of seeds as well as the preffered transfer modalities. In the past, this has included specific consultations with vulnerable people to assess the risks and benefits of cash based interventions, as well as the preferred types of seeds for various community members, including mothers of malnourished children/caregivers in IYCF programmes.

Feedback /complaint mechanisms?

Yes

Briefly describe the mechanisms

Feedback mechanisms are already established in all project locations, and include reporting to community volunteers, Tearfund staff, quarterly focus group discussions, meetings with local leaders, and feedback boxes at water points and distributions. The feedback mechanisms have been shown to be accessible, appropriate to the context and preferred by the community during other Tearfund projects. Tearfund will ensure that targeted communities are aware of all feedback and complaints mechanisms and the locations of feedback boxes during community sensitisation meetings, and at the beginning of distributions. Tearfund staff are trained on how to handle feedback ensuring anonymity and confidentiality where appropriate, and pass on any concerns to relevant staff members. All feedback is recorded and responded to, after which it is incorporated into the current and future project designs. Any feedback regarding critical or sensitive protection issues will be referred directly to Tearfund Executive Team in Juba, investigated appropriately and responded to according to Tearfund’s Safeguarding Policy.

Protection Risk Analysis?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Tearfund is committed to mainstreaming protection and gender in all FSL activities. In the target locations, there are differing gender roles in agriculture and aquaculture. While both men and women are involved in planting and land preparation, men are often away from the community engaging in cattle herding, which is the responsibility of men and boys. Ownership of livestock also depends on gender; men own the household’s cattle, whereas women own small animals such as chickens. Weeding and harvesting are the responsibility of women, boys and girls, and children are involved in activities such as chasing birds away from crops. Land is owned by men, however the crop harvests and farming tools are shared between men and women. Nevertheless, vegetable gardening and food preparation is the responsibility of women and girls. Fishing is conducted by men and women. Where households have a male head, the man usually takes precedence in attending training or receiving information, therefore Tearfund actively encourages the attendance of both men and women at training, during which all identified and registered beneficiaries are given equal access to agricultural and fishing information. The proposed activities will target the most vulnerable within the community, and take consideration of the needs and special adaptations required to accommodate the most vulnerable. The community and other stakeholders will be sensitised on the project and informed of the beneficiary selection criteria, which will be agreed by community members. During community sensitisation the importance of prioritising women and vulnerable groups will be emphasised in order to gain community acceptance of the selection criteria. Vulnerable households will be prioritised for assistance, including female, elderly and child headed households, those with malnourished children and people living with disabilities.

Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

As fishing activities have limited adaptations for those with disabilities or the elderly due to the need to travel to the water sources, those households with elderly or disabled members that are targeted for assistance will be able to nominate an able-bodied member of the household to participate in the training on behalf of the family. Women and vulnerable groups will be encouraged to travel to fishing sites in groups and to restrict their fishing times to the middle of the day, not at night or early in the morning.

Addresses inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

The proposed project takes into account the different social and cultural inequalities that exist within the project locations, particularly with regards to the roles and responsibilities of women. The project will prioritise women and other vulnerable groups, while also applying the Transforming Masculinities approach throughout project activities, aimed at changing the way society views women and girls.

Does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Access is a key issue which in built into all Tearfund monitoring activities, particularly as Tearfund aims for full coverage of the operational counties. This includes identifying specific cultural, social and economic barriers that communities face in relation to the project. This is done through frequent focus group discussions. In consideration of the sensitivity of the subject, Tearfund undertakes separate discussions for groups such as IDPs as well as women, and ensures that all groups are aware of individual feedback mechanisms. Tearfund has well established relationships within the targeted communities through long standing partnerships with local NGO’s, direct implementation of nutrition activities as well as WASH activities. Therefore, Tearfund aims to take a multisectoral approach to access, ensuring that beneficiaries have access to all the different interventions.

Does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Safety (both physical and otherwise) is measured through individual and group community discussions as well as frequent field visits and field reports detailing the safety and security situation in the operational areas. Beneficiaries will frequently be asked to provide individual feedback, which will remain confidential. Reports will be generated which separate feedback related to safety and protection, and all incidents will be logged and reported. Appropriate actions will be taken and this will inform future interventions.
