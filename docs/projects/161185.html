Project Summary

Despite a period of relative stability over the past year, the malnutrition and food insecurity situation across South Sudan remains critical. According to Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) projections for January - April 2020, over 5.5 million people (47% of the population) are predicted to be severely food insecure (IPC 3, 4 & 5), even considering the presence of humanitarian assistance. Levels of acute malnutrition and food insecurity are expected to rise in early 2019 as post-harvest stocks become depleted. Recent SMART surveys conducted in all major counties of Action Against Hunger’s interventions show GAM rates above the emergency threshold of 15%: GAM prevalence in Fangak, Gogrial West, and Aweil East was 16.2%, 20.1%, and 15.5%, respectively, while SAM prevalence was 3.3%, 4.3%, and 1.6%, respectively. Ongoing insecurity, poor hygiene and sanitation infrastructure, limited availability of diverse and nutritious food in local markets, low coverage of health and immunization services, and constrained and livelihoods opportunities exacerbate these conditions. To address these issues, Action Against Hunger will continue its programs for the treatment and prevention of acute malnutrition in Warrap, Northern Bahr el Ghazal and Jonglei States in 2020. The program will directly target 40,772 children under 5 and 26,106 pregnant and lactating women in treatment of acute malnutrition at 3 stabilization centers and 33 nutrition sites (OTP/TSFP). In total, 33,833 caregivers will be sensitized on MIYCN topics. Treatment programs will prioritize the identification and treatment of children under five with SAM and MAM, as well as PLW with MAM, through outpatient therapeutic programs (OTP) and targeted supplementary feeding programs (TSFP). Stablization centers (SCs) in Alek, Malulakon, and Paguir will provide treatment to children with SAM and medical complications. In the hard-to-reach area of Paguir, we will also introduce ICCM and mobile delivery of OTP/TSFP (ICCM+) to increase coverage for communities facing seasonal stress migration. Caregivers will receive sensitization and counselling on MIYCN, health, and hygiene/sanitation practices, and critical gender- and age-sensitive hygiene items. To ensure early detection and referral of acute malnutrition, community volunteers will be trained to lead active screening and referral at the household level. During these screenings, volunteers will also provide sensitization on nutrition and hygiene/sanitation practices. Prevention activities will focus on community mobilization and sensitization as well as addressing the underlying causes of malnutrition. These activities include training community leaders, hosting mass awareness campaigns around MIYCN and hygiene/sanitation topics, promoting mother-to-mother support groups (MtMSG), and facilitating men’s discussion groups (MGDs) to encourage discussion among male caregivers on critical nutrition and health subjects. To address underlying causes of undernutrition, Action Against Hunger will train lead mothers of MtMSGs in kitchen gardening and cooking demonstrations. In addition to these static interventions, Action Against Hunger’s emergency response teams will also continue to provide malnutrition surveillance, treatment and prevention services, and capacity building throughout the country in areas of highest need as identified by the Nutrition Cluster’s Integrated Rapid Response Mechanism.

1 - Needs

South Sudan has experienced a protracted humanitarian crisis since 2013. As a result, over 4.2 million people have been displaced, with 2.2 million fleeing to neighboring countries. Despite a period of relative stability over the past year, the malnutrition and food insecurity situation across South Sudan remains critical. According to Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) projections for January - April 2020, over 5.5 million people (47% of the population) are predicted to be severely food insecure (IPC 3, 4 & 5), even considering the presence of humanitarian assistance. Levels of acute malnutrition and food insecurity are expected to rise in early 2019 as post-harvest stocks become depleted. A combination of factors exacerbates need and complicates humanitarian response. These include a lack of access to a steady and diverse food supply as a result of disrupted markets, low health services coverage, limited WASH infrastructure, and limited knowledge and practice of healthy nutrition and WaSH behaviors, among others. While improvements in availability and access to food are expected, particularly in former Warrap State, climatic shocks are expected to result in below average harvests in former Northern Bahr el Ghazal State. These shocks have also eroded livelihoods and access to essential services and markets. Nutrition needs remain high throughout the country despite partners’ efforts. 2019 SMART surveys conducted in all major counties of Action Against Hunger’s interventions show GAM rates above the emergency threshold of 15%: GAM prevalence in Fangak, Gogrial West, and Aweil East was 16.2%, 20.1%, and 15.5%, respectively, while SAM prevalence was 3.3%, 4.3%, and 1.6%, respectively. While this demonstrates some marginal improvement from previous years, continued interventions are necessary to encourage further progress and limit deterioration.

2 - Activities

Action Against Hunger will continue to deliver high-quality nutrition services in the catchment areas of its 3 stabilization centers (SCs) and 33 static nutrition sites in Aweil East and Aweil Center, Gogrial West and Gogrial East, and Fangak. These services focus on both treatment and prevention of malnutrition in compliance with national CMAM protocol. Community Volunteers and mothers will provide regular MUAC screening of children under five and PLW at the community level to ensure early case detection and timely referral. Children presenting with SAM and medical complications will receive treatment at the nearest SC. Child-friendly spaces and early childhood development stimulation are offered at each SC. Children with SAM and no medical complications will be treated through an Outpatient Therapeutic Program (OTP) at nutrition sites. Finally, children with MAM and PLW will receive treatment through a Targeted Supplementary Feeding Program (TSFP). Furthermore, ACF will lead sensitization sessions on nutrition, MIYCN, WASH, and promotion of health-seeking behavior for mothers and caregivers of children under 5 and PLW at all nutrition sites, as well as at the community level. The organization will train staff and CVs on CMAM, MIYCN, GBV, and key messages on nutrition, WASH, health, and gender. Community members will be further engaged to prevent undernutrition through mother-to-mother support groups (MtMSGs) and men group discussions (MGDs). Action Against Hunger will train lead mothers of the MtMSGs on facilitation techniques and key MICYN concepts in line with South Sudan’s MIYCN Guidelines. Additionally, MtMSG members will be trained on kitchen gardening and cooking demonstrations to provide both an alternative source of income and nutritious food at the household level. To encourage men’s participation as change agents, MGDs aim to initiate a dialogue around and sensitize men on MIYCN and women’s issues (e.g., SRHR, GBV, etc.) and promote gender equality. This increases male awareness on nutrition interventions and thereby enables women’s participation, as women often need men’s consent to participate in activities outside the home. All prevention and treatment programs will be closely aligned with the organization’s WASH and FSL programs, which ensure the equitable access of families of SAM and MAM cases to water and sanitation supplies and services and agricultural inputs. This integration also aims to embed resilience into conflict- and malnutrition-affected communities. To further the reach of basic nutrition and health services, ICCM+ will be introduced in hard-to-reach areas of Fangak. This will incorporate mobile delivery of OTP/TSFP to increase coverage of communities facing seasonal stress migration. The organization also aims to introduce mobile response in Gogrial East. Our programs also encompass the emergency response teams, which provide malnutrition surveillance, treatment and prevention services in areas of high need as identified by the Nutrition Cluster. Our nutrition programs are fully integrated with gender, age and protection considerations: patient flow is optimized at site level to mitigate waiting time, waiting areas are shaded and guarded by security personnel, latrines are provided which are sex-segregated and lockable, and at least one female staff is present in case mothers require consultation on sensitive issues. The organization will collaborate closely with other health and nutrition actors, and build the capacity of key health and nutrition staff whenever possible. We will conduct regular monitoring of the nutrition situation in the project area through SMART and rapid response assessments; the information will be used for appropriate actions at the project level. SQUEAC assessments will also evaluate access to and coverage of services. The organization will also introduce MIYCN Knowledge, Attitudes and Practices (KAP) surveys to assess behaviors critical to mitigating undernutrion.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

Where displaced affected communities are identified during the project identification, they will be incorporated. According to the latest IOM DTM, approximately 30% of the NBeG and Warrap populations are internally displaced persons. These persons will be involved into community action planning processes to incorporate their perspective and plans for the future. Participants from a wide range of communities and gender and age groups, including displacement-affected people, participate in mass sensitization and awareness campaigns conducted by the organization. Projects are also supported by community management committees, which are comprised of representatives from both host and displaced populations.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

Action Against Hunger consults with state-level Ministries of Health and other local authorities to ensure synergy and capacity building for these bodies. In 2018 Action Against Hunger trained several state Ministry of Health staff on CMAM and other nutrition-related issues. The organization also participates in and supports state-level nutrition and health campaigns, such as de-worming and Vitamin A supplementation, through either training or providing personnel (e.g. community volunteers). Nationally, the organization works in collaboration with the relevant line ministries of the government of South Sudan, including the South Sudan Rehabilitation and Recovery Commission (RRC) and the Ministry of Labour. At national and state levels, Action Against Hunger continues to coordinate with the Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry and the Ministry of Water and Irrigation. Additionally, in all its regions of operation, the organization supports national assessments such as the FSNMS and IPC through the provision of SMART data as well as program personnel.

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

Action Against Hunger’s nutrition programs are fully in line with national CMAM and MIYCN protocols, on which all nutrition staff and volunteers are trained. The project is also aligned with the priority framework set forth by the Nutrition Cluster, including providing lifesaving treatment for SAM and MAM in children under five years of age, preventing undernutrition, increasing access to integrated interventions, and enhancing nutrition situation monitoring, analysis, and utilization of information. The organization also participates in the Rapid Response Mechanism Technical Working Group, Nutrition Information Working Group, CMAM and MIYCN Technical Working Groups, Quality and Accountability Working Group, and WASH Strategic Advisory Group. Through these working groups, we assist the MoH with development of national, gender-sensitive nutrition policies. Furthermore, our emergency response program works closely with the Nutrition Cluster as part of the national Rapid Response Mechanism.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

The project protection environment is stable: beneficiaries are able to move to and from services with relative security. Northern Bahr el Ghazal, Warrap, and Jonglei States have enjoyed reduced armed violence since 2016. Warrap in particular has been affected by inter-communal conflict in previous years, however has witnessed significant improvement in 2019. ACF will also conduct periodic GBV safety audits to enhance GBV risk mitigation. Furthermore, nutrition staff will be trained on non-specialized protection package to identify and refer children with protection concerns for specialized care.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

Action Against Hunger prioritizes building resilience and locally-led sustainability in its target populations through its community-based, scalable approach. South Sudan remains in an emergency context and currently lacks an adequate public health system. Therefore, the organization focuses on beneficiary leadership in all activities to promote sustainability and risk preparedness. Engaging community members, such as mothers and community volunteers, in active screening, referral, and sensitization builds local capacities to provide nutrition and health services. Similarly, the organization strengthens partner and Ministry of Health staff knowledge on CMAM, MIYCN, and SRHR through trainings and workshops. Whenever possible, we incorporate town hall meetings that include male and female community leaders and support the creation of gender-balanced committees (e.g. Water User Committees) for management of project activities. These efforts aim to strengthen ownership by targeted communities and contribute to sustainability.

8.18 - Please indicate how the risks selected above will be mitigated.

o Some nutrition sites have extensive catchment areas. In these cases, mothers, caregivers, and children must often travel or walk long distances to access the sites, sometimes staying overnight in the bush. This journey presents several safety risks, including harassment and sexual violence. In order to identify these risks, Action Against Hunger conducts regular GBV safety audits at its nutrition sites. Feedback from these safety audits will be used to understand the risks that beneficiaries face and thereafter implement solutions to mitigate them.o As discussed above, Action Against Hunger has a comprehensive feedback mechanism integrated into all programming to ensure active engagement with beneficiaries. However, many beneficiaries are illiterate which reduces the range of modalities available for them to use to provide feedback and could therefore affect feedback quality. For example, feedback presented in a face-to-face session may not be as honest or comprehensive as feedback offered anonymously due to the power dynamics at play. To address this, Action Against Hunger also uses focus group discussions to garner feedback in a less individualized manner. The organization will continue to ensure that these FGDs are gender-sensitive (i.e., female FGDs are led by females, etc.) by recruiting additional female community volunteers and health workers who will be trained on the feedback mechanisms.o The long distance traveled by some beneficiaries to receive services at nutrition sites exposes them to risks of violence and exploitation. Action Against Hunger is developing several mechanisms to mitigate these risks. In addition to undertaking site safety audits, the organization is also piloting mobile delivery of OTP/TSFP in Fangak to increase program coverage and reduce the distances traveled and time burden to receive services.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

Three groups are prioritized for feedback solicitation: 1. First-line program implementers (including volunteers and field staff); 2. The local communities where our programs operate; and 3. The direct beneficiaries of our programs. First line implementers work directly with beneficiaries on a day to day basis: they can often provide the most useful readily available information regarding the challenges beneficiaries face. Five modalities have been identified as “minimum standards” for the site level: 1. Monthly staff meetings with first-line implementers, 2. Focus Group Discussions with Direct beneficiaries, 3. Individual interview form, 4. Comment Box at Field Office Gate, 5. Hotline Number. The identities of all feedback and complaints mechanism (FCM) complainants are kept strictly confidential by program management staff when they are provided. We avail a comment box and anonymous hotline for those who wish to keep their identity strictly confidential. We have standard, context appropriate FCM IEC materials (posters, stickers, flyers), which are displayed at all project sites and on all project structures (e.g. equipment used by beneficiaries). IEC materials include images and text in both English and the local dialect to ensure comprehension by a maximum number of individuals. During all community meetings, trainings, and sensitization sessions, a standard script is used by staff to inform beneficiaries on available FCM modalities. Depending on the urgency, feedback will be delivered to the first line program implementers on a monthly basis during the monthly meetings. Specific responses can also be discussed bi-monthly at the beginning of focus group discussions at the site level to inform beneficiaries of what changes are being made given previous feedback, or if any concerns are being addressed at the national level. Urgent issues are addressed immediately by the Field Coordinator and PM through appropriate channels (community meetings, Ministry).
