Project Summary

West Darfour, White Nile and Blue Nile States depend largely on agricultural production for household food security and livelihoods. Low farmer capacity, poorly functioning market systems, increased climate related shocks and stresses, and decades of insecurity, continue to stunt agricultural production and drive households to face food insecurity. Limited access to climate-smart improved inputs, financial services and technical skills training, further undermine potential agricultural production for the target areas. Medium to large-sized farms are beginning to benefit from growing irrigation schemes, government and private sector financial services, and access to improved inputs, however smallholder farmers remain marginalised. This market marginalisation, coupled with a high dependence on agriculture, increases farmers’ vulnerability to social, economic, and environmental shocks and stressors, directly impacting household livelihoods and food security.
ADRA, seek to tackle this dynamic, with a goal to improve food security for 323,550 male and female smallholder farmer households through enhanced gender equitable market access and agricultural production. The Project, will strengthen the capacities of key market actors, contributing to improved resilience capacities to shocks and stresses. The Project is guided by a Market Systems Development (MSD) approach, in addition to ADRA’s resilience approach, to sustain food security gains despite the complex risk environment in which smallholder farmers are exposed to. The Project is centred around four key objectives:
Objective 1: Male and female smallholder farmers have increased and more climate resilient yields: Agricultural production yields and a household’s food security are closely linked for smallholder farmers. With limited alternative livelihood opportunities, smallholder farmers face a cycle of poor inputs, variable yields and low profit margins, all greatly influenced by environmental, economic, and political shocks and stresses. The Project will disrupt this cycle through knowledge building, access to climate-smart inputs and market oriented investments contributing to increased yields among 197514 male and female smallholder farmer households.
Objective 2: Male and female smallholder farmers increase sustainable natural resource management practices: Poor agricultural practices, coupled with increased climate related shocks and stresses (droughts, flooding, wind), have stripped soil nutrients, increased soil erosion, and contributed to poor agricultural yields. Household and communal level natural resource management plans are greatly needed for smallholder farmers to navigate this dynamic. Through the introduction of Community Action Plans, alongside strategic investments in resilient building infrastructure, the Project will empower communities to actively manage individual and communal natural resources, helping to mitigate environmental shocks and stresses.
Objective 3: Male and female smallholder farmers have improved market linkages: Poorly functioning markets are detrimental to a community’s income generation potential. Market actors, including financial service providers, input agents, transporters, and end buyers, reflect a market dynamic that supports investment and long-term economic growth. The Project will focus on increasing market linkages across key agricultural value chains, increasing access to key goods and services and end markets, subsequently increasing investments to help drive production yields.
Objective 4: Increased equality between men, women, boys and girls and increased empowerment of women and girls in smallholder agricultural market systems: Gender dynamics play a critical role in resource allocation, decision making and the long-term economic well-being of a household. the Project will tackle this through proven approaches, helping to drive increased gender equity and empowerment within households and the larger communities

1 - Needs

With significant increases in food and other commodity prices, a reduced harvest, and continued conflict, acute food insecurity in Sudan continues to worsen rapidly. Latest acute food insecurity data indicates that around 9.65 million people across Sudan were highly food insecure and classified in Crisis (IPC Phase 3 or worse) from April to May 2022. This includes 3.1 million people in IPC Phase 4 (Emergency) and 8.6 million in IPC Phase 3 (Crisis). The prevalence of the population in IPC Phase 3 and above has moved from 13% (October 2021 – February 2022) to 20% (April-May 2022) to 24% (June-September 2022), driven by a plummeting economy, poor harvests and conflict. The situation is projected to worsen between June and September, with up to 11.7 million people (a further increase of 2 million) likely to be in Crisis (IPC Phase 3 or worse). This includes 3.1 million people in IPC Phase 4 (Emergency) and 8.6 million in IPC Phase 3 (Crisis). The most affected groups are internally displaced people (IDPs), returnees, those stranded in conflict areas, refugees from South Sudan, Ethiopia, and other neighbouring countries, and poor groups from agro-pastoral and pastoral communities in rural areas of Western, Eastern and Northern Sudan, whose livelihoods are directly affected by the impact of lean season and macroeconomic crises. 2 - Activities/Outputs

To improve access to food supplies through increased access to agricultural inputs and resilient practices while also increasing household income from new and restored livelihoods through developing strategies to improve food systems and reduce food losses and waste, Strengthen and support initiatives addressing cross-cutting issues of food security and nutrition of vulnerable groups. Subsidise agriculture inputs such as seeds and fuel, and improve investments in extension services and support to protect productive assets, especially areas impacted by flooding, conflict or other shocks, Ensuring Land Access for Vulnerable Beneficiaries. Establishment of the FFS will follow a “foci model” (in which groups grow outwards from a core nucleus), where successive FFSs are established in the immediate neighbourhood to form a cluster. Provide improved seeds and tools to 15 000 farmers—20-25 farmers in each of the targeted villages. The seeds will be selected through a technical committee to ensure that they do not pose a threat to the crops in the area and do not damage the ecosystem. ADRA will also further enhance integration, by referring farmers with production capacity to market development programs ADRA is currently implementing in the area with other donors. This will include ADRA Farmer Market School trainings to help strengthening linkages between farmers and goods and service providers. Activities will also include Non-conditional Cash assistance, Farmer Field School Training, Provision of training, and livelihood assets, Permaculture and Cash for work in targeted areas to support 197514 population and improve their food security and nutrition. This will provide opportunity of food and cash support to targeted communities, the activities will ensure equipping farmers groups with necessary skills to provide for themselves and their families with to plant and enhance their agriculture production through training and farmers farming schools, the project will enhance and strengthen the connection between communities and markets to ensure their accessibility to markets and buy their farming needs as well as sell their products with fair price and are able to sell what they produce.

5.2 - Impact on environment/natural resources

Some of the agricultural methods used by small-scale farmers that lead to land degradation and environmental damage include: clear cutting of land to make way for cultivation, the lack of crop rotation, and the practice of monocultures. In addition, due to the low productivity of their farmland, small scale farmers often resort to cutting trees all around to make charcoal or sell as fuel wood to earn income for buying food crops. To remedy this situation, ADRA will distribute high yielding crop varieties in addition to training using the FFS curriculum in which productive farming using conservation methods is one of the main premises of the trainings. Farmers will be encouraged to adopt agroforestry instead of clear cutting, and the establishment of shelter belts through growing of gum Arabic trees to encourage intercropping and reduce reliance of monocultures.Through the FFSs, the project will provide technical support to beneficiaries to ensure they have the information and hands-on knowledge for how to reduce the risk of pest and weed damage to their crops. The Project will focus on technical—rather than material—support, using FFSs as a vehicle to train beneficiaries on implementing integrated pest management (IPM). The IPM component of the training will have modules focusing on:• Balancing crop productivity with the height of the annual “pest” infestations when planning planting times.• Intercropping—the use of intentional planting of different crops together to mitigate pest infestation problems associated monoculture planting.• Crop rotation, to mitigate planting crops that certain pests prefer and where they may have left eggs/larva for the following year.• Natural pest deterrents utilising plants such as neem and hagel.a. Project locations and surrounding area will not drain into any bodies of water. All project locations are far from the Nile and other river sources.• Ensuring optimal environments for natural pest predators.• Enhancing or creating linkages between farmers and agriculture extension offices in the target localities.

6.2 - Explain how your project aims to ensure protection mainstreaming during its design and implementation

Protection is a concept that encompasses humanitarian operations in response to complex emergencies or natural disasters aimed at obtaining full respect for the rights of the individual in accordance with the letter and spirit of human rights, refugee, and humanitarian law. The project design is sensitive to the heightened vulnerability of women and girls in the current crisis and ADRA’s transparent working style will mitigate the risks for harm, exploitation and abuse and builds trust. ADRA will build on our existing relationships with communities and build rapport with new communities, to ensure that beneficiaries are comfortable in reaching out to them if and when issues arise to provide support to those who may not have any way to report issues; particularly surrounding GBV. In program design and set up, ADRA will work closely with women and children to ensure that program designs, including proposed training sessions and FFSs, are implemented in a way to not expose vulnerable individuals to situations that would expose them to potential GBV. Community engagement is at the core of the program, especially the protection component. In order to successfully deliver the services in the area, ADRA will ensure that channels of communication continue to be open, regular, and accessible throughout the program cycle. At the outset of the program, the program team will conduct meetings with the different communities, including the tribal leadership, and those from specific vulnerable groups (women, girls, elderly, youth, PWDs, PLW) to inform them that the program, to which they contributed to the planning process, has been approved. Together the program objectives, expected outputs, methodology of activities and timelines will be reviewed and agreed on. In order to foster inclusivity in decision making, ADRA will actively aim in engaging different age groups . Youth are usually the main community groups that volunteer to undertake such responsibility due to their physical abilities. However, ADRA will also provide youth with the knowledge to participate in decision making and allow them to follow-up on the program activities.
