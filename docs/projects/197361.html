Project Summary

Overall Objective:
661,697 conflict affected girls and boys aged 3 to 17 years and 34,967 youths in Adamawa, Borno and Yobe States of Northeast Nigeria realize the right to education through completion of basic education with the necessary foundational skills and the skills for gainful employment and the ability to live productive lives.
Specific Objectives:
1. To improve equitable access to inclusive, age- and level-appropriate learning opportunities (formal/non-formal) for 661,697 children (ensuring atleast 50% girls) and 34,967 youths.
2. To improve retention and transitions within and between learning pathways that are reflective of real learning progress enabled by quality teaching and education delivery.
3. To strengthen the education systems at community, local government, and state levels to sustain a robust and adaptable education infrastructure and service continuity

1 - Needs

Since 2009, Borno, Yobe and Adamawa (BAY) states in North-East Nigeria have been impacted by civil armed conflict. The long crises had left approximately 8.4 million people in need of humanitarian assistance on an estimated total population of 12.4 million , including 8 million children. Of these, 2.2 million are internally displaced; 1.5 million are returnees who lack essential services and livelihoods; and 3.9 million are members of communities who are also in need of assistance . The fragility of the context makes the crisis one of the most acute in the world, and all humanitarian actors predict that the conflict will continue, generate new displacement, and steadily constrict access. The impact of the crisis on the education system has been severe, leaving generations of children without learning opportunities. 1.9 million (1.3M Borno; 430K Adamawa; 167K Yobe) conflict-affected boys, girls, and education actors are without access to basic quality education . According to the displacement tracking matrix 2021, 56% per cent of displaced children across the BAY states do not attend school. More broadly, one in three children ages 6 to 11 in the north-east are out of school , compared to one in four nationally and in the north-central, north-west, and south-south zones and one in five in the south-east and south-west. In addition to large numbers of out-of-school children, the education system struggles with low completion and transition rates. In the early grades, primary one to three, only 40 per cent of children are participating in education. This number substantially reduces to 4 to 17 per cent at the secondary level, the average secondary completion rate for Nigeria is 44 per cent but is much lower at 18 to 25 per cent in the BAY states . Moreover, the crisis disproportionately impacts the most vulnerable groups, namely girls and children with disabilities, leaving them even further excluded from quality education. For example, the gender parity index for public primary enrolment in these states ranges from 0.83 in Yobe to 0.91 in Adamawa and 0.93 in Borno. By gender, the out-of-school rate for girls in Adamawa is 29 per cent compared to 19 per cent for boys. In Borno and Yobe, however, the rate for boys is higher: 45 per cent for boys compared to 16 per cent for girls in Borno and 53 per cent for boys compared to 27 per cent for girls in Yobe .The lack of foundational literacy and numeracy (FLN) among school-aged children is both a consequence of low participation and transition rates and a factor that further drives drop out in the north-east region. Over 70 per cent of all children who have completed primary school in the north-east region cannot read a simple sentence (compared to 56 per cent nationally), and 29 per cent cannot add numbers (Nigeria Education Data Survey 2015). These challenges in education access, participation and learning outcomes are driven by a complex interplay of both demand- and supply-side factors. Poverty, for example, remains the greatest barrier to accessing education, with parents being unable to afford education fees and levies. Social and religious norms impede education access, especially for girls and particularly at the secondary level, placing them at risk of child marriage and dropout. Formal education continues to be perceived as a “Western” system in the BAY States and is thus perceived negatively, and nonsecular, Islamic and Qur’anic education is often preferred, especially in rural communities. Although the government, supported by development partners, has introduced Integrated Qur’anic Education (IQE), the number of IQE schools remains insufficient to address current needs, especially in hard-to-reach communities. Further, amongst communities receptive to formal education, negative perceptions of the quality of education provided in schools weighed against the opportunity cost of engaging children in other activities (such as income generation).

2 - Activities

In 2023, UNICEF strategy is focused on the following areas of intervention through which access to inclusive, quality education within a more resilient education system can be rapidly improved: • Expand alternate pathways to learning through a flexible, adaptive, and culturally sensitive remote learning modality delivered through radio. This modality will both ensure continuity of learning in high-conflict settings, where schooling is frequently disrupted, and enhance delivery of literacy and numeracy instruction in centers of non-formal traditional instruction.• Strengthen quality of literacy and numeracy teaching and learning in centers of non-formal traditional instruction through expanded provision of teacher training and support.• Improve collection and analysis of empirical data for better education planning and implementation through innovative data collection system that use technology-based solutions such as EduTrac, a mobile phone-based data collection system.• Programme integration through contextualized interventions such as provision of psychosocial health and well-being, WASH in school including menstrual hygiene, life skills and convergence with social policy through cash assistance. UNICEF will embark on the following specific activities: ACCESS: 1. Construct of 120 temporary learning spaces, inclusive of latrines; 2. rehabilitation of 95 schools; 3. Construct and rehabilitate 145 blocks of gender-sensitive WASH facilities/latrines with adequate connection to water sources; 4. Distribute of learning material to 388,000 children attending formal and non-formal schools; 5. Provision of instructional materials to 190 formal and non-formal schools; 6. Provide cash transfers support to 22,500 children to remove financial barriers to education, facilitate mainstreaming to formal education, improve retention and transition; 7. Provide 29,167 girls with MHM kits and skills to increase attendance and retention; 8. Provide school grants to 284 formal and non-formal schools to implement school/center improvement plan; 9. Establish/Support 184 School Based Management Committees (SBMC) and build capacity on effective governance and management of the learning centers and schools including conducting enrolment drives, developing and implementing school/center improvement plans and implementing safe school mechanism; 10. Conduct WASH-in-school activity through the training to 60 officials; 4750 teachers and the establishment of 180 clubs including environmental health clubs; 11. Provision of life skills to 23,967 children; 12. Support enrollment drive campaign in 22 LGAs; 13. Increase capacity of 2826 teachers’ (non-formal and formal schools) on psychosocial support; 14. Train communities, children, and adolescent groups from 159 communities/schools on how to identify and report child protection risks in and around educational facilities and take action to mitigate them; 15. Support the operationalization of the safe school minimum package in 210 schools.QUALITY: 1. Provision of ALP to 39,000 children; 2. Provision of remote learning opportunities to 71,220 children;3. Provision of inclusive education to 15,000 children enrolled in Tsangaya and Islamiya centers, integrating literacy and numeracy instruction; 4. Provision of foundational literacy and numeracy through TaRL to 96770 children; 5. Provision of TVET to 11,000 youth; 6. Provision of training to 3,979 teachers on TaRL; 7. Support the upgrading of 12,350 teachers to acquire the qualifications to teach in primary grades; 8. Conduct formative and summative assessments to monitor students’ learning.SYSTEM STREGHTENING: 1. Strengthen system capacity on data collection for reflective and responsive educational planning (EduTrac rolled out in 410 schools to complement work on strengthening EMIS); Support state and local authorities to improve teaching practices and teacher workforce management (implementation of Teachers’ Management Information System at Sate level)

8.6 - Please describe briefly the modalities your project will put in place to ensure protection mainstreaming (particularly referring to the above questions).

UNICEF considers protection of all persons affected and at risk when planning humanitarian response and protection is central in UNICEF’s preparedness and life-saving activities throughout the duration of project. The population at risk would be identified considering the specific vulnerabilities that underlie these risks, including those experienced by men, women, girls and boys, and groups such as internally displaced persons, older persons, persons with disabilities, and persons belonging to sexual and other minorities. Primary consideration will be given to UNICEF’s accountability to affected populations, to identify, understand and support their own protection measures.

9.5 - How will your project ensure feedback is collected from the most vulnerable community members?

The proposed action is defined by daily interaction and collaboration with affected communities through our third-party facilitators in targeted LGAs and IDP camps, including support to community-based structures such as the SBMC’s and their participation in public awareness-raising, referral processes and collaboration in identifying and implementing initiatives which contribute to improving the protective environments. Furthermore, UNICEF ensures independent consultations with specific groups, including women, girls and children, to ensure their voices are heard and ideas are incorporated into all activities. As part of the emergency response activities, beneficiaries will be consulted through regular monitoring visits and post-distribution monitoring exercises, during which individuals will be consulted on matters including the quality and relevance of assistance received.
