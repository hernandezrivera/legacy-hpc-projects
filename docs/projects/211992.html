Project Summary

After 13 years of conflict, Syria remains one of the most complex humanitarian emergencies, with an estimated 14.9 million people in need of life-saving primary and secondary care health assistance. In October 2023, heightened hostilities displaced 120,000 persons, impacting 40 health facilities (HFs), 27 schools, and 20 water systems in northern Syria including Deir-ez-Zor (DeZ) Governorate. In addition to continued hostilities with sporadic active conflict and military operations and a persistent economic crisis, lack of functioning HFs presents a considerable challenge to meeting the increasing needs, including for recurrent disease outbreaks, such as cholera. Of the 279-public health centers in Northeast Syria (NES), only 26 are fully functioning (HeRAMs), of which RI supported 14 HFs (12 in Dez and 2 in Hasakeh) in 2023.

The Health Information System (HIS) data collected from Relief International (RI)-supported HFs in NES in 2023 shows acute respiratory infections, diarrheal diseases and water-borne diseases (leishmaniasis and typhoid fever) being one of the leading cause of morbidities, which trend can be attributed to the decline in living standards, poor water supply and sanitation conditions, and lack of preventive health services.
Leveraging 10 years of sustained service provision in Syria and in line with the HRP 2024’s strategic objectives of saving lives, alleviating suffering and increasing access to services, RI proposes a comprehensive health, institutional WASH, nutrition, and protection intervention in NES with health as an entry point. This intervention targets areas with severe needs and ensures equitable access for highly vulnerable and special needs groups, preserving their dignity in receiving the quality care they require. RI will support provision of primary and secondary healthcare services through a multidisciplinary approach that integrates crucial health, nutrition and protection activities at the community level. The health intervention encompasses preventive and curative services including clinical management of communicable diseases (CDs) and non-communicable diseases (NCDs), vaccination services, laboratory and radiological diagnostic services, trauma management, mental health services, and Sexual and Reproductive Health (SRH) services with comprehensive obstetric care and inpatient care and including clinical care for GBV survivors. A network of community health workers (CHWs) will play a key role in delivering awareness-raising and nutrition assessments, and facilitating referrals as needed.
Protection Risk Analysis:
Security situation in target locations deteriorates may threaten staff and service-users, impeding access to areas of operations. As a mitigation measure, RI will adapt the implementation modality from static HFs to Mobile Medical Units (MMUs) if necessary, change the location of implementation or suspend HFs as appropriate, continue upholding neutrality and impartiality to ensure vital services reach communities in need, and maintain an up-to-date security communication tree and staff notification system.
Safeguarding incidents (SEA and GBV) could increase due to a rise in negative coping mechanisms as living conditions deteriorate. As a mitigation measure, RI will raise awareness on PSEA and other types of exploitation and abuse by mainstreaming protection across health program activities and ensure that service-users/staff are aware of their rights, what constitutes exploitation and abuse, and how to report safeguarding incidents and violation of Code of Conduct provisions. Victims also may not disclose SEA incidents due to mistrust in the system and fear of stigmatization. To mitigate this risk, RI will continue strengthening relations with communities, ensure that compliant and feedback mechanisms are accessible and confidential for everyone, and enforce robust accountability mechanisms, including disciplinary procedures for individuals found guilty of SEA.

0 - Project Summary (extended)

In Syria, most functioning HFs’ operations remain dependent on humanitarian aid, therefore the substantial decrease in funding as of 2024 poses a significant challenge to the uninterrupted provision of critical and lifesaving services. In the absence of sustainable support from the local health administration authorities, RI’s health assessment highlighted the need for ongoing support to ensure the continuity of reproductive, maternal, newborn and child health services, especially in humanitarian settings. In addition to the activities described under the Project Summary section, RI will continue using evidence-based information utilizing protection monitoring in implementing health and protection activities, to inform advocacy efforts. In accordance with its core strategy, RI will continue to integrate nutrition services, encompassing nutrition screening at both facility and community levels, IYCF counseling and promotion, and outpatient management of acute malnutrition for CU5 and PLW. Regarding RI’s implementation strategy, the Syria Management Team (SMT) provides technical support to RI’s overall Syria response, led by the Country Director. The implementation team will be led by the Area Director and Health Program Manager based in Syria, while the Amman-based SMT will supervise and support the team through oversight management, monitoring, and guidance with a focus on the quality of services. The program teams will manage the implementation under the technical leadership of the Health Program Manager, supported by the Medical Coordinator. MEAL Field assistants and Program Officers in Syria will remain responsible for the day-to-day monitoring of activities and providing supportive supervision to HF staff. Protection Risk Analysis (Extended) Loss of community support, especially for sensitive services, such as SRH is a risk that RI will mitigate by strengthening partnerships with local authorities/communities, providing capacity building and technical supervision to promote acceptance, enhance understanding of activities and reduce the risk of stigmatization, and conducting regular feedback mechanisms and information sessions to ensure active community participation and program adaptability in response to evolving community needs. Furthermore, an uncontrolled CD or pandemic outbreak could overload health systems and impose mobility restrictions. To mitigate this risk, RI has clear protocols and standard operating procedures (SOPs) for resource allocation and service delivery during a health crisis, continue strengthening the capacity of health systems through training for healthcare professionals, and ensure the availability of necessary medical supplies.Lastly, failing to properly screen staff increases the risk of individuals with harmful intentions gaining access to vulnerable service-users. As a mitigation measure, RI conducts thorough background and reference checks.
