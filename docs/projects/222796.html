Project Summary

The humanitarian situation in South Sudan remains dire, characterized by a protracted humanitarian crisis, widespread displacement, food insecurity, and limited access to essential services, including healthcare (United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs [OCHA, 2023). The country has experienced multiple emergencies, including disease outbreaks, floods, and conflict-related displacement. In 2024, an estimated 9 million people need humanitarian assistance. Of these, 6.3 million need health services, with the health cluster targeting 3.2 million. South Sudan is facing multiple and concurrent disease outbreaks, including measles, yellow fever, poliovirus, meningitis, hepatitis E and cholera. The ongoing conflict in Sudan and reported disease outbreaks such as cholera put the country at risk of imported cases. The most recent WHO/UNICEF estimates of measles and Penta 3 coverage are 72 per cent and 73 per cent, respectively, below the standard threshold. Due to this low coverage, multiple measles outbreaks have been nationwide since 2022. Malaria remains endemic and the leading cause of morbidity and mortality, often exacerbated by frequent flooding events
In 2023, South Sudan faced outbreaks of measles, cholera, and other infectious diseases, worsened by the impacts of climate change, particularly flooding (World Health Organization [WHO], 2023). Key health indicators continue to fall short of global targets, with a maternal mortality ratio of 295 per 100,000 live births in 2017 (WHO, 2024) and an under-5 mortality rate ranging from 30 to 112 per 1,000 live births across different states (WHO, 2024).
Findings from the 2024 ISNA underscore the need for improved access to health facilities in South Sudan. Only 57 per cent of the population can reach the nearest health facility in less than an hour, while 44 per cent face long distances as a barrier to healthcare. Women and girls in remote and crisis-affected areas face additional challenges due to limited trained personnel and recurrent supply chain breaks affecting family planning commodities and essential drugs for clinical delivery assistance and treatment of sexually transmitted infections. Thirty-eight per cent indicated that their children were not vaccinated due to lack of a nearby health facility. While the Health Sector Transformation Programme (HSTP) currently supports 50% of functional health facilities, 18% are non-functional. The July 2024 GBV health analysis revealed that only 28 per cent of health facilities provide GBV care, with 43 per cent offering services. Lower-level facilities struggle with staffing and infrastructure, limiting early intervention to referrals.
IOM provided life-saving health support to vulnerable IDPs and host communities in Abyei, Wau, Rubkhona and Malakal counties and through the deployment of 9 Rapid Response Missions to crisis-affected locations of Abyei, Twic, Ezo, Nagero, Raja, Renk, Yei, Morobo, Kajokeji. IOM specifically responded to the Sudan crisis by reinforcing its existing operations at the points of entry in Wau (Wau Airport), Juba (Juba International Airport), Rubkona (Panakuach PoE and Roriak PHCU), Renk( Wunthou PoE, Wunthou PHCU and Abayok PHCU) and in Malakal (Bulkat and Malakal Airport) to support South Sudanese returneses with emergency medical care, pre-departure medical screening and to provide referral services to available health facilities for patients requiring secondary level medical care. Additionally, IOM specifically stepped up vaccination at PoEs in response to ongoing measles outbreaks in Sudan and continued to support health cluster efforts to prevent, detect, and respond to various public health threats and emergencies at the respective PoEs.
IOM’s HRP health project for 2024 is a significant undertaking that aligns with the three health cluster strategic objectives. It aims to improve equitable access to essential life-saving healthcare services, reduce excess morbidity and mortality

1. Needs

The current health status of the population is poor and characterized by high malaria morbidity and mortality, with 50.2% of cases and 39.4% of deaths attributed to malaria; low measles vaccination coverage, with only 66% of children vaccinated and measles outbreaks reported since December 2022; cholera outbreak in Malakal; Hepatitis E in Bentiu IDP (Internally Displaced Persons) sites, Western Bahr El Ghazal and Jonglei states (Draft 2024 Health cluster HNO). Access to primary health care (PHC) services continued to be unavailable for a large majority of South Sudanese, with only 56 percent of the population able to access health facilities in an hour or less, while Only 12% of health facilities in South Sudan are providing the complete package of BPHNS, whilst 53% are moderately functional, with many poorly equipped and staffed, with humanitarian and development partners responsible for the provision of support for a significant proportion of healthcare services in the country.In 2023, South Sudan faced outbreaks of measles, cholera, and other infectious diseases exacerbated by climate change impacts, such as flooding (World Health Organization [WHO], 2023). Key health indicators lag global targets, with a maternal mortality ratio of 295 per 100,000 live births in 2017 (WHO, 2024) and an under-5 mortality rate ranging from 30 to 112 per 1,000 live births across different states (WHO, 2024). The leading causes of morbidity remain communicable diseases; malaria is the top cause of morbidity (64%) and mortality (45%) among outpatients, followed by pneumonia and diarrhoea.20 Several Counties report malaria cases above the threshold perennially, especially during the rainy seasons, affecting mainly children under five years. The last malaria indicator survey (2023) estimated malaria prevalence to be 32%, 34%, and 18% among children under five, protection of civilian sites, and internally displaced persons, respectively.Non-communicable diseases are on the rise, particularly hypertension and diabetes, which account for 44% and 24% of the five tracer indicators seen in outpatient departments (OPDs) across the country. South Sudan is also facing acute food insecurity; the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) projected that over half of South Sudan’s population experienced high levels of acute food insecurity (IPC phase 3 or worse) according to the April to July 2024 projection. Most health facilities (47%) are operating at a moderate functional level. However, many facilities are either non-functional (18%) or have limited functionality (17%). Only 12% of the facilities are classified as highly functional, highlighting a substantial area for improvement in health service delivery. Additionally, 6% of the facilities lack sufficient data to assess their functionality, indicating potential gaps in reporting or data collection processes. (Health Cluster, 2024). Assessments conducted by IOM in Bentiu, Wau, and Malakal from 2015 to 2021 indicate persistent experiences of cumulative distress, grieving of multiple losses, accumulative stress, loss of self-esteem, fatigue and persisting trauma.

2. Project activities

IOM provided life-saving health and MHPSS support to vulnerable IDPs and host communities in Abyei, Wau, Rubkhona and Malakal counties and through the deployment of 9 Rapid Response Missions to crisis-affected locations of Abyei, Twic, Ezo, Nagero, Raja, Renk, Yei, Morobo, Kajokeji. IOM specifically responded to the Sudan crisis by reinforcing its existing operations at the points of entry in Wau (Wau Airport), Juba (Juba International Airport), Rubkona (Panakuach PoE and Roriak PHCU), Renk( Wunthou PoE, Wunthou PHCU and Abayok PHCU) and in Malakal (Bulkat and Malakal Airport) to support South Sudanese returneses with emergency medical care, pre-departure medical screening and to provide referral services to available health facilities for patients requiring secondary level medical care. Additionally, IOM specifically stepped up vaccination at PoEs in response to ongoing measles outbreaks in Sudan and continued to support health cluster efforts to prevent, detect, and respond to various public health threats and emergencies at the respective PoEs. IOM’s HRP health project for 2024 is a significant undertaking that aligns with the three health cluster strategic objectives. It aims to improve equitable access to essential life-saving healthcare services, reduce excess morbidity and mortality by timely detection and coordinated response to epidemic-prone diseases, enhance resilience, and promote humanitarian-development linkages to strengthen health system recovery and coping mechanisms. The project targets a total of 865,237 beneficiaries, including 129,786 persons with disabilities, to provide integrated emergency life-saving primary health care, including outpatient consultations, routine immunization, nutrition screening for children under five years, sexual reproductive health services, clinical case management of sGBV survivors including psychosocial support. The project also focuses on increasing surveillance and response to disease outbreaks among underserved and conflict-affected IDPs, returnees, migrants/travellers, and host communities.IOM will provide comprehensive health services through 17 static, seven mobile clinics, and 12 additional health facilities. This will be achieved through 6 Rapid Response mission deployments, ensuring it maintains its comparative advantage in rapidly deploying rapid response teams to address emerging health needs nationwide. IOM will focus on leveraging existing community systems for sustainable solutions and strengthening community-based health initiatives and mental health and psychosocial support. Therefore, IOM will provide direct service delivery in partnership with the respective county health departments while continuing to explore options for partnerships with local NGOs and CBOs to realize the localization agenda of the health cluster
