Project Summary

Protection needs will continue as a result of men, woman, girls and boys exposure, vulnerability, and inability to cope with conflict and violence, human rights violations and abuses, contamination from explosive hazards, breakdown of rule of law, and major challenges related to impediments to accessing critical services. DRC will therefore promote the self-protection and resiliency of vulnerable conflict-affected groups to cope with displacement in Tripolitania. DRC protection activities include protection monitoring/needs assessements, information dissemination and awareness raising, protection case management which includes individual protection assistance and structured and non-structured psychosocial support for individuals and groups of IDPs, refugees and mixed migrants. During this period, DRC will prioritize and strengthen protection monitoring and information management and sharing. Using a protection mainstreaming approach, serious mental health cases and vulnerabilities related to disabilities will be referred for specialized assistance within the humanitarian referral pathways. DRC will continue to support to protection coordination in Libya by co-leading the Protection Sector and will enhance its own internal emergency response and contingency planning capacities to ensure that the response remains adaptable and responsive to the unpredictable Libyan context. DRC also aims at improving the protection sector’s response and coordination by providing training on the protection information management system (PIM) to NGOs and UN agencies. In addition to co-coordination, DRC participates in the sector working group, is a member of the protection SAG, chair of the INGO forum and is a member of the global protection cluster.

1 - Needs

The needs addressed in this project are identiﬁed by ongoing protection monitoring in Tripolitania and Al Jabal al Gharbi through needs assessments and DRC's presence in the target locations, and through secondary data. According to the 2020 HNO, 1.8 million people are affected by the conflict, with around 897,000 people in need of humanitarian assistance due to instability, conflict and insecurity, the absence of rule of law, disrespect of IHL by armed actors, a deteriorating public sector and a dysfunctional economy. The reduced number of people identified as in need of humanitarian assistance in 2019, compared to 2018, should not be interpreted as an improvement in the humanitarian situation in Libya. Indeed, the decrease is rather a direct result of a more refined data collection process. Assessments confirm the prevalence of the following IHL violations in Libya: indiscriminate attacks in populated areas, ill-treatment, rape, torture and extortion in detention, attacks on civilian properties and the civilian population, use of human shields, recruitment of children below the age of 18 into military activities. A major violation that is widely reported is the grave conditions of detention of migrants and refugees in Libya. The HNO notes over 301,000 Libyans remain displaced, including 128,000 people due to the Tripoli conflict, reversing the declining trend in displacement. According to IOM reports, 97%, of these IDPs are displaced due to the deterioration of the security situation, causing a significant increase of displacement in the west region. The number of IDPs forced to leave their homes due to armed conflict in Tripoli since April 2019 now constitutes 40% of the total displacement in Libya. A small minority of IDPs(around 4,000) are hosted in newly established collective shelters including unfinished buildings, schools, dorms, etc. Water, electricity cuts and long fuel queues is now a daily occurrence in Tripoli. The armed conflict has also more broadly affected markets, negatively impacting the food security situation and the provision of services. According to the DTM rapid assessment, markets in 26% of assessed locations were closed, 53% of the respondents reported the lack of financial services was a problem and public services such as education and waste removal were the highest affected by the ongoing clashes.In addition to these new displacement dynamics, the latest conflict further exacerbates the vulnerability of populations already exposed to significant protection risks especially in Western Libya. Many IDPs displaced since 2011, now face secondary or tertiary displacement, thus compounding the erosion of coping mechanisms and reduction of personal resources after years of protracted displacement. Adults report the psychosocial impact of insecurity and displacement on themselves as well as on children. Moreover, they have fewer options to flee to the safety of families and friends. They are being forced to return prematurely to their regions of origin such as the remote mountain areas of Al Jabal al Gharbi where basic services, homes and infrastructure have not yet been restored. Mountain communities, such as Alawiniya, have experienced a spike in spontaneous returns with families arriving from Tripoli to live in their burnt-out homes. In addition, DRC remains extremely concerned about the safety and wellbeing of 85,891 refugees and migrants (87% male; 13% female) in Tripoli, of which an estimated 4,754 are trapped in detention centres. Many, especially those from sub-Saharan Africa, remain highly vulnerable and in need of international protection due to their undetermined legal status, precarious living conditions and exposure to the conflict. They are subject to rights violations including torture, extortion, and sexual violence, with limited access to protection services, . Many are released from detention centres or returned to Libya by coast guard patrols after attempting to reach Europe.

2 - Activities

1. Protection assessments are carried out, including rapid needs assessments, to identify emerging needs and inform a coordinated responses. Assessments are widely shared for partners' information and use. 2, Protection monitoring is carried out to collect consistent, reliable ongoing data on the needs of beneﬁciaries, both IDP and refugees/migrants, that is used to analyse protection risks, identify trends, and inform programmatic and sector response. 3 Information is disseminated on rights and services to assist conﬂict-affected individuals to access services and become more aware of rights and services available to them, to assist in better-informed life decisions. 4. Individual protection assistance is provided to particularly vulnerable IDPs, refugees or migrants to respond to and mitigate immediate protection risks, including elderly, female-heads of households, people with disabilities, and other individuals facing protection risks. Referrals are made for specialized services like gender-based violence, child protection, health, or serious mental health sectors. 5. Psychosocial support is delivered in structured and non-structured activities to support the well being of conﬂict affected individuals, families and groups, particularly those affected by trauma and post-conﬂict 6. Protection information management (PIM) training will be organized and delivered to staff from protection NGOs and UN agencies in order to improve information sharing and inform a more effective needs-based sector response

4 - Protection risk analysis

It is recognized that DRC is working in a high-risk environment and as such as a risk management framework is in place that is regularly reviewed and updated. The Country Director is responsible for balancing risks and urgency of the activities and will avoid putting staff, operations or assets under unnecessary risks and will determine the acceptable risk that can be taken in any given context. The beneﬁt from work that can be achieved must always outweigh the risks involved in doing it. Should this not be the case, the options of suspension or closure of programs, withdrawal or evacuation are considered. DRC will continue to support to protection coordination in Libya and will enhance its own internal emergency response and contingency planning capacities to ensure that it design remains adaptable and responsive to the unpredictable Libyan context. 1. Security risk: Escalation of conﬂict in project areas limits access. This also affects community (including beneﬁciary and stakeholder) desire/ability to participate in program activities 2. Displaced populations may be inaccessible and service providers are unavailable to provide assistance or local authorities/community members may not provide permission or may restrict agencies from accessing target areas 3. Programmatic risk: Refugee and migrant community mobilizer safety at risk due to them being migrants. 4. Access to migrants/refugees is reduced if there is more abuse by authorities or community

5 - Mitigation measures

1. DRC has an established security management plans and standard operating procedures including contingency plan for hibernation/evacuation. All international staff are required to attend specialized security training. In the event of a major incident, the Incident Management Team will be activated to address urgent situations. Safety teams are in regular bi-lateral contact with INGO’s, whilst the CD raises major issues of concerns and discusses key security trends/issues during the INGO coordination meetings. Plans are updated regularly with the involvement of security focal teams and security advisors. Security assessments are regularly undertaken to manage risk and mitigate potential threats. In case of direct threats to staff, activities would immediately be suspended. 2. As a key mitigation measure, DRC carries out stakeholder analyses and develops an engagement plan to clarify the extent of its activities and seeks permission before operating. Working in a collaborative manner with others limits risk and staff do not pose unnecessary risks to operations. 3. Outreach mobilizers as well as other refugees, migrants and asylum seekers, face signiﬁcant restrictions in movements due to the risk of arbitrary arrests by the police and armed groups’ activity. 3. The DRC prioritizes the safety and security of mobilizers, vetting and tracking their movements on a daily basis and providing up-to-date security information. 4 Protection teams will monitor the protection environment for migrants and refugees, and provide ongoing advice on shifts in the environment and ways to access populations and mitigate increased protection threats. Provision of certain services may be reduced if there is increased risk to migrants or to staff. DRC has established expertise on Libya. Considerable emphasis is placed on comprehensive and regular context analysis and consultation with Libyan networks to ensure that activities and interventions are conflict-sensitive.

6.1 - Other accountability mechanisms

DRC has accountability mechanisms to ensure beneficiaries are able to provide feedback (both positive and negative) on the implementation of the action. DRC will adhere to CHS Standards and has already in place a specific Complaints/Feedback Response system. The information collected will be used to adapt appropriate approaches and to improve the level of safe programming. Through comprehensive feedback loops in the programme design, DRC will ensure adaptive programming, which is responsive to the dynamic context and complex needs of IDPs, returnees and complex needs of IDPs, returnees and other vulnerable groups. DRC's Monitoring, Evaluation, Accountability, and Learning (MEAL) unit carries out a series of monitoring activities during project implementation. The team conducts regular assessments and interviews, as appropriate, to ascertain a detailed understanding of the impact of the interventions, including identifying areas of improvement during the project period and future interventions
