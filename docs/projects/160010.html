Project Summary

The scope and range of protection issues affecting children in Somalia is ever widening and deepening due to the prolonged nature of the conflict. Children are exposed to persistent and extreme violence which includes recruitment and use by parties to the conflict. Population displacement due to chronic conflict, droughts and famines has resulted in the separation of children from their families, which exposes them to exploitation, violence and abuse children of IDPs and minorities are particularly vulnerable. Boys and girls continue to endure multiple protection risks and violations of their rights including physical abuse, psychosocial distress, family separation, sexual and gender-based violence (GBV). The proliferation of small arms and light weapons, a steady rise in the use of improvised explosive devices (IEDs) and widespread contamination of explosive remnants of war (ERW) are the results of the prolonged armed conflict in Somalia.
UNCEF will respond to the above needs in the following ways:
• Provision of psychosocial support services through open spaces reaching 100,000 (45,000 boys & 45,000 girls) children and 10,000 (5000 men and 5000 women) caregivers
• Provide community-based reintegration for 2200 (1700 boys & 500 girls) child formerly associated with armed forces/groups another vulnerable children
• Trace and reunify 8000 unaccompanied and separated children with their families (Boys: 5000 & Girls: 3,000)
• Raise awareness on the threats posed by unexploded remnants of war reaching 37,000 people (14,500 boys; 14,500 girls, 4,000 women & 4,000 men)
• Raise awareness on main CP concerns on child labour, child marriage and prevention of family separation to 200,000 people (80,000 boys, 80,000 girls, 20,000 men, women 20,000)
• Train 1000 duty bearers and CSO on child protection and child rights (600 men & 400 women)

Project Activities

Needs;Persistent violations of children’s rights continue to be a critical concern in Somalia as children and adolescents are often targets of killing and maiming, abduction, recruitment into armed forces or armed groups, detention, and sexual and physical violence. Armed groups attack schools or hospitals and deny children access to humanitarian assistance. From January to September 2019, the Country Task Force on Monitoring and Reporting (CTFMR) verified and documented. 2,226 cases (1,887 boys and 339 girls) of violence against children, with abduction and recruitment/use of children accounting for 38% and 53 % of the overall number of children affected. As of 1 October 2019, the CTFMR figures, indicate that a total of 1,170 children including 14 girls were recruited by armed forces and groups in various areas compared to 1,811 (46 girls) in the same period in 2018. In general, the most common method of recruitment of children between 7 and 17 is persuasion by local leaders, relatives or parents to join armed forces or groups (14%), forceful removal from their homes or schools by armed groups (82%) of verified cases and unknown (4%). As per the 2019 Child Protection Assessment in Somalia report, the second most prominently cited child protection concern was the issue of children who have been separated from their usual caregivers. 42.0% of the respondents cited that they are aware of boys and girls who have been separated from their usual caregivers. It was more common for respondents in IDP camps (48%) and urban areas (44%) to cite separated children as a concern, compared to rural locations (7%). Children in Somalia have grown up under the stress of conflict, displacement, separation and fear, compounded by a lack of access to basic services and food insecurity. According to the 2019 Child Protection Assessment in Somalia report, 25.6% of the respondents had noticed negative changes in the children’s behaviour in the last 3 months. Mitigation Measures

1a.- Consider safety risks associated with landmines, explosive remnants of war (ERW), busy roads,exposed wires and dangerous materials on the ground.- Identify safe residential locations and avoid placing facilities near military installations and conflictfrontlines.- Situate play areas in clearly visible and safe locations- Ask children (especially adolescent girls) and women about the safety of their travel to and presenceat safe spaces.- Provide lighting along routes or distribute personal flashlights to minimise night-time risks. 2a.- Ensure that assistance and services are reaching the most vulnerable - Identify and prioritise the mostvulnerable groups in the community and prevent discrimination or exclusion of marginalised groups- Collect and use data disaggregated by age, gender and diversity to effectively inform programming.- Identify groups who face difficulties accessing services (e.g. women, girls, persons with disabilities,older persons, persons from minority groups, persons belonging to marginalized groups) and workwith community leaders, religious leaders and service providers to support equal and safe access toservices without discrimination.- Train animators in CFS on how to work with children with physical disabilities- Train staff to work with individuals with intellectual disabilities, including how to ensure properconfidentiality and informed consent. 2b.- Consult women, men, boys, girls, youth, persons with disabilities, older persons and marginalisedgroups to understand their needs, coping strategies and preferences for protection services.- Engage the community and committee representative to play an active role in identifying solutionsand in the decision-making processes that affect them, so as to promote a sense of ownership, buildtheir self-esteem and improve the relevance and sustainability of the response- Arrange for female staff to talk to girls and women directly and separately from men and boys aboutthe barriers they face, privacy norms in shared shelters, latrines, wash areas and hold discussionswith groups who face added difficulties- Ensure meaningful representation of all groups within the community (e.g. gender, ethnicity,socioeconomic group, persons with disabilities).
