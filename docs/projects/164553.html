In 2020, Big Heart Foundation will focus on the continuation and extension of our existing education programs in Northern Syria. At present, this includes an EU-funded project in partnership with People in Need (PIN) to support four schools through 31 May, as well as an OCHA-funded project to support another ten schools through 14 June. Our aim is to ensure that these schools remain open following the end of the current funding periods, so to offer informal education services in the summer months, as well as access to formal education from September onward. If successful, these programs will reach an estimated 5,600 children (ages 6-17, 50% female), 280 educators (75% female) and 700 caretakers (60% female) across Afrin, Atareb, Dana and Jisr al-Shugur.
Structured around our holistic model for education during times of crisis and recovery, the proposed programs align with the sector’s overall strategic objective 1 on Access and objective 2 on Quality, and includes a range of academic and protection-related activities. For the formal school year, academic instruction follows recognised curriculums in Arabic, math, English and sciences. In the summer months, out-of-school and educationally at-risk students receive support with basic numeracy and literacy to re-start, or continue, their education. All program instructors are trained in child-centred pedagogy, and benefit from provision of teacher stipends, educational supply and school operational costs. In both formal and non-formal settings, academic teaching and learning is coupled with activities focused on student safety and wellbeing. School personnel receive training in child protection and psychosocial care, and all students participate in weekly, age-appropriate PSS sessions led by dedicated facilitators. These activities aim to promote positive social-emotional development among children, and include a youth Life Skills curriculum for older students. Further, each school has a Protection Facilitator who is responsible for monitoring protection mainstreaming measures, such as a case management and referral system for students requiring external support. School personnel are also trained on emergency preparedness and response, and develop plans to deal with threats like attacks on or near schools. When necessary, school sites are also rehabilitated to meet minimum standards for safe and protective learning environments.
In recognising the rapidly deteriorating security situation in our target areas, the proposed program also includes contingency plans should access to our schools be lost, such as Self-Learning activities for displaced and informally-settled children.

4 - Needs

The war in Syria has devastated educational access and opportunity across the country. More than 2.45 mil children are out-of-school, 1/3 of all school buildings have been damaged, destroyed or co-opted, and 120,000 qualified teachers have been lost due to injury, insecurity, or death. For children who have managed to continue their education, 5.8 mil students still require educational assistance, ranging from basic school supplies to highly targeted academic, psychosocial and protection- related support. If these and other needs go unmet, there is significant concern that additional students will be forced out of the classroom, including the estimated 1.6 mill children currently believed to be at risk of dropping out (HRP 2019, 2020). Such needs are especially acute in NWS, where the recent intensification of hostilities has forcibly displaced some 200,000 people, shuttering educational access across districts hit hardest by fighting (UN OCHA 2020).In a continuation of trends from the previous year, our research confirms that key barriers to education are: the dearth of safe, useable school facilities, especially during winter months; the challenge of training and compensating teachers, resulting in a high turnover of unqualified and/or volunteer instructors; and unmet school supply needs, which undermines the quality of instruction and exacerbates the pervasive challenge of managing large class sizes and multi-level cohorts. For children and their families, these limitations are compounded by threats to students’ physical safety and psychosocial wellbeing. Many female adolescents and youth also miss out on education as a result of family pressures to assume domestic and caretaking responsibilities, while young males often abandon schooling to earn money and contribute to HH livelihoods. Ongoing exposure to violence and exploitation has also resulted in high levels of “toxic stress” with psychological trauma now common among young Syrians (Save the Children 2017).

5 - Implementation of activities

The proposed program will support 14 schools through a range of educational, psychosocial & protection activities. To begin, schools will undergo light rehab works. Concurrently, all teachers will be trained on pedagogy for student‐centred and mixed‐level classrooms. During the summer period, academic instruction will focus on basic numeracy and literacy skills for children who are out‐of‐school or at risk of dropping out. During the formal school year, schools offer a core set of academic classes following recognised curriculums. In both formal & non‐formal settings, academic teaching and learning is coupled with activities focused on student safety and wellbeing. School personnel receive training in child protection and PSS care, and all students participate in weekly, age‐appropriate PSS sessions led by dedicated facilitators. Further, each school has a Protection Facilitator who is responsible for monitoring protection mainstreaming measures. School personnel are also trained on emergency preparedness and response, and develop plans to deal with threats like attacks on or near schools. Our community outreach component also includes back‐to‐school campaigns and family awareness sessions on topics such as PSS, mine action and health and hygiene. Gender will be considered throughout the range of program activities, including in rehabilitation of WASH facilities, school personnel training, and community outreach activities. Further, our dual focus on both primary and secondary students means that our programs support age and level‐appropriate quality instruction, especially for older adolescents and youth. All education activities are monitored, verified and/or assessed through a variety of tools and practices. Our education team in each area includes a dedicated MEAL officer, who oversees key monitoring and reporting activities during weekly site visits. This includes teacher and classroom observations, the ASER exam (academic), and the SDQ tool (PSS).

6 - Protection Risk Analysis

(RISK 1) Attacks on and around schools (HIGH): We will closely monitor the security situation in target areas, and establish emergency response plans, threat notification systems, and sheltering or evacuation procedures for program sites. Sites will be de-conflicted using the OCHA-administered deconflict mechanism, and all attacks will be reported appropriately. School personnel will also be trained in first aid and school safety, and schools will be outfitted with health and safety kits. (RISK 2) Child safety when traveling to and from school (MEDIUM): In instances where there is forewarning, a notification system will be established to inform students and their families about security threats. A protection facilitator at each school will create and maintain a ‘Walking School Bus’ program to ensure students travel to and from school safely. First aid and emergency response trainings will also prepare school personnel to respond to student injury if necessary. A child friendly feedback mechanism will be established to allow for these issues to be raised and discussed with children and teachers. (RISK 3) School-related child protection concerns, such as bullying from peers, corporal punishment from teachers or gender- based harassment or abuse (MEDIUM): Child-protection considerations are mainstreamed into all relevant program activities. Teachers also receive child protection training that covers identifying and managing bullying and harassment, as well as training on non-violent skills for classroom and behaviour management. To further protect students, all program staff sign and agree to uphold child safeguarding standards regarding the appropriate treatment of the children in our care, including Sexual Exploitation and Abuse. (RISK 4) Community tensions resulting from perceived inequalities related to services (MEDIUM): All project activities and procedures will be based on transparent, needs-focused criteria for engaging with local communities and markets.

7 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

CONTEXT: (RISK 1) Changes regarding the security situation in the target areas: We closely monitor the security situation in Northern Syria, and establish emergency response and contingency plans to deal with changes in the stability and access. These plans include relocating program sites if necessary, as well as using alternative modalities of service delivery, such as home schooling interventions. (RISK 2) Border closures and delays in transporting project goods and supplies: If supply lines are disrupted due to border closures, we rely on alternative channels for procurement in local markets, where possible. As a contingency measure, we also divide program supplies among warehouses in Syria. (RISK 3) Safety of field staff is threatened or undermined: Safety trainings and contingency planning, as well as a Duty of Care policy.COORDINATION: Big Heart will ensure that the project is adequately coordinated with other actors at the local and national levels. Locally, this involves engaging with all relevant community groups and civil society actors, such as community leaders, councils and local education authorities. At the national level, Big Heart will continue to play an active role in the education cluster.ACCOUNTABILITY: The opinions and feedback of beneficiaries are collected throughout the program cycle through consultation exercises, as well as through our final evaluation practices. All beneficiaries also have access to a child-friendly complaints mechanism. We also engage the community at each school through Parent-Teacher Associations.FRAUD & DIVERSION: As it relates to the influence or infringement of violent, political or religious groups, all program staff are briefed on and agree to uphold the core humanitarian principles of neutrality, impartiality and independence, as well as the Do No Harm approach.
