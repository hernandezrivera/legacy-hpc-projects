Project Summary

Comprehensive Protection program covering humanitarian response, enabling durable solutions, and environment building, for IDPs (in and out of camps), returnees, and host community. Services will include both individual protection assistance and community based programming, with the objectives of 1) improved access to justice mechanisms and legal rights, 2) effective prevention, response and remedial actions for rights violations, 3) effective humanitarian, coordination and duty bearer responses meet the needs of vulnerable populations.

Needs

While the Government of Iraq (GoI) declared operational victory over Islamic State (IS) in December 2017, protection and conflict concerns across the country remain multi-layered and self-reinforcing. The context of Iraq is moving towards an ’early recovery’ level of stability that can enable durable solutions, whilst simultaneously many parts of the country still require an emergency response modality of protection services. Despite the changing context in Iraq, different groups remain in need of immediate protection assistance. This includes persons at risk of or subject to: arbitrary detention; violence, exploitation, and harm; persecution and neglect on the basis of ideological, familial, sectarian identification; and those facing barriers to (re)integration to local areas or areas of return. Moreover, vulnerable groups remain at risk due to lack of documentation and the ability to navigate the administrative system of legislation in Iraq; the inability to overcome the compounded protection vulnerabilities and the resulting continued need for a structured case management service; and the lack of PSS in places of displacement or return to enable successful reintegration. Finally, portions of the population face substantial risks of collective punishment and individual persecution on the basis of perceived or alleged affiliation with IS. Iraq is a nation-state built upon a strong legal, administrative, and bureaucratic apparatus. This involves multiple forms of identification and certificates, and a complex network of Directorates and authorizations that enable citizens to access essential services and social welfare systems (as yet not fully operational). While courts, civil directorates and government offices are increasingly reopening in retaken areas, the process of reopening is slow, and those that are operational are heavily over-burdened by requests and are faced with a lack of resources. Additionally, there are blockages on accessing justice mechanisms for groups facing collective punishment, including those, but not limited to holding a perceived affiliation with IS. Many IDP or stayee persons and households are lacking civil documentation that was lost during the process of displacement or is regularly confiscated by security actors or armed groups. Many of those displaced are unable to access essential services such as healthcare, education, social welfare from MoLSA, the Public Distribution System (PDS) through the Ministry of Trade, or basic rights such as freedom of movement. Finally, housing, land, and property (HLP) is and will continue to be a major issue across the country. This includes the protection related issues of prevention of eviction, access to tenancy agreements, access to title deeds (Tapu), compensation, and safe secondary accommodation. Rights violations and protection risks are still myriad across the country. Returnees face risks including UXO contamination, secondary displacement due to insufficient or destroyed shelter, persecution or exploitation by armed groups and/or the community, forced recruitment (including children), and lack of services and livelihoods opportunities resulting in negative coping mechanisms. Likewise, following years of displacement and often demographic changes in areas, there is a risk of increased social tension between groups, and a risk of radicalisation or extremism, particularly of young men, through alienation, disenfranchisement, and lack of life opportunities in areas of return. Those in displacement continue to face and experience substantial abuses, exploitation, and harassment. This is particularly the case as returns continue across the country, those remaining in displacement are generally those with higher needs and/or risks, preventing their return. Moreover, protection monitoring findings indicate that women and children in particular suffer a high level of psychosocial stress as a result of living in extremely confined collective spaces, often with unrelated families, while men and boys are at increasing risk for detention and arrest. In some locations, assistance and access to services has been denied to IDP children. As a result, children are frequently withdrawn from school and engaging in forms of child labor, which exposes them to further risks including recruitment into armed groups.As of January 2018, Iraq is no longer delegated as a Level Three Emergency. However there remains a high need for effective coordination mechanisms in the humanitarian response, due to the size of the response and complexity of needs in Iraq. There is also a need for further collaboration and handover of services to duty bearers, most notably local authorities and the wider Government of Iraq (GoI). This is particularly the case as the Social Safety Net continues to be rolled out in 2019. Activities

DRC Protection services will include the following:- Protection monitoring, including HLP assessments- Rapid Protection Assessments through the DRC hosted RPA project- General Protection Case Management- Housing, Land, and Property services – DRC is currently and will continue to implement the following HLP activities: Awareness raising, legal counselling, and training on HLP matters; awareness raising and legal support for the submission of compensation claims; legal support for the application or replacement of ownership documents; development of tenancy agreements and MOUs between landlords and renters, in collaboration with DRC Shelter teams; legal assistance and counselling on matters relating to secondary occupation; emergency cash-for-protection support for those at severe risk of eviction.- Referrals – DRC will carry out 8,800 referrals in 2019. Across the 10 governorates of operation, this is an average of 880 referrals per governorate team. This also includes internal referrals made through DRC’s integrated programming approach which will enable referrals to Shelter (sealing-off kits and durable upgrades support), Livelihoods (MPCA, vocational training, skills training, cash for work), and CCCM (camps based services).- Service mapping on a monthly basis in each location- Legal awareness raising, counselling, assistance and representation for civil documentation, family law, linkages to services and GoI social safety net, and HLP.- Structured group-based PSS – DRC will continue to implement the War Child IDEAL PSS curriculum for children, adolescents, and adults.- Emergency cash payments for protection assistance (Individual Protection Assistance)- Protection awareness raising sessions, including HLP- Community capacity building – this will include the delivery of protection based training to community members, local leaders (mukhtars, teachers, school principals, professional associations), and local civilian authorities. - Community Based Protection Committees - DRC’s work with community-based protection committees may include limited short-term peaceful co-existence elements, with the aim of minimizing inter-communal tensions and contributing to the protection of all community members with a focus on the most vulnerable. These activities will primarily target areas of displacement, and will also target areas of origin as a bridge to longer-term social cohesion interventions by RRP actors.- Community Safe Spaces – DRC will run 23 Community Safe Spaces across Iraq. These are separate from Community Resource Centres. Locations are: Diyala – Baquba, Muqdadiyah; Kerbala – Kerbala Camp; Ninewa – Qayyarah Airstrip, Zummar, Rabiaa, Ayadiah Kisik, Ayadiah Abu Wany, Tel Keif, Bashiqa, Wana; Anbar – Fallujah city, AAF Camp x 3, Bzeibiz camp, HTC Camps x 4; Salah Al Din – Beiji, Tikrit, Al Alam, Balad. Each Community Safe Space costs an approximate $50,000 per year to run, with a total cost of $1.15 million per year. - Mobile services- Recreational activities- Life skills sessions- Cluster coordination support- Advocacy - Protection Mainstreaming

Protection Risk Analysis

Beneficiaries:1) Incorrect identification of beneficiaries as affiliated to extremist groups when presented before court for legal matters. 2) Raising false expectations of service provision, or identification of vulnerabilities without ability to respond, while carrying-out protection monitoring. 3) Accidental provision of harm while attending to child protection or GBV cases in General Protection Case Management. 4) Provision of incorrect legal and HLP guidance, causing inadvertent harm to beneficiaries. 5) Potentially putting persons at risk of theft or exploitation if community members are aware they have received cash for protection support. Staff:6) Staff, particularly lawyers, exposure to harassment from security actors on perceived basis of ‘supporting those affiliated with IS’. 7) Personal security and safety threats to those staff working in hostile environments, particularly while undertaking Rapid Protection Assessments.

Mitigation Measures

1) All lawyers explain the process to legal beneficiaries, that a security clearance may be required as part of the legal support, with beneficiaries to provide informed consent to continue with the service. 2) All Protection Monitors to be well trained on communication approaches, and to clearly state that participation in monitoring does not guarantee humanitarian assistance. 3) All CP and GBV cases will be referred to specialist agencies or to the relevant cluster. 4) Use of NPC and HLP SC awareness raising materials and/or guidance whenever possible. Verification of all legal / HLP awareness raising materials developed by DRC. 5) Case-for-protection cash delivery carried out in quiet and confidential fashion. 6) Good relations with local authorities and actors. Strong and clear internal DRC security incident reporting mechanisms. Clear guidance to DRC lawyers on what to say if impeded by security actors. 7) Safety assessments conducted on all new areas of operation as part of site selection, with regular internal review. Clear safety protocols in place.

How is your project accountable to affected population

Extensive community consultation is conducted in any area that DRC is planning an intervention in. This includes mapping of people’s needs and wishes from protection services. Awareness raising topics, and sessions with legal awareness raising generally follow which topics community members identify as important to them. Similarly life skills topics and skills learning topics are those identified by the community as needed. All DRC centres also contain anonymous feedback boxes, as well as posters and notices for DRC’s code of conduct reporting mechanism (via phone or email). Furthermore, all centres contain information about the IDP call centre, and mobile teams carry information cards on the call centre. Finally, the DRC MEAL team carries out extensive monitoring of our services to identify any issues.
