Project Summary

In 2019, NRC’s Camp Management Team seeks to implement programming to ensure holistic, evidence-based and community-driven support to displacement-affected populations, thereby empowering and positioning them towards durable solutions. NRC will achieve this by increasing access to information, humanitarian assistance and government services for displacement-affected populations in out-of-camp areas of return. Community engagement and community governance will be enhanced, in a move towards durable solutions for displacement-affected populations, and NRC will also maintain access to assistance and protection in formal settlements through direct camp management or camp management support. In 2019, NRC aims to continue to inform policy and decision-making towards dignified and durable solutions to ending displacement.

Needs

Though 2018 has seen significant departures from formal camps and return to areas of origin, assumptions that camps would empty out throughout the year have not materialized. In the REACH Intentions Survey (Dec 17 - Jan 18) conducted in Anbar camps, 43% of IDP respondents indicated that they were planning to return to their AoO. Though consolidation has begun, the population of Kilo 18 camp in Anbar is 1,070 individuals, most of whom have not received security clearance due to perceived extremist affiliations and restrictions on freedom of movement. Humanitarian planning therefore proved overly optimistic, leaving increasing funding gaps for those who remain displaced. Formal and informal sites are expected to have continued large populations due to secondary displacement, and prolonged displacement for families who cannot return to AoOs.As the push to close camps in Anbar increases in speed, families find themselves in secondary and tertiary displacement and are seeking refuge in predominantly peri-urban areas. An assessment conducted by NRC on November 11 in Kilo 6 and Kilo 7 informal sites, which have begun receiving households from Kilo 18 who left prematurely, identified that the population experienced significant livelihoods, education, and protection needs. Households in these sites expressed ongoing indebtedness due to lack of services and lack of income, and a result, they resorted to dangerous negative coping mechanisms. Average HH income is 113 USD a month. From this an average 202 USD is spent on food per month, and 28 USD on rent, health care and other costs. On average, each household is already in debt by 2,300 USD and has an average of 9 USD in savings. To cope, 281 HHs reported pulling their children from schools, 432 HHs reduced on health care, 312 reduced their food intake, 530 HHs buy on credit, and 590 rely on their relatives for support. In Kirkuk and Salah Al-Din, the RASP indicates that there are 10,488 IDPs in informal sites and informal settings across Kirkuk city, Daquq, and Tooz, who face similar constraints and protection risks. In order to ensure that they receive adequate services, NRC will use mobile site management teams, which will rove across areas of out-of-camp displacement and informal settlements to provide support in monitoring and advocacy, referrals, accountability mechanisms to report protection risks such as evictions and corruption, and linkages to services for affected IDP and host communities. For those unable to return, camps remain an option of last resort. Reductions in service provision and standards, resulting in decaying infrastructure and reduced access to assistance and protection, will penalize the most vulnerable IDP households who face significant barriers to return. Living standards will reduce, and essential services such as food and water provision will begin to see significant gaps in consistency. This deterioration of camp/site infrastructure and services is likely to be compounded by the fluidity of the displacement/return dynamics. This fluidity will result in reduced community ownership and persistent reliance on humanitarian assistance. In many areas of return, risks for returnees include ongoing insecurity and asymmetric attacks, incomplete transfer of security responsibilities from military forces and pro‐government armed groups to civilian police forces, widespread explosive hazard contamination, and extensive damage or destruction of property and critical infrastructure. The threat of these risks is high due to a lack of information in areas of return at the household level. Though the establishment of Community Centres has increased access to services and information, families still have limited information about their areas of origin before return, prohibiting informed decision-making. Families who return to destroyed or unsafe homes face secondary displacement. Returnees, IDPs, and remainee host community also struggle to access public services, and suffer from uncertain timeframes regarding when such services will be restored. Tribal/sectarian disputes contribute to a lack of social cohesion and renewed tensions in some areas of return (specifically in Kirkuk and Salah Al-Din). The post-conflict return process to urban centres will require significant commitment by and close cooperation between GoI authorities, host community, and humanitarian actors. The re-establishment of basic public service provision will be key not only to facilitating safe, informed and voluntary return, but also to create the conditions for people to rebuild their livelihoods. Moreover, given the intra-communal mistrust which sectarian violence has generated over the past few years, and the increasing number of female-headed HH, child-headed HH, and elderly-headed HH, providing psychosocial support to displacement-affected families and restoring social cohesion and confidence building will stand out as a crucial priority within the early recovery process.

Protection Risk Analysis

Key protection risks in relation to camp management include evictions, both inside and outside of camps. As camps are an option of last resort for displaced people, those in camps are highly vulnerable to camp closures and forced evictions. For many, it is unsafe to return to their home areas, and unsafe or unprincipled returns may result in injury or death to returnee households as a result of unexploded ordinance (UXOs) or retributive/collective punishment. Upon returning to their areas of origin, households with perceived extremist affiliations risk being targeted by the community in their area of return and subsequently be at risk of renewed displacement. In camps or settlements, freedom of movement of IDPs may also be limited due to attention from armed actors or local authorities. Maintaining the principled humanitarian space of camps is a key challenge for camp management actors going forward, in a context whereby perceived IS affiliation of households may cause authorities to carry out unlawful arrests or detentions. Within camps or in urban settings through community centre programming, some groups may be marginalised or prevented from participation based on their perceived tribal or armed group affiliations. In addition, vulnerable groups such as women, disabled people or the elderly are at risk of exclusion from camp services or activities, whether due to lack of access or cultural exclusion. As humanitarian funding in Iraq decreases during the movement the early recovery phase of the crisis, gaps in camp services could lead to semi-voluntary returns for families who are unable to return to their home locations. If services in camps (for example food provision, non-food items or medical services) decrease to an unsustainable level or become unavailable altogether, camp actors may see a return of households resorting to negative coping mechanisms such as survival sex or child labour, or households leaving the camps altogether as staying in the camp has become unsustainable.

Mitigation Measures

NRC will continue to cultivate open communication channels with Iraq’s Joint Coordination and Monitoring Centre (JCMC), military commands, local authorities and community leaders in order to advocate against forced evictions and early camp closures. Communication with these stakeholders will also be utilised to ensure continued freedom of movement of camp residents in NRC’s area of influence, as well as maintaining the integrity of humanitarian space. At the centre of NRC’s camp management response is the protection of the rights of the affected population, and NRC’s Camp Management teams will continue to coordinate with UNHCR to prevent the unauthorised detentions of camp residents. NRC will also coordinate actively with the CCCM and Protection Clusters to ensure coherent advocacy and prevention of or mitigation against the protection risks encountered.To mitigate against the marginalisation of vulnerable groups in camp management or UDOC activities, NRC will continue to ensure that the voices of a wide variety of community members are heard through camp management coordination and feedback structures, including Women’s Groups, Youth Councils and representation committees. NRC will also work with service providers, local authorities and the JCMC to ensure the inclusion of marginalised groups into community committee and feedback forums.In order to limit the impact of service provision reduction in camps and settlements, NRC will make full use of the UN Cluster coordination system to advocate for assistance where current or future gaps are identified. When gaps are identified, NRC will also utilise relationships with institutional donors in order to obtain funding to meet needs as they arise.

How is your project accountable to affected population

NRC’s Camp Management interventions keep accountability to affected people at the core of its methodology. In both formal camp management and the delivery of Community Centres in urban areas, NRC ensures that the affected population have access to multiple feedback mechanisms and platforms to express opinions on services provided. NRC manages a Complaints and Feedback Mechanism (CFRM) through its Erbil-based Accountability department, which consists of a hotline as well as feedback an complaints desk per project location. This CFRM holds NRC accountable as well as partner organisations providing services in the area of intervention. Mobilisation services by outreach staff allow NRC to proactively conduct outreach within the affected population, including single-headed households, people with disabilities, single elderly people and families with young children. These feedback mechanisms as well as the presence of outreach staff, have bolstered NRC’s PSEA approach – both ensuring that affected populations are aware of their ability to report as well as the means to do so. NRC’s Middle East Regional Office has supporting the Iraq Country Mission on several occasions throughout the past two years with trainings and awareness raising on whistleblowing and reporting on SEA internally as well as how to communicate this information to beneficiary populations. Community Outreach Mobilisation teams work in NRC’s formal camps and through Community Centres to carry out focus group discussions (FGDs), awareness raising activities inside and outside of camps, management of the information desk and organisation of education events and household level visits. In addition, NRC’s community representation systems in camps and through Community Centres provide another form of two-way communication between NRC Camp Management teams and the affected population, with Women’s Councils, Youth Councils and Community Councils functioning to provide this level of accountability.Across 2019, NRC will seek to not only be accountable population, but also empower them to respond to needs arising – specifically in areas of return. Through community action planning and community-based advocacy, NRC hopes to contribute to durable and sustained returns where possible.
