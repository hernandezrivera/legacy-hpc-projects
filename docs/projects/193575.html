Project Summary

This project aims to strengthen the capacity of the Palestinian health system to prepare for and respond to the different emergencies, including those related to communicable diseases, military escalations, and other potential emergency scenarios identified in the Health Cluster contingency plan. The proposed activities will enhance emergency planning and coordination through establishing satellite Public Health Emergency Operation Centers for managing crises at the peripheral level. Interventions will also enable the Ministry of Health (MoH) to adequately manage outbreaks of (re)emerging communicable diseases, through technical guidance, provision of supplies, and capacity building in the areas of diagnostics, case management, surveillance, and infection prevention and control, including supporting International Health Regulations (IHR) core capacities. The project will contribute to strengthening quality of care at all levels of the trauma pathway (prehospital, hospital, and tertiary care) through technical guidance; adopting unified triage and case management systems and protocols; prepositioning of essential supplies; and building the capacity of health personnel. Additionally, more than 200 partners will benefit from the implementation of the Health Resources and Services Availability Monitoring System (HeRAMS). To advocate for the right of Palestinians to Health, WHO will continue to document and monitor barriers to healthcare services access, including for patients, companions and health workers requiring permits to reach essential healthcare services, as a basis for evidence-based advocacy.
Around 5,150 health care workers, including more than 2,500 women will benefit from capacity building activities. Approximately, 354,500 people across the oPt, including 173,705 females and 35,450 elderly and people with disabilities (PWD), will directly benefit from planned interventions.
All project activities have been coordinated at various levels with all relevant partners, counterparts, and Health Cluster working groups.

1 - Needs

According to the Health Cluster, around 1.6 million Palestinians, including 778K females and 79K PWD, across the oPt were identified as requiring health-related humanitarian assistance in 2023, two-thirds of them in the Gaza Strip and one third in the West Bank. Adequate response requires promoting and ensuring equitable access for all vulnerable groups, including elderly, women and people with disability, to essential health care.Out of this total, more than 317K people have vulnerabilities for contracting communicable diseases (CDs), developing severe symptoms, and accessing essential CD services. In 2022 alone, more than 244K Gazans have required COVID-19 testing, including 120K females and 12K persons above 60 years old. COVID-19 positivity rate reached around 42% in July 2022, during the fifth wave of the outbreak in the Gaza Strip. On top of the ongoing pandemic, in 2022, the oPt reported outbreak cases of meningitis, and hepatitis of unknown etiology, in addition to the Polio and Monkey Pox outbreaks in neighboring countries. This requires strengthening IHR core capacities to prevent, mitigate, and respond to potential future outbreaks. It necessitates enhancing coordination, laboratory capacity, epidemiological surveillance, case management, and vaccination for the different emerging and re-emerging CDs; as well as improving infection prevention and control measures. This is achieved through technical guidance to scale up the health system’s capacity to early detect and manage cases of CDs; maintaining the provision of critically needed supplies, including laboratory items, drugs, medical consumables and equipment; in addition to building the capacity of healthcare workers to adequately respond to outbreaks. Health promotion and awareness also remain crucial for an effective response.The fragile health system, compound with frequent military escalations, calls for improving the quality of trauma and emergency health services, across all three levels of care, to minimize avoidable mortality and morbidity. Adopting a national triage system for mass casualty incidents is essential to enhance service provision at the prehospital and hospital levels. This requires having a unified triage protocol for mass casualty incidents and a national emergency medicine protocol; supporting a centralized ambulance dispatch centre; and prepositioning emergency drugs, equipment and supplies for Emergency Medical Services (EMS), Emergency Medical Teams (EMTs), and hospital emergency departments (EDs). Building the capacity of staff along the trauma pathway is also critical for proper management of cases. The latest response assessment of hospitals, which conducted following the August 2022 escalation, indicated the importance of the mass casualty management training, and highlighted the need to expand it to include all relevant staff working in EDs. Service provision for traumatic cases and those with post-op complications also needs to be strengthened, through minor renovations and equipping of operations theaters, personnel capacity building, and improving blood bank services. At the tertiary level of care, adopting and strengthening a multidisciplinary approach is vital for a comprehensive response, through provision of essential drugs and supplies, and building the capacity of healthcare workers.Strengthening coordination and emergency preparedness, and supporting the health information system are also vital for an adequate and timely response. A Public Health Emergency Operation Center (PHEOC) is a physical location in which designated emergency management functions are implemented, supported by appropriate legislation and regulations, and designed and resourced with sustainability in mind. PHEOCs play an important role in coordination of information and resources for efficient and effective response. WHO has supported the MoH in establishing two central PHEOCs- one in Ramallah and one in Gaza City. However, there is a need to establish five other satellite PHEOCs, 3 in WB and 2 in GS, to manage crises at the district level. In addition to that, the ever-changing health situation in the oPt, due to the impact of frequent military escalations and disease outbreak, requires access to strong and timely information tools that can identify needs and gaps, disaggregated by age, sex, and disability, when possible. WHO and other partners, through the Health Cluster, have a responsibility for undertaking timely health data collection and analysis, to inform decision-making for a better response to pressing and urgent needs of the vulnerable population. The Health Resources Availability Monitoring System (HeRAMS) allows timely collection, processing, and analysis of data related to the availability and sufficiency of essential health services and resources, to guide response. Therefore, it is vital to sustain HeRAMS in 115 health facilities in Gaza, and expand it to include more than 200 facilities in the WB. On another note, barriers to humanitarian health access have continued in 2022. As per the WHO access reports, only 63% of patient permit applications from the Gaza Strip, and 82% from the West Bank were approved in 2022. Movement limitations and blockade also affect health workers, companions, and medical supplies. In addition to that, health attacks in the oPt have increased in recent weeks, with a total of 153 attacks on health care documented since the start of 2022, causing injury to 96 health workers, damage to 27 ambulances, and obstruction to the delivery of health care. Therefore, strengthening equity monitoring, protection and advocacy are needed to reduce humanitarian needs.

2 - Activities

This project is fully aligned with the needs and priority interventions identified in the Health Cluster HNO and HRP for 2023. Through its planned activities, which are in line with the Strategic Health Cluster Objectives 1 and 3, WHO will focus on supporting the capacity of the national health system to prepare for and respond to the different emergencies, and promote and advocate for the Palestinians’ right to health. Activities will aim to minimize the equity gap in service availability and accessibility and to achieve an equitable access to essential services for the vulnerable population, including women, girls, elderly, and people with disability, across the different age groups. Capacity building activities will also provide equal, and non-discriminatory opportunities for participants. Through its different interventions, WHO will promote protection (confidentiality, dignity, respect,..) and accountability to the affected population. 9.1c - How will feedback be used?

WHO is committed to ensuring a people-centred humanitarian response that is accountable to the affected population. For this project, the affected people will participate in the following ways:- Lists of procurement of supplies and equipment will be drafted and shared with the Health Cluster and endorsed by the MoH, to ensure that there is no duplication and procurement responds to the most acute needs of the health system.- Where possible, WHO will continue to conduct regular visits/meetings to collect feedback on the quality and availability of services from patients and health staff. WHO will consider the feedback and identify and implement remedial actions to improve response, in consultation with affected people and counterparts. This also includes any SEAH complaints. WHO will continue to advocate through the Health cluster for strengthening the feedback mechanism for the entire response. - For advocacy, feedback will be used to strengthen processes and activities, and complaints will be fully investigated, with the involvement of impartial bodies where required. All complaints related to serious ethical concerns will be raised through WHO’s ethics mechanisms.- Feedback on training topics, content, and modalities will be collected from healthcare workers and will be used to contextualise the training and adapt it to address the need, taking into account the health system's capacity.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

The centrality of protection is at the heart of WHO emergency response as mandated by the IASC. WHO works to ensure the prevention of harm to all persons it engages with, including through upholding due processes of consent, the protection of confidentiality and the proper collection, communication, storage, dissemination and disposal of information on affected persons. Additionally, activities supported by WHO will contribute to ensuring that beneficiaries are not exposed to further contamination risks through adherence to IPC. WHO will also ensure that all interventions are context-appropriate and culturally sensitive. Affected people will be consulted on this where and when appropriate. The prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse has also been integrated across the WHO-supported projects through mandatory training of all staff on PSEA and seeking feedback from the affected people, which will be acted upon. WHO’s response team ensures that PSEA is integrated into the response from an early stage, to build the capacity of national PSEA focal points, and to ensure continuity of activities on the ground despite access constraints. Based on lessons learned from the independent report of the abuse perpetrated in the DRC, WHO oPt will organize a training session for staff, and has rolled out the inter-agency Code of Conduct in alignment with the national, and sub-national PSEA network and encouraged all staff and personnel to sign. WHO willparticipate in the GBV sub-cluster as well as the national and sub-national PSEA networks through a designated focal point. Currently, plans are under way to roll out more trainings, work on prevention, and to strengthen inter-agency feedback and reporting mechanisms, as well as case management and response services through linkages with GBV actors. In harmonization with the SOPs and ground work laid by the national and sub-national PSEA network to identify and mitigate PSEA risks, WHO is planning an agency specific risk assessment that is complimentary and identifies the specific risks associated with WHO’s scope of work, and guides a plan that is fully integrated within the IASC PSEA strategy and plan for oPt.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

The project activities will clearly focus on the provision of life-saving health interventions to the most vulnerable, including women, the elderly and people with disabilities (PWD) based on the latest available impartial assessments. PWD will be considered as one of the main targeted vulnerable groups in the proposed project. All indicators will be gender-age disaggregated where possible. WHO will continue to work to strengthen the empowerment of affected persons, including through the provision of platforms to promote patient voices as well as capacity building with health service providers to promote participatory mechanisms within health services design and delivery. WHO also works with the Ministry of Health and partners to strengthen monitoring and analysis of equity and barriers to health access, focused on vulnerable groups and protected characteristics.
