Project Summary

The intervention will support the most vulnerable households (HHs) in the five governorates of the Gaza strip whose food security was impacted by the May-escalation and the COVID-19 pandemic, on top of the prolonged economic deterioration caused by more than 14 years of siege. It also aims at supporting farmers whose livelihoods are impacted by reduced capacity to acquire basic inputs and whose productive assets are affected by these natural and man-made hazards. Through the proposed action, the productive capacities and food security of small-scale
farmers will as such be improved through a mix of cash assistance and in-kind modalities,
thus contributing to increased resilience for themselves and their wider communities. The
project is informed by ACTED assessments (between September and October 2021), the
Multi-Sectorial Needs Assessments (MSNA), conducted by REACH and OCHA in July 2021,
extensive consultations with key stakeholders, and recommendations from the Ministry of
Agriculture (MoA), Food Security Sector (FSS) and Cash Working Group (CWG) in Gaza. Specifically, ACTED and its implementing partner Arab Center for Agricultural Development (ACAD) will implement a multi-pronged approach to protect livelihoods and strengthen food security through the following means:
• Providing multi-purpose cash assistance (MPCA) to help HHs suffering from limited access to food and income opportunities meet their basic food needs and
alleviate the impact of the COVID-19 crisis and the May-2021 escalation;
• Facilitating the full and/or partial rehabilitation of land and agricultural assets using a
self-rehabilitation approach through the provision of cash-for-assets directly to selected
farmers;
• Improving water storage to increase agricultural productivity though the rehabilitation and installation of PV system for agricultural water ponds and wells through using a vendor approach;
• Facilitating the rehabilitation of greenhouses to increase the growing season through the provision of cash-for-assets directly to selected farmers;
• Facilitating the rehabilitation of poultry farms and the installation of solar panels through the provision of cash-for-assets directly to selected farmers;
• Providing Cash-for-work (CfW) opportunities to unskilled workers who will support the implementation of the aforementioned activities;
• Strengthening existing Community-based Protection Committees (CBPCs) through the
conducting of refresher trainings to mainstream protection principles within their
communities as well as ensuring community engagement and meaningful access to aid.
The combined population of targeted localities, namely Shokat as Sufi, Al Bayuk, Orayba,
Wadi as Salqa, Khuzaa, Abassan and Alfukhary (in the Rafah, Middle area, Khan Yunis
governorates) will indirectly benefit from the land, water ponds, greenhouses, water wells
rehabilitation as well as the strengthening of CBPCs, through the injection of cash into the
local economy, the increase of fresh produce within local markets, and the enhanced
protection delivered by the CBPCs. The MPCA will support the most vulnerable HHs
throughout the Gaza strip, while the rehabilitation of poultry farms will target the North
Gaza and Gaza governorates. More specifically, the intervention will directly benefit 820
beneficiaries and their household; in total the intervention will thus directly benefit 4,510
beneficiaries.
The project will be supported by the mainstreaming of an Integrated Protection Approach (IPA), with specific focus on the most vulnerable groups, including women, girls, boys, PwD and the older people. Gender principles will be mainstreamed throughout the entire action and CBPCs will be trained on referral mechanisms of protection cases such as GBV with a particular focus given to women and children. Finally, Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) will be core to the proposed Action.

1 - Needs

The Gaza strip continues to be affected by the prolonged economic deterioration caused by more than 14 years of siege and sustained undermining of potential economic rehabilitation, with an unemployment rate reaching 44.7% for the second quarter of 2021. Women disproportionately experience unemployment in the oPt: 20% of men vs 43% of women are unemployed. Gaza: 91% women + 66% of Gaza youth unemployed. The impact of COVID-19 has increased protection risks and GBV, especially IPV: underreporting indicates that survivors do not have confidence in existing SGBV services, particularly the CMR In addition to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic in Gaza, the May escalation only aggravated the situation further, notably in terms of food security. ACTED conducted in May 2021 a rapid multi-sectorial needs assessment informed by interviews with MoSD, MPWH, MoA, PWA and the CMWU in the Gaza strip, the representatives of the five most affected municipalities, as well as its implementing partners: Unlimited Friends, Aisha Association, UAWC and ACTED’s Committees (CBPCs) established under HF funded projects. This assessment confirmed that the humanitarian consequences of the last hostilities had been devastating, and exacerbated the impact of the 14-year long Israeli blockade, internal Palestinian political divisions, and recurrent escalations. For instance, the destruction of one of the largest agricultural inputs stores (Khudair store) in the North Gaza governorate caused a 50% decrease in the availability of agricultural inputs, and put the agricultural season in the Gaza Strip at risk. According to the World Bank (June 2021), the agricultural sector suffered a direct damage of USD 45 million, while the MoA estimated it was accounting for USD 55 million. More than 26,000 dunums of orchards, vegetable crops and greenhouses, and more than 450 livestock and poultry farms, were fully or partially destroyed. Due to the May hostilities, the total number of affected/damaged water wells is 18; 45 greenhouses have destroyed arches; 433 greenhouses need nylon sheet, according to the MoA. The Palestinian Ministry of Labour estimated that more than 19,000 workers were affected by direct damages to active facilities, 37% of them working in the agriculture sector. Exports of fresh agricultural products were suspended during and in the aftermath of the escalation for over a month, and did not manage to resume to pre-May levels nor to the levels of same period in 2020. As such, there has since been an urgent need to restore the capacities of farmers in the Gaza strip to ensure the continuity of food production activities, and that food supply remains stable and covers consumers’ nutrition needs. It must be noted that the current situation adds up to an already fragile context impacted by the aforementioned blockade and worsened by the COVID-19 outbreak in the Gaza strip since last year. According to the FSS HNO 2022, 1,137,140 persons in the Gaza strip are in need of FSS support. Based on the MSNA conducted by REACH and OCHA in July 2021, increased food prices and shortages have led to poor food-consumption levels among most vulnerable HHs, and negative coping strategies including buying food on credit through borrowed money from relatives, friends or financial institutions (75% of HH), reducing expenditure on non-food items like health and education (52% of HH), and selling their household properties to cover their food needs, such as their refrigerator, television, or jewellery (34% of HH).More generally, the MSNA revealed the impact of COVID-19 crisis and of the May-escalation on HHs’ monthly income: 53% saw it decrease due to COVID-19 and 51% of HHs also had to face a decrease in their monthly income due to the May hostilities in Gaza. In total, 53% of HHs have been unable to afford basic needs and are taking on debt due to healthcare, food, and/or basic expenditures and 80% of HHs reported challenges in obtaining enough money to meet its needs in the month preceding data collection for the MSNA. In parallel, 42% of HHs are also reporting that at least one adult in their HH is unemployed and seeking work. This MSNA thus revealed the extent of the major economic crisis that HHs in Gaza have to face and its impact on their ability to meet their needs, as a result of the May-escalation, the COVID-19 crisis, and the protracted crisis in Gaza in general. ACTED also conducted in September 2021 an assessment on 548 HHs which benefited from food voucher assistance under another ACTED project. Thisassessment has enabled ACTED to better understand the needs to urgently address at HH level and notably in terms of FSS. In fact, 80% of respondents indicated that they were facing major challenges in securing their families basic needs. When asked about the priority needs of their family, 60% mentioned cash assistance. 98% indicated preferring cash modalities, more specifically through bank rather than in-kind assistance. ACTED will thus adapt this project’s modality based on BNF preferences. Additionally, ACTED and ACAD conducted in October 2021 a needs assessment covering 167 farmers in Deir al Balah and Khan Yunis, under their OCHA-funded project. Data shows farmers’ prioritization of needs: 62% are facing major challenges in continuing their agriculture-based livelihoods activities; 61% indicated facing challenges in securing the agricultural inputs they needed as they could not afford it; 20% assessed that access to water was one of the major challenges that threatened their ability to keep planting their lands. The proposed action thus responds to the needs identified in the 2022 HNO and takes into account FSS priorities, such as to protect livelihoods of vulnerable HHs, and restore/enhance their resilience, food security and productive capacity, as well as to ensure that HHs suffering from limited access to food and to income opportunities are able to meet their basic food needs, as highlighted for the HRP 2022

2 - Activities

A1. Beneficiary Selection: 820 beneficiaries (BNF) and their HH will be selected through coordination with the MoA and the MoSD lists from affected/vulnerable HHs, and through calls for application, advertised through multiple platforms including information sessions to introduce eligibility criteria and the overall application process, ensuring the possibility to apply for PwD, older people and women (200 for A2, 100 for A3, 210 for A4, 50 for A5, 50 for A6, and 230 for A7). All applicants will be made aware of ACTED’s complaint response mechanism and PSEA materials will be disseminated. ACTED will conduct assessments and verifications of HHs confirmed as eligible by the MoA and MoSD, and a panel will score the assessed farmers to select the most vulnerable. ACTED will ensure to apply the UVAT as recommended by CWG and following the current experience on another HF’s project. In each activity, a special focus will be dedicated to ensure the inclusion of PwD, older people and women, both prioritizing their HHs or targeting them as direct BNFs of the assistance. ACTED’s AMEU will conduct a verification exercise to ensure fair selection. The final list will be shared with the MoA and key actors to avoid duplication. ACAD agronomists will produce tailored BOQs and clear workplans to implement the activitiesA2. Provision of MPCA: ACTED will provide 3 instalments in local currency via bank transfer of MPCA to 200 vulnerable HHs, selected under coordination with MoSD to help meet their immediate basic needs, allowing them to fully utilise assistance for its intended purpose. In line with the Gaza CWG, the agreed transfer value for MPCA is 60% of the Minimum Expenditure Basket (MEB) at 1,046 ILS (around 325 $). If this MPCA were to be distributed in times of emergency, ACTED would discuss with donors, FSS, and the CWG to adapt the transfer value to Emergency MEB in order to reach more BNF and reduce the number of instalmentsA3. ACTED will provide 100 farmers with cash assistance to facilitate the rehabilitation of 200 donums of land (2 donums per farmer). A self-rehabilitation approach will be used through the provision of cash-for-assets (3,000 ILS = around 935 $) directly to selected farmers to purchase agricultural inputs needed for rehabilitation works such as small irrigation network components, fertilizers, seeds etc., and cash-for-work to external workers to assist the workplan. Cash-for-assets will be disbursed in 3 instalments (40%, 40%, 20%) via bank transfer/cheque, following the signature of an agreement including tailored BoQ developed following the technical assessment. The number of instalments could also be reduced during the project implementation, depending on the evolution of the COVID-19 outbreak, to limit the risk of contamination or could be converted to a cash-in-hand modality due to potential restrictions taken by the banks. The workplan will be monitored through visits to each HH by ACTED and ACAD to review progress and decide whether to trigger, postpone or cancel subsequent instalmentsA4. Improving water storage through the rehabilitation and PV systems installation for wells and water ponds. This activity will target in total 210 farmers, with the rehabilitation of 15 agricultural water ponds including PV systems, and the installation of PV system and urgent/needed maintenance works of 4 communal wells nominated by MoA. 15 groups of an average of 3 farmers will benefit from the rehabilitation of 15 water ponds. For this activity vendor approach will be used, with average cost of 12,932 ILS (4,000 $) per water pond and 80,567 ILS (25,000 $) per well. This activity will be based on a strong coordination with the MoA and FSS. The workplan will be monitored through visits by ACAD and will provide corrective actions. A competitive tender will be launched to select the contractor(s) for water well rehabilitation works and installation of solar energy for both water wells and pondsA5. Facilitating the rehabilitation of 50 greenhouses: To extend the growing season, a self-repair approach will be used through the provision of cash-for assets (3,000 ILS = around 935 $) based on MoA guideline) to 50 small-scale farmers (1 donum per farmer)A6. Rehabilitation of poultry farms and installation of PV: ACTED will provide 50 farmers in the Gaza and North Gaza governorates with cash assistance to facilitate the rehabilitation of 50 poultry farms, through the distribution of cash directly to farmers to cover the essential rehabilitation works needed. In total, farmers will receive 6,466 ILS (2,000 $) to conduct the rehabilitation work and install solar panels on their farm, relying on a vendor approach. When possible, female-headed HHs and women farmers will be prioritised in the selection of BNF. Female staff will be included to conduct the assessment in order to understand specific needs/protection concerns, and will be staff to which women involved in the project could refer to if needed throughout its implementationA7. Providing Cash-for-work (CfW) opportunities to unskilled agricultural workers: 230 of them will be selected to support vulnerable small-scale farmers in the targeted localities and will benefit from 40 working days with a monthly wage of 1,160 ILS (361 $) based on CWG-endorsed CfW minimum standards.A8. Strengthening 5 CBPCs through the conduct of refresher training. The Committees - one in each governorate and 6 members each - will be strengthened through refresher training, including on PSEA. CBPCs will ensure community engagement throughout the project’s lifespan as well as referring any identified protection issues. ACAD will also administer training on food security and best agricultural practices. CBPCs will be tasked with conducting awareness raising sessions to disseminate information to ensure any assets rehabilitated or installed are are efficiently maintained to maximize impact. 30% of the CBPC’s membership will remain composed of females.

3 - Indicators

Project implementation will be supported by a comprehensive monitoring system to gather all relevant data to steer the action, maximize its impact, measure its effects, anticipate and mitigate any challenge. Specifically, ACTED’s Appraisal, Monitoring and Evaluation Unit (AMEU) is independent from the Program Department and will be in charge of reinforcing the projects’ accountability throughout the project cycle management by regularly assessing project implementation. AMEU is an essential part of ACTED’s project management structure, providing a mechanism to crosscheck and give recommendations on the work of the project team and partners. AMEU leads assessment of baseline situations of beneficiaries through data collection by disaggregation based on age, gender and disability, monitors ongoing implementation through regular field visits and consultations with beneficiaries, and evaluates the outcome of the activities. This methodology ensures the AMEU uses an outcome monitoring approach to analyse the effect of the action on target groups. ACTED will measure the following indicators: # of farmers, herders, breeders, fishers receiving inputs and services (# of individuals not HHs) – target: 3,355; # of dunums made productive or improved production capacity – target: 200; # of greenhouses rehabilitated – target: 50; # of water wells rehabilitated – target 4; # of water ponds rehabilitated – target: 15; #m3 of water storage rehabilitated or provided – target: 2,850; # of individuals benefiting from other conditional cash support – target: 2,365; % of the MPCA transferred amount have been spent on food – target: 38%% of HHs who report being able to meet the survival needs of their HHs (all/ most/ some/none), according to their priorities% of targeted households with borderline or acceptable food consumption score.# of individuals benefiting from other unconditional cash support – target: 1,100 ; % of female members are participating actively in the community based agriculture committees – target 30%; # of Community-based Protection Committees (CBPCs) reinforced throughout a refreshing training – target: 5, % of CBPCs' members who demonstrate improvement in their knowledge after attending the refreshing training – target 80; % of HH reporting that the humanitarian assistance delivered is appropriate and satisfying – target: 80. ACTED’s project management team will use the Project Management Framework (PMF) tool to plan and monitor implementation to assess progress and highlight blocking points. The action will promote the use of best practices in accountability and transparency, in line with Core Humanitarian Standard (CHS) principles, by using ACTED’s Complaints Response Mechanism (CRM). The CRM will enable beneficiaries and other stakeholders to provide feedback and suggestions directly to ACTED staff through a phone line, post, email, and complaint boxes in relevant locations, as well as directly to partner’s staff members. The CRM should ensure that beneficiaries can easily and without fear of repercussion communicate cases of abuse of power by financial institutions, community leaders and/or other key actors involved in the cash-transfer program and/or aspects of program and/or transfer modality that inadvertently have negative implications. ACTED’s AMEU is in charge of handling this mechanism to provide timely response or adequate action to address any concerns. Financial monitoring will be undertaken by all project partners and then consolidated by ACTED, with external audits of the action taking place on an annual basis. ACTED staff will request from ACAD monthly reporting on activities on top of contractual obligations to ensure all indicators are on track and register the beneficiaries’ satisfaction and any difficulties encountered. This monthly reporting will minimise the risk of delays in the activities’ implementation, that any change in the implementation of the project is notified and approved by oPt HF and finally that all indicators are on track and if needed corrective measures are taken to reach the target. Finally, it will enable ACTED to provide comprehensive and clear interim and final reports to oPt HF.

5.2 - Please briefly explain why the specific modality/ies for this response were chosen.

As noted in the 2021 MSNA findings, cash assistance is beneficiaries’ preferred method, especially for women and PwD, as it provides them with more choice and flexibility, while safeguarding their dignity. In addition to being a cost-efficient method for humanitarian actors, the multiplier effect associated with injecting cash within the local economy will contribute towards restoring the Palestinian economy. Finally, cash assistance ensures multisectoral coverage, while keeping beneficiaries involved all along the activities, as they can choose the more adapted items to cover their multiple needs. . Service provider solution is chosen for those activities, such as rehabilitation work or trainings, which require specific technical skills. In this way ACTED will ensure the quality and effectiveness of the results and ensure a no harm approach for beneficiary, especially for the shelter and WASH rehabilitation. For this methodology, ACTED will identify experienced and professional contractors who will be selected through a transparent procurement process thereby ensuring the completion of activities on time whilst fulfilling all technical requirements.

Accountability to Affected Populations
9. - Feedback and complaints mechanisms

Yes

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

The project will be underpinned by ACTED’s programmatic approach of meaningful community engagement and participation of the population, in line with the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) Five Commitments on Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP), namely (1) leadership/governance; (2) transparency; (3) feedback and complaints; (4) participation; (5) design, monitoring and evaluation. More specifically, AAP will be ensured throughout the intervention by taking account of, giving account to, and being held to account by the affected population as follows. ACTED will be held to account by the beneficiaries in line with Core Humanitarian Standard (CHS) principles, by using ACTED’s Complaints Response Mechanism (CRM). The CRM is overseen by ACTED’s AME Unit, which is independent from the delivery of activities. Beneficiaries can access ACTED’s CRM through a dedicated phone line, whatsapp, email address and complaints boxes as well as directly to ACTED staff during field visits. Through this CRM, beneficiaries can raise concerns, make complaints and give feedback on ACTED and co-applicants’ work in a secure and confidential way, and receive a response in a timely manner. ACTED’s AMEU is in charge of handling this mechanism to provide timely response or adequate action to address any concerns. Having a well-designed and well-managed mechanism for handling beneficiary complaints will improve the quality of the action, enhance the trust and confidence of beneficiaries and identify areas of the project which need to be improved, and ensure that ACTED teams learn from the feedback provided through this process.

9.1d - Explain how you have ensured that mechanism are accessible to all population groups?

Beneficiaries can access ACTED’s CRM through a dedicated phone line, whatsapp, email address, complaints boxes and directly through ACTED staff during field visits. This plurality of means aims to ensure CRM accessibility to all, including vulnerable groups with specific needs. During the assessment, field officers will provide a CRM card to each BNF, including all communication channels mentioned above, and explanations on the CRM, to ensure that all BNF feel comfortable and in measure to reach it if needed, including in case of SEA. Female staff, part of the team to which BNF will be able to refer to, will notably ensure female BNF feel safe to complain. AME staff will dedicate specific time to vulnerable groups during field visits to re-explain the CRM and emphasise that BNF can directly complain to them during visits - if one person would not have the capacity to use/access a phone/email address or the complaint box (notably children, PwD, and the older people). ACTED’s commitment to AAP is fully integrated into its recruitment process, staff inductions, trainings and performance management and partnership agreements. All ACTED staff working for the ACTED oPt mission are trained on the Code of Conduct, on the Policy Against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) and Child Protection Policies. Trainings on PSEA aim to ensure that all staff understand the Code of Conduct and the PSEA policy, their obligation to report through ACTED's grievance mechanism, any allegation or suspicion and the sanction measures taken for any breach of the Code of Conduct. Staff are also trained on recognizing sexual exploitation and abuse situations and concrete steps to follow for reporting any concerns or suspicions. In early March 2021, all ACTED staff also participated in the induction session on PSEA organized by the Protection Cluster, to strengthen their capacities to recognize exploitation and abuse situation, as well as adopt a good behaviour to report.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

In all programming, ACTED ensures respect and promotion of the rights of all individuals and communities involved, in accordance with the relevant bodies of law (international human rights law and international humanitarian law). In order to take account of affected populations, the action will also take strong consideration of the needs and interests of beneficiary communities, particularly in regard to designing activities with their safety and dignity in mind. As part of protection mainstreaming, ACTED analyzes protection risks; anticipates possible consequences that may arise; and ensures that issues can be reported/referrals can be made to appropriate agencies/host government structures if required. In particular, ACTED and ACAD will undertake regular visits to the communities and beneficiary HHs to ensure that protection concerns are incorporated throughout the intervention, thus enhancing safety, security and privacy for women, children and other vulnerable groups. ACTED is firmly committed to the humanitarian principles of humanity, impartiality, independence and neutrality, and strives to ensure the ‘Do No Harm’ approach by making sure that its activities do not inadvertently worsen tensions among different groups due to its mere presence and/or provision of assistance. To this end, all staff will be made aware of and in adherence to the Do No Harm principle as outlined in ACTED’S Code of Conduct and related policies including Child Protection; Anti-Fraud, Bribery & Corruption; Anti-Terrorism; and Policy Against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse. Under this framework, ACTED has developed mechanisms that can contribute to prevent sexual exploitation and abuse such as: signing of the code of conduct by all staff in order to clarify unacceptable behaviour and in particular sexual exploitation and abuse; information to the beneficiaries of existing remedies (Complaints and Response Mechanisms); implementation of training and awareness sessions in order to train and empower the teams and identify risk factors on an ongoing basis. Finally, all activities will be conducted in strict adherence of COVID-19 mitigation measures including social distancing and personal protective equipment (PPE) to minimise the risk of COVID-19 transmission among beneficiaries, staff and stakeholder alike.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

In order to maximize the reach of the intervention, the project will adopt a deeply integrated protection approach (IPA) throughout all stages of the project cycle. The action fully acknowledges that different groups and individuals have specific needs and assistance will therefore be adapted accordingly. To this end, ACTED’s Protection Officer will ensure that information is provided through accessible channels, formats, languages and family and community support networks to ensure that the beneficiary community are kept fully informed of the assistance available under the project. ACTED’s AMEU will also ensure equal and impartial access to aid by overseeing the CRM, as well as by conducting various verification exercises. For example, ACTED’s AMEU will conduct a verification exercise to authenticate the fairness of the selection process and all applicants will be notified of the final decision and provided with the means to raise concerns regarding the selection process through ACTED’s CRM. Similarly, to ensure the agency of beneficiaries and further local ownership of project outcomes, the affected population will be actively consulted during the assessments to ensure BoQs and workplans are responsive to their self-identified most pressing needs. Where applicable, at least one male and one female per HH will be consulted to ensure gender-specific needs and concerns are taken into account, as well as those of boys, girls, PwDs, the older people and other marginalised persons to ensure assistance reflects their unique vulnerabilities. In addition, CBPC members and ACTED staff will be present to ensure the integration of HHs concerns into BoQ and workplan design. In the event beneficiaries are dissatisfied with their respective BoQ and/or workplan an appeal mechanism will be offered through the CBPCs and ACTED’s CRM. If the CBPCs or ACTED/ACAD staff consider the request justified, a new BoQ and/or workplan will be produced by a different agronomist. To mitigate this, all staff involved in the assessment process will undergo extensive refresher training in protection standards prior to the assessments to ensure equal and impartial access to assistance. ACTED will put particular emphasis on informing beneficiaries on the functioning and use of the CRM in Gaza, since in the last MSNA conducted by REACH and OCHA in July 2021, only 18,5% of HHs which have benefitted from humanitarian aid, had access/knowledge of complaint mechanisms. Finally, the project will deliberately target the most vulnerable groups for inclusion into the program. For instance, women involved in farming activities in the targeted areas will be approached and highly encouraged to participate in the CBPCs to ensure that membership remains at 30% female at a minimum. After all, women represent a significant but underserved segment of the agricultural workforce in the target areas. Similar efforts will be made to ensure that vulnerabilities do not act as a barrier to accessing aid. For instance, CBPCs will conduct HHs visits to assist in the completion and collection of applications for the vulnerable (including older people, people with disabilities) that might be unable to reach the registration site.
