Project Summary

In 2019, the humanitarian community in Iraq is prioritizing the centrality of protection by responding to protection needs of affected people as well as increasing access to basic services for the most vulnerable populations.
To contribute to this objective, UNICEF intends to support 180,000 internally displaced and conflict affected children, through the provision of winter clothes, in order to protect the most vulnerable children from the harsh winter elements.
UNICEF’s winter response programme falls within UNICEF’s mandate to protect vulnerable children from risks and to guard their safety and well-being and their rights to grow in an environment in which they are enabled to reach their full potential in life. The provision of life sustaining and saving winter supplies to children ensure adequate protection to cope with extreme weathers and proves to be a lifesaving response for children living in precarious situations.

Needs

Multi-layered conflict and political crisis have created a protracted, complex humanitarian crisis, affecting millions of children across Iraq. In 2019, humanitarian partners estimate that 6.5 million people across Iraq require some form of humanitarian assistance, with over 1.9 million people (including 1 million children) remaining displaced in camp and off camp settings. In these locations, existing facilities and systems are struggling to support equitable and consistent basic access to services. With loss of livelihood, unemployment, and economic downturn across Iraq, displaced families have been increasingly unable to meet essential needs and services, especially basic non-food items. According to recent assessment findings, most IDPs reported they did not have sufficient quantities of non-food items (NFI) including winterization materials. Even in areas of return where markets have started functioning once more, assessments showed many to still lack purchasing power to access essential basic items. Due to the complexity of the situation, widespread destruction and lack of basic services, large numbers of people are expected to remain displaced during 2019 and into 2020, including through winter. Because of its diverse topography, northern Iraq experiences extreme contrasting temperatures ranging from higher than 50 degrees Celsius in summers to below freezing in winters. In Iraq, winter starts early in October and lasts until March, bringing with it heavy rains, snow storms and freezing temperatures. For displaced and conflict affected families, the winter season becomes a trial of hardship and survival. Under freezing conditions, staying warm inside poorly-insulated tents and makeshift shelters in which the majority of IDPs live is a struggle. Children are especially vulnerable to the cold and respiratory infections. Keeping children warm in sub-zero temperatures is a challenge for families due to lack of access to electricity, fuel and poorly insulated tents, schools and makeshift shelters. The provision of winter clothes can prove to be a lifesaving response for children living in makeshift tents, open shelters, abandoned or unfinished buildings particularly those in mountainous regions where temperatures dip below freezing points. Per post-distribution and assessment reports from UNICEF’s previous winter response, the provision of winter clothing was the priority for the majority (76 per cent) of the displaced population.

Protection Risk Analysis

Due to the dynamic political and security situation in Iraq, particularly outside the Kurdistan Region of Iraq (KRI), the overall political and social environment is subject to change, and there are moderate risks of access constraints affecting parts of the country, the presence of security actors and armed groups, and sectarian tensions which may impact UNICEF and partners’ operational presence in some areas. UNICEF also anticipates Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (SEA) as a substantial risk during project preparation. Women and girls in particular are at risk of SEA especially those who are perceived to have affiliation with ISIL group.

Mitigation Measures

UNICEF negotiates access for partners through OCHA’s civil military coordination unit. UNICEF takes a proactive approach to protection from sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA): a Code of Conduct, PSEA reporting mechanism, support to and guidance for the survivors in case any instances of SEA were to occur within the context of the project, establishing a survivor-centred approach with multiple entry points for anyone experiencing SEA to seek the help they need; and these measures are taken in close coordination with the Iraq PSEA Network and partner organizations. Furthermore, UNICEF partners are trained on the ‘Do No Harm’ Principle, PSEA, and Community-Based Complaints mechanism. As part of partnership monitoring, UNICEF conducts monitoring visits and holds partnership review meetings with implementing partners to review progress and help identify challenges and solutions, including protection risks. UNICEF requires that all staff working on the project sign the organisation’s code of conduct, which includes the obligation to report any abuse by staff, or suspicion thereof. UNICEF has strict protocols against SEA.

How is your project accountable to affected population

UNICEF is accountable to women, boys, girls and men whose lives it aims to improve and places this responsibility at the core of its humanitarian policy. Continued dialogue between UNICEF staff and affected populations is facilitated to ensure effective engagement of beneficiaries and timely information provision. Participation by IDPs and returnee communities enables them to play a role in decision-making processes that affect them through the design of UNICEF emergency interventions and the review and evaluation of programmes. UNICEF interventions are informed by needs assessment of affected populations to determine the appropriate responses. In addition, UNICEF has a strong working relationship with the Government Ministries and their Directorates at governorate level and ensures that these bodies remain the key duty bearers, alongside local non-government organisations (NGO) and civil society organisations (CSO), in the identification of services and supplies needed in the local area, and that these bodies remain accountable to the children and families they serve. UNICEF maintains a strong and regular lines of communication both with partners and beneficiaries, ensuring timely feedback into operational and strategic levels of decision-making. Involvement of beneficiaries is at the ‘post distribution’ stage, where data is collected through a questionnaire administered to a sample group to assess their satisfaction with the items they’ve received. Families receiving winter clothes provide feedback on quality and timely assistance received through partners. In addition to this post distribution monitoring and feedback mechanism, partners set up a help desk during the distribution process to collect beneficiary complaints and feedback, which are regularly reviewed and addressed.
