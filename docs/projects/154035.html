Project Summary

Ninewa is the main originating Governorate of IDPs in Iraq and currently also hosts the majority of the returnees (1,550,000 IOM November 2018) whereas Dohuk Governorate host the majority of the IDPs in the Kurdish Region of Iraq (350,000 IOM November 2018). Both returnees as well as IDPs and remainees face significant protection issues. Dorcas has been executing 68 FGDs in 2018 including 940 male & female respondents in Ninewa along with 725 HH assessments to identify most prominent protection issues and their root causes. Most frequent mentioned issue that acerbates HH’s vulnerability is absence, loss or barred civil documentation like ID cards, passports, PDS cards and birth-, marriage- and death certificates, obstructing HH’s access to basic services like healthcare, education, social support, disability allowances, etc. Second pertaining protection issue is incidents of recruitment into government-affiliated armed groups reported in parts of Ninewa; groups who control the areas make returns conditional on the commitment of families to enlist one or more male family members (UNHCR Protection Update – July 2018). Thirdly, for many (Sunni/Shia) IDPs from Tel Afar, Sinjar, Mosul & Hamdaniya, returns to their AoO are considered as unsafe due to perceived extremist affiliation and associated risk of (unjustified) detention (UNHCR Protection Update – July 2018). Remainees in Ninewa face similar protection issues. Fourthly, risks of secondary displacement are rampant due to lack of available services in AoO, lack of livelihood options and perceived lack of safety, which is particularly troubling in Sinjar, Tel Afar and Tilkaif (REACH ROAR, April-May 2018; IOM Return Index October 2018). Fifthly, in Sumel and Zakho, between 31.3% and 45.5% of the IDP population faces HLP issues (UNHCR Protection Update – July 2018). Similarly identified issues are common in Sinjar and Wana where 324 returned HH reported lack of documentation to proof ownership over their housing and land. Not only does this obstruct people from re-settling in their AoO, it also disables them to take up (agricultural based) livelihoods which subsequently affect each sphere of family and economic life. Sixthly, the armed conflict and associated atrocities such as witnessing violence, disappearance of family members, forced displacement, (S)GBV and (fear of) imprisonment have been identified as having severe potential negative mental health impacts (Ibrahim et al., 2018).
Based on each partner’s years of technical expertise, (Dorcas in general protection and legal support, Harikar in (S)GBV protection) Partners propose the implementation of a holistic protection package covering in total 32,400 IDP-, Returnee- and Remainee populations in Northern Ninewa (Sinjar, Tel Afar, Telkaif) and Dohuk Governorate (Zakho & Sumel).
This will be done through a combined mobile and static approach providing the following services through 6 centers: protection monitoring and HH-level assessments, awareness raising and community mobilization to tackle protection issues, provision of legal counselling and legal assistance by lawyers (related to HLP, Documentation, Detention), case management, provision of SGBV services (and Child Protection services through coooperating with WarChild), provision of structures PSS and ensuring adequate referral pathways for protection cases. Specific attention will be paid to the plight of women, girls and boys who are assessed to be extra vulnerable when there is a breach in the protective atmosphere (i.e. armed forces recruitment, (S)GBV, etc.).

Needs

Iraq continues to suffer from the crisis after IS’ declaration of the caliphate in the west of the country and subsequent military campaign, which displaced 6 million Iraqis at its peak, and economically, legally and psychologically impacted many more. The “defeat” of IS has not yet resulted in life returning to normal for many Iraqis. 1.9 million remain displaced, and 4 million returnees face significant protection challenges (IOM DTM, Oct. 2018). The no of people in need of protection (including IDPs, returnees and host community) throughout the country is identified to be 4.3 million in 2019, with Ninewa and Dohuk considered among governorates with most pressing needs. General Protection – IDPs (in & out of camp), Returnees, Remainees in Ninewa & DohukNinewa is the main originating Governorate of IDPs in Iraq and hosts the majority of the returnees (1,550,000 IOM Nov 2018) whereas Dohuk Governorate hosts the majority of the IDPs in the Kurdish Region of Iraq (350,000 IOM Nov 2018). Returnees, IDPs and remainees face significant protection issues. Dorcas conducted 68 FGDs in 2018 (940 respondents) in Ninewa along with 725 HH assessments to identify most prominent protection issues and their root causes. Most frequent mentioned issue that acerbates HH’s vulnerability is absence, loss or barred civil documentation, obstructing HH’s access to basic services. In Sumel & Zakho, 3.2% to 10.1% of the in- and out of camp IDPs report missing documentation, which seems a gross underestimate. Based on Dorcas’ protection monitoring in Sinjar and Wana, 70%-90% of the returnees, IDPs and remainees suffer from missing/outdated documentation. Returnees reported absence of birth certificates and ID’s in Zummar and Rabe’a as well (DRC, Social Inquiry 2018). The split jurisdiction between Dahuk in the KRI and Mosul, disables returnees and IDPs to access the right services (REACH ROAR, June 2018).Second protection issue is incidents of recruitment into government-affiliated armed groups reported in parts of Ninewa; groups who control the areas make returns conditional on the commitment of families to enlist one or more male family members (UNHCR Protection Update – July 2018). Thirdly, for many (Sunni/Shia) IDPs from Tel Afar, Sinjar, Mosul & Hamdaniya, returns to their AoO are considered as unsafe due to perceived extremist affiliation and associated risk of (unjustified) detention (UNHCR Protection Update – July 2018). Remainees in Ninewa face similar protection issues. Fourthly, in Sinjar, Tel Afar and Tilkaif (REACH ROAR, April-May 2018; IOM Return Index Oct 2018) there are risks of secondary displacement due to lack of services in AoO, lack of livelihood options and perceived lack of safety. Fifthly, in Sumel and Zakho, 31.3% - 45.5% of the IDP population faces HLP issues (UNHCR Protection Update – July 2018) but alson in Sinjar and Wana returned HH reported lack documentation to proof ownership over housing and land. This hampers re-settling in AoO and uptake of (agricultural based) livelihoods. Sixthly, the armed conflict and associated atrocities have severe potential negative mental health impacts (Ibrahim et al., 2018). In a recent study amongst Yezidis Ibrahim et al. (2018) found that 80% of 416 Yezidi women and girls met the criteria for a probable DSM-5 PTSD diagnosis. Hoffman et al. (2018) found a probable prevalence of 70.9% of PTSD amongst 108 female Yezidi survivors. Not having experienced first-hand physical torture or abuse doesn’t safeguard someone from psychological distress. Long-term displacement embodies substantial stressors which may lead to mental distress/ disorders. MHPSS services in Ninewa and Dohuk are scarce and in some locations absent.Gender Based ViolenceDue to a fragile security, social and legal context in newly accessible areas in Ninewa, female IDPs and returnees remain one of the most vulnerable groups (UNFPA GBV Assessment, Iraq 2016; Care, GBV Challenge Workbook). Growing gaps in humanitarian aid in Dohuk Governorate have heightened the likelihood of females being engaged in negative livelihood coping strategies (DPWG MoM, Oct. 2018) (UNHCR Protection Update – July 2018). Female-headed HH (6.4% - 12.5% of the HH in Sumel/Zakho and 10-25% of the HH in Ninewa), are particularly vulnerable to (S)GBV due to lack of financial and social resources and perpetrator’s awareness of their vulnerability. And provisions in the Iraqi Criminal Procedure Code (No. 23 of 1971) complicate prosecution when the victim is a woman (IILHR 2010). Child ProtectionConflict, violence, and prolonged displacement can degrade the psychosocial wellbeing of children, which can have long lasting impact. Without proper lower level psychosocial intervention (structured PSS and case management) psychosocial distress can become mental health disorders. There are cases that children are not able to attend school due to lack of Civil ID (DPWG MoM, Oct 2018).

Activities

Based on each partner’s technical expertise, Dorcas and Harikar (in cooperation with WarChild) propose the implementation of a holistic protection package covering in total 32,400 IDP-, Returnee- and Remainee populations in Northern Ninewa (Sinjar, Tel Afar, Tilkaif) and Dohuk Governorate (Zakho & Sumel) with general protection-, SGBV and legal services. Linkage will be established with CP services provided by Warchild in these same areas. The consortium enhances a combined mobile and static approach. Dorcas will be responsible for protection monitoring, case management and legal services in each 6 locations, Harikar will be responsible for (S)GBV services. Dorcas will host the 2 centers in Tilkaif (Wana) and Zakho (Chamishko) , Harikar will host the 4 centers in Sinjar, Sinuni, Telafar (Zummar) and Sumel (Sharia). General Protection (65% of the budget)In six target locations (Sinuni, Zummar/Rabea, Wana, Shariya & Chamishko) a community center has been or will be established for multi-service provision. Harikar has two existing centres which are well established in the community providing GBV services in Sinuni and Sinjar which are funded by UNFPA and are anticipated to be funded until the end of 2019. Dorcas will expand services in these centres to provide general protection services (protection monitoring and awareness), legal counselling and legal assistance (civil documentation and HLP). This holistic service package subsequently will be replicated by two new centers set-up by Harikar in Zummar and Sharia and by in two new centers set up by Dorcas in Wana and Chamishko. Centres in Sharia (run by Harikar) and Chamishko (run by Dorcas) villages will be established with separate satellite spaces in Sharia and Chamishko camps where structured PSS will take place. However referrals of in-camp IDPs will be made towards the out-of-camp centers to ensure there is still a cohesive comprehensive package of services to people in need both in and out of camp settings. Throughout all 6 locations, Dorcas will establish and train 50 community volunteers (25 teams). These community volunteers will do house-to-house protection assessments, raise awareness on rights and protection services available and refer those in need to services in the centres. These community volunteers will be overseen by protection monitors who are also deployed to collect information in critical areas after a (potential) crisis incident (i.e. like when there was a significant of Sunni Arab returnees in Sinjar which increased social tensions in September-October 2018). Protection monitors will also conduct area service mapping which will be updated on a quarterly basis and shared with all local stakeholders and relevant clusters. Legal AssistanceDorcas will also manage a legal team of +/- 21 lawyers covering all of the 6 centers who are deployable/mobile to meet the needs of the full coverage area. 4 Lawyers will be specialised on HLP issues, 15 lawyers will be fully dedicated to (civil) documentation and 2 will be detention lawyers. All lawyers will provide in their area of expertise legal counselling and assistance (with crossover for SGBV case support). Moreover, the lawyers will be trained on how to interact with- and follow the SOP for SGBV by Harikar. Main activities under civil documentation will be: Main activities for Civil Documentation and HLP will be:-Conducting HLP assessment-Conducting awareness raising on HLP rights, services and redress mechanisms-Provision of legal counseling and services -Providing support in establishing lease contracts/resolve lease disputes/ownership documentation/property restitution or compensation SGBV (35% of the budget)Harikar will be responsible for (S)GBV case management and care in all 6 centers. Main activities will be:-SGBV case management (referred by Dorcas' protection teams)-PSS for survivors and vulnerable women-SGBV case referrals (for example to SGBV lawyers)-Dignity kits for survivors-PFA for survivors and mentoring support-Provision of recreational activities-Training on specialist and non-specialist staff on SGBV-SGBV assessments-Safety audits-Training for community members on SGBV prevention-Awareness raisingThe 2 centers that already exists (Sinuni & Sinjar) will see an increase in SGBV staffing of 4 persons who will reach out to the rural villages in the area where there is an identified SGBV service gap (conclusion based on 8 safety audits conducted by Harikar funded by UNFPA in Sinjar & Sinuni). For the other new centers, new SGBV teams will be established compromised out of a case manager, two social workers, two community mobilizers and one recreational trainer per center all focussed on SGBV service provision. Both in Ninewa and Dohuk two (female) (S)GBV lawyers from Dorcas will also be deployed to support (S)GBV cases with all legal matters. Child Protection will be linked to Warchild's operations in these areas (see Warchild HRP Protection Plan).

Protection Risk Analysis

During the implementation of the project the staffs and beneficiaries might face the following risks:1. Swift changes of the political dynamics that prevent Partners from gaining the necessary approval to operate in the targeted areas; 2. A sudden increase of military combat operations in or around the target areas that limit or stop access of Partners from implementing activities;3. Project staff are affected by security incidents;4. International staff of the project have insufficient access to monitor the implementation in the target areas;5. Beneficiary concerns on the quality of project services, particularly about safety, confidentiality and respect of survivors;6. Audit reveals inadequate financial documentation and processes;7. Partner’s staff conduct financial mismanagement or corruption;8. Sudden restrictions are imposed that limit the cash transfer of USD to banks in Iraq and/or the availability of cash in local accounts;9. Cultural restrictions prevent the involvement of women and girls in some key activities;

Mitigation Measures

The followings are the ways to mitigate the above mentioned risks:1. Regularly monitoring of political and conflict situation. At the start of program, partners will conduct stakeholder sensitizations of the project (governmental and non-governmental stakeholders). Partners also work with other local NGO partners with significant footprint and influence including good networks with the key stakeholders in the target areas. However it is anticipated, given the current political and military dynamics that access to Telafar, Zumar, Wana and Sinjar areas will be challenging every now and then due to security constraints. Partners will however engage with relevant Gov. and international stakeholders to gain access to operational areas, including UN OCHA.2. Partners have no control over potential military combat operations against ISIS or other violent extremist groups. At the start of the project, Partners will hold a Security Risk and Mitigation workshop to identify areas of risks, the likelihood of an event to occur, the impact and suitable mitigation measures. This will result in an agreed approach (SOP) on how to deal with certain issues including relocation personal case management files to a secured location or to destroy files if it is not possible to move them.3. Partners will reduce this risk by aligning its security assessments with other international and local organizations operating in Iraq, including the International NGO Safety Organization (INSO). Moreover, staff policies are in place how to support and deal with staff in the case of different sorts of events (i.e. traumatic experience)4. National and international staff will be in charge of M&E. Iinternational staff might not always be able to travel due to visa constraints. In case there is a gap of access and capacity to monitor by both national and international staff, the Partners will hire third party monitors to independently monitor the implementation in the field as well as to verify information provided by the staff of the center. In such a case, capacity building will be conducted for third-party staff on M&E tools.5. Project staff will be trained on PSEA, Humanitarian Principles, SGBV and CP SOPs, Confidentiality, Informed Consent and other privacy safeguarding principles to ensure confidentiality. Personal files will be stored in locked cabinets and a strict key protocol will be put in place. Dorcas’ invests in digital safeguarding to ensure that all digitally stored beneficiary data is secured. As for the beneficiary’s concerns, Partners will continue to have complaint's mechanisms (feedback boxes and hotlines) and will conduct regular beneficiary satisfaction surveys in order to monitor and ensure quality of services. Codes of conduct are signed by each Partner staff including PSEA references and process description of CoC’s are breached. 6. A start-up training for all Partners will be conducted to ensure compliance with donor requirements. Consortium lead will organize internal auditing. A licensed audit firm will conduct project audit. Partners work according their financial policies and procedures with internal control mechanisms and cash counts.7. Payment schedules are based on the implementation plans included in the Partnership Agreement signed upon the award of the grant. Partnership Agreement will outline each organisation’s approved budget, payment schedules and mandatory documents to be shared before the transfer of funds can be realised. 8. Partners will conduct community sensitive outreach and community mobilization throughout the project and engage on a regular basis with (informal) community leaders as well as with men and boys to achieve greater acceptance of the project and its specific assistance to women. Where necessary, negotiation with families will take place to allow women's participation in the project activities. How is your project accountable to affected population

Collecting community input: Partners work through a network of community focal points and volunteers that pro-actively inform on specific needs and vulnerabilities, which are included in the project design and implementation. Moreover, structural FGD’s and KII are organised to collect input from local communities. As per November 2018, 68 FGD’s (covering 940 individuals) are conducted by Dorcas in Sinjar and Wana, providing valuable input for project programming and ensuring the concerns of the local communities are heard and understood. Moreover, separate men, women and youth FGD’s are organised to ensure all layers of society are heard and understood and Partners make use of facilitators with the same gender to lower barriers for sharing opinions by communities. Providing information: All communities and relevant stakeholders including community leaders will be informed about the final outline of the project activities which have incorporated the local needs and concerns. If selection takes place on certain criteria (i.e. for certain trainings or certain kind of support), these will also be shared with the communities so they can provide further feedback and/or provide acceptance. Feedback procedures: In each of the 6 centers feedback/question/complaint procedures (following Core Humanitarian Standards) are in place. This is done through the establishment of feedback boxes in each location with local language description of the purpose, as well as through the operation of a hotline, running 5 days a week. The hotline is hosted by staff not directly involved in the day-to-day running of the project and the hotline “responder” is trained on the principles of anonymity and confidentiality. Partner’s staff managing the hotline report on a bi-weekly basis to the Project Manager. During this meeting follow-up actions, including appropriate redress of complaints/feedback, are agreed upon. Information for communities how to express feedback is also written down on information leaflets, banners and other communication materials. Banners are put up in each center. These banners include symbols to ensure information is also understandable for individuals that are unable to read. Staff behaviour: Specifically for protection sensitive cases, SOP’s are followed (for example in SGBV) which put priority to the will of the survivor. This means that if a beneficiary doesn’t want any action to be taken upon his/her case and/or doesn’t want further involvement of authorities or other stakeholders, this will be granted at all times. Moreover, staff is trained on respectful interaction with beneficiaries, avoidance of abuse of power relations (i.e. PSEA), and cultural sensitive communication along with the Humanitarian Principles regarding humanity, impartiality, independence and neutrality.
