Project Summary

In 2020, dramatic changes in the operational context, including a shift in the conflict and the COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated the humanitarian crisis in Libya. COVID-19 forced the country to adopt stringent measures, including full lockdowns, roadblocks, partial or full closure of facilities (including schools and health facilities), and severe limitations in provision of services and availability of basic goods. The pandemic affected people’s mental health and psychosocial wellbeing, particularly women, children, migrants and refugees. COVID-19 exposed not only fears but also heightened distress and feelings of hopelessness and helplessness. Conflict-related displacement continued throughout 2020. The escalation of the conflict further exacerbated the threat from Explosive Hazards (EH). Parties to the conflict have increasingly relied on the use of heavy and explosive weaponry in densely populated settings. During the month of June alone, nearly 28,000 people were displaced following an intensification of the conflict, bringing the total number of IDPs in Libya to 392,241. 600,392 migrants and 46,247 refugees are currently in Libya. Violations of international human rights law and international humanitarian law against migrants and refugees remain a grave concern. Migrants and refugees continue to be subjected to torture, sexual violence, abduction for ransom, trafficking in persons, forced labor and unlawful killings throughout Libya.
The fragile economic situation, further hampered by the pandemic, has reduced individual and family income, and increased risks linked to livelihoods and security of tenure. The majority of tenants do not have written lease agreements, leaving them exposed to predatory behaviors by landlords, including arbitrary increases in rent and threats of, or actual evictions.
Women and girls have been disproportionally impacted by the economic crisis and pandemic. Increased food insecurity may place women, who have the primary responsibility to prepare food for the family, at heightened risk of intimate partner violence due to tensions in the household and being in confinement with their perpetrator. Female-headed households are at heightened risk of not being able to access services and meet basic needs. Migrant and refugee women, many of which have no recognized status and face language barriers and discrimination, have an increased risk of sexual exploitation and abuse. Movement restrictions, curfews, closures of services and limitations in social contacts led to a stark increase of GBV risks and risk of not receiving timely care. Reduced access to sexual and reproductive health information and services lead to increased risky behavior, especially among youth. Finally, the limited services available has reduced safe entry points for survivors to receive timely and quality care.
The breakdown of the electricity, water, oil, and cellular network systems affect the provision of and the accessibility to life saving services. This is particularly concerning for survivors of intimate partner violence due to the imminent danger of living with their perpetrators. The economic impact of the closures of businesses due to the conflict and the pandemic has resulted in a loss of livelihoods, especially for women often working in the informal sector. Female headed households are at high risk of not being able to cover their rent and thus face evictions and homelessness which is exacerbated for IDPs, migrants, and refugees. The stigma exacerbated by gender and ethnic discrimination leaves women and girls from migrant and refugee communities with limited financial options and renders them vulnerable to sexual exploitation and abuse, including requests for sexual favors by landlords, taxi drivers, and public service providers. The presence of humanitarian actors, even on a small scale, was in itself providing some form of protection by presence in the hard-to-reach areas and detention centers.

1 - Needs

The current situation of security instability in the country, the spread of covid-19 has led to an increase in the negative coping mechanisms within families including domestic violence, psychosocial and emotional abuse. These factors are also associated with higher risks of domestic violence, neglect and physical or sexual abuse of children and women. Conflict and displacement also increase the occurrence of GBV, especially sexual violence, domestic violence, and early marriage to meet basic survival needs. The most vulnerable IDPs are obliged to opt for collective shelter arrangement, subjecting women, girls and children in particular to an increased risk of GBV. GBV is rooted in gender and power inequalities that existed even prior to the conflict and humanitarian crisis, but have been exacerbating during the crisis. The protracted conflict, displacement, humanitarian crisis and import restriction has exacerbated individuals and households vulnerabilities and risks specifically for women and girls due to socially prescribed gender identities, entrenched gender discrimination and socio-economic inequalities and powerlessness experienced prior to the crisis. As a result, women and girls are not only experiencing violence; but multiple forms of GBV throughout their lifespan. Thus, women and girls especially displaced, migrants and refugees are disproportionally suffering not just from the byproduct of the conflict and humanitarian crisis but also as a target, both at the household and societal level, for violence, abuse and exploitation. UNFPA through its international and local partners will ensure to provide a comprehensive response to survivors of GBV. The approach encompasses coordination among key humanitarian sectors on provision of GBV services from coordination and referral to reintegration of survivors to the community, such that a holistic approach in responding to the current crisis is adopted. On the other hand, a survivor centered approach is adopted by way of saving and protecting the lives of survivors and at risk women and girls through the establishment of comprehensive, multi-sectoral GBV services. Their basic needs are unmet and this creates further vulnerabilities to multiple forms of GBV: UNFPA through the operated health facilities aims to expand the number of health facilities that are providing GBV services through partnerships and provision of kits, equipment and trainings on clinical management of rape. UNFPA aims to ensure a comprehensive provision of services to GBV survivors who require clinical care and emotional support that result from their experience of GBV, which includes, fear, resentment, blame and hopelessness. These clinical and psychological services will be complemented with facilitated referral to other needed services chosen by the GBV survivor. The GBV case management services will be provided at all levels by the health workers and targeted service providers trained in GBV case management, including GBV referral, GBV psychosocial counseling and GBV data management.During the conflict, women and girls need basic items that are equally life saving in order to interact and move comfortably in public spaces and access to services. Women and girls have distinct personal hygiene needs that are distinct to their gender and reproductive cycles/needs. Without access to culturally appropriate clothing and hygiene items. Therefore, in 2021 UNFPA as part of the RRM along with UNICEF, WFP and IOM that enables an initial first-line response are planning to respond to a total of 6,000 households where 17318 women and girls that could be affected by conflicts or natural disasters (2,000 households affected by the conflict in Sirte, 2,000 households affected by the conflict in the South and 2,000 affected households in the eastern area).

2 - Activities

The proposed project aims to ensure availability, accessibility and affordability of qualified multi-sectoral GBV services to the people in needs and ensure GBV survivor centered approach adopted. The Project will maintain the existing services and scale up the interventions of Women and girls safe spaces to ensure survivors access comprehensive GBV services without stigma and fear of retaliation from their families and communities. It is critical that women and girls especially in the current humanitarian context access safe spaces that enable them to obtain information, PSS, case management, life skills and livelihood interventions. Main activities are:Provide multi-sectoral GBV services (medical, psychological services and legal assistance) through tailored case management approach to GBV survivors.Provide Training to health providers and social workers to deliver quality care to survivors including CMR, GBViE case screening, case management, identification male survivors and to provide services that are responsive to specific needs of women, girls, men and boys from clinical and psychological perspective.Conduct community outreach and awareness sessions on GBV prevention and response, including service availabilityMaintain the existing Women and girls safe spaces and establish new ones.Maintain the national hotline to provide Psychosocial and legal counselling.Establish new and improve existing referral pathways for GBV services in the targeted locationsProvide structured PSS activities to women and girlsProvide life skills and recreational activities for women and girlsConduct safety audits to identify key barriers and risks for women and girlsProcure and distribute dignity kits to women and girls ( displaced, migrants and refugees) of reproductive age effected by the conflict to reach out to the needed services( health,..etc)

5 - Mitigation measures
5.1 - For each risk mentioned under 4 above, please describe the ways you plan to mitigate, prevent and/or address these.

4.6, 4.10,4.11, 4.13 To mitigate the risks, UNFPA and its partners will make sure to apply the key guiding principles to ensure we do no harm to survivors of GBVRIGHT TO SAFETY: Safety refers to both physical safety and security from harm, as well as to a sense of psychological and emotional safety for people who are highly distressed. It is important to consider the safety and security needs of each survivor, his/her family members and those providing care and support. RIGHT TO CONFIDENTIALITY: Confidentiality refers to the right of a person to have any information about them kept private unless they explicitly request it. It promotes safety, trust and empowerment. Maintaining confidentiality means not disclosing any information at any time to any party without the informed consent of the survivor. RIGHT TO DIGNITY AND SELF-DETERMINATION: All those who come into contact with survivors have a role to play in restoring dignity and self-determination that have been taken away by GBV. The survivors have the right to choose who they wish to disclose to and which services they want to access. RIGHT TO NON-DISCRIMINATION: All people have the right to the best possible assistance without unfair discrimination on the basis of gender, age, disability, race, language, religious or political beliefs, and societal levels.4.4 Putting in place the prevention measures of Covid-19 (PPE, social distance) as well as adopting the remote services to avoid such huge gatherings and close interactions.4.14 Partners will take the online trainings related to the PSEA and adhere to the UNFPA policies and procedures. UNFPA has its own procedures to mitigate the risks of sexual violence and exploitations and has already engaged local communities, national non-governmental organizations and has active women and girls safe spaces with trained community workers to implement the project with opportunities for capacity building and hands on practice sessions for their staff on the application ofglobal standards in programmatic and operational areas to prevent sexual exploitationand violence.4.20 Ensure staffs complies with safety and security controls; Continuous update and monitoring of local actors, mapping and strong networking with local communities for ownership and support to enable stronger and smoother communication channels with both state and non-state actors. Access information locally about movements/ activities of various groups. Mandatory field security trainings for all staff before going to the field. Utilization of partners’ experience in conflict prevention, conflict analysis and the implementation of the Do No Harm approach. 4.22 Through the GBV SS, UNFPA will ensure updating regularly the referral pathway with the existing available, accessible and affordable services in the targeted locations with different actors.

6.2 - Other accountability mechanisms

UNFPA Libya country programme stakeholders and in particularly beneficiaries play an active role in the decision-making process in regards to the planning, implementing, and evaluating of UNFPA’s activities. This is done through conducting focus group discussion with the GBV survivors in the women and girls safe space and other facilities. UNFPA will use beneficiary feedback mechanism which includes; for health facilities and providers-client feedback forms and key informant interviews will be conducted. UNFPA will also roll out the client feedback forms in the safe spaces and other service points and conduct focus group discussions and key informant interview with survivors and case workers. The submissions and report will be used to produce quarterly analysis, risks analysis and identify needs and demands. The programme will support the development of self-protection capacities and aim at improving beneficiaries’ daily lives through improved formal and informal protection mechanisms as well as life skills and livelihood interventions. This is considered the most effective way of tackling gender inequality as the root cause of GBV. At the outset, the programme will set-up a beneficiary feedback mechanism through which affected populations will be able to inform of the challenges that the programme may cause or will be face and evaluate the adequacy of programme interventions. This information will be utilized to guide work planning and implementation. Implementer's will be made accountable to address beneficiaries’ concerns and complaints within a limited time-frame and report on (or to the broader public, if complaints are anonymous) on how they have addressed. Beneficiaries’ feedback is a key indicators to measure performance under this programme. In addition, the project will rely on the CFM (based on the SOPs validated with WFP and the ETS) as a second accountability mechanism and ensure functionality and use of the establish GBV referral pathway.
