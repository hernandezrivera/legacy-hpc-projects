Project Summary

According to Humanitarian Need Overview 2019, it is estimated that 5.8 million children and youth from pre-school to secondary school-age (in and out of school) and 245,000 education personnel are in need of education assistance inside Syria. A 2018 assessment of 226 camps in Aleppo and Idlib governorates found that 73 percent of camps and settlements have no education services at all, showing no improvement from 2017 (Ministry of Education 2018).
A statistical report in HNO Draft 2019 indicated that 853,292 school-age children (455,607 boys and 397,681 girls) in Idlib and 1,304,139 school-age children (681,078 boys and 623,064 girls) in Aleppo are in need of educational assistance. In addition to 35,554 (18,984 males and 16,570 females), education personnel in Idlib and 54,339 (28,378 males and 25,961 females) education staff in Aleppo are in need of educational assistance.
Furthermore, the education system inside Syria is overburdened and overstretched to the extent that schools and learning environments are unsafe, overcrowded and under-resourced; lack of teaching and learning materials; poor quality teaching; and damaged education infrastructure.
The need for textbooks and stationery is urgent. Children in most areas must share a textbook while others have to reuse second-hand textbooks from upper-grade levels.
According to the Assistance Coordination Unit (ACU) November 2018 report, the shortage of educational materials, books and stationery was the highest ranked difficulty within the schools of six out of eight assessed clusters. The Key Informants (KIs) reported that teachers have to write full lessons on the chalkboard because of the acute shortage of textbooks, and students cannot copy these lessons on their notebooks because of the limited time. High fuel prices added an additional burden on schools’ administrations in terms of providing fuel, based on an education needs assessment report conducted by Big Heart Foundation in Jisr-Ash-Shugur and Harim, there is a strong need for fuel for heating and for generating electricity.
As a response to the aforementioned factors, Rahma Relief Foundation (RRF) is seeking to provide three schools with educational assistance to enhance the schools’ performance in terms of educational quality for the duration of nine months, the assistance includes: IT equipment, learning materials, incentives to students, staff’s salaries, and heating fuel.

Needs

According to the humanitarian need overview 2019, 2.1 million, over one third, of Syrian children are out-of-school and 1.3 million children are at risk of dropping out. 5.85 million school-aged children and about 245,000 education personnel are in need of education assistance inside Syria, 61 percent of whom are in acute and immediate need. Moreover, the shift of control in Central and South Syria in early and mid-2018 and increasing hostilities in Northwest Syria in September 2018 caused new waves of civilian displacement into Idleb and Aleppo governates. Existing education capacity in the areas is already, and the ability of schools to absorb more IDPs into the education system is very limited. Some schools operate in a double shift (morning and afternoon classes) to accommodate the large influx of displaced children. This reduces the teaching-learning hours per day, adversely affecting learning outcomes for IDPs and host community residents alike.Based on (North-West Syria Population Overview REACH Resource Centre CCCM, Mar 2018), the largest estimated number of IDPs among assessed communities was reported for Dana subdistrict (403,430) in northern Idleb governorate, followed by A’zaz (167,463), in Aleppo governorate. At a community level, the largest IDP populations were reported in Atma (126,071).The latest dashboard shared by the Education cluster (Jan 2019) indicated that both Azaz and Dana sub-districts share the same severity which indicates to four points on the severity scale. Badama sub-district showed five points on the severity scale as well.In order to get a better understanding of the situation in these locations, Rahma Relief Foundation team tracked the situation of some schools inside Atma, Khirbet Al-Joz and Suran communities and conducted a rapid needs assessment to capture the situation of the students and teachers and highlight their education needs.According to the rapid needs assessment conducted by Rahma Relief foundation team in Albil camp, Suran, Azaz, Aleppo, there is only one available school in the camp. It consists of four caravans and it is only for children aged 6-11 years old. The lack of learning spaces and the missing school items are the main reasons for non-attending schools at all age at the camp. Rahma Relief foundation Also conducted rapid needs assessment in Atma School for girl, Atma, Dana, Idleb. Atma school for girls is the only school in the area that provides education to high school-aged girls.According to the school psychosocial counselor, the biggest problem they face is school absenteeism. “Learning environment is safe but not accessible to all girls, girls who are living near the school have higher attendance rates. The Security situation has a particular impact to the girls’ attendance, because the school is far away from some girls' homes. Parents have also been reluctant to send their girls to school, worrying about their safety.”In addition, there is a strong need for heating fuel, salaries, teaching and learning materials to improve the access of education for girls in Atma village.The Assistance Coordination Unit report, conducted in Northern Syria camps Nov 2018 (including Salah al Aldin camp in Kherbet al joz Jisr Al shagor, Idleb) showed that, all assessed schools in Northern Syria camps are temporary educational places and lack the basic components for proper educational process. These schools were established as a quick solution to prevent the dropout of IDPs students within the camps. Rahma Relief foundation’ needs assessment disaggregated the data by gender and age as follows:1- Atma School: 550 Girls between ages 11-18 and 28 female education personnel.2- Baraem Almustakbal in Salah al Aldin camp: 148 girls and 155 boys between ages 6-11, 18 females and 8 males education personnel.3- Albil Camp School:175 girls and 200 boys between ages 6-11, 8 females and 11 males education personnel.

Implementation of activities

The project will be achieved by implementing the following activities: 1- Atma Schools for girls (Level 5-12)a. Providing the classrooms with IT equipment such as computers, projector...etc.b. Providing incentives to students such as presents, Certificates of Appreciation, graduation’s celebration.c. Supporting the school’s staff with salaries.d. Providing heating fuel. 2- Baraem Almustakbal and Albil Camp School (Level 1-5)a. Providing incentives to students such as presents, Certificates of Appreciation, graduation’s celebration.b. Supporting the school’s staff with salaries and teaching materials.c. Providing heating fuel. d. School’s rehabilitation (Paint job, Electricity, water, Fence)e. Providing Schools’ furniture (Desks, tables, whiteboard, etc...)f. Child Safe Spaces Before initiating the implementation process, Rahma Relief Foundation will conduct a comprehensive educational assessment to recognize other implementing partners that are working in the same field. Furthermore, the foundation will establish a bilateral communication with the cluster to fill any potential service gaps and prevent any duplications.During the project implementation, Rahma Relief Foundations' staff will ensure compliance on Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP) and on Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA). Under the proposed project, RRF will remain accountable to affected populations through open dialogue and feedback/complaint mechanisms provided to these populations; including:- The provision of complaint boxes that can be seen and accessed easily,- Boxes will be checked periodically, and the response will be generated within a week (except urgent complaint)- Using social media and mobile applications to receive complaints from beneficiaries.- collecting gender and age-disaggregated data from project implementation and impact, which will be analyzed and routinely reported on while project activities are monitored for improvements in self-reliance as well as beneficiary satisfaction and resilience of both women and men.
