Project Summary

In 2025, UNICEF will provide a comprehensive CP programme to prevent and respond to all forms of violence, abuse, and exploitation of children. UNICEF will reach 274,846 individuals, of whom 182,666 children and 92,180 adults. Girls and women will constitute 51%, and persons with disabilities 4.6 % of total. UNICEF will target Rohingya refugee camps and host communities – covering 24 camps in accordance with Rationalization and CPSS coverage analysis – while making strategic investments in strengthening government-led systems towards a public health approach to protect children at scale across the H-D nexus. Where possible, UNICEF will strive for Localization - building capacities of government stakeholders and investing in national partnerships. UNICEF has robust and well-developed government partnerships in Bangladesh, which will be leveraged for the benefit of all children in Cox’s Bazar including Rohingya children. UNICEF will advocate for greater government presence in JRP areas, incl. expansion of MoWCA Accelerating Protection of Children programme, MoYS Sports for Development initiatives, and better justice outcomes for children. This will complement the expanding coverage of DSS across the refugee context. UNICEF will continue to accelerate Birth Registration through BDRIS for all Bangladeshi children in host communities. UNICEF will develop a reliable continuum of care for children. We will advance equitable access to child-friendly justice, regardless of citizenship. Informed by UNICEF’s Justice Study, UNICEF will improve coordination between judges, law enforcement agencies, probation officers, social workers, and community groups. We will strengthen the social service workforce, operationalizing the MoU with DSS across all 31 camps. UNICEF will invest in social- and caseworkers to provide quality case management using interagency tools and CPIMS+. A longer-term vision will be brought through support to CBCPCs, and volunteers, linked to up-to-date referral pathways and specialized services. Attention will be given to addressing negative social norms and harmful practices, taking learnings from UNICEF’s Study on VAC, and seeking to address root causes and drivers of harm. UNICEF will have NGO partnerships in 24 camps with a network of at minimum 77 MPCs as service hubs for group activities and outreach. MPCs will allow delivery of essential lifesaving services for children and caregivers including Case Management, age-appropriate Psychosocial support, Life Skills-based learning for 10–18-year-olds, and Positive Parenting. Adolescents will benefit from dedicated adolescent clubs with access to professional guidance on topics including but not limited to Community Mobilization, Social Cohesion, and Sexual & Reproductive Health. MPCs will also facilitate a joint approach to service provision with Education and other sectors. UNICEF is lead agency for the Child Rights Monitoring Mechanism for serious violations in the situation of armed violence, gathering reliable, accurate, and timely information for more targeted prevention and response. In linkage, UNICEF will contribute to the Protection Sector Peace & Security Campaign to address violence in the camps. UNICEF will ensure an equitable, survivor-centered approach that recognizes the specific needs of girls, adolescents, and children with disabilities. UNICEF’s project will align with the CP Minimum Standards and UN Guidelines on Alternative Care, and the principles of Non-Discrimination, Child Participation, Do No Harm, and the Best Interests of the Child. UNICEF is lead agency for the CP AoR and is a key member of the SAGs for Protection, CPSS, and GBVSS. UNICEF sits on the working groups for Case Management, GiHA, PSEA, Age & Disability, and others. UNICEF set up and spearheads the CRMM, and co-chairs the Country WG at national level. UNICEF, partners, and personnel will uphold the strictest adherence to PSEA and Safeguarding across the project.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

Bangladesh faces acute and protracted humanitarian emergencies, with the Rohingya refugee crisis and the severe impacts of climate change taking precedence. Nearly 1 million refugees reside in 33 highly congested camps across Ukhiya and Teknaf, with children making up 52% of the refugee population. The high levels of poverty, limited access to basic services, and overcrowded living conditions exacerbate child protection risks. Conditions in the refugee camps expose children and women to all forms of violence, abuse, and exploitation including GBV, child marriage, child labor together with widespread mental health and psychosocial. As per CPSS reports, CRMM and Joint Protection Monitoring Reports the main child protection issues in the camps in the 1st and 2nd quarter of 2024 are child trafficking, child recruitment and use, and abduction, which are on the rise. An escalation of armed violence, with armed groups from Myanmar increasingly active on the Bangladesh side of the border. The constant threat of violence and recruitment has forced many to remain in hiding or restrict their movement, limiting their access to essential services, including psychosocial support, education and health care. Parents and caregivers ongoing fears for their children's safety, reflect a deep-seated need for improved security and protection mechanisms across the camps.Case management trend analysis from CPIMS+ as of October 2024 reported 31.86% children being neglected, 11.57% affected by physical distress, 10.48% affected by physical abuse, 7.84% experienced emotional and physical abuse, 7.58% engaged in child labor, 4.53 % child marriage, 5.49% children with disabilities. CPSM KIIs informs 33.95% adult respondents were aware of child marriage incidents in their area. 65.57% adults stated child labor is a major barrier preventing children from attending school. Child-headed households are particularly affected, with 37.68% respondents stating that such children take on adult responsibilities in the absence of parents. 51.59% highlighted that children in households with specific needs are engaged in labor. 69.15% adults reported incidents of child neglect. These having dire consequences as 24.6% informants reported children imitated violent behavior, 22.7% observed school absenteeism or diminished seriousness in studies, and 16.5% noted low self-esteem. The ISNA 2024 also reports in the Host community 33.32 % children reported signs of distress; 13.0% left the house to seek employment, 3.8% are working to contribute to HH income. These highlights the importance of continuum of child protection responses and care to address an increasing trend of child protection concerns both in the refugee camps and host community. The justice system in line with the Children’s Act 2013 needs to be child-friendly with capacity building in operationalize diversion and strengthen the monitoring of children as they move through the justice system. Support in strengthening Children’s Courts, Child Welfare Boards, Child Affairs Desks at police stations for strengthened coordination is vital. DSS needs technical support in strengthen role as the government lead agency in dealing with the protection concerns including responses for children. Social Service workforce needs capacity building on case management to support children access to continuum of care. Advocacy and technical support to the Officer of the Registrar General and District Administration in continued acceleration of registrations, including capacity building of local government unit on using BDRIS supported with inter departmental collaboration much need.The vulnerabilities are exacerbated by climate-related natural hazards with seasonal monsoon, cyclones followed by heavy rainfall, flooding, and landslides. These conditions putting immense strain on households increasing stress, causing the break-up of family units, and leading to adoption of negative coping mechanisms.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

UNICEF incorporates the protection principles of safety and dignity, avoiding causing harm, ensuring meaningful access, upholding accountability, promoting participation, and fostering empowerment, in planning and implementation of the programme for children including adolescents, families, and members of the community in the Rohingya refugee camps and host communities. Through service delivery nodes (Multipurpose Centres, Adolescent Clubs, etc.) and ensuring robust outreach to the most vulnerable following do no harm and inclusion of diversity, the project will ensure access to assistance and services without discrimination or barrier. Embedded within project design is an information and feedback mechanism through which the children, adolescents, parents, caregivers including the community people can demand accountability for the adequacy of interventions, and for UNICEF and implementing partners to address concerns and complaints. Children and adolescents’ participation are promoted through creating child friendly environment and participatory approaches to identify safe and unsafe places, along with the underlying reasons for these designations. Their views and recommendations in developing strategies for mitigating risks, contributing to a safer environment are acknowledged as pivotal.

2.3 - Age & Disability

UNICEF will ensure all child protection facilities and services are accessible to all target groups including person with disabilities. UNICEF will exercise the Washington Group's set of questions to inform project design and planningto ensure the rights and inclusivity of person with disabilities is fully realised.UNICEF will promote and build the capacities of staffs and volunteers on tools and skills on disability and inclusion. Capacity building training modules have been developed by Handicap International. The capacity build modules introduce types of disability, and barriers to overcoming disability. They provide guidance on teacher-learning strategies and classroom management, covering isssues of Access, Acceptance, Active Participation, and Achievement.UNICEF will strengthen community outreach, adolescent clubs, and parents groups within the community to encourage and promote participation and access to activities and services for children including adolescents. All interventions will be tailored to ensure that all beneficiaries can actively engage with our services and interventions and derive maximum benefit from them.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

UNICEF will ensure that AAP feedback and response mechanisms are established according to the AAP principles of participation, inclusion, communication, and transparency, at all stages of the programme cycle. UNICEF will ensure all staff, volunteers, children including adolescents, caregivers, and members of the community have access to reliable and timely information on organizational procedures, structures and processes on affected population capturing age, gender and specific groups at risk of discrimination and vulnerable. UNICEF and partners will take measures to reduce barriers so that children of all age groups and those who are at risk of discrimination are participating in planning, implementation and monitoring process while implementing the projects by the partners. UNICEF will support partners to strengthen organizational child safeguarding policy through conduct risk assessment and increase staff capacity to take appropriate measures. Also engage the community and target groups to participate throughout needs assessment, programme planning and implementation using standard assessment tools while inclusion of Children with disabilities and their caregivers.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

UNICEF upholds stringent standards and protocols related to Child Safeguarding and the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA). UNICEF has zero tolerance for sexual exploitation & abuse (SEA) and sexual harassment (SH) and actively advocates for this principle across the organization, IPs, vendors, service providers & beneficiaries. The organization is also committed to preventing SEA and providing immediate support to victims. Internally, UNICEF champions an organizational culture that promotes a safe, respectful, inclusive, and enabling working environment that is free from sexual exploitation and abuse. UNICEF organizes regular PSEA and child safeguarding training for its staff. UNICEF’s Implementing partners undergo thorough assessments to ensure a high level of adherence to these standards, with the requirement to achieve a low-risk rating. Furthermore, UNICEF plays a pivotal role as a key member of the PSEA Network. UNICEF is committed to developing appropriate Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) materials on PSEA and child safeguarding to raise awareness and sensitize the affected population about the Complaint and Feedback Mechanism. All partners are expected to have multiple feedback and reporting mechanisms in place to introduce feedback and response mechanisms into their activities. These may include tools such as U-report, complaint boxes, hotline numbers for PSEA and child safeguarding, and the assignment of an Accountability of the Affected Population (AAP) focal person where necessary. UNICEF delivers an annual refresher training to its partners and monitors their performance to cascade the training to the national staff, volunteers, community leaders, and members of all communities, focusing on the safe identification and confidential referral of cases. UNICEF also ensures that all its partners are members of the PSEA network and have policies in place on child safeguarding, whistle blowing and PSEA.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Yes. A central tenet of UNICEF's work is to ensure preparedness for, and adaptability to, climate change and weather-related events. This is primarily achieved through a systems-strengthening approach, combined with community-based child protection, both of which complement and mutually reinforce each other. The aim is to reduce reliance on humanitarian service provision over time. This approach enables the programme to enhance the resilience of children, caregivers, and communities to climate related shocks and contributes to Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) with a child protection focus. UNICEF is committed to ensuring adequate preparedness and response plans that pre-empt the impact of natural disasters, including monsoons, cyclones, and other hazards. UNICEF will continue to mainstream the programme interventions in its emergency preparedness and response plans through specialized capacity building for the community, contributory stocks of emergency kits, awareness raising activities, enhanced case management and follow-up services during the emergency. UNICEF will ensure partners having plans for child protection prevention and response for the emergency preparedness and responses. Identify and build capacity of CP focal points and volunteers on e.g., child protection in emergencies, preparedness and response, rapid child protection assessment, PFA. Undertake CP facility risk assessments to inform development of mitigation plans and response pre-disaster and during disasters. Ensure, partners will follow CMTWG referral pathways for family tracing and rapid reunification. Ensure UASCs are prioritized and receive expedited access to emergency services including PSS.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

Yes. All activities are guided by UNICEF’s Emergency Preparedness and Response (EPR) plan, which is reviewed and updated annually. This plan includes sector-specific guidance and aligns with existing emergency coordination structures in Cox’s Bazar. Each partnership includes partner project documents with a specific budget line as contingency funds and pre-positioned items in the warehouses e.g. recreational kits , family kits , dignity kits as part of the emergency preparedness and response strategy, ensuring the following: Children including adolescents, caregivers, and community members, including at-risk groups, are actively involved in community-level preparedness activities, program design, and the targeted beneficiaries are reached with emergency response; Quality, coordinated, survivor-centered, and age-appropriate response services are promptly available and accessible during emergencies, including mobile outreach services as needed; prevention activities are implemented to mitigate risks; Child protection projects integrate actions to address and reduce GBV risks; and community groups, along with partner staff and volunteers are capacitated and empowered to support the community during any emergency. Partners’ staff are trained on PFA and PSS to respond to emergency.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

Yes. The project will take a socio-ecological approach that recognizes the roles of children, including adolescents and youth, in fostering a protective environment and driving change toward gender equality. Specifically designed to address the needs of children, adolescents, and youth, the project emphasizes gender equity across all aspects. This includes acknowledging the unique protection challenges faced by adolescents and youth, particularly the issue of GBV.To meet the needs of children including adolescents and youth, the project will continue to utilize dedicated Safe Spaces for Women and Girls, adolescent clubs, and multi-purpose centers to provide tailored case management, psychosocial support, and skills development services, delivered by trained professionals. Adolescents will also benefit from life skills-based learning, which includes guidance on Adolescent Sexual and Reproductive Health (ASRH), pre-vocational training, social cohesion, resilience-building, sports, and engaging recreational activities. All activities designed for adolescents will be gender-sensitive and inclusive, providing both support and education. This support will empower adolescents as agents of positive change within their families and communities.
