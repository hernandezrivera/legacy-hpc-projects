Project Summary

The program seeks to respond to the prolonged humanitarian crisis affecting the Palestinian population under occupation, exacerbated by the COVID-19 crisis, by contributing to the protection of their rights and dignity in accordance with IHL and IHRL, and with a cross-cutting focus on gender equity and community participation. The program is to be implemented by the Action Group for Organised Local Response (Action Group) and other partners of the protection cluster: a group of civil society organisations that seeks to promote the localisation of humanitarian response and to respond to the multiple needs and demands of Palestinian communities in an organised manner, by working on their respective sectoral and geographical areas of expertise. The program as a whole is multi-sectoral and includes actions linked to protection, with a special emphasis in gender-based violence, WASH, livelihoods, education, mental health and advocacy, which also targets populations affected by the impact of COVID-19 in areas throughout the West Bank. While the program is multi-sectoral, it has been divided in sectoral projects to facilitate the management of such a comprehensive program. In this sense, the present project includes protection and WASH-related activities and the whole program is in line with the strategic objectives (SOs) of both the Protection and WASH Clusters. It includes 1 ) Increased respect for IHL and IHRL and accountability for violations; 2) Putting in place protection response mechanisms such as Community Protection Committees or the provision of protection services, in order to prevent and mitigate the effects of the occupation and conflict-related violence, like settler violence, property damage and confiscation, restrictions, violations against children and gender-based violence; 3) Meeting the basic needs of vulnerable Palestinians living under occupation by providing them with quality basic services and improving their access to resources, such as water and sanitation facilities; and 4) Promoting the capacity of vulnerable Palestinians to cope with and overcome protracted crisis, including from environmental threats, like waste pollution. The population targeted under the program is highly vulnerable to IHL/IHRL violations due to the presence of their communities in Area C and East Jerusalem and to their proximity to settlements, military bases and Israeli military checkpoints. The program pays special attention to the different vulnerabilities, needs and capacities of women, men, girls and boys. Specific target groups include children affected by the conflict, women affected by GBV, people with disabilities and others vulnerable to conflict related violence, such as agricultural workers and youth.

1 - Needs

The current program is based on different multi-sectoral assessments done by the Action Group and other protection cluster partners:3 conducted in 15 communities of the Governorates of Qalqiliya and Salfit in 2017, 2018 and 2019-20, 1 in 6 communities of East Jerusalem in 2019, 1 done during 2020 to analyse the needs caused by the COVID-19 crisis, in Hebron, Bethlehem, Qalqiliya and Salfit, and a 1 done in 2021 in 17 communities of the Salfit, Bethlehem, Hebron and Jericho, and the HNO. These assessments cover a population of 195,152 people (93,977 women/girls, 101,175 men/boys; 7,044 people aged over 65 years, and around 4,098 people with intellectual and/or physical disabilities (1,974 women/girls, 2,125 men/boys)). Through focus group, more than 600 people from the communities participated in the assessments (50% women). Also specific focus groups were conducted to incorporate a gender and child protection perspective (161 women,149 children (73 girls, 76 boys) participating). The geographical scope of the project responds to the capacity of the Action Group to deploy the already built and ongoing mechanism they constitute in the West Bank to address the most urgent humanitarian needs. These assessments found that the protection of the rights of the target population in accordance with IHRL and IHL is affected by various factors (worsened as a result of the COVID-19 crisis): to tackle these needs we have developed a multisectoral approach that includes, among others, protection and WASH responses. The main problems identified are:(1) The difficulties people face to practice their agricultural livelihoods as a result of confiscation and isolation of land and harassment of settlers and the ISF: the assessment found that agricultural land and resources are being abandoned progressively in the target communities and the appropriation of the land by settlers and Israeli authorities and the increase in the risk of confiscation. Families are losing the ability to continue with their traditional agricultural and livestock activities. The western communities are more affected by the Wall and the Seam Zone, leaving lands isolated on the other side with the consequent difficulty of access. In the target communities around 40% of the lands have been already confiscated or are at risk of confiscation due to the construction of the Separation Wall or their presence in Area C (ARIJ).(2) Lack of accountability, isolation from HR offenders and lack of legal support: Incidents related to settler violence (increased during the COVID-19 crisis and the recent escalations), which lead the population to take long and costly legal actions to protect their property and themselves. Lacking resources and support, the main response strategy of families is to monitor these violations, often insufficient and unable to provide necessary evidence for legal defence procedures.(3) Lack of protection and support services for GBV survivors: There are some service providers but physical, economic and cultural issues to access them, such as stigma, lack of time for women, lack of resources or lack of transportation. 19,000 women and girls are estimated to be suffering violence in the target communities (PCBS 2019 and AG assessments). Women’s organizations also reported an increase in the incidence of GBV and a 10% rise in calls to hot lines during COVID-19 pandemic (UN Women 2019). Other protection-related problems are: Cultural norms, frustration and stress exacerbated by occupation; GBV which has its root causes in unequal power relations between women and men.(4) Movement restrictions imposed by Israel: Mobility is limited by the multiple controls, barriers, checkpoints, settlements, etc., which prevent the population from accessing their livelihoods, and basic services such as education or health.(5) Limited access to MHPSS: The main barriers to accessing MHPSS are stigma, unavailable services, economic access and lack of qualified personnel. Teachers are generally unable to address these problems due to a lack of capacities and motivation, so they are in need to improve their knowledge and awareness in psychosocial distress and GBV. It is estimated that around 55% of children in the West Bank are affected by some kind of physical and psychological violence, affecting 70,805 children (34,421 girls, 36,385 boys) in the target communities.(6) Social stigma regarding mental illness: The main barrier to accessing support services is social stigma. Likewise, there are many members of the community who deny that there is violence against women, boys and girls. In relation to the WASH needs identified in through the HNO and the different assessments conducted by the AG, the main problems are:(7) The deficient infrastructural system of the WASH facilities in the West Bank, with 620,000 people affected by poor or deteriorated connection to the water system (7,250 people in the target communities) and 290,000 households by a lack of sewer networks (840 households in the target communities) (MSNA assessment survey 2021). This problem is also linked to the dependence of Israeli water companies for the supply of the water, which produces continuous shutdowns and a lack of access to water;8) the expansion of the settlements particularly affect the vast majority of the communities included in this program. There is continuous dumping of waste from the settlements to the agricultural lands and water sources of these communities to safe water.(9) There are schools with insufficient WASH facilities(primarily toilets, drinking and handwashing stations) to meet the standards set up by the Ministries of Education and Health.(10) All the above problematics, along with the inexistence of efficient waste management policies and the continuous demolition of sanitation facilities by the Israeli authorities, have exacerbated the sanitary crisis of COVID-19, with overcrowding facilities and undermined hygiene practices, increasing infection risk

2 - Activities

The program combines activities that include response to protection problems and the reduction of WASH-related needs. Regarding protection, it relates to the provision of services for GBV survivors and vulnerable groups, in a sustainable way (responsive and remedial) involving the community and the provision of legal support:*Psychosocial support for 726 women survivors of GBV and/or other women showing symptoms of psychosocial distress.*Psychosocial support for 159 men ex detainees and/or other men showing symptoms of psychosocial distress.*Legal awareness, counselling, assistance and representation in courts and consultation on HLP and family law, for 92 and 30 women respectively, with a special focus on survivors of GBV.*Legal awareness sessions for survivors of GBV or at risk of GBV, for 370 women.*Awareness sessions with 300 teachers and 404 parents on addressing the specific psychosocial situation and needs of girls and boys showing signs of psychosocial distress (PSCCW coordinates these actions with the Directorate of Education and schools in the target communities).*Awareness sessions on GBV and women’s rights for religious leaders in the community (35 men/35 women).*Conditional cash assistance for the economic enablement of 10 young female GBV survivors through the development of 10 initiatives.*Conditional cash assistance for the incorporation of 25 young women GBV survivors into the labour market. The program also involves the promotion of the community-based protection approach in the target communities:*The participatory design of Community Protection Response Plans (CPRP) and related tools, based on participatory workshops with key stakeholder groups (women, children, youth, men and PWD) for the CPRP;*Central workshops, composed by different groups of the community to collectively identify and prioritise risks and responses to be included in the CPRP.*Strengthening of Community Protection Committees (CPC) in 5 communities, through trainings on the role of women in conflict resolution and peace-making as per UN Resolution 1325; SGBV; the rights of the PWD and other special vulnerabilities; WRLHP; and child protection, among other topics. Through this standardized process, 100 members (50% women) of the communities increase their knowledge and capacities to report IHRL/IHL violations and access legal assistance for vulnerable and risk-threatened groups. The reinforcement of the CPC are the core protective mechanism for the communities. PSCCW (engaged with the Protection cluster and the GBV working group) is responsible for the implementation of all the activities mentioned. They have offices in Salfit and Bethlehem for the implementation and monitoring of the program. Another pillar of the advocacy strategy embraces the monitoring of IHL/IHRL violations and the legal aid for the victims of HR violations. The activities are: Yesh Din (Legal Task Force):*Accompaniment of victims to file complaints with the Israeli police and coordinate access to police stations and make complaints to the IDF (legal assistance to individuals at court).*Registration of testimonies of victims affected by crimes against property, violent crimes, land confiscation or environmental damage committed by Israeli settlers/military.*Registration of the cases through GIS by type of incident and geographic location, and with data disaggregated by gender.*Use of data from registered cases for advocacy. BIMKOM:*Legal and technical assistance to Palestinian communities threatened by demolition orders, confiscations… issued by the ICA through the creation of a report to present an objection to the ICA in coordination with the municipalities of the affected communities. B´Tselem (protection cluster)*Monitoring, documentation and dissemination of violations related to IHL/IHRL violations committed by Israel against the Palestinian population under occupation, targeting local and international stakeholders; Advocacy campaign on settlements directed at responsibility holders.*Monitoring and updating of data on Israeli settlements and outposts and their links to IHL/IHRL violations.*Presentations, workshops and field visits in EJ and WB.Regarding the activities related to WASH needs, the program is focused on *The rehabilitation of 2 community reservoirs (22,373 people);*The installation and/or rehabilitation of 38 household water cisterns in 5 communities (355 people);*The rehabilitation and construction of 9,223 meters of water supply network in 9 communities, in addition to 65 house connections in 3 of the previous communities (1,478 people);*The construction of 1 septic tank in 1 community (443 people) and the rehabilitation of 1 septic tank (578 people);*The construction of rainwater drainage systems in 1 community to reduce the risk to the population, in addition to reduce pollution and flooding (20,000 people).*The rehabilitation of the WASH facilities in 2 schools of 2 different communities, in order to improve access of pupils, people with disabilities (PWDs) and teachers to sufficient, safe, hygienic and dignified WASH services (400 people);*The rehabilitation of 1,600 meters of the sewer collection network in 5 communities (4,042 people);*The provision of 21 household latrines in 4 communities, in addition to the provision of household hygiene kits to the people of 3 of these communities (530 people);*The provision of 72 domestic solid waste containers in 4 communities (1,815 people). These above-mentioned activities will be implemented by our partner, the Palestinian Hydrology Group (PHG, engaged in the WASH and FSS clusters), in full coordination with the main stakeholders (PWA, Governorates, municipalities of the different communities and so on). They have offices in Nablus and Ramallah for the implementation and monitoring of the program. ACPP, PSCCW and PHG are partners and have fully coordinated in order not to duplicate the geographical locations targeted in our corresponding HRP projects.

Yes

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

As mentioned above, the feedback received by the Local Committee translates into a different response depending on the tasks of each organization. In the case of the implementing partners, there is a Technical Committee that meets every month to discuss the implementation and challenges; therefore, feedback and complaints are discussed here in order to find the best response and solutions. Sometimes, adjustments for the activities are required, looking out for new and improved ways to approach the activities. Other times, the implementing partners need to coordinate to develop more adequate tools, surveys, questionnaires, templates, timelines, etc. Some challenges are difficult to deal with such as those related to cultural and traditional issues that need time, sensitivity and patience to address and see the impact, so in these cases they increase the follow up of the challenge being mindful and proceeding with a clear road map. In all cases, feedback and complaints are noted, filed and used to improve the design of the project. In addition, There are established referral mechanisms within the programme, mainly through the CPCs. These community based groups have among their tasks the referral of cases of different violations of HR to different actors (municipalities, governorates, INGOs, NGOs, hospitals, etc.). This referral list is received by the members of the CPC when they sign the code of conduct and it is updated regularly by the Technical Committee of the Action Group. In addition, within the AG there is a constant communication between the partners to refer different cases to expert organisations, in case the AG members don’t have the capacity. Specifically regarding GBV cases, PSCCW is part of the GBV sub-cluster and Al Muntada and in constant collaboration with the member organisations if there is the need of referral.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

The intervention has been designed in a way that responds to the needs of the target communities and taking into account existing and emerging risks that may occur during the implementation. In order to meet the "do no harm" principle, the intervention remains sensitive to the cultural reality of the targeted communities and puts the focus on local capacities. The first measure is to rely on the implementing partners and their deep understanding of the communities and their reality. They have also reached a trusted environment after many years as reference organisations in this context. This approach allows the activities to be sensitive and take into account all the issues which may exacerbate the conflict. One example of this is the relevance of engaging the Municipalities with the strengthened Community Protection Committees and the selection of the target population of the activities, in addition to the care that must be taken when workshops are planned with participation of both women and men, in some communities. Activities such as MHPSS require special discretion to be effective and avoid harming the participants as stigma and social pressure remains present. Visibility and communication are also an essential part to ensure compliance with the "do no harm" principle as far as the political context of the conflict compels us to be very careful in terms of the level of visibility we give to our work. This is particularly relevant when implementing advocacy activities, legal assistance and denouncing rights violations. As mentioned in section 8.6, feedback and complaints are gathered and not only answered and addressed but discussed among the implementing partners (maintaining the confidentiality of the persons involved) in order to assess the context, potential social tensions and over-expectations that may prejudice the population. In this sense, the participation of the target population and the communities stands out as a key element not only for the relevance and ownership, but also as a way to minimize potential risks.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

The target population is chosen through a precise selection process based on humanitarian needs and vulnerability criteria. Assistance is delivered to those most in need and greatest situation of vulnerability. Responsive activities that provide services directly to those who are most vulnerable start with a careful planning to make sure that they will be the ones that will receive assistance in an equal manner. To this end, each respective implementing partner makes sure the coordination with reference organizations such as women’s community-based organizations, local institutions (municipalities, sectorial ministries...) and any other sector-related actors in the scope of intervention. Each case should be dealt with according to its specificities, thus avoiding any favourable treatment. Here again the expertise of the implementing partners ensures professionalism and the use of adequate and standardized criteria. This project in particular addresses women affected by GBV, regardless of age, as well as men, mainly youth, affected by detention and/or imprisonment. Legal support and assistance, as well as the WASH-related activities, will be provided to those people identified by the partners that meet specific vulnerability criteria. This standardised vulnerability criteria has been developed for the target on for the target population of the different activities through years of joint work and lessons learned by the Action Group. The fact that the target areas include Area C and East Jerusalem (though cases from other areas may be addressed if they meet the same vulnerability criteria), is indicative of the aim of addressing the most in-need cases. Awareness raising and advocacy do not provide services directly but put the focus on the most vulnerable, seeking to improve their situation and holding duty bearers to account for ensuring the rights and well-being of those under their responsibility.

In terms of PSEA, ACPP has a written Code of Conduct containing provisions promoting ethical behaviour, applicable to Board, management, staff, partner organisations and sub-contracted organisations. The CoC clearly identifies (a) the types of prohibited conduct and clearly states that staff must refrain from any acts of misconduct; (b) states the consequences of engaging in conduct that breaches this policy; and (c) includes a statement of receipt and an understanding section that staff are requested to sign and date. This CoC is annexed to all agreements signed with implementing partners and sub-contracted organisations. Annexes to the CoC include protocols on moral and sexual harassment; and on prevention and action in the event of possible situations of lack of protection and abuse, harassment and sexual abuse of children and adolescents, that are in line with best practices (IASC’s Six Core Principles Relating to Sexual Exploitation and Abuse), and that reflect zero tolerance for sexual exploitation, abuse and harassment as well as any type of unethical behaviour. ACPP regularly assesses safeguarding risks in its work, including identifying key staff involved in sensitive posts with risk related to safeguarding and child protection. PSCCW also has a code of conduct related to PSEA and gives different trainings on this topic to service providers, being one of the expert organisations in this area. In terms of addressing possible complaints from the target population, the Action Group will make use of the complaints box set up at the start of intervention in the different communities. At the public launch of the intervention in each community, PSCCW will lead the carrying out informative sessions on PSEA and will explain the purpose and functioning of the complaints mechanisms to register any concerns regarding SEA (and any other concerns). Previous to this informative sessions, a meeting will be held with the committee of the project and members of each municipality to explain to them what kind of information will be given and what is expected from them (anonymity if they receive any complain, how to encourage the beneficiaries to use the mechanisms, etc.) This will be followed up with reminders in face-to-face sessions (psychosocial support, legal awareness sessions…). However, as part of the follow-up of the program are recurrent field visits, the existent mechanisms of complains about PSEA cases will be mentioned again. Any complaints received will be referred to ACPP and PSCCW, in addition to the PSEA network. It will be ensured that this referral channel is used by all the partners of the Action Group.In addition, given the role of expert of the partner PSCCW in PSEA, we will explore the possibility to include trainings on PSEA for aid workers and service providers throughout the programme.
