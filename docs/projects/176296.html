Project Summary

The UNICEF frontline services project will contribute to the Education Cluster objectives CO1, CO2 and CO3 to improve equitable access to safe and protective education services and improve the quality of education services for emergency and COVID-19 affected IDPs, returnees, spontaneous refugees, and host community children including children with disabilities, and youth (girls and boys) by 2021 in counties under IPC 4 and 5. To achieve the objectives, the project will enroll 346,156 children in Pre-primary, Primary and secondary schools/learning centers; Establish 195 temporary learning spaces and classrooms; Rehabilitate 104 temporary learning spaces and classrooms; Establish 995 gender segregated latrines and water points and hygiene promotion; Train 1,560 teachers in EiE pedagogical skills and Child-centred methodologies to improve teaching -learning process for emergency-affected children; Train and update 1,560 teachers on PSS and the impact of COVID-19 and its protection mechanisms; Train 1638 SMCs/PTAs and education authorities on school management and governance, psychosocial support (PSS) and Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR). The proposed budget for the project is USD 23,538,632 with a cost per beneficiary unit of USD 68.
As mentioned in the 2021 HNO, the protracted crisis coupled with COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 resulted in unprecedented challenges and closure of schools in South Sudan affecting 1.9 million children. It is worth-noting that the closure of schools not only interrupted educational progresses, but also affected livelihood and hindered children’s access to essential basic needs and services including school feeding, health information on disease prevention, protection and access to clean water and sanitation services. The project will contribute to the Education Cluster objectives CO1 and CO2 to improve equitable access to education for emergency/crisis affected boys and girls aged 3-17 years (40 per cent girls) but also improving the quality of education services in the country.
Over the years, South Sudan has been confronted with many challenges including protracted conflict, floods, large population displacements, hyperinflation, and severe food insecurity (HRP 2020 Education Cluster Narrative). The already fragile education system drastically deteriorated at both the systems and service delivery levels and is characterized by high numbers of out-of-school children and adolescents, particularly those affected by conflict and other emergencies estimated to be 2.2 million (UNESCO 2018, Global Initiative on OOSC: South Sudan Country Study). With the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic and its impact, the appalling education situation of the country has been severely aggravated, hence the need for resources to address it.
Through the project, children and youth (3-17 years) will enroll in pre-primary, primary and secondary schools/learning centers; establish/rehabilitate temporary learning spaces with gender sensitive WASH facilities; train teachers on EiE, basic pedagogy and learner-centered methodologies; train and equip SMCs/PTAs and education authorities on school management and governance; and provide schools with appropriate IEC materials on COVID-19 prevention and mitigation measures.
The importance of ensuring that the right for equitable access to quality education for all children (boys and girls} in South Sudan, particularly the most vulnerable and those affected by conflict and floods are safeguarded. Without the provision of education, the future of children will be at stake.

1 - Needs

According to EMIS data collected in 2018 and published in 2019, the gross enrollment rate in South Sudan stands at 8.1 per cent at pre-primary and 61.8 per cent at primary level. The trend in girls’ enrollment in schools is particularly alarming with the Gender Parity Index (GPI) ranging from 0.86 at pre-primary to 0.75 at primary and ultimately 0.54 at secondary level (EMIS 2018). Over the years, the enrolment of boys has been higher than that of girls. Although EMIS 2018 indicates that there are more pupils that dropped out in lower grades than in higher grades in 2018, the lower primary grades have more male dropouts than female dropouts while upper primary grades have more female. Therefore, there is need to invest on interventions that will close the enrollment gap. The South Sudan Education Sector Analysis 2016 notes that any modest gains in enrollment are immediately undercut by dropout rates of over 10 per cent. The continuity of access to education drops off significantly as children move through the system. While 99 per cent of schools provide Grade one education, only 27 per cent offer up to Grade eight education. The protracted conflict, floods and COVID-19 pandemic impacted negatively on the education sector and led to the damage, occupation and closure of schools. Some families who fled the conflict ended up residing in areas without access to protective learning spaces or in host communities where education resources are overstretched. Furthermore, due to the excessive floods in some parts of the country, especially in Jonglei, Upper Nile and Unity, internally displace people (IDPs) occupied schools for shelter. Therefore, the number of out-of-school children in South Sudan is likely to remain the highest in the world unless nation-wide efforts are geared towards boosting school enrollments. The growing insecurity, coupled with hyperinflation and low and delayed payment of teachers' salaries has also led to higher rates of teacher absenteeism and attrition. The participation of volunteer teachers has partially filled the gap, but only 30 per cent of South Sudan’s primary-level teachers are qualified. All these supply-side challenges are magnified in the nascent early childhood education system, responsible for preparing children to succeed in primary school. The quality of education remains a major concern, with the majority of primary school children (boys and girls) attending schools that are not achieving foundational literacy, numeracy and life-skills. Learning achievement among primary school-aged children remains low: at the end of Grade three, most learners cannot identify a single written word in their native languages or English (EGRA/EGMA Report 2019). Without basic literacy skills, it is expected that South Sudanese learners will struggle to engage with other subjects on the curriculum and will be at higher risk of dropping out of school. With the school-aged population (aged 3-17 years) set to grow by 0.5 million to an estimated five million by 2021, and if there is no significant improvement in the political and economic environment by then, it is expected that these trends will continue and, perhaps, worsen. This will place a further burden on a public education system that at present lacks the resources and capacity to deliver quality education at scale. The scarcity of gender and age appropriate teaching and learning materials is particularly worrying given that so many teachers are untrained. Children and youth (3-17 years) will enroll in pre-primary, primary and secondary schools/learning centers; establish/rehabilitate temporary learning spaces with gender sensitive WASH facilities; train teachers on EiE, basic pedagogy and learner-centered methodologies; train and equip SMCs/PTAs and education authorities on school management and governance; and provide schools with appropriate IEC materials on COVID-19 prevention and mitigation measures.

The project will be implemented by carrying out the following activities: 2.1. Enroll children and youth (3-17 years) in pre-primary, primary and secondary schools/learning centers; 2.2. 7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

UNICEF has a zero tolerance policy on Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA). A lot of Information, Communication and Education (IEC) materials have been produce on the subject, which will be used for disseminating information to the beneficiaries.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Feedback from vulnerable community members who are not literate will be collect through the existing Parent-Teacher Associations (PTAs) and School Management Committees (SMCs).

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

UNICEF and its implementing partners have established Parents-Teachers Associations in each school and School management Committees to provide continuous feedback to parents and the community. The project will ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members is provided through the Parents - Teachers Associations, teachers (male and female), and children (boys and girls). Regular meetings will be conducted with parents and influential members of the community to enroll children in the schools and provide support for the effective management ofschools.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Feedback collected from beneficiaries will be collated, analyzed and lessons learnt will be used to adapt the project.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

As part of UNICEF's set standard protocol of zero tolerance, sexual exploitation and abuse is considered a gross misconduct. Emphasis will be put on the principles of no sexual activity with a child (a person under the ages of 18), no exchange of money, employment, goods, or services with anyone doe sexualfavors, including sex with sex workers to strongly discourage relationships with beneficiaries. Any sexual exploitation and abuse concerns and suspicions must be reported, investigated and actions taken to create and maintain a sexual exploitation and abuse free environment. Flexibility in programming – including the integration of programming with other sectors and partners, community-based groups, and via direct implementation – is built in as necessary to ensure service delivery continuity to themost vulnerable. Mainstreaming of peacebuilding activities for peaceful coexistence, including within other sectors beyond education, and continue illuminating the plight of South Sudanese children and their right to education.
