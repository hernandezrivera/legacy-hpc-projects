Project Summary

Action Against Hunger (AAH), together with the Union of Agricultural Work Committees
(UAWC), proposes to provide immediate relief responses to support vulnerable
households across 5 governorates of the Gaza Strip to cope with shocks following lasting
consequence of COVID-19 and May violent escalation affecting their food security status;
enabling them to protect their livelihoods, increase productivity and improve their food
and nutrition security status. The impact of the recent and lasting shocks destabilized
markets further affecting prices fluctuations and unemployment and poverty rates to
increase. Proposed responses target households who obtain their main source of income
from agriculture activities and cannot maintain or rehabilitate their key productive assets;
and households who are at risk of food insecurity, whose livelihoods were affected by
COVID-19 restrictions and the aftermath of the May 2021’s escalation limiting
employment opportunities, with family members unable to work (with physical or mental
disabilities, medical cases), with a high number of dependents and do not have a stable
source of income. This project has been developed to contribute to the achievement of
the HNO (2022) strategic objectives (SO3) The ability of vulnerable Palestinians to cope with the protracted crisis, including environmental threats, is supported, while solutions to violations and other root causes of threats and shocks are pursued and the
two Food Security Sector (FSS) objectives of the oPt HRP: (O1) Livelihoods of
vulnerable households are protected, and their resilience, food security and productive
capacity restored/enhanced, (O2) Households suffering from limited of access to food
and to income opportunities are able to meet their basic food needs, and aligned with
the strategic priorities of the Ministry of Agriculture, and has been coordinated with
similar interventions within the target areas. AAH and UAWC will focus on rehabilitating
productive assets, such as damaged greenhouses, water ponds and solar energy units for
irrigation pumps, agricultural wells and installation of solar units, and poultry farms and
installation of solar energy units, while supporting workers’ households through cash for
work (CfW) activities to support the rehabilitation process. Additionally, AAH will support
a number of households with IGAs in the services, commercial and agriculture sectors
with financial grants and coaching to recover from damages and/or suspension of
business activities due to the consequences of COVID-19 and the May escalations.
The project will enable 490 households to increase their resilience to man-made and
natural shocks, make use of the available natural resources for their agricultural activities
and reduce the costs associated, including gaining an income for those employed in cash
for work activities. This will reduce the food insecurity and improve livelihoods of an estimated 2,697 people (591 men, 753 women, 639 boys, and 714 girls). Small scale
farmers and poultry breeders who have accumulated levels of debts and decreased
income from recent restrictions and inability to restore productive assets will be
prioritized. Female headed households or breadwinners with high dependency ratio with existing IGA’s will be prioritized to be supported further in improving their food security. Accountability and participation will be ensured through a detailed MEAL framework
specifically developed to ensure that beneficiary selection is conducted in a transparent and accountable manner and through applying AAH’s thorough FCM to guarantee the inclusion of communities and individuals’ suggestions into the project. AAH seeks to achieve equal and impartial access to vulnerable populations, aiming for all community members to be involved as much as possible
including through community consultations to further achieve beneficiaries’
empowerment and sustainability beyond the project lifetime.

1 - Needs

Since the start of the pandemic and its socioeconomic consequences in the Gaza Strip, households living in chronic poverty are facing worsened conditions with further limited livelihood opportunities. Moreover, the results of May 2021 violent escalation added a layer of challenge for individuals, producers and markets to recover. According to the oPt FSS HNO 2022, almost 2 out of 5 Palestinian’s are suffering from food insecurity in 2021. Out of the 1.5 million people in need, 73% are located in Gaza. According to the world Bank Group (RDNA 2021), the poverty rate in Gaza is expected to have increased to 56.4% as a result of the May hostilities, considering that in Gaza limited financial access and stability is a main driver to food insecurity. WFP (Food Security Analysis 2021) estimates that 62% of the population of Gaza were food insecure in May 2021 following the hostilities. The financial consequences of the hostilities May were estimated to an economic loss up to 70 million USD within productive sectors including the agriculture sector (45 million in damages). UAWC conducted a rapid needs assessment (Sept-Oct 2021) with 175 farmers engaged in plant, animal and/or fish production. 80% reported damages and the reduction of cultivation/production area due to the inability to cover financial needs and accumulated debts. Some of key limitations include lack of financial capacity to reinvest in production, limited access to water for irrigation and high costs of water extraction due to fuel costs and electricity shortages. Farmers and their household members’ socioeconomic challenges result from lack of regular income and fluctuating debts. Special needs of household members cannot be then covered regularly, considering that 17% of households in Gaza are headed by elderly people, out of which 54% have at least one difficulty (PCBS 2021). MoA estimates that about 26000 dunum of land and about 450 poultry and livestock farms were partially or fully destroyed. FAO (bi-weekly monitoring 2021) reported thatfarmers in Gaza reported since May 2021 various obstacles to production including the closure of crossings, subsequent shortage in the availability of inputs and chicks, increase of input prices, high chick mortality as result of the heatwave and diseases. Limited power supply constitutes further structural challenges that the sector and farmers have to face daily leading to limited access to water and unstable conditions to maintain poultry farms. The Palestinian Ministry of Labor estimated that about 19000 workers lost their income opportunities following May (2021), out of them 37% working in the agriculture sector. Women in agriculture are particularly disadvantaged, despite being a significant unregistered portion of the workers (22.5%). This is reflected in limited economic opportunities, restricted mobility and limited technical skills, hence marginalizing their role in the agricultural production process. In Gaza, at least, 7.1% of female-headed households identify that 50% of their income comes from agricultural activities particularly impacted by the limited services available in the sector (Oxfam 2020). In 2021, unemployment among men in Gaza reached 39% and up to 64% among women. Based on AAH assessment (August 2021) on IGAs in the agriculture, service and commercial sectors, it is noticeable throughout all sectors that men reported double the monthly income (1667 NIS) compared to women (950 NIS), with services, food industry and commerce sectors being more profitable (period of May-July 2021). Nonetheless, women reported having more cash savings then men at the end of the month and contributing more to household expenses with the remaining profit (32% against 10%). The main reported challenges since COVID-19 and after the hostilities were loss of income (59%), loss of customers (50%), increase in stress levels within the household (25%) and temporary closure of activities (23%).

2 - Activities

Building on previous experiences, the proposed intervention answers to the needs identified within the oPt HNO 2022 and aligned with the FSS cluster objective SO1 and SO2, considering focus interventions as per MoA guidelines. The selection of the target groups will be done through announcements in coordination with the FSS, UAWC and the MoA and MoL to avoid duplication of assistance. Information sessions will be provided in each target communities to inform about the intervention activities, communication channels to reach and share complaints to AAH and UAWC. Households who depend on agricultural activities (including daily workers) for their livelihood will be prioritized based on head of household age and gender (women and elderly prioritized and/or with disability), nb of people with disability, location, registered with MoA for damages, among others, will be supported to restore their income and enhance their resilience to shocks. A prior coordination was done with FSS and MoA to identify targets and the priorities.200 small-scale farmers who own greenhouses (200m2 area) will be supported to rehabilitate their partially damaged greenhouses (50 farmers in Rafah, 20 in Khan Yunis, 50 in Middle Area, 50 in Gaza and 30 in North Gaza). The unconditional assistance will enable farmers resume agricultural activities, reduce their debts linked to production costs by receiving cash transfers (1000 USD) in two instalments, the second conditional to the intended use of the first instalment. As per experience, about 10% of women farmers will be supported through this activity.Rehabilitation (in-kind) of 10 rainwater harvesting ponds (average size: 220 m3,25 kw/2.5 kw per each pond) in Khan Yunis by AAH. This activity aims to improve the availability and the quality of water for watering a wider variety of crops that cannot be irrigated with highly saline groundwater. The needed solar energy system will be installed for each water pond to ensure functionality and reduction of production costs. The assistance will be provided in-kind to the selected farmers.Rehabilitation of 9 partially damaged by escalations registered agricultural wells and installation of solar units (90 kw/10 kw per each well) (in-kind) profiting 90 farmers. This activity aims to recover water access and enhance the farmers’ ability to cope with the electricity shortage crisis, improve the productivity of the agricultural lands and reduce production costs. UAWC will target 4 wells in North Gaza, and AAH 3 wells in Middle Area, 1 well in Khan Younis, and 1 well in Rafah).Rehabilitation of 50 Poultry farming units affected by the escalations and installation of solar units (farm size is averaging between 250 m2 and 500 m2 - 50 kw/1 per each farm). Under this activity, small-scale breeders will receive unconditional cash assistance (3800 USD) to restore inputs including new feeders, watering cans, as well as replacing defected ceiling plastic sheets and improving lighting and ventilation. UAWC will target 15 farms in Rafah, 15 farms in Khan Younis, 10 farms in Middle Area, 5 farms in Gaza, and 5 farms in North Gaza. Cash for work activity in support to the rehabilitation of the damaged greenhouses, 40 breadwinners (8 skilled and 32 unskilled workers) will be selected by UAWC through announcements and agricultural committees to target households with no regular sources of incomes who suffered from decreased revenues in the targeted areas. Households depending on unregular income from daily work with high socioeconomic vulnerabilities will be prioritized and benefit from short term job opportunities (CFW – skilled workers 22 USD daily and unskilled workers 18 USD daily)) supporting the rehabilitation of agricultural assets. As part of all UAWC and AAH implementation, information sessions within targeted communities will be carried to disseminate information about intervention, Feedback and Complaint Mechanisms and PSEA channels.These households will benefit from financial access to immediate food and basic needs, preventing further negative coping mechanisms. 100 Households with IGAs in the service and commercial sectors across Gaza affected by market fluctuations and/or hostilities consequences will be supported with financial grants (2500 USD) and business coaching to recover. AAH will target households relying on IGA’s as main source of income, at risk of sinking deeper into poverty without assistance identified through AAH and UAWC activities, crosschecking with the MoSD, the MoL for daily worker status. Relevant business activities will be selected upon ongoing market and value chain studies who suffered in the last year of temporary business closure and decrease of profit. Women business owners will be prioritized to facilitate income opportunities to women, along with households with high socioeconomic vulnerabilities.

3 - Indicators

AAH and UAWC will develop a comprehensive MEAL Framework for the action. AAH will support UAWC staff responsible for project monitoring to ensure the monitoring system relating to impartial beneficiary selection and on-going project implementation is as effective as possible. Joint MEAL planning through the development of project tracking tool will be conducted through participatory approaches including UAWC. AAH technical and MEAL teams will conduct regular field visits (if the context allows) and meetings with UAWC to follow up the progress and ensure activities are implemented according to the project workplan. AAH MEAL team will collect gender, age and disability disaggregated data and monitor the desired outcomes and impact of the activities on households’ ability to restore their livelihoods and face shocks. The following are the main MEAL activities: Developing project tracking tool to provide timely evidence on key results achieved using planned indicators, timely monitoring of the assumptions and external risks and provide support management through project progress overview. Carrying baseline/ end line exercises to assess the provision of support to farmers, daily workers and IGA owners led by AAH and UAWC. Baseline/end line assessments will use of gender sensitive tools and mixed method approach with women headed households to assess in depth outcomes on women access to income opportunities, income management and access to quality and sufficient food. Baseline and end line reports will be produced illustrating key findings and progress achieved through all activities, aiming at capturing progress but also addressing key recommendations for the agriculture sector and programmatic learning. Close coordination will take place with relevant ministries and NGOs through the FSS concerning the verification of the applicants’ data and access to the cross checking to avoid overlapping. The expected results are the following: Improved production and productivity capacity for vulnerable farmers; rehabilitation of agricultural assets (greenhouses, water ponds, agricultural wells, installation of alternative source of electricity); Increased capacity of households, with particular focus on women to prevent the adoption of negative coping mechanisms; Conditional cash assistance to support economically vulnerable households who recently lost their income or saw their income decreased through CfW opportunities and IGA support to improve households’ financial access to food and other basic needs, increased food security and reduced negative coping mechanisms.. Expected indicators and targets: 1,375 people receiving inputs and services 165 kW of renewable power supply systems installed 200 greenhouses rehabilitated 9 water wells rehabilitated 2,200 m3 of water storage rehabilitated (Area of each pond is 220 m3) 100 Households supported with IGAs 220 individuals benefiting from Cash for WorkOther indicators include: 60% of farmers recovering their income from before the escalation; 60% of back to work businesses; 80% of beneficiaries (disaggregated by sex, age, and disability) reported that humanitarian assistance is delivered in a safe, accessible, accountable, and participatory manner.Proxy indicators to measure food security and use of coping mechanisms will be measured: (i) reduced Coping Strategy Index (CSI), (iii) Livelihoods Coping Strategy Index (L-CSI) and Food Consumption Score (FCS).

5.2 - Please briefly explain why the specific modality/ies for this response were chosen.

According to the results of the Multi Sectoral Needs Analysis (MSNA) completed in July 2021 by REACH, surveyed individuals reported preferring cash assistance (77%) followed by in-kind assistance (36%). Based on AAH recent PDMs and data collected through baseline assessments from previous interventions supporting the agriculture sector and farmers, majority of farmers reported being satisfied with the cash modality as it can contribute to alleviate their debts holdings with suppliers while covering about 80% of the needed inputs. Cash assistance will be delivered through financial inclusion accounts upon availability or through ATM cards with the Bank of Palestine and Palpay.

9. - Feedback and complaints mechanisms

Yes

9.1c - How will feedback be used?

Beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries alike will be able to reach out to the project with complaints, suggestions, and any other feedback through the mechanism managed by AAH MEAL Unit. AAH actively promote the existence of its feedback mechanisms through information sessions and information material (e.g. flyers) handed over during projects’ activities (e.g. assessments, distribution, training session etc.) Our program teams maintain fluid communication with all rights holders regularly and extensively throughout the project management cycle. Our team consults and meets with rights holders when carrying out needs assessments in relevant beneficiary areas and employs a series of methods to ensure that their perspectives and priorities are addressed in the project design. These methods used to receive feedback include interviews with key informants, home visits, group discussions (separating men and women to ensure a safe space when discussing sensitive subjects), questionnaires and regular monitoring sessions to ensure that the project is appropriate and relevant to their needs. The information collected through complaints and feedback mechanism or directly in the field will be regularly fed into our programming to ensure no harm is done and to guarantee continuous improvements of assistance delivered respecting internal humanitarian standards, including informing the budget. Beneficiary feedback will be analysed and presented in a systematic way, including to project partners that we work with. According to needs observed and recorded referrals to relevant clusters will be done for further targeted assistance not covered under this intervention. Learnings following this feedback can often be used across projects and we make sure this is shared among relevant stakeholders so that complaints are taken seriously. The AAH MEAL unit will analyse the data, address concerns and respond. The PSEA channel will direct claims to HR when necessary.

9.1d - Explain how you have ensured that mechanism are accessible to all population groups?

AAH pays particular attention to the way vulnerable groups are included in our work. Information on the feedback and complaints mechanism is given to every beneficiary individually when distributing assistance at household level. To ensure that our beneficiary feedback mechanisms are accessible to all, AAH have a variety of different methods so that people can choose which they prefer (e.g. email, phone, field, office) but AAH promotes mainly the use of a phone to give feedback and complaints to avoid having vulnerable population spending time and money in coming to AAH offices. AAH will disseminate information about the FCM channels with UAWC staff and through UAWC activities by transferring all required practices and follow up on referral practices with protection cluster focal points (Child protection and GBV working groups) for the whole of the intervention. To accommodate and cater to some of these different groups, home visits or private spaces can be provided, to investigate and discuss a complaint received. During regular monitoring visits and MEAL activities visiting selected farmers and their household members (during hours agreed with and convenient with household members most at risk), UAWC and AAH field staff will ensure that all members had access to the information about the FCMs and are well understood. Field staff will record mobility, visibility, educational or other difficulties impairing access to the information to adjust channels and content of the communication.

10. – Do No Harm
10.1 - Do No Harm

Through the whole project cycle AAH ensures protection mainstreaming and Do No Harm. AAH considers safety and dignity, meaningful access, accountability as well as participation and empowerment of paramount importance. AAH has mechanisms in place to prevent and respond to sexual exploitation and abuse by all staff. AAH has in place focal points at base and mission levels, as well as safe and confidential ways to report abuse. All AAH staff have the obligation to adhere to the organisation code of conduct and our held accountable if they do not respect it. AAH has made use of cluster specific Protection and Gender Mainstreaming Tip Sheets, along with learnings from various interventions and external evaluations. Overall AAH’s aim is to take decisive humanitarian action with attention to risk mitigation thereby avoiding doing harm to the greatest extent possible. Risks assessments and analysis of the protection context are let for all the projects, and even more so when implemented with at risk groups (e.g. minors) or in areas where risk of doing harm is exacerbated. In order to inform its assessments and implementation AAH also continuously works on understanding gender roles and how its interventions might impact them without aggravating existing power relations between genders. By understanding gender roles and household dynamics, AAH believes that the organization mitigates the risks of doing harm. In 2019, AAH conducted a masculinity study in both Gaza and the West Bank, which focused on identities/roles of men in order to fully understand men, women, girls and boys and gender dynamics. As part of AAH accountability to affected populations, a feedback mechanism emphasizing fluid and transparent communication is be in place. COVID-19 protective measures and equipment will be applied throughout all interventions to further prevent harm being done to beneficiaries, communities and staff members.

11. - Equal and impartial access to aid
11.1 - Equal and impartial access to aid

AAH upholds the humanitarian principles of Humanity, Impartiality, Neutrality, and Independence. During the course of the project, AAH will ensure all projects are easily accessible to those most in need, avoiding risks to the beneficiaries. AAH is committed to operate in an ethical manner and on a programmatic risk mitigation strategy for risk of fraud, waste, and abuse within the proposed activities. At a global level, AAH relies on the organizational Policy on Anti-Bribery, Corruption and Abuse of Power and Code of Conduct. Organizational whistleblowing mechanisms and procedure are in place, `ZERO TOLERANCE ́ towards fraud, bribery or corruption is applied. Reporting mechanisms have also been implemented to report these behaviours to headquarters. Ethical Risks related to any kind of abuse(sexual, power, etc...) are mitigated by providing compulsory briefing to each new staff member on ethical policies. AAH Code of Conduct is aligned with IASC principles. AAH has a Policy for Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) and in 2019, AAH conducted in its Middle East country offices a Protection Mainstreaming Internal Evaluation for which the consultant spent March in oPt. Particular attention was paid to the way AAH includes vulnerable groups in its work, for instance through inclusive participatory assessments. As one of AAH’s core values, AAH thrives to prevent discrimination and to ensure equal and impartial access though transparency and accountability to affected populations. AAH aims for all community members to be involved and consulted as much as possible in project design and implementation, as well as evaluation, including through household and community consultations and focus group discussions, accessible to all. In order for their participation to be meaningful, AAH focuses on ‘qualitative inclusion’ meaning safe spaces for beneficiaries to participate. AAH is reporting under the HRP disaggregated data by sex and age; though pending the reporting format, AAH usually includes further disaggregation on vulnerability such as data on people with disabilities. Random checks will be carried out by the MEAL team members to ensure transparency, neutrality and objectivity and spot potential bias or incongruency.
