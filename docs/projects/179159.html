Project Summary

More than ten years of destructive conflict in Syria has hit those who are least responsible the hardest – children. Humanitarian and protection needs are still acute. With basic infrastructure and services decimated by the conflict and 6.7 million people internally displaced, there remain 13.4 million people in need of some form of humanitarian or protection assistance, including 6 million in acute need. Children have experienced psychological distress due to violence and instability, many have missed years of education, with 2.4 million children in Syria currently out of school (UNOCHA, 2021 HNO Summary). Across Syria, 137,000 children under 5 suffer from acute malnutrition (wasting) half a million children are stunted and 41.6% of children under 23 months suffer from anemia, meaning they are unlikely to reach their full potential in life. The current economic downturn and worsening food insecurity across Syria will likely contribute to the deterioration of the nutrition status of both mothers and children (HNO 2021).
Pregnant lactating women (PLW) and children under 5 years are particularly vulnerable to morbidities and mortality in a crisis. In Syria, even before the crisis, there was low levels of exclusive breastfeeding amongst infants under 6 months and this continues, with infants who are not exclusively breastfed at higher risk of becoming sick and of dying from illness. Due in part to food insecurity and reliance on micronutrient-poor food distribution baskets, children and PLWs are found to have poor dietary practices putting them at risk of malnutrition. Although wasting remains low, on average, 1 in 5 children are stunted highlighting a chronic lack of proper nutrition with maternal malnutrition also on the rise. Over the last year in the face of the COVID-19 pandemic, food prices across Syria have hugely increased and the value of the Syrian Pound has plummeted. This has left communities vulnerable to food insecurity amongst many other risks. Food insecurity is directly related with the risk of malnutrition, highest for PLWs and children under 5 years who have an increased nutritional need (Save the Children, Nutrition SMART Surveys Final report – North-east Syria camps, March 2021).
In September 2020, Save the Children published its Hidden Hunger in Syria report, which calls for investment in multi-sectorial efforts to improve nutrition status, specifically calling out a need for interventions to improve household food security and dietary diversity. Save the Children proposes a holistic response to support the most vulnerable communities with a special focus on children under 5 and pregnant and lactating women (PLWs), and caregivers with nutrition, water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) and psychosocial assistance. Malnutrition and poor hygiene and sanitation practices remain highly prevalent across Syria, and these have been exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic and the lack of clean water. In order to address these needs, the proposed intervention will focus on improved infant and young child feeding (IYCF) practices through counselling, one-to-one support (this includes psychosocial support targeted at PLWs), group education sessions in mother-baby areas, and providing monthly fresh food to caregivers. In addition, recently displaced households will benefit from food distributions. The proposed project will also enable access to clean water, and mitigate the risks of COVID-19.

4 - Needs

With 7.9 million people food-insecure inside Syria, maternal malnutrition rates are on the rise and have increase five-fold since 2019. Pregnant and lactating women also experience higher rates of anaemia which affects at least one out of three mothers. 42% of children under 23 months suffer from anaemia, and half a million children are chronically malnourished (stunted), meaning they are unlikely to reach their full potential in life. An additional 137,000 children under 5 suffer from wasting (HRP 2020). The current economic downturn and worsening food insecurity across Syria will likely contribute to the deterioration of the nutrition status of both mothers and children.The recent SMART survey in NES by Save the Children shows that households are dependent on food distributions to meet their nutritional needs which focus on reaching the daily macronutrient requirements but are micronutrient poor, particularly in iron, calcium, and zinc; all crucial for optimum health and growth. Additionally, one out of every three pregnant women are suffering from anemia and other micronutrient deficiencies, and at least 51,000 are acutely malnourished – and rising. The SMART surveys also show concerning IYCF practices.The proxy prevalence of chronic malnutrition (stunting) among children between 6-59 months across North West Syria is now 32% compared to 19% last year. Alarming rates of acute malnutrition are also observed among pregnant and lactating women, rates are above 15% in Idlib and reaching 22-24% in hard-to-reach areas. This situation, along with very high rates of maternal anaemia, and recent decrease in access to antenatal and postnatal care can pose a great risk on mother and infant health.

5 - Implementation of activities

SC will support integrated IYCF-E and PSS activities in camps and communities. SC will support integrated IYCF-E and PSS activities in camps through continuing the existing mother-baby areas (MBAs) and establishing new MBAs in areas where no current services exist. SC will establish mobile IYCF-E support in the communities by collaborating with existing health providers to train health staff in IYCF-E and to, where needed, provide direct, mobile IYCF-E support in the health facility by establishing an IYCF-E corner in the health center where mothers with feeding challenges can be referred for specialised support.SC will conduct screening for acute malnutrition using Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) in camps where treatment services are available or a functioning referral system is in place. Community screening will be organized in coordination with the service provider of treatment and other health and nutrition partners. Children under 5 years and PLWs identified to be acutely malnourished will be registered and referred to nearest therapeutic center or service provider. There will be follow-up of such cases till resolution. Where there is no treatment service or functioning referral system, SC will work with the Nutrition WG and partners to find a solution to fill this critical gap in services.Community educators will provide key messages on nutrition topics such as breastfeeding, maternal nutrition, complementary feeding and hygiene practices. Educators will also conduct nutrition orientations at key points, such as SC-supported schools and Child Friendly Spaces (CFS).

Yes

7.1 - Please list any risks related to sexual exploitation and abuse that have been identified during the project design phase and any risk mitigation and prevention measures that have been adopted and built into the project

SC is invested in the Centrality of Protection and is developing an action plan that focuses on capacity building of staff and partners, embedding of conflict sensitivity and protection analysis, strengthening safe programming and child safeguarding approaches, improving risk management and integrating protection into advocacy work. In addition, SC integrates protection mainstreaming across its programs, and these principles guide the implementation team to ensure safe and quality programming. Building on the foundation of all staff members being trained on Child Safeguarding, protection against sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA) and Safe Programming, SC is also expanding capacity building of staff in conflict sensitivity, protection analysis and participatory approaches. A child safeguarding risk assessment, gender and GBV audit, and conflict sensitivity analysis are conducted for each program. These identify gaps, inform the project design, and mitigate protection risks for children and their families while accessing SC facilities and using SC services. In addition, SC has prioritized more sustained capacity-building for staff related to disability and disability inclusion, as well as on gender mainstreaming and GBV.

8 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

Unstable security situation: conflict escalation, poor acceptance from local stakeholders and/or change in lines of control can prevent access to different program areas, either temporarily or permanently. Inappropriate project design can exacerbate tensions between communities and lead to unintended negative or harmful outcomes during implementation including suspension of activities. In order to manage and mitigate this risk, SC regularly monitors the local context, and SC employs security focal points. SC staff complete mandatory security trainings. Context-specific Safety and Security Management Plans are regularly reviewed and updated.Failure of money transfer mechanisms or change in banking regulation restricts movement of funds; and currency fluctuations: SC may be unable to deliver program activities due to lack of funds or program staff may be put at risk due to a lack of personal funds to hibernate or due to deterioration in local acceptance as a result of unpaid balances to other staff, suppliers etc. SC mitigates this risk by using multiple money transfer channels and agents who have strong reputations and have been able to deliver funds in almost all circumstances inside Syria. All SC agreements are denominated in donor currency.Risks due to the COVID-19 pandemic: The situation may result in risks to children and their caregivers due to lack of access to programming sites; further exposure to protection concerns, including violence; and health risks to beneficiaries and SC staff members. Relevant programme adaptations have been developed and are currently in place. SC has also introduced Standard Operation Procedures for staff working remotely and to identify COVID-19 focal points in each programme area.
