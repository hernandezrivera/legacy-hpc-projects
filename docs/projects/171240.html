Project Summary

There is 1.37 million crisis affected children (691,295 girls) in need of education assistance in Somalia . The country has one of the world’s lowest enrolment rates for primary school-aged children. Enrolment figures vary across the country with the highest proportion of households reporting not sending any of their children to school in the following districts: 62% of households in Banadir, 59% in Middle Shabelle, 54% in Lower Shabelle and 51% in Awdal, Galgaduud, Bay and Nugaal, and 50% in Sool . In Puntland, there are significant number of ‘secondary age’ children (14-17 years old) attending primary school , a net enrolment rate of 46% and a drop out rate within 17% in Eyl, Burtinle and Galkayo Districts . Primary completion rates are low in Puntland at 22.5%; boys (29.9%) and girls’ (18.9%) . In Somaliland, the primary school attendance for school aged children is less than 50% (49% boys and 40% girls) . Children in rural areas and in IDP settlements are reported to encounter even more difficulties to be enrolled in primary schools. Inadequate learning facilities, trained teachers, teaching and learning materials, as well as WASH facilities and school feeding are some factors that hinder positive education outcomes. Extremely high rates of poverty make it difficult for parents to afford school fees. Girls’ participation in education is consistently lower than that of boys and only 25 per cent of women aged 15 to 24 were literate . The low availability of sanitation facilities (especially separate latrines for girls), a lack of female teachers (less than 20 per cent of primary-school teachers in Somalia are women), safety concerns and social norms that favour boys’ education are cited as factors inhibiting parents from enrolling their daughters in school.
WASH in Schools significantly reduces hygiene-related disease, increases students’ attendance and learning achievements, and contributes to dignity and gender equality . The 2016 Somalia Education Baseline Report found that in Southern and Central regions 62% of schools do not have latrines and 73% of schools do not have access to safe drinking water. The Education Cluster Children’s Voices survey conducted with 1,290 school children in 7 regions in May 2019 showed that 46% of the girls do not receive sanitary materials, 36% of the children reported that their schools do not have hand washing facilities, 15% of the children reported that their schools do not have gender segregated latrines and 67% of the children reported that their schools do not have a girl friendly space in schools where partners are responding. In Waajid, 79% of learning facilities have no gender segregated latrines on site and 81% have no fence and no clear demarcation exposing learners to different forms of risk and violence.
One in every ten children Somalia has some form of disability – a case which is exacerbated by armed conflicts and natural disasters. Within any crisis-affected community, children and adults with disabilities are among the most marginalized, yet they often are excluded from humanitarian assistance. In response to the above stated situation, WV Somalia will implement a mix of interventions that will improve access to quality education targeting 8000 children (5000 girls and 3000boys) from marginalized groups such as internally displaced people 800 children living with disabilities and minority clans mainly in the rural areas of Puntland (Ely and Garowe district in Nugal, Doolow and Waajid in South Central and Burao and Gabiley in Somaliland states. In total the project will target 8000 children (5000Girls and 3000 Boys) as follows: Burao 1167 (800 girls and 367 boys); Gabiley 984 (640 girls, 344 boys); Doolow 1217 (800 girls, 417 boys); Waajid 700 (400 girls, 300 boys); Garowe 1000 (660 girls, 340 boys); and Ely 1216 (700 Girls, 516 boys). 10% of all the targeted populations will be spared for prioritization of children with disabilities.

Project Activities

To ensure that emergency and crisis affected children and youth (5000 girls and 3000 boys 628 of which live with disabilities) have access to safe and protective learning environment WV will: construct or rehabilitate 11 schools (3 in South West State, 4 in Puntland and 4 in Somaliland) and provide desks to the 4 schools in Somaliland. World Vision will also construct fences on 2 schools to improve on the schools security situation, pit latrine blocks and hand washing facilities for improved sanitation and hygiene and will install water tanks. In surrounding communities WV will facilitate and support formation of 3 temporary learning spaces. WV will also provide cash in emergencies assistance (EIE) for vulnerable children and raise awareness on child protection in schools. WV will ensure vulnerable children and youth are engaged in lifesaving learning that promotes personal well-being and social cohesion by procuring and distributing teaching and learning materials for age-appropriate education in schools (includes books, pens, teacher reference books ,geometric sets, chalks, dusters, alphabetical charts, curriculum materials), provision of school uniforms and sporting materials, incentives for 208 teachers (20 in SWS and Jubaland, 30 in Somaliland and 30 in Puntland) and provision of safe drinking water in schools and facilitate MoE, CECs and school administrator led hygiene awareness forums to children (boys and girls) and communities. To ensure that children attend school, do not drop out and that learning abilities are improved by addressing the hunger with which children come from their homes WV will support school feeding programs. To strengthen the capacity to deliver effective and coordinated education in emergencies (EiE) preparedness and response within the education system WV will: conduct capacity building training for CECs on school management, child protection and school safety; conduct additional capacity building training to education cluster partners and MoE staff on emergency preparedness and response planning and coordination; and support teachers to undergo psychosocial training and finally conduct training in inclusive, protective and socio-emotional teaching and learning and psychosocial support for teachers.Finally, World Vision will provide support through hiring of vehicles and conduct routine and joint monitoring of interventions with the Ministry of Education – where technical backstopping will be provided to the frontline staff to ensure quality service delivery. WV will ensure that 800 children living with disabilities are targeted and are given priority in delivery of services. Provision of scholastic materials for learners, feeding programs and cash transfers interventions will be deliberate in ensuring that children with disabilities are given a priority. Since one in every 10 children has some form of disability, a quota of 10% for all the targeted beneficiaries will be reserved for children with disabilities. All interventions and constructions (including latrines, handwashing stations and water points as well as classrooms) within the project will be designed in a way that they are disability inclusive. WV will ensure that there is lighting and lockable doors in all the latrines to ensure that children feel safe to use the facilities. Complaints and response mechanisms will be put in place and an IVR toll free line will also be made available to all beneficiaries for accountability.World Vision will also ensure that all COVID-19 prevention protocols are followed in the implementation of the project. During trainings, participants will be required to observe social distancing, wear masks, sanitize their hands or wash hands with soap. Trainings will also be conducted in well ventilated rooms with a limited number of participants to avoid over-crowding. Where possible, trainings and meetings will be conducted outside - in open spaces.

What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

Impact and cost-effectiveness of cash programming is better than actual item vouchers and NFI/food transfers. The logic is that the provision of and support to the communities with cash addresses the immediate child’s scholastic needs and as well as food and nutrition insecurity. This is so because assistance in the form of cash generates the largest welfare gains because it allows beneficiaries to use the transfers as they see fit than in the case of food distributions - getting a lot of kilograms of maize floor that may go to waste due to expiration. Mobile cash transfer is also identified as a better modality as it significantly improves the quantity and quality of food consumed by the beneficiaries since they get food that leads to significantly larger increase in calories consumed because of choice. This may also result in increased dietary diversity thereby improving food security outcomes. There is freedom enjoyed when spending earnings from unrestricted and unconditional cash transfers as beneficiaries have autonomy by choosing to spend on things that best fits their needs. By allowing the beneficiaries to have purchasing power this modality provides the communities with greater choice and dignity through the ability to prioritized family and individual needs while at the same time stimulating local markets and improving the market cash flow. It’s argued that there is less stigma attached to cash transfers when compared with in-kind or vouchers or food stamps. Cash transfers are perceived to be less costly to administer than in-kind transfers associated with a large deadweight loss due to substantially lower benefits relative to the cost of providing it.

Explain how the project design considered market functionality ?

World Vison assessed the feasibility of the cash transfers in the light of targeted supply chains to meet the objectives of the project. After the government opened the economy under strict COVID-19 precautionary measures, movement of goods has normalized and prices seem to have return to trend in June. Field level monitoring by WV confirms that markets from which the beneficiaries will access food are in full operation with sustainable supply of goods. Somaliland continues to enjoy stability hence there is an assurance of continued market functionality and availability of commodities in the markets. As of June, cross border trade between Puntland and Ethiopia was ongoing normally, allowing the flow of imported items to Ethiopia and local cereals to Puntland. Cross border trade was also ongoing between Somaliland and Ethiopia, with the first 12 kilometres of the Berbera Wajaale corridor inaugurated. In Hiraan (Hirshabelle), cross border trade with Ethiopia was also active. In Dollow trade with Kenya was happening on a condition that all drivers must be screened for COVID-19 but foodstuffs were reportedly smuggled in through porous points by donkey carts. In Elbarde (South West), the border with Ethiopia remained closed but accessible to trucks carrying food and non-food items. According to the Cash Working Group (CWG) June quarterly dashboard it is identified about main cereals that in at least half of the 55 assessed districts, the April–June 2020 average price of the main cereal consumed (red sorghum, white maize, or red rice depending on location) was 10% more expensive than the January–March 2020 average. While Zeylac and Wanla Weyne recorded price decreases (-3 and -14), districts such as Diinsor, Jilib, and Baraawe recorded price increases of over 30%. In regards to key local crops and imported foods the CWG reveals that while the average price of white maize increased in all selected districts, average prices of other local crops varied. In Kismayo, the average price of white maize increased by 27%, to a level relatively higher than the five year average. In Jowhar, even though the average price of white maize increased by 29%, it is actually lower than the five year average. At the same time, the 16% price decrease of cowpeas observed in Bossaso has actually driven the price closer to the five year average. Looking at imported food commodities, average prices remained relatively stable, with the main exception being vegetable oil. In Bossaso and Kismayo, average prices of vegetable oil increased by 25% and 64%, respectively. In Baidoa, the correction of an unusual spike in the average price of vegetable oil in February led to a reported average price decrease of 33%.

Mitigation Measures

To ensure that the project does not expose its beneficiaries directly or indirectly to insecurities WV will use its existing security system which provides timely and targeted information for decision making. WVS has national and field-based Security Officer who closely monitor the context on the ground in close collaboration with UN Department for Safety and Security (UNDSS) and NGO safety Program. Weekly Security Update are produced and disseminated to all stakeholders. WV’s security protocols are based on best practices in Security Management according to the prevailing security risk levels in the area. Organizational and structural changes were made which allow WV to operate within acceptable levels of security risks regularly re-assessed and managed. World Vision will ensure that the project is aligned to the SPHERE and CHS principles when identifying and working with beneficiaries. Efforts will be made to promote gender equality/equity and the empowerment of women as an effective way to combat vulnerabilities. The project shall safeguard the rights of vulnerable groups especially women and children by giving them first preference in response activities. However, the project will ensure that nobody is left out, harmed, neglected, abused, discriminated or exploited. Further to this, WVS has in place a safeguarding policy framework on prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA) with a zero-tolerance approach towards abuse and exploitation. WVS will actively involve and allow the community to participate in program implementation and maintain their sense of ownership and responsibility. WVS shall adopt a do no harm (DNH) approach whilst striving to ensure that the proposed interventions do not generate disagreement or conflict and that staff adhere to the humanitarian principles. WVS will ensure equality/meaningful access to the project interventions while ensuring beneficiaries’ access to impartial assistance and services proportionate to need and without any barriers or discrimination whatsoever. WVS shall provide equal chance, inclusiveness, and participation of minority and majority community members as well as disabled groups in the district/s to be able to be transparent enough in terms of employment, capacity building, empowerment and resource sharing hence strive to foster comprehensive community engagement in the target locations. Of concern are those from IDPs and minority clans and other marginalized tribes at risk of being neglected, boys and girls, men and women living with mental illness and physical disability. Community-based workers selection and training will also consider gender sensitivity including both men and women. All this will ensure gender issues and equity are addressed. WVS will put accountability mechanisms in place to allow all groups with suitable channels to voice their concerns. Their feedback will influence the program so that it can continuously adapt to cater for the well-being. WV enjoys strong and positive working relationships in the target geographies. To ensure that there is no interference from external stakeholders who may bring about partiality in selection of beneficiaries, WV will work to ensure regular community consultation in all the targeted regions, particularly in the South Central, where insecurity can interrupt daily project site access and create confusion in target communities. To ensure that all voices are being heard in the course of humanitarian assistance, a deliberate efforts will be made in order to get feedback from the elderly, women and the illiterate through face to face dialogue sessions on monthly basis so that their pressing issues including those of gender are responded to in time.
