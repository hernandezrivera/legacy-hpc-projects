Project Summary

Street Child will address the critical barriers to quality learning and retention for 50,000 conflict-affected children across North East Nigeria. The focus will be on both improving access to quality, protective education for out-of-school children and improving the quality of teaching and learning for children in formal schools. In addition to basic literacy and numeracy, teachers will be trained on PSS, child protection and positive pedagogies to ensure that schools are safer, more protective spaces for all children. Street Child will work closely with communities and with local partners and stakeholders to build the capacity of those working in education in the region, improving sustainability and contributing to long-term systems strengthening.
In addition, Street Child’s integrated approach to EiE and CP in all areas of operation will ensure that spread of COVID19 among vulnerable population is prevented through a continuous awareness campaign and that personnel involved with children are organized and trained to respond or prevent protection concerns with a system for safe reporting of incidents and are equipped with preventive and protective equipment.
In Yobe, LGAs, which will be targeted, are Gujba and Gulani. In Adamawa, the targeted LGAs will be Mubi, Michika and Madagali. In Borno the targeted LGAs will be MMC, Jere, Gwoza, Damboa, Monguno, Biu, Hawul, Mobbar, Dikwa and Konduga

1 - Needs

A characteristic of the conflict in North East Nigeria has been the targeting of schools, teachers and students by armed groups. The protracted conflict has severely impacted the already-weak school system in the North East, leaving the limited system unable to absorb the high numbers of displaced children looking to attend school. In addition to high numbers of out-of-school children, learning outcomes for children in-school (including foundational literacy and numeracy rates) are low and drop-out rates are high. An estimated 1,060,129 conflict-affected children across Borno, Adamawa and Yobe states are estimated to be in need of education services. 697,683 of these children are displaced. A further 17,598 teachers are in need of training on child-centred pedagogy and psychosocial support (PSS). Factors limiting access to education in North East Nigeria have affected both boys and girls, but access to quality education has tended to be even more limited for girls than for boys, and adolescent girls are amongst the most vulnerable to dropping out of school. Rates or enrolment, retention and learning are even lower for children with disabilities (CwD) who are often excluded from mainstream learning and social life - a consultation conducted by Street Child has found that 57% of CwD are out of school. Challenges faced by CwD includes high levels of stigmatisation, a lack of trained teachers, a lack of inclusive facilities and inclusive learning aids, and the high cost of education and specialised education materials. There is a need to better integrate and respond to the barriers that particularly vulnerable groups, including girls and CwD, face in accessing quality learning opportunities in safe and protective learning environments. Recognising the key role education plays in providing immediate physical, psychosocial and cognitive protection in emergency contexts, there is a need to build safe and protective learning spaces. Building the capacity of teachers on pedagogical skills, psychosocial skills and child protection knowledge is crucial to ensure that education is protective and builds life-savings knowledge on topics such as health, hygiene and GBV (gender-based violence). At the same time, given that the low educational outcomes in NE Nigeria pre-date the current crisis, there is also a need to ensure that the support provided contributes to strengthening the system as a whole and in the long-term.Northern Nigeria has a large Islamic and Quranic school sector. These schools focus primarily on recitation or memorization of the Quran or teaching of basic Islamic texts. The federal government and the state governments in northern Nigeria has been keen to introduce the teaching of modern subjects within different types of Islamic schools to ensure that children in these schools learn basic literacy and numeracy. Such efforts had, however, largely failed. The malams (Islamic teachers) were viewed to be ideologically resistant to modern education due to its association with colonial rule (ESSPIN, 2014). In northern Nigeria, Islamiyya, Qur’anic and Tsangaya Education (IQTE) is important because, in some states, it accounts for over four-fifths of all schools. Many of their pupils come from poor families. In many Islamiyya schools, two thirds of the pupils are girls. Communities value these schools and many parents prefer them. Like state schools, many of the Islamiyya, Qur’anic and Tsangaya schools are demanding more and better teachers, better facilities and buildings, and better supplies of textbooks and teaching materials. Both state governments and communities appreciate the value of integrating non-religious and religious subjects into the curricula of Islamiyya, Qur’anic and Tsangaya schools. This integration is regarded as essential for children to succeed in modern society (ESPIN, 2014).

2 - Activities

Street Child will provide Education in Emergencies support to a total of 130,000 (70,000 Formal; 30,000 Non-formal; 30,000 IQTE) children in Borno, Yobe and Adamawa states. 2,000 (1400 formal; 300 non-formal; 300 IQT) teachers will be involved in the project with 60% of them female. The OOSC will be placed into temporary learning spaces (TLCs) supervised by Community Volunteer Teachers (CVTs). CVTs will be trained and receive an allowance to facilitate these non-formal classes, including training on PSS and child protection. Where structures are lacking, semi-permanent TLCs will be constructed in line with best practices prioritising children's safety and security, including safe locations, perimeter fences and the availability of first aid kits. Construction and renovations will also aim to meet the accessibility needs of CwD. The implementation of the national EiE curriculum, which Street Child has worked on, will be scaled-up and used as the accelerated learning curriculum in our TLCs. The learning progress of children attending Temporary Learning Spaces TLCs will be regularly assessed, with the aim of mainstreaming children into the formal system to allow them to continue their education. Street Child will work closely with the local and state education authorities to facilitate this mainstreaming. Similarly, Street Child will work with the education authorities to achieve official accreditation of CVTs through the Federal Teacher Scheme, allowing these community members to become government teachers and be included on the government payroll for sustainability. To support the strengthening of the formal education system and improve learning outcomes in formal schools for all children, Street Child will train government primary school teachers on pedagogical skills and psychosocial skills. This will include training teachers to implement the innovative Teaching at the Right Level (TaRL) approach, with the aim of remediating lack of skills in foundational literacy and numeracy. Formal school support will also include the provision of teaching and learning materials and adaptation of school structures (including WASH facilities) to be more inclusive, and the training of teachers on inclusive, child-friendly pedagogical skills. This will empower teachers to be able to meet the differing learning and support needs of different children, particularly with the aim of ensuring the needs of CwD in accessing education and quality learning are met. Community Education Committees (CECs) or School-Based Management Committees (SBMCs) will be established or strengthened, and trained on children's rights and protection mechanisms, inclusion, school management and disaster risk reduction. These committees will be responsible for managing and maintaining the learning centres, and for sustained community engagement on the importance of education. In addition to building sustainability, this will also support the process of community entry and acceptance. In line with Street Child's commitment to integrated education and child protection programming, teachers, teacher mentors, CEC/SMBC members and other school personnel will be trained on basic child protection principles and on how to prevent, identify and respond to child protection concerns. This will include supporting schools to develop safeguarding clear preventive and responsive safeguarding mechanisms, in order to build more protective learning environments. Covid 19 prevention awareness messaging will continue in 2021 through targeted awareness raising so that children, teachers and the entire community continue to protect themselves, to reduce the spread of the virus. These messages will be provided in local languages (hausa and kanuri) and child friendly tailored. Several means of disseminations will be utilized according to their availability in each targeted location as SMS messages, radio programs and other audio and visual materials.

7.1.d - In which LGA are you planning to undertake Nexus activities?

Street Child's approach to EiE bridges the humanitarian-development nexus, providing immediate protection whilst also contributing to resilience-building and system strengthening. A key element that Street Child will work on is ensuring that the learning children and teachers gain in learning centres and non-formal settings during the current crisis is validated and recognized, so that they can move into the formal system. Nexus activities will therefore be undertaken in all targeted LGAs: Gujba, Gulani, Mubi, Michika, Madagali, MMC, Jere, Gwoza, Damboa, Monguno, Biu, Hawul, Mobbar, Dikwa and Konduga.

8.6 - Please describe briefly the modalities your project will put in place to ensure protection mainstreaming (particularly referring to the above questions).

Street Child is committed to ensuring that protection is central to all of its activities and projects, acknowledging that prioritising protection, safety, dignity, accountability and minimising any unintended negative effects of our activities are crucial to meaningful, dignified access to support in the communities where we work. To achieve this in this proposed project, specific risks at all project stages have been identified and mitigation strategies integrated into the project design, clear protection reporting and response mechanisms have been included, and protection mainstreaming indicators have been included in the monitoring and evaluation framework. At the beneficiary selection stage, Street Child will use tested vulnerability assessment tools and clear selection process to ensure that beneficiaries are selected based on need and through predetermined and clearly communicated criteria of vulnerability. In order to ensure participation and minimise inter-group tensions, targets have been set to include different communities (IDP, host community and returnee) as well as both men and women. Community leaders and members will be informed of the selection process and criteria during the inception stages, and continuous sensitisation of the selection criteria will be used to ensure that possible intra- and inter-group tensions are minimised. Sensitisation on these topics through regular community visits and feedback sessions has proven successful during past projects, where dissatisfaction about not being included in the project has been reduced by clearly explaining the aims of the project and how beneficiaries were selected. As part of our commitment to accountability and protection, Street Child commits to work towards the elimination of exploitation and abuse in the communities where we works and in our engagement with vulnerable populations. Street Child has a clear safeguarding policy in place which applies to all personnel and affiliates, including implementing partners and community members who are affiliated to our work. All Street Child staff is trained on the safeguarding policy and is aware of their responsibilities. Similarly, all partner staff receive a training on Street Child’s policy and how to report incidents. This training is also given to all community members who are affiliated to the project – community leaders, PMC members, community committee members and community volunteers, for example. Sensitisation during activities through community forums and through the use of visuals such as posters also seeks to empower community members to better understand their rights, and to know how to report any concerns they may have. Finally, Street Child is an active participant of the PSEA working group in Borno, as an extra level of accountability, and follows the best practices and lessons learned shared through this working group. 9.5 - How will your project ensure feedback is collected from the most vulnerable community members?

Throughout project implementation, Street Child will use proven mechanisms to engage community members and stakeholders and to ensure accountability and transparency. Community participation will be integrated into all stages of the project, from the involvement of community leaders in beneficiary selection to putting in place effective Complaints and Response Mechanisms (CRM) to collect feedback to inform monitoring and evaluation. All feedback received through these diverse mechanisms will be fed into review sessions and management decisions, allowing activities to be adapted to respond to feedback where appropriate in line with Street Child's commitment to accountability and evidence-based learning. A volunteer project management committee (PMC) will be established in each community of implementation. This PMC will be empowered to hold Street Child accountable to the planned project aims, activities and implementation timeline, and will be able to raise complaints and concerns at monthly meetings with Street Child staff. A dedicated complaints mechanism (CRM) officer will be identified for all targeted communities. This person will be responsible for collecting feedback and complaints, and liaising between community members, partners and Street Child to share this feedback. The CRM officer will hold weekly help desk sessions in each community, where beneficiaries and community members can share feedback. A register of all complaints made, and responses to these complaints, will be kept. The help desk will also be set up during all key activities, such as grant disbursement. In addition to programmatic feedback mechanisms, Street Child has a toll-free line which can be called to report emergency incidents and safeguarding concerns. The number for this line will be displayed at all project sites.
