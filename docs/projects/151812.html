Needs

As of September 2018, there are 31,991 refugees & asylum-seekers registered in Somalia, mainly from Ethiopia and Yemen. In addition, others originate from countries including Syria, Tanzania, and Eritrea. In total 121,810 Somali refugees have voluntarily returned to Somalia since December 2014. Of those, UNHCR has provided assistance to 108,639 persons.Somalia is still establishing justice and governance institutions and many disputes are settled through the clan system. Refugees and asylum-seekers accessing the formal legal system often face retaliation. Sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV) against women and girls remains a serious and deeply rooted protection concern, social and economic independence for women is generally not accepted, and social patterns and participation in the political process is limited in the patriarchal/clan-based context of Somali society. Other risks, flowing partly from lack of clan affiliation, include xenophobia, risk of refoulement, arbitrary arrest and detention, exploitation and abuse, and discrimination with regard to access to justice, basic services and livelihoods compounded by high rates of unemployment, low income levels, low levels of education and literacy, weak capacities in production sectors and a depleted natural resource base, combined with limited access to finance and external assistance. While Somalia has no camps, with all refugees residing in urban areas, implementing the out-of-camp policy poses difficulties as the absorption capacity of host communities is limited.Access to civil documentation including birth certificates is a challenge for refugees and asylum-seekers due to costs, language barriers, and lack of a centralised registration system. Refugees and asylum-seekers who lack documentation face a risk of arbitrary arrest and detention. Registration activities are not yet at a satisfactory level and asylum seekers do not have access to timely Refugee Status Determination (RSD) processing.

Activities

UNHCR conducts mandate Refugee Status Determination (RSD) in Somaliland and Puntland and the state governments generally accept UNHCR decisions, allowing refugees and asylum-seekers to remain pending durable solutions. UNHCR will continue to work with authorities to support the implementation of the National and State asylum regulations with capacity-building in RSD procedures, for government authorities to take over RSD procedures. Multi sector rights based lifesaving protection assistance including legal interventions to address physical protection risks; Improved registration, identity and civil status documentation including birth certificates for refugee children by Somali authorities, and targeted RSD. There will be increased focus on community mobilization (e.g. on SGBV), self-reliance initiatives and community outreach mechanisms / peaceful coexistence including expansion of access to legal assistance. Efforts will be made to integrate activities into the national systems where available (e.g. health, education). Skills training and other livelihood initiatives will be implemented to promote self reliance while care and maintenance interventions will increasingly focus on the most vulnerable to minimize dependency on aid/assistance. In line with the Comprehensive Refugee Response Framework (CRRF), UNHCR will work in close collaboration with inter agency partners under the leadership of the FGS and FMS. Alignment to the National Development Plan and durable solutions frameworks will be ensured. Partnership with the local authorities will be strengthened to enhance sustainability of all interventions, with a focus on promotion of a favourable protection environment at regional and federal levels.UNHCR provides assistance to refugee returnees coming back to Somalia. The assistance at the way stations during the return travel, including a one-off cash grant, is planned for under the Refugee Chapter.

How is the project linked to durable solutions/resilience?

UNHCR’s activities in the refugee response are much focused on building the capacity of the authorities to take ownership in the asylum system. UNHCR will continue to work with authorities to support the implementation of the National and State asylum regulations with capacity-building in RSD procedures. UNHCR will advocate for a national legal framework for asylum to be established and will support capacity building for government authority to undertake RSD procedures. UNHCR will also apply the CRRF approach in other aspects of the refugee response. In advancement of the principles of the Comprehensive Refugee Response Framework (CRRF), and in line with the whole of society approach, UNHCR will enhance its strategic links with key Government Ministries at the federal level and regional levels. In line with the CRRF approach, UNHCR will develop refugee response projects together with a wide range of partners, including government agencies and humanitarian partners, as well as the beneficiaries.

Protection risk analysis & Mitigating measures
Preventing risks of exclusion based on displacement

While the government and people of Somaliland and Puntland are tolerant to Yemeni refugees, the situation for Ethiopian refugees and asylum seekers is not as favourable. School children complain of harassment while adults report robbery, SBGV, social exclusion and being denied wages due to their status as refugees. The negative perception of host communities and local authorities towards refugees and asylum-seekers as potential security threats and a strain on local resources also creates challenges. In its refugee response activities, UNHCR aims to involve the host community as well, to counter social tensions and foster cohesion. While providing direct support to refugees and asylum seekers, the approaches to protecting and assisting refugees and asylum seekers in Somalia also aims to create improved mutual understanding, trust and confidence between them and their host communities.

Preventing risks of exclusion based on gender and age

SGBV is an extremely sensitive issue as a cultural taboo and needs continuous capacity building, sensitisation and raising awareness at all levels in the communities and with stakeholders. Community participation will be a fundamental element in UNHCR’s interventions in ensuring effectiveness of the programmes. UNHCR will also continue to lobby for alternatives to detention and minimum detention standards and ensure that detained persons have access to legal aid. The issue of under-reporting SGBV will continue to be addressed through sensitisation efforts, proactive identification and an effective referral mechanism. Cases of SGBV against female-headed households and female survivors will continue to require greater attention and direct intervention by UNHCR, including through providing shelter and urgent resettlement. Legal, medical and psychosocial assistance as well as shelter are available for SGBV survivors. Specialised psychosocial support and access to justice for survivors of SGBV are limited to partners funded by UNHCR. In its assistance, UNHCR applies vulnerability criteria to prioritize, among others support to survivors of violence, as well as elderly and single female heads of households. UNHCR’s protection sensitive approach seeks to prevent exclusion of vulnerable groups taking into account gender and age, among others, through Age Gender Diversity mainstreaming and representation of different groups in assessments.

Mitigating the impact of armed conflict and violence

In South Central, security is affected by threats from the Al-Shabaab (AS) terror group. A December 2017 report by UNSOM’s Human Rights and Protection Group documented 4,585 civilian casualties (2,078 killed and 2,507 injured) in 2017, 60 per cent attributed to AS and the remainder to militia, state actors, AMISOM, and unidentified actors. AS abducted 729 civilians, targeted 86 assassinations and executed 46 persons in 2017. The insecure environment affects the delivery of protection in general, and to the refugees and asylum in particular. Some families prefer not to enrol their children in school, to engage them in child labour, either part time or full time depending on the need to supplement the family income. The most common forms of labour are shoe shining, car washing and other menial jobs in the informal sector. UNHCR will continue to monitor closely all protection incidents occurred within the context of forced displacement in Somalia. By advocating and putting in place response systems, every protection incident should get a referral or direct assistance. Additionally, the information gathered through a thorough monitoring system feeds into the wider system to better analyze main protection incidents in the country and better tailor programming to needs.
