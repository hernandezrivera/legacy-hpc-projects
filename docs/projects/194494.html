Project Summary

Malnutrition remains a persistent problem in South Sudan that continues to adversely affect the well-being of women and children. In 2023, close to 2.2 children and women will be at risk of acute malnutrition and will need urgent treatment. These include 353, 606 children for severe acute malnutrition (SAM), 1, 084,129 moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) and 737,812 pregnant and lactating women (PLW). High food insecurity, elevated prevalence of diseases, and poor hygiene, health, and environmental sanitation services, along with inadequate maternal and childcare are the main drivers of acute malnutrition in South Sudan. According to the preliminary results from Food Security and Nutrition Monitoring System (FSNMS) Round 28 and the 2022 SMART surveys, the proportion of children 6-59 months suffering from acute malnutrition remains above the emergency threshold of 15 percent (very high acute malnutrition level- WHO classification) was at 16.0% in lean season. The high levels of food insecurity seen among children under five years old in the targeted areas can be partially attributed to the scarcity of food and the consequent lack of variety in diet. This is further compounded by the lack of knowledge of minimum dietary diversity required. Poor Infant and Young Child Feeding (IYCF) practices, low food production and consequently low food reserves are also preventing children from receiving nutritionally dense foods.
Tearfund has scaled up its nutrition services in flood affected Twic East County with the aim of reaching as many people as possible in hard to reach highlands areas where most of them are currently stationed. It has established five mobile sites to address the nutritional needs when the existing static sites collapsed by the ongoing floods. Over the past year, Tearfund was able to manage 10,622 SAM, 14,764 MAM children and 5,256 malnourished PLWs newly admitted in 27 Nutrition sites in 3 Counties. Out of these cases, 95% in OTP and 98% U5 children in TSFP were discharged cured. However, there are still notable gaps in terms of scaling up and expansion of services; integration of nutrition with other sectors such as FSL, WASH and Health. In 2023, Tearfund targets to reach 12646 SAM, 31354 MAM U5, 27544 PLW, 48025 Caregivers, 124064 VAS and deworming. Tearfund is the sole organization in supporting nutrition interventions in 27 nutrition sites in three Counties (8-in Twic East; 8-in Pochalla and 11-in Uror) and 4 Stabilization Centers (2-in Uror; 1-in Pochalla and 1-in Twic East).
The integrated approach will address the high levels of malnutrition by targeting the families of SAM and MAM children for health, FSL and WASH interventions thereby acknowledging that these sectors are a key driver of poor health and malnutrition. Returnees and IDPs will be of special interest to this project because of their high levels of vulnerability.

1 - Needs

In South Sudan, high levels of food insecurity drive humanitarian needs in most parts of the country. According to the IPC Acute Food Insecurity & Malnutrition Snapshot, about 6.6 million people, or over half of South Sudan’s population (54%), are experiencing high levels of acute food insecurity, classified in Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse between October and November 2022. Acute malnutrition has been a protracted problem in South Sudan and one that requires sustained investment for life-saving nutrition service delivery. Its key drivers are multifaceted, including severe acute food insecurity, sub-optimal feeding practices, high prevalence of disease, inadequate sanitation conditions and hygiene practices, and limited access to health and nutrition services driven by the elements noted above. Those who are malnourished are more vulnerable to common and infectious diseases such as cholera, malaria, diarrhea diseases, acute respiratory infection and measles.Jonglei is one of the states where Counties of extreme concern are found. According to the food security analysis Feb 2022, Counties of extreme and severe concern will need immediate emergency food assistance complemented with emergency livelihood and livestock support across farming, agricultural and pastoral communities, for the medium- and longer-term to build both absorptive and adaptive capacity. There are 13 counties with extreme levels of food insecurity in 2022 and one of the geographic area targeted by this project, Uror County, is among those 13 Counties. In addition, an estimated 2 million people, including 1.4 million children under the age of 5, and 737,000 pregnant and lactating women, are expected to be acutely malnourished (HNO 2023). The high numbers are attributed to increased food insecurity and the decrease in coverage and of nutrition services due to conflict and flooding. In Counties like Twic East, roads have been destroyed and most of the affected sites can only be accessed by either speed boats or canoes, therefore making transportation difficult and expensive. Heavy rainfall over the past three years, has led to overflows of the Nile and other rivers as well as inland flooding. This has caused large-scale displacement of people, some of whom moved with their cattle and Crops and property damages were widespread in the affected areas. Over the past three years, local authorities in Twic East estimate that as much as 85% of Twic East was affected by flooding with as many as 80,000 people having fled the area for safer, drier ground. Communities find shelter in churches, schools, and outdoor spaces (Seasonal Flood Report 2021). The SMART survey conducted in Uror and Twic East by Tearfund and Care International indicated a GAM of 14.4% and SAM 2.8% (June 2021) and GAM 17.5% and SAM 2.6% (Sep 2022) respectively. The high rates of malnutrition in those Counties were attributed to several factors including high levels of food insecurity, poor infant and young child feeding practices as well as high morbidity due to a weak health system. Food consumption gaps are extreme, and people have largely exhausted their emergency coping strategies. Furthermore, poor access to nutrition services in all the three locations during the rainy season due to insecurity along the roads and poor road infrastructure have significantly affected service delivery. Tearfund is currently implementing UNICEF, WFP and BHA funded integrated Emergency nutrition, WASH and FSL projects. Each county has well equipped storage facilities and full time Nutrition staff as well as a network of IYCF groups, Community Nutrition Volunteers (CNVs) and community-based staff.

2 - Project activities

To respond to the above needs being aligned with Nutrition sector strategy and cluster objectives, Tearfund will continue its interventions primarily focusing on provision of quality preventative nutrition-specific services to children 6-59 months, pregnant and lactating women and adolescents in Twic East, Pochalla and Uror Counties in 27 OTP/TSFP sites and 4 stabilization centers. Tearfund will consider the mobile approach whenever needed to ensure access to populations displaced by either floods or conflict.Key activities include: 1. Train 106 nutrition staff, health workers, CNVs and Lead Mothers on MIYCN and integrated CMAM using MoH Guidelines; 2. Establish and Strengthen mother to mother support groups to deliver key MIYCN messages at nutrition sites and community level to improve optimal MIYCN practices; 3. Conduct passive and active screening through the Family MUAC approach and referral of SAM/MAM cases to nutrition sites. Atthe community level, screening will be done by Community Nutrition Volunteers (CNVs) and the Mothers of the children U5; 4. Demonstration of developing kitchen gardens and cooking demonstrations at the nutrition sites; 5. Medical and nutritional treatment for severely malnourished children and infants in the stabilization Center (SC) will be conducted according to the standard inpatient treatment protocols. Admissions to the SC will be children who are severely malnourished and who have bilateral pitting oedema grade +++ or any grade with medical complication. All discharges will be through the OTP where they will continue with treatment and will be given a priority for outreach follow-up during their first week in the OTP. Child friendly spaces and early childhood development stimulation are offered at the SC; 6. Therapeutic treatment of children (6-59m) with SAM without medical complications at OTP according to the CMAM guideline; 7. Targeted supplementary feeding for children (6-59m) and pregnant and lactating women with MAM through TSFP; 8. Promote appropriate MIYCN key messages and good hygiene practices with the use of appropriate pictorial IEC materials and conduct micronutrient supplementation (Vit A and deworming) services in nutrition sites and community level. Community Nutrition Workers (CNVs) will deliver health and nutrition related messages at the community level through individual counselling, group discussion and encouraging other women to share their experiences with the group; 9. Train Health and Nutrition Workers on GBV including safe referrals for survivors; 10. Encourage male participation through group discussions to initiate a dialogue around and sensitize menon MIYCN and women’s issues and promote gender equality along with GBV mitigation at the nutrition sites. This boosts male awareness on nutrition interventions and thereby enables women’s participation, as women often need men’s consent to participate in activities outside the home; 11. Carry out malaria testing and treatment/referral and provide LLIN for children admitted with SAM/MAM; 12. Undertake joint programmatic supportive supervision and monitoring visit to nutrition sites; 13. Strengthen a community complain and feedback mechanism in all Nutrition sites; 14. Conduct anthropometric and mortality SMART surveys and needs assessment; 15. Provide WASH kits to caretakers of children diagnosed with SAM/MAM; 16. Provide timely nutrition reports/data/updates from the project locations to Nutrition cluster and attend cluster meetings at all levels.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

Carrying out need assessment will help us understand Information sharing and feedback mechanisms in order to adapt information to the literacy levels amongst specific vulnerable groups to avoid excluding vulnerable groups. As part of the program design, risk assessment will be conducted to ensure Tearfund work does not cause harm to vulnerable people. The information tools will be contextualised and explained to generate understanding and be relevant. The project will disaggregate data to match the project affected population or sub-groups where practical actions will be embedded into planning and delivery of integrated nutrition services to ensure inclusivity and needs of all gender groups. The engagement of the vulnerable groups will help us to understand expectations, involve them in the decision making process and enable us to receive timely feedback. The steps will help address the needs of the most vulnerable in reducing inequalities and help everyone benefit equally from the project.Tearfund will build and strengthen the capacity of staff, partners and relevant stakeholders to integrate gender and intersectionality into project design, planning, implementation and monitoring and review of project messages. Key messages on gender, disability, social inclusion will be developed and translated into the local language to guide staff as they implement activities. The project will work closely with the local actors to identify the individuals with mobility needs and mentally handicapped to facilitate timely provision of support.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

To support better performance and Nutrition outcomes at the community, Tearfund and partners will ground their response in the needs, priorities and expectations of the beneficiaries through a number of approaches for example, by engaging in dialogue and joint analysis with beneficiaries on feedback and complaint mechanisms to empower the communities in ways to communicate their needs and concerns and provide the space to make decisions that affect them to enable beneficiaries to act on their own independently. Communities will be included in the leadership of Nutrition response activities and community participation in monitoring and evaluating response programs. Complaint help desks will help in providing basic assistance, psychosocial support and counselling to returnees/IDPs/ host communities through the use of community volunteers at the community centres and sites so that the communities can provide regular feedback. Additionally, referral pathways will provide the communities with information on the existing service providers, their services and locations and how to access them.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

Tearfund will strengthen staff capacity on PSEA and will share with beneficiaries information about protection from sexual exploitation and abuse during all project activities. In targeted locations, Tearfund will work with GBV actors and community based protection actors to spread awareness about the GBV referral pathway including a range of services for survivors. Tearfund will collaborate with protection cluster to provide information that seeks to prevent and respond to abuse, neglect, exploitation and violence against women, men, boys and girls, and ensure that a comprehensive complaint mechanism is in place and sagefuarding reports and concerns are investigated. Tearfund will develop Information Education Materials (IEC) and disseminate information to the beneficiaries at the host communities/IDPs/Returnees on the available service providers, locations and services available. The IEC materials will also convey knowledge and skills to young men to adopt gender equitable behaviour.Tearfund has safeguarding policies in place, the staff will be trained on PSEA to undertake safeguarding initiatives to strengthen local capacities and assure their full compliance and understanding on safeguarding policies and practices. PSEA messages together with entitlement cards will be translated into local language to ensure transparency and accountability. Tearfund will embed a robust and timely referral mechanism and form linkages with national child protection actors to refer any identified child protection case.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

Tearfund will gather data about communications channels in communicating with communities and consider their rights and entitlements in order to identify feedback mechanisms to be adapted in delivering food assistance in emergencies. Tearfund will analyse information provided by the returnees/IDPs and host communities through the use of field staff and community volunteers to triangulate the information analysis for service improvement. Tearfund will look at other types of information such as, life saving information, where people can access support, people’s rights and entitlements, sexual exploitation and abuse to the communities, security risks, corruption etc to provide adjustments if necessary throughout the project. Information on people’s rights will take into consideration international humanitarian standards (Sphere, HAP, IASC GBV guidelines, Red cross code of conduct) in the provision of impartial and timely access to services and right to protection. Tearfund will ensure beneficiaries participate in decision-making concerning activities and to lead or support the implementation of activities.Tearfund will undertake feedback and review meetings with PMC on a regular basis to identify challenges and gaps to integrate learnings and improve project implementation.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

The project will provide sustainable and high-quality standard services with a disaster risk reduction component and integrated WASH component. There is a need to provide climate-proofing nutrition infrastructure, drainage systems, services, and promote behaviours to build community resilience against flood-related displacement. Through the hygiene promotion activities, Tearfund will enhance beneficiaries coping mechanisms and local individual measures to build resiliencies to the concurrent shocks through identifying and implementing various flood mitigation measures to reduce risk and exposure during flooding, working to create flood friendly environments, improve the community’s ability to respond during a flood, and identifying strategies for recovery after a flood. This project will address live-saving needs in disaster contexts in restoring immediate access to basic WASH services either temporarily or permanently. The project will also target locations that were affected by flooding in the past three years. It aims at addressing the needs of the most vulnerable with durable solutions and community engagement in finding solutions and maintenance of facilities.

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

Mindful of these unequal power relations fueled by negative social norms, Tearfund will facilitate awareness raising and gender sensitization. This will include targeted information dissemination to ensure GBV risk mitigation, and facilitating awareness of the referral pathways for existing GBV services within the target locations. Tearfund’s needs analysis revealed that women have limited mobility because of the reported risk of attack when travelling within and outside their communities. This coupled with social limitations around access and control of income implies that women cannot access and benefit from resources and services which are mostly located outside their communities. Nutrition activities will take into account the security and safety needs of women and girls, ensuring that all activities are carried out in a secure location and during the day. The exact timing will take into account the most convenient times for women and girls, in order to ensure that activities do not add to the already heavy burden of household chores.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

While a majority of HHs in the three Counties rely on markets as a source of food in the lean season, markets are performing at suboptimal levels, access is constrained, and the variety of food and other goods available is limited. Tearfund will integrate nutrition with ongoing BHA funded food security and livelihood projects and provide agricultural inputs along with training on field preparation, sowing and transplanting, weeding, use and conservation of resources and pest control to reduce crop losses to vulnerable HHs to increase HH food production. It will focus on organic agricultural practices that have access to proper resources and learn best practices that are more likely to generate much higher crop yields. This allows farmers to not only secure enough for their own needs, but also to sell in local markets. In turn, this enhances the family’s livelihood, strengthens community resilience and furthers the local economy. It will also enable the local communities to earn money and provide for the basic needs of their family. Furthermore, Tearfund will strengthen MIYCN counselling and hygiene promotion activities to boost their knowledge on the importance of diversified and healthy diets for better nutrition.
