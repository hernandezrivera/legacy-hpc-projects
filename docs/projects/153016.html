Project Summary

The conflict in South Sudan continues to have negative impact on access to quality education for children and youth. As the conflict continued into 2018, the displacement, attacks on school, as well as hunger and economic crisis, continued to deprive children of education opportunities. An estimated 2.2 million children are not attending school in South Sudan . The purpose of the project is to ensure conflict-affected children and youths in South Sudan continuously access lifesaving and quality learning opportunities that are relevant to their psychosocial, emotional and cognitive development in a safe, inclusive and protected environment.
NRC will work to ensure that conflict-affected children and youth in conflict affected areas of Jonglei, Unity, Eastern Equatoria, Central Equatoria, Western Bahr el Ghazal, have access to life saving education, have strengthened resilience to current and future crises, and can contribute to the peaceful socio-economic development of South Sudan. The project will focus on ensuring access to safe and protective education for IDPs and returnees, strengthening and improving quality in the delivery of the Education for vulnerable children and Youth. This will be achieved through three strategic response areas: 1) support to formal basic education in static operations; 2) first phase response through the mobile education Rapid Response Mechanism (RRM). And 3) Alternative Education Programme (AEP) for children and youth who have missed out on education due to conflict. The project will coordinate with the Education Cluster and MoGEI to support examination registration for Primary 8 (P8) students in POCs; implement school feeding programmes in areas without general food distribution. It will also construct or rehabilitate learning spaces, existing classrooms, gender-segregated latrines, and water points and storage tanks. It will enhance teacher professional development to improve the quality of teaching and learning and enhance teachers’ use of child-centred, inclusive teaching methods and positive discipline, improve their subject knowledge, and develop teachers’ skills in formative assessment and classroom management.
The project will respond to the education needs of children in the most emergency-affected and hard-to-reach locations in South Sudan as part of a wider multi-sectoral rapid response mission. This includes conducting needs assessments within two weeks of a rapid onset emergency, and being able to initiate an emergency response within the subsequent twenty days. NRC’s Rapid Response Team (RRT) will work in coordination with the Education Cluster and the Inter-Cluster Working Group to respond in hard-to-reach locations, which are less visible but have significant needs but insufficient education actors. The RRT will target conflict-prone areas of the Greater Equatoria region, and position itself for responses in former Unity and Jonglei states, where access and security protocols can be met. The project will be delivered in an integrated manner to ensure safety and protection of girls in schools through the provision of dignity kits to girls, segregated WASH facilities for boys and girls, clean school environment, sensitisation of school communities on protection, child safeguarding, teachers’ professional code of conduct, as well as referral pathways for reporting cases of abuse.

Needs

According to Global Initiative on Out of School Children: South Sudan Country Study (UNESCO 2018), an estimated 2.2 million children are not attending school in South Sudan and this number is expected to increase to 2.6 million in two years, if conditions in the country do not improve. Children also experience widespread incidences of sexual violence: nearly 25% of documented incidents of conflict-related sexual violence affect children. The conflict also increased the incidences of girls being married early. Additional barriers to girls’ attendance and performance in school include: economic and social demands such as household chores and caring for younger siblings; strong cultural norms that favour boys’ education when a family has limited resources; insufficient numbers of female teachers to act as role models; and inadequate school sanitation facilities. Implementation of the education policy and plans are affected by lack of funding and high inflation rates. As a result, the essential inputs required for the provision of equitable, quality education such as qualified teachers, female teachers, text books and learning materials, learning spaces for Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs) and returnees, and water and sanitation facilities in schools remain in extremely limited supply. Also, long distances to schools, incomplete schools, unsafe schools, and little to no support services in schools for children with disabilities are key barriers to access and inclusion. 67% of teachers are untrained or have unknown teaching qualifications according to the Education Cluster Assessment, South Sudan (2016). Also, currency devaluation and high inflation rates have greatly reduced the real value of government funding for schools. Classroom teachers’ wages, worth $100 a month at Independence, are now worth less than $5 a month according to Hodgkin E & Thomas E (2016) ‘Education and Conflict in South Sudan’. Humanitarian Practice Network According to the Rapid Needs Assessment carried by NRC in October 2018 the crisis has increased vulnerability of school communities in locations targeted by this project due to internal displacement, food insecurity, malnutrition, protection risks (e.g. recruitment by armed groups, sexual exploitation and abuse, physical violence) and disruption of family bounds among others. The effect of crisis has denied children opportunities to access inclusive quality education. Besides, the joint assessment conducted by Education Cluster in September 2018 reveals that 20% of schools are not in operation due to insecurity, lack of essential teaching and learning materials as well as displacement of teachers and learners. In addition, up to 95% of teachers did not receive salaries in the last 6-10 months. High levels of food insecurity in South Sudan have manifested themselves in increased hunger at the household level, which has led to children’s drop-out from school. The quality of education in South Sudan continues to be of concern , and the lack of government financing and deteriorating economic conditions have led to schools imposing school fees. Youth often lack the basic education level, skills, trades and assets or capital support to enable them to find employment or self-employment opportunities. The situation is exacerbated by the disruption of markets in South Sudan. This leaves youth vulnerable to negative coping mechanisms and becoming involved in the conflict through their lack of agency, empowerment or alternative options. Data from the recent joint education needs assessment by the education cluster in September 2018 indicates that up to 57% of learners in Unity were not present in school on the day of assessment. Besides, the education indicators in Mayom County of former Unity state remains very low with only 33% of children able to access education facilities and 17% of assessed settlement indicates that the education facilities were destroyed by the past conflict (REACH, 2018).

Activities

Key Education in Emergency (EiE)/ALP activities proposed and targeting Primary schools include;• Conducting community sensitization meetings on the importance of sending children to school especially girls in all the locations – EiE and ALP• Establishment/rehabilitation of Temporary Learning Spaces (TLS)• Constructing gender segregated latrines (separate for boys, girls and teachers) and installation of hand washing facilities in schools – EiE and ALP. • Construction of schools’ meals stores and basic kitchen facility • Procurement and supplies of students, teachers and schools’ kits – EiE and ALP. • Procurement and distribution of recreational materials to all the targeted schools – EiE and ALP.• Menstrual hygiene education and dignity kits distribution to adolescent girls in schools. • Training of teachers in Education in Emergency, Gender Responsive Pedagogy for primary and ALP schools, PSEA, and PSS – EiE and ALP• Establish and train PTAs and SMC in schools – EiE and ALP.• Provision of classroom teaching and learning materials like scholastic materials for pupils and teachers (including scheme of work and lesson plan books) – EiE and ALP. • Establishment of children clubs like hygiene clubs, etc. in schools and train them on peacebuilding and gender, etc – EiE and ALP• Support to education campaign events like awareness on girl-child education, etc.• Support and monitoring/supervision of teachers and schools.• Support to success stories development and documentation.

Strategic Objective 1

Accountability to affected population
Does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

Yes

How does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

NRC will ensure that the project will systematically engage with all beneficiaries and relevant stakeholders, including the most marginalised, to deliver information about assistance to be provided in such a way as to maximise meaningful participation and consultation. Information shared will include the scope of the proposed interventions, entitlements of those involved, clarify any targeting of vulnerable groups, and information essential to accessing companion services, as well as other relevant information. NRC will consult with communities about the optimal way to share information, and provide information in forms accessible to those with limited literacy and in local languages, to the extent that it is possible. Taking precautions to make the project as transparent and inclusive as possible will both service to make the program more inclusive and contribute to a sense of local ownership, as well as mitigate possible negative consequences of targeting or prioritising vulnerable persons and minority groups.

Are messages context-specific or/and tailored?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Messages are both context-specific and tailored for gender and age-groups. In particular, NRC revisits all protection messages after each protection needs assessment, to ensure that messages are addressing identified needs and targeted to the most vulnerable groups. Messages are particularly tailored to reach children, people with disabilities, and older persons. NRC strives to include local context in its messages, such as alerting beneficiaries to existing referral pathways for complementary services. NRC also considers messages from a Do No Harm perspective, moderating messages as appropriate.

Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse

Yes

Briefly describe how

NRC takes prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse very seriously. All NRC staff engaged in the project will be trained in PSEA; further, staff will be trained in PFA and identification of potential survivors of SEA, and receive training and mentoring in how to respond appropriately and with empathy. All communications with affected populations will stress their right to receive services without coercion or exploitation. NRC will work with communities to develop confidential, appropriate complaints and feedback mechanisms, and respond to any complaints promptly and transparently, to the extent that we are still able to assume both the survivor and perpetrator of their safety. NRC will strive to recruit gender-balanced teams and take every effort to ensure that vulnerable women are able to work directly with competent woman staff.

Nature of the consultations

Yes

Briefly describe the nature of the consultations

NRC believes that all projects must be informed by and reflect the voices of the communities in which we work. In preparation for this proposal, NRC conducted several gender-sensitive protection needs assessments, targeting not only community leaders, but also people with disabilities, older persons, recently displaced people, young mothers, and female-headed households. In all communication with communities, NRC stresses the importance of inclusion and seeking out a diversity of respondents. It is essential that those affected by the programme also have the means to affect it in turn, and NRC teams actively seek to consult a wide range of persons, most explicitly those from vulnerability groups we target with our programming.

Feedback /complaint mechanisms?

Yes

Briefly describe the mechanisms

The project will offer several complains and feedback mechanisms, depending on the needs of the community. In initial assessments, NRC will consult with targeted persons about the best approach to establish Complaint and Feedback Mechanisms and strive to be responsible and flexible in our implementation.

Community Engagement?

Yes

Briefly describe how

In line with NRC’s global commitment to ensure that all aspects of humanitarian intervention – including monitoring and evaluation – are informed by and reflect the voices of the communities with which we work, NRC will ensure that monitoring and evaluation activities are inclusive and accessible. Whether utilising IPA case closure and evaluation forms, PDM tools, or more general community evaluation FGDs and KIIs, NRC teams will seek to engage a range of community members from both sexes, all relevant ages, and across a range of diversity characteristics. In assessment stages and throughout implementation, NRC teams will discuss with communities the ideal mechanisms for monitoring and evaluation to ensure inclusive approaches. In hard to reach areas, or those with emergency responses that might mitigate the kinds of monitoring and evaluation approaches possible in more static locations, NRC will take particular care to discuss with communities safe, realistic options for monitoring and evaluation and means of improving accountability to affected populations.

Protection Risk Analysis?

Yes

Briefly describe how

NRC conducted protection needs assessment in several locations targeted by the project, both those intended to be served by static and mobile activities. Primary and secondary data collected during the assessments informed a protection risk analysis that particularly considered the needs of women, recently displaced persons, older persons, persons with disabilities.

Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Based on NRC’s protection needs assessment and protection risk analysis, we carefully considered a range of possible barriers to meaningful access during the design of the implementation, including security, financial, and perceived discrimination. NRC’s analysis reveals that it is likely beneficiaries will face a diversity of barriers, particularly according to AGD-related issues and local conflict dynamics. For example, a newly arrived IDP women in Wau, some single young educated young man in Akobo, and an unaccompanied elderly person Yei are all likely to face sever access constraints, if for different reasons (assumption of discrimination, fear of physical assault, mobility limitations). NRC will approach its implementation with nuance, recognising that its target groups are likely to encounter a range of access constraints that NRC teams will need to be aware of and reactive to. Teams will regularly re-evaluate access constraints throughout the intervention to ensure that beneficiaries are consistently able to reach, receive, and benefit from services.

Addresses inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

The project will ensure protection of students from harm especially sexual exploitation and gender based violence while at schools or on their way schools working in collaboration with PTSs and school authorities.

Project activities address inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

In recognising that every individual faces a unique array of protection risks, depending upon their own vulnerabilities, capacities, and external threats, NRC conducted a gender-sensitive needs assessment, consulting more than 100 women, girls, men and boys in targeted areas, including older men and women, female-headed households, and persons with disabilities of both sexes. The assessment identified a range of individualised protection threats and potential inequalities that NRC is considering in programming interventions and approach modalities. An example of one such consideration is that that men must be explicitly targeted and significantly engaged. In all target locations, a majority of respondents reported that men enjoy sole control over assets and decision-making in households At the same time, respondents across gender and location reported high rates of GBV. A failure to proactively engage men will likely result in significant harm to women and girls.

Does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

NRC prioritises the safety of beneficiaries in all activities. Thus, all assessment and monitoring tools take questions of community and individual safety as mandatory. Likewise, all M&E tools will ask about improvements in safety generally, and related to the intervention. Beneficiaries will be asked, before, during, and after an intervention what steps would contribute to feelings of safety, and if they felt that NRC was failing to account for safety in meaningful ways. Beyond perceptions of safety, protection monitors will be asked to report on specific instances of rights violations, both related to NRC interventions and those otherwise occurring in targeted communities. NRC will also include an internal incident reporting mechanism for staff to monitor our own observations regarding beneficiary safety.
