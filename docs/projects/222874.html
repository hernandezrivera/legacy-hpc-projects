Project Summary

"The program complements formal schooling with the Myanmar curriculum for the Rohingya refugees and with the National curriculum for the Bangladeshis by providing life skills education (LSE) and other extracurricular activities, including on Child Marriage (CM) prevention to adolescents in Rohingya refugee camps and in host communities in Cox’s Bazar by helping the formal school and out-of-school learners develop essential abilities to efficiently navigate life's challenges.
UNFPA follows the eight elements of transformative LSE programmes that address gender inequities and empower girls & boys. When adolescent-centered programming is implemented using the protective asset-building approach in education, it can create a virtuous circle meeting the immediate & strategic needs of adolescents, so that they can navigate their transition from adolescence to adulthood in a safe, healthy & fulfilled way.(UNFPA-UNICEF, 2019).
UNFPA will implement the Girl Shine Program for adolescent girls and the Champions of Change program for adolescent boys which were tested in the Rohingya refugee context and developed adhering to international methodologies and standards to equip the participants with skills & knowledge to tackle the lack of access to education and age-appropriate activities, which both exacerbate and result from protection e.g., GBV & CM and health e.g., sexual & reproductive health (SRH) issues.
The program will reach 12,160 LSE learners (F: 6,080; M: 6,080) aged 10 to 18, among Rohingya refugees and children from host communities including 365 learners with disabilities (3%) in 23 learning facilities of UNFPA's Adolescent & Youth Centers, and in 46 host community schools to provide the LSE. UNFPA will also pilot digital LSE and explore the feasibility of offering contextualized LSE into the formal education system, leveraging the learning facilities and schools as the safe spaces. Throughout the year, UNFPA will focus on sensitizing school headmasters, building the capacity of teachers, youth mentors, and other education stakeholders on the specified LSE topics.
The program will also target the caregivers. A social-ecological model involving the whole-of-family approach will be adopted. The approach aims to mobilize family units to catalyze social norm change within their communities while also providing the adolescents who undergo LSE programs with a conducive environment to put into practice what they have learned.
UNFPA will raise awareness and provide education to caregivers, adolescents, and the community at large to address sociocultural barriers hindering adolescents, especially girls, from participating in formal and informal learning opportunities, including deterring CM. The focus will be on marginalized girls who are often pulled out of educational activities upon reaching maturity, increasing their risk of CM and negatively impacting their protection & health outcomes.
Additionally, interventions will be implemented to ensure girls can manage menstrual health & hygiene with dignity, including distribution of MHM kits, sessions on reusable pad production, and ensuring access to MHM-friendly toilets. Provision of MHM support will enable girls to access protection, health and education services. Conditional cash transfers will be given to families of vulnerable girls in host communities to prevent dropouts due to economic reasons and/or child marriage.
Finally, based on latest findings of research, including on child marriage, UNFPA will implement innovative activities that engage community and raise awareness on GBV, CM, SRH, and other issues and challenge preexisting harmful gender norms. This will include Sports for Development and Theater for Development activities, and other creative modalities for community engagement. These activities also serve the purpose of providing adolescents with positive recreational and social activities to improve their overall wellbeing."

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

According to ISNA 2024, 59% of children aged 3 to 18 are enrolled in and accessing education services from various learning facilities. However, enrollment rates are much lower for older adolescents: only 8% of girls and 30% of boys aged 15 to 18 years are attending educational institutions. The main reasons for the high rate of out of education adolescents are age-related and economic constraints. Age-related constraints, particularly for girls, often stem from harmful social norms that restrict their access to education. These norms may prioritize early marriage or domestic responsibilities over education, leading to higher dropout rates among older girls. In addition, economic constraints, discrimination, the limited access to quality education, and the increased vulnerability to child marriage further hinder adolescents' ability to continue their education. The 2023 MSNA data supports these findings, revealing a significant gender gap in educational participation. Girls aged 12 to 18 years have the lowest enrollment and irregular attendance rates, at just 21%, compared to 51% for adolescent boys and 85-90% for children aged 5 to 11 years. Adolescent girls also most commonly miss education because education is not a priority (21%), she needs to help out at home (19%), there is a lack of male-female separation in facilities(18%), or she has reached puberty (9%) or is already married/pregnant (8%). The Menstrual Health & Management Survey and FGDs conducted by UNFPA (2023) corroborate this as parents and caregivers opposed the continued education of adolescent girls after their menarche, resulting in limited access to educational opportunities, as well as economic, social and recreational opportunities. A report by NRC in 2022 revealed that refugee adolescents and youth remain mostly on the margins of humanitarian programming with limited interventions that target and are responsive and appropriate to the developmental phase of the age group. This contributes to the increased rate of child marriage, participation in informal (child) labor, and negative coping mechanisms like drug peddling. Rohingya adolescent girls also experience a disproportionate burden of poor SRH, including high rates of GBV, child marriage, maternal mortality, unintended pregnancies and unsafe abortions. These risks are exacerbated by the lack of information on and sensitive nature of GBV & SRH topics leading caregivers and other authority figures to provide limited or incorrect information to adolescents and youth on puberty and other topics. Child marriage is also quickly becoming a growing concern as both a form of and a contributing factor to GBV. FGDs and consultations with both Rohingyas and Bangladeshis in Cox’s Bazar also suggest that families, adolescents & young people resort to marriage and cohabitation as a response to the lack of educational, economic, and recreational opportunities. Inversely, global evidence suggests that keeping girls in school, both formal and in formal, significantly reduces their risk for child marriage. Finally, when it comes to leadership & participation, the majority of Rohingya youth (72.46%) feel they lack opportunities to contribute to their communities or take on leadership roles, with 35% of respondents reporting a lack of skills necessary for participation (ISNA-2024). Given this context , it is critical to provide access to LSE skills development opportunities to equip adolescents with knowledge, attitudes and values that will empower them to realize their well-being and dignity, develop respectful social and personal or intimate relationships, consider how their choices affect their own well-being and that of others, and ensure the protection of their rights throughout their lives. The program will also contribute to dismantling taboos around GBV to build a gender equal society and to contribute to the improvement of the enrollment and attendance rates of adolescent girls in education.

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

The project prioritizes the protection, safety, dignity, and rights of affected populations, following the principle of "do no harm." This principle is applied throughout the project cycle, including facilities, services, and outreach activities. UNFPA, through the project, will ensure that protection mechanisms and principles are in place through assessments, safety audits, and adherence to IASC protection standards across its supported facilities and services. Regular needs and vulnerability assessments, as well as participatory discussions with the target populations, will monitor this. These assessments' results will support vulnerable groups' special needs, such as children, adolescents, persons with disabilities, female-headed households, and youth-headed households. Lastly, UNFPA will promote the engagement of affected populations through community engagement and awareness efforts on protection topics and referral mechanisms.

2.3 - Age & Disability

UNFPA adheres to and supports partners' capacity development regarding the IASC Guidelines on Working with and for Young People in Humanitarian and Protracted Crises. This collaboration enables UNFPA to provide age-responsive and adolescent-friendly services, and possesses a range of resources, such as toolkits and checklists, focusing on disability inclusion. These resources are incorporated into UNFPA's planning, implementation, and monitoring mechanisms. Additionally, UNFPA facilitates the participation of people with disabilities and young people in its regular feedback and consultation processes to ensure their voices are heard and perspectives are included throughout the project's life cycle. Whenever feasible, the project aims to engage with community teachers from the refugee population. The project prioritizes recruiting staff with appropriate language, gender, age, and cultural competencies, including staff with disabilities, to work effectively with Rohingya populations. Collected data will be disaggregated by sex, age, and disability to enhance understanding of service uptake among various groups. The UNFPA Dedicated Youth Centers are constructed to be fully accessible to individuals with mobility challenges. Additionally, the program is working to enhance accessibility by having their programs through a digital offline version. This will allow participants to engage with the content from the comfort of their homes, with support from a peer leader to ensure a smooth and inclusive experience.In 2025, UNFPA will continue consultation with communities, and based on feedback from people with disabilities, necessary accommodations will be made to enhance the accessibility of services for individuals with various disabilities.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

As part of UNFPA's accountability to the affected populations (AAP), the organization engages with community members throughout the project lifecycle, from the design phase through implementation, monitoring, and evaluation. This engagement includes preparing for joint response plans (JRPs) using the following tools:Client satisfaction surveys for UNFPA-supported facilities and activities.Regular and diverse community engagement through focus group discussions (FGDs), interviews, courtyard sessions, meetings, and other feedback mechanisms with various target population groups. Bilateral dialogues and consultations with government officials and community leaders also facilitate engagement.Evidence generation for informed programming and decision-making.Accessible feedback channels, such as suggestion boxes in UNFPA-supported facilities and hotlines for PSEA complaints. Raising awareness among the community about these channels and mechanisms further enhances their effectiveness.Close collaboration with implementing partners (IPs) to regularly collect and utilize community feedback. This collaboration ensures that community voices are heard and considered in decision-making processes.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

UNFPA has a zero-tolerance policy against sexual exploitation & abuse (SEA) and sexual harassment (SH). Since 2017, UNFPA has been an active member of the ISCG PSEA Network in Cox's Bazar and has designated PSEA Focal Points for the organization. Partners selected by UNFPA are also required to be part of the PSEA network. UNFPA offers extensive training on PSEA, emphasizing the obligation to report any misconduct, to staff, non-staff personnel, IPs, vendors, and service providers. To facilitate reporting, UNFPA's Office of Audit & Investigation Services (OAIS) has implemented an online platform/hotline and designated PSEA focal points. UNFPA has an established policy to prevent retaliation against survivors, victims, and whistleblowers who come forward with reports. UNFPA conducts awareness-raising sessions within communities and strategically posts IEC materials in all supported facilities to ensure communities are informed about SEA and the available reporting mechanisms. When necessary, UNFPA conducts capacity assessments and provides support for implementing capacity-strengthening plans, ensuring IPs maintain the required PSEA and safeguarding mechanisms.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

UNFPA is committed to Disaster Risk Reduction/Management (DRR/DRM) standards, aligning its efforts with Government of Bangladesh's National Plan for Disaster Management (2021-2025) and the EEN Climate Action Strategy (2023-2025) for the Rohingya response. This commitment is reinforced by the adoption of the Social & Environmental Safeguarding principles, which serve as a compass, guiding the organization's strategies and interventions in climate change adaptation and mitigation. All actions are ethically sound, environmentally sustainable, and socially inclusive. In practical terms, UNFPA is implementing solar energy across all its youth centers, and the project prioritizes the installation of solar fans in community households to enhance comfort and sustainability. Furthermore, the construction of these youth centers took into consideration the availability of natural light, utilizing transparent roofs wherever applicable to maximize daylight use, in addition to planting three trees within the area of the facility. Recognizing that women and girls are disproportionately affected by disasters, the project ensures that their needs and perspectives are central to all DRR and climate change interventions. This includes promoting youth leadership in disaster preparedness and response, addressing gender-based violence during disasters, and providing reproductive health services in emergency settings. Finally, UNFPA's dedication to DRR, climate change adaptation, and mitigation is driven by the belief that every individual has the right to live in a safe and healthy environment. The organization's commitment to Social & Environmental Safeguarding principles ensures that all interventions are conducted ethically and responsibly, leaving no one behind.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

Within the framework of the climate change adaptation and mitigation plan, emergency preparedness and response (EPR) measures are incorporated as integral components of the project. Firstly, Community Mobilizers supported by UNFPA are equipped to respond to disaster-related emergencies during the preparedness, acute, and post-disaster phases. Additionally, UNFPA's interventions, such as its Life Skills Education program, integrate basic disaster risk reduction (DRR) information. UNFPA-supported facilities, including Adolescent and Youth Centers, serve as temporary shelters during emergencies, offering access to adolescent-friendly and gender-sensitive DRR preparedness and response information. Secondly, UNFPA proactively stockpiles tailored Dignity Kits and emergency Menstrual Hygiene Management (MHM) Kits to ensure prompt distribution to women and girls and during emergencies mitigates their vulnerability. Thirdly, it prioritizes the development of community recovery and resilience plans (incorporating preparedness) for hazardous events, integrating DRR and climate change adaptation (CCA), in a manner that leverages the skills and recommendations of the young people and the community. Lastly, to uphold best practices in DRR, climate change adaptation, and mitigation, UNFPA adheres to Social & Environmental Safeguarding principles.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

The project follows UNFPA’s global strategy for adolescents and youth, placing young people at the heart of its programs and embracing the rich diversity of all adolescents (aged 10 to 19) and youth (aged 15 to 24). It recognizes the varied needs and aspirations of this demographic and is dedicated to addressing the inequities they face. Designed in accordance with the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) guidelines on working with and for young people in humanitarian and protracted crises, the project involves youth throughout the humanitarian response process—from the initial design stage to needs assessment, implementation, monitoring, and evaluation. This engagement includes: i) Hold consultations with young people – grouped by age and sex of participants – to identify needs and barriers to education.ii) Build links in the response plan between learning and future employment and livelihood activities.iii) Offer flexible learning opportunities to young people, as they may have limited time available to attend education due to family or livelihood commitments.iv) Give young people opportunities for employment as volunteers, mentors and trainers,etc., within education and training programs. By co-leading the Youth Working Group (YWG) under the inter-agency coordination mechanism, UNFPA ensures that the project remains aligned with current best practices for coordinated programming for Rohingya and Bangladeshi host community youth. The YWG is dedicated to advocating for the youth agenda and ensuring that the needs and capacities of young people are integrated at strategic and operational levels across various sectors of the humanitarian response. It promotes the use of the IASC Guidelines across relevant sectors and prioritizes meaningful youth participation in its activities and decision-making processes.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

It seeks to cultivate self-reliance & resilience in refugees, particularly girls & women, equipping them with the necessary skills they need to navigate their lives successfully. Upon graduating from the LSE program, these girls & women gain access to a range of opportunities that empower them to take charge of their lives. Adolescents are linked to relevant volunteer opportunities, which provide them with a platform to contribute their skills & talents to their communities. This hands-on experience helps them develop a sense of purpose & builds their confidence. Additionally, the project connects them to livelihood opportunities, such as vocational training, mentorship programs etc. These initiatives aim to create sustainable sources of income for the adolescents & women, enabling them to support themselves & their families. By linking them to these opportunities, the project not only supports their immediate needs but also invests in their long-term well-being & self-sufficiency.

4.2 Do project activities in the host community link to development initiatives, and/or align with development priorities in the Cox’s Bazar district? If yes, briefly describe.If yes, briefly describe.

The project will increase the capacity of educational institutions to expand life skills education and adolescent-responsive sexual and reproductive health services for in-school and out-of-school adolescents and youth, including by incorporating life skills education more comprehensively into policy frameworks. It will enhance the livelihood and life skills knowledge among girls, including married girls, by promoting job fairs within Cox's Bazar. Additionally, it will strengthen community engagement in advancing gender-equitable attitudes and improving access to life skills education, while establishing and expanding youth platforms to foster meaningful youth participation and leadership.
