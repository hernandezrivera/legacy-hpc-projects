Project Summary

UNICEF will provide access to and deliver quality, inclusive and equitable early childhood development, pre-primary, primary and secondary education, and skills development opportunities for 283,500 children (50% girls) refugee crisis-affected children and adolescents aged 3-18 years in the camps and 2,160 in Teknaf and Ukhyia upazilas of Cox’s Bazar District.
UNICEF will implement Myanmar formal education curriculum for kindergarten to grade 10 as well as ALP for the over-aged learners, when appropriate, by working with implementing partners (IPs) on learners’ enrolment, attendance, transition of learners to the next grade, and teachers capacity development as well as community engagement. 4,096 learning facilities (LFs) will provide access to the necessary teaching and learning materials.
For inclusive education, UNICEF, in partnership with Humanity Inclusion, will ensure inclusive practices are mainstreamed by providing services directly to children, capacitating partners, teachers and the community, Rohingya disability volunteers will be assigned per camp, teachers will receive pedagogy training for working with children with disabilities, model LCs will be established to serve as examples as well as a policy for children with moderate disabilities will be developed.
For equitable education, UNICEF will mainstream gender-responsive education activities through capacity development of key stakeholders, teacher training on gender-responsive pedagogy, and strategies for improving the attendance of adolescent female learners (AFL). Access to education for AFL will be facilitated by providing gender-segregated classes for which female Rohingya volunteers will be assigned; they will walk with the AFLs to and from the LCs and attend classes with them.
For quality education, teacher professional development (TPD) programs will be implemented for teachers and master trainers (MTs) to teach the Myanmar curriculum for KG to grade 10. Teachers from KG to grade 10 will receive grade and subject-specific TPD to improve pedagogical skills and content knowledge. Teachers will also be enrolled in language courses and general pedagogy training and receive PSEA, Child Safeguarding, and DRR training. Mentor teachers and master trainers will be available to guide and support teachers further. For learning assessment, teachers will receive training to conduct formative assessments during the year, and children in grade 3 and higher will be given summative exams for transition to the next grade. A record of learning will be provided for enrolled children upon completion of the school year. The use of technology for education (T4E) will support taking attendance and recording learning assessment data, TPD and support teachers with interactive audio lessons based on the Myanmar curriculum.
Community engagement will continue with Rohingya caregivers and the community education support groups (CESGs), who will be established per cluster per sector guidance to meet monthly. The Rohingya caregivers, the CEGSs, and implementing partners (IP) staff will receive training on PSEA, Child Safeguarding, gender and girls’ education, disability and inclusive education, and DRR. Capacity development of IP staff will also include training on data management to meet the Data Quality Assessment standards for reliability and integrity.
UNICEF will implement skill programmes for out-of-school (OOS) adolescents through cross-sectoral interventions in Multi-Purpose Centres; those aged 15 to 18 years will have access to Lifeskills as well as literacy and numeracy courses.
In the host communities of Teknaf and Ukhyia, UNICEF will support 2160 adolescents, including those with disabilities, in accessing vocational skills.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

As the Rohingya response enters the 7th year, with the military coup in Myanmar, the return of Rohingya can take longer, and in the meantime, the number of Rohingya children continues to grow, with approximately 30,000 new-borns every year. With the growing number of Rohingya children and with their movement being restricted to the camps and without refugee status, only the humanitarian response can meet their growing needs. In such a context, education continues to be a crucial need for the Rohingya community, as access to quality education can reduce instances of exploitation and support community resilience with positive coping mechanisms. With the education needs growing, the reduction in funding will pose a great risk to ensuring quality education is available for all children. There will be an increased need for learning spaces as the change in curriculum requires more classrooms, and the growing number of children who will transition from one grade to the next will also require more learning spaces to be available. Along with learning spaces, having sufficiently qualified teachers is a challenge. It will take time to train all teachers to improve their language and pedagogy skills as well as for them to develop a stronger grasp of the required content knowledge. Finding the needed number of qualified teachers to teach higher secondary, especially female teachers, is currently very challenging. Having insufficiently qualified teachers conducting lessons can further exasperate the perception of education that adolescents share, where they do not see education as a priority (JMSNA, 2023). Along with ensuring qualified teachers are available in the learning facilities, efforts need to be made to change adolescents' perceptions of education. IRC report further highlighted the state of learning of Rohingya children by pointing to key education needs: gender responsive and inclusive education, teacher recruitment as there is a lack of qualified teachers for Myanmar curriculum and mitigating further perpetuating the marginalization of the Rohingya community. With this project proposal, UNICEF is looking to address these challenges by advocating for learning spaces, capacitating teachers and IP staff, and continuing effective community engagement activities. Cox’s Bazar district is one of the low-performing districts in Bangladesh regarding access to education and learners’ performance, with the lowest Net Enrolment Rate (NER) (91.39%) in Bangladesh. The school enrolment rate of children with disabilities is very low (1400 students), and teachers and school management committees are not equipped with the skills and knowledge for inclusive education. The primary survival completion rate is between 74.10- 78.60%, with an overall student dropout rate of around 22% - this points to the need for skills-based education for older adolescents who dropped out of formal education. The latest national student assessment results for Grades 3 and 5, covering Bangla and mathematics, showed the performance of students in Cox’s Bazar was among the lowest in Bangladesh, with a combined mean score of 233 points as compared to the national average of 274 points. Post COVID, there has been limited change in primary grade level competencies in Bangla and mathematics - this implies the education children are receiving at the primary level is not adequate, and there is a learning crisis (National Student Assessment, 2022).

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

UNHCR will be handing over 320 learning centres and 526 community-based learning facilities to UNICEF, and these facilities will start to be managed in January 2024. UNICEF and IPs finalized the assignment of camps per partner, taking into account facilities from UNHCR. Only one partner is assigned per block, and only one to two partners are present in each camp. This means that there shouldn't be two providers of education programs in the same block. The management of the newly assigned facilities will start in January 2024.With the rationalisation, teachers and volunteer workers will remain in their learning facilities for program continuity, which will help ensure children do not experience any drastic changes. Along with streamlining program delivery at the field level, UNICEF is also harmonizing and standardizing indicators, positions, salaries and common budget items to ensure consistency and predictability across different implementing partners.To adopt an integrated approach, UNICEF will continue to work in conjunction with our Child Protection (CP) team in MPCs, with shared partners, and work with CP to ensure protection measures and referrals across the learning centres. Additionally, UNICEF will also begin to use spaces belonging to other sectors/agencies, such as UNWOMEN spaces, as an integrated approach. To ensure predictability and continuity, UNICEF will work with existing partners across the camps, with the national partners leading the operationalization of services. Integrated ECD facilities, which will include nutrition screening. In addressing the needs of vulnerable adolescents, UNICEF and implementing partners set up MPCs as one-stop hubs for adolescents to access an integrated package of services. The package includes training on basic literacy and numeracy skills, life skills and vocational skills, psychosocial support, case management, and community-based child protection.

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

UNICEF engages with the education sector, protection sector, child protection (CP) sub-sector, and relevant stakeholders to ensure that accessible reporting and referral mechanisms that support a survivor-informed response to incident notifications are in place. For example, UNICEF works in conjunction with our CP team to ensure protection measures and referrals across the learning centres and multi-purpose centres. Protection mainstreaming focuses on the four key elements:-Prioritising safety and dignity by providing safe and secure learning facilities where IP staff and teachers are trained in child safeguarding and PSEA reduces vulnerability to physical and psychosocial risks. Additionally, Rohingya volunteers are specifically available to ensure safety and dignity of children with disabilities and adolescent female learners, two vulnerable groups. Rohingya disability volunteers will be assigned to each camp. Rohingya female volunteers will be assigned to AFL classes; they walk with the AFLs to and back from the learning centres and attend classes to ensure safety. -Ensuring meaningful access to education for all through focused efforts for improving and retaining children with disabilities and adolescent female learners through targeted strategies, capacity development, and community engagement. -Fostering accountability by ensuring feedback is regularly collected by implementing partners from the Rohingya community, teachers, and learners, as well as monitoring visits and spot checks conducted by UNICEF and third-party monitoring institution. -Strengthening participation and empowerment through community engagement activities and capacity development of the community as well as IP staff. CESGs are an example of how UNICEF and implementing partners ensure that affected populations know their right to education, including where/how to access education services and are supported, involved, and consulted about the education of Rohingya children.

3.3 - Age & Disability

UNICEF will provide access to and deliver quality, inclusive early childhood education, primary and secondary education, and skills development opportunities for refugee crisis-affected children and adolescents aged 3-18 years. Adults aged 18 or above will be employed as volunteers in the schools. For the JRP 2024, UNICEF will reach 90% of children aged 5-14, 35% of children aged 4, 25% of children aged 3 and 30% of children aged 15-18. The reach for children with disabilities across all age groups and programs is 2% UNICEF will focus on the inclusion of Children with Disabilities in schools, aiming to reach 2% of all enrolled learners. Activities will include outreach in communities to identify children who may have a disability through Rohingya Disability Volunteers and then screening these children using the Child Functioning Module of the Washington Group Questions (WGQ). Following this, children identified as having a disability will be referred to Humanity Inclusion (Handicap International) for further assessment and to identify if assistive devices or other reasonable accommodations are required to support that child. The Rohingya Disability Volunteers will advocate with parents to allow Children with Disabilities to access education and will work with the children and HI to put in place reasonable accommodations, including home learning, if this is required. Disability and inclusion will be mainstreamed across interventions such as teacher training and collaborative and cooperative pedagogical techniques.

3.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

UNICEF’s work in the context of the Rohingya response, as with all emergency contexts, is informed by the Core Commitments to Children in Humanitarian Action, Accountability to Affected Population (AAP) and Building Back Better principles. For accountability to the affected population, UNICEF will continue to strengthen the feedback mechanisms deployed by implementing partners. These mechanisms include feedback from the monthly meetings of community education support groups, meetings with caregivers, teacher learning circles and the suggestion boxes that are placed at the learning centres. Additionally, UNICEF staff conduct field monitoring visits; a third-party firm is also contracted to conduct monitoring visits to learning centres. UNICEF officers and specialists follow up with partners to ensure appropriate actions are taken to address feedback from these mechanisms in a timely manner. Implementing partners are also held accountable by UNICEF and are assessed for responsiveness and timely actions that are taken to address feedback.

3.5.1 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

A zero-tolerance policy and approach to the exploitation and abuse of children and young people is in place to ensure the protection of children, adolescents and adults from sexual exploitation and abuse and sexual harassment (including staff). Importantly, these policies also apply to partners who support the implementation of programmes in support of UNICEF and partners. This includes access to incident reporting mechanisms, affording protection to individuals under whistleblower policies and referral of survivors to appropriate assistance services in line with the United Nations Victim Assistance Protocol including MHPSS and health services. Further, UNICEF supports implementing partners by responding to safeguarding and PSEA, ensuring clear findings, actions, and recommendations are in place. All implementing partners must comply with the policies on the protection of sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA). Safeguarding requirements are also stipulated for contractors and suppliers. Staff, volunteers and associates of the UNICEF and their implementing partners will be trained on the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) guidelines on GBV, and community outreach on PSEA will be undertaken. For PSEA, training will continue to be provided to all volunteers and IP staff, reporting mechanisms/channels will be strengthened, and PSEA focal points will receive regular capacity development on investigations. Monitoring visits will also be conducted to spot-check understanding of PSEA and the reporting mechanism by IP staff, teachers, and learners.

3.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

UNICEF and partners promote and ensure emergency preparedness for disasters through training community and parent support groups, teachers, and children. The CESGs will prepare DRR contingency plans, and implementing partners' focal points on the ground will coordinate in an emergency situation and provide technical support to the CESG. These DRR plans are to reduce the impact of natural hazards such as cyclones, tropical storms, flooding (and drowning) and fires which are common threats in the Rohingya camps. Additionally, there will be an awareness-raising event for the community to celebrate World Environmental Day. There will be greening projects where possible in the learning centres, and solar panels are used for generating power which is environmentally friendly. New construction techniques will be used to reduce reliance on bamboo and to be more weather-resistant.
