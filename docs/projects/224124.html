Project Summary

The RAMP program is designed to meet the most urgent gaps in basic needs and support the restoration of livelihoods of 73,867 vulnerable and conflict-affected populations living in Gao, Timbuktu, and Segou regions in Mali. The overall purpose of the program is to increase access to essential food, basic services, and early recovery assistance among underserved populations in need across central and northern Mali - aligning with the the following Theory of Change:
ToC: IF IDPs, host, and returnee communities receive a holistic package of humanitarian assistance responding to their most immediate needs, satisfying their food consumption and basic needs, and IF these communities are able to adapt their livelihoods, production, and economic opportunities activities to increase their income and preserve or rebuild their assets WHILE taking part in activities led by local organizations to facilitate the use of cash transfers to meet their needs, build social cohesion, manage conflicts, and address proximate conflict drivers, THEN they will be able to improve their food security, maintain their well-being, and strengthen the conditions for their protection.
To achieve the ToC, Mercy Corps will implement a two-pronged approach across the three primary sectors - Multi Purpose Cash Assistance (Sub-Purpose 1), and Early Recovery and Market Systems + Agriculture (Sub-Purpose 2). MPCA assistance will target vulnerable populations to meet their basic survival needs, while economic recovery support and agricultural support will target participants who have lost their means of livelihood due to the crisis/disaster. However, as households transition out of MPCA support, we will aim to set them up on an ERMS or agricultural pathway, depending on the extent of their vulnerability. Across the response, Mercy Corps will also organize sensitization meetings around social cohesion across the intervention zones – and develop and broadcast messages around peaceful cohabitation, building on our existing programming footprint - in addition to ensuring delivery of our Accountability to Affected Populations protocols. RAMP will have a total cost of $10,000,000 - and the overall ratio per participant is $135.

1 - Contexte

After more than a decade since a rebellion in the north triggered the first phase of a collective breakdown in the security situation across central and northern Mali, the country continues to be plagued by instability and deteriorating humanitarian conditions as we move into 2024. The Al-Qaeda aligned group, Jama'at Nusrat al-Islam wa al-Muslimeen (JNIM) has a firmly established foothold across much of the rural center (Mopti, Segou, Timbuktu), while the Islamic State in the Central Sahara (ISGS) continues to carry out attacks against both military and civilian targets around Gao, Menaka, and the north. These groups have been adept at co-opting local ethnic grievances to their own causes, particularly among herder communities, while heavy handed responses by state-security actors and their affiliated militias have helped to further fuel the cycle of violence. This ongoing conflict that has ravaged the center and north of the country for much of the past decade has had a crippling impact on governance, security, market functionality, land accessibility and investment – and is leading to pervasive humanitarian needs among both displaced and autochthone populations. A.1. Coordination and Gap Analysis: In total, 32% of the population (7.1 million people) are considered in need of humanitarian assistance through 2024 – of which 4.1 million are to be targeted. However, the humanitarian response architecture often prioritizes rapid-response mechanisms (RRM) – targeted primarily at displaced populations – who are indeed extremely vulnerable, but at 391,000 people, they only make up a comparatively small proportion of the population considered to be in need. Mercy Corps and a few other actors make up the “post RRM” response architecture – delivering cash and early recovery programming to both IDPs and wider populations – but even through our existing programming (such as PASERREL III), our reach is in the tens of thousands with the resources available and limited to the regions of Mopti (with BHA funding) and Segou (with FCDO). There are nevertheless enormous needs present elsewhere (particularly across Gao, Timbuktu, and Menaka) that are not met through the current response architecture for 2024 – as outlined in the HNRP extract below. HNRP 2024: Priority geographical areas include the center, east and north of the country, where current and protracted conflicts are increasing levels of vulnerability, but also where humanitarian access is maintained. Although the target figures have decreased overall, there has been a clear increase in severity among the population. This is mainly due to the modest level of assistance provided to the population as a result of the under-funding of previous humanitarian response plans and the deterioration in the security situation in the north of the country. For example, of the 37 priority cercles involved in the EHA humanitarian response, 30 cercles have a severity level of between 3 and 5, including 2 at level 5 (Anderamboukane and Inekar in the Ménaka region); 11 cercles at level 4 (Abeibara, Bandiagara, Bankass, Bourem, Dire, Douentza, Gao, Goundam, Ménaka, Niafunké and Tessalit); and 17 cercles at level 3.However, because of our substantial non-BHA programming footprint in Mopti, Segou and Gao, and a recent-historical presence in Timbuktu, Mercy Corps is well-positioned to quickly scale its response to help meet the needs identified by the HNRP in underserved locations and towards underserved populations. Where we do not currently have a functional base, such as in Timbuktu, Mercy Corps can be hosted by its local partner for office space sharing. This allows for easy access, ensuring cost effectiveness and rapid start-up. Collaboration with other actors such as, coordination groups, clusters etc. through the Cash Working Group will ensure complementarity and prevent duplication. A.2. Vulnerabilities and Capacities: The targeted population across Mali have major intersectional vulnerabilities linked to cycles of repeated displacement, on top of the challenges faced to achieve sustainable means of livelihood subsistence in an environment subject to recurrent climatic shocks. Beyond food-security vulnerability, populations are often having to deal with the impact of trauma and violence with very little in the way of support networks or structures in place in new displacement sites. Women and girls remain highly vulnerable to sexual and gender based violence - and young men vulnerable to recruitment into armed groups. Nevertheless, from our surveys, displaced households do present resilience and a willingness to move as quickly as possible away from reliance on humanitarian assistance, towards self-reliance. The program will help them to achieve this through its intended actions. Specifically, we will scale delivery of lifesaving assistance while iteratively working to develop early recovery activities in Timbuktu, Gao, and Segou region
