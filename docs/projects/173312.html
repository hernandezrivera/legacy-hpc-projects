Project Summary

The project, developed in collaboration with the Libyan Ministry of Health (MOH) and the United Kingdom’s Department for International Development (DfID) aims to build the capacity of PHC service providers and managers and thereby improve people’s access to quality PHC services. Three interventions were designed to build the managerial, service delivery and community-care capacities of the MOH. Six PHC centers (two each in south, west and east Libya) were selected to implement the project. In line with WHO’s Regional Framework for Action on Advancing Family Medicine, a comprehensive family medicine model was introduced in each center. A total of 716 health professionals (63% female and 37% male) were trained on the 13 core elements of introducing family practice into PHC. This training allowed each center to respond more effectively to the routine and humanitarian health care needs of host populations and internally displaced people (IDPs). WHO procured essential medicines and supplies and distributed them to all six centers. In parallel, WHO worked with the MOH to strengthen central and regional planning and management. Links were forged between the centers and the communities they served through a network of trained facilitators and volunteers who played an instrumental role in promoting the importance of integrated health care services within their communities.
Building on the accomplishments of the mentioned interventions and in line with the basic premise of humanitarian and development (H&D) nexus, the proposed project aims to strengthen MoH capacity at both developmental and humanitarian fronts involving strategic interventions at national and sub-national level through the following measures. The project will target 60 PHC facilities, (20 in each of the West, South and East region) across Libya. The selection of PHC facilities will be based on the findings of the recent assessment which was conducted by WHO in preparation for HRP 2021

1 - Needs

Primary health care (PHC) services are delivered through a network of 1355 facilities with a very poor utilization of health services and no referral system to secondary care level. The PHC facilities are not required to provide a standard package of services or maintain essential medicines, equipment and laboratory services to support the delivery of high-quality care. In many facilities, doctors are either not available full time or they are young and inexperienced. There are frequent stock-outs of essential medicines and there are no electronic or paper medical records that allow different physicians to monitor individual patients over time. Many facilities remain open only three to four hours a day. Lastly, many PHC facilities are thought by patients to be unclean and unsanitary. Therefore, private health care services have expanded to meet needs arising from the inadequate public health system. In 2010, private health care was estimated to account for 31% of total health expenditures. Currently, there are 415 private outpatient and 103 private inpatient clinics with a total bed capacity of 2088. In addition, there are 297 dental clinics, 311 medical laboratories, 1934 pharmacies and several unregulated clinics practicing herbal and traditional medicine. Whereas public services are free, private sector expenses are paid directly by patients

2 - Activities

1- Strengthen MOH service delivery capacity at national, regional and municipal levels:a. Train targeted clinical staff on the family practice model by means of short training courses.b. Train clinical staff, midwives and nurses on WHO’s updated guidelines on reproductive, maternal, neonatal, child and adolescent health.2- Improving access to and utilization of essential health care services, with a focus on underserved areas:a. Establish mobile outreach services if required and in line with the findings of the baseline assessment;b. deliver essential medicines, supplies, equipment and emergency health kits to these facilities. c. Strengthen the referral system and improve continuity of care by orienting staff on the UHC-BHP referral guidelines; identify referral hospitals and train their staff on referral mechanisms and practices. 3- Improving quality of care and patient safetya. Train health professionals in the PHC facilities on the new framework for assessing the quality of care. 4- Strengthen community engagement in the provision of PHC servicesa. Selecting a cohort of CHWs from the catchment population for each selected PHC facility and train them on the newly adapted training manuals for CHWs in Libya. b. Assigning and deploy the CHWs to implement integrated, basic health promotion and curative services under the supervision of trained supervisors and thus bridge the gap between health care facilities and the communities they serve. c. Strengthen the capacity of the private sector and civil society in health care provision and support the MOH in designing and implementing a strategy for effective engagement with the private sector. d. Facilitate the inclusion of the private sector in disease surveillance in order to broaden the capacity for detection and response to outbreaks.5. Supporting the disease surveillance system a. Train and equip 100 surveillance officer of case definition and case diagnosis b. Train and incentives for 35 RRTs

The health sector strengthens accountability towards affected people by developing tools to analyse feedback from beneficiaries and modify projects accordingly. Any operational plans involve details of details of anticipated public health measures based on national and sub-national context. A series of training workshops on community engagement will be implemented through engagement of existing public health and community-based networks, media, schools and universities, national and local governments and other sectors using a consistent mechanism of communication.
