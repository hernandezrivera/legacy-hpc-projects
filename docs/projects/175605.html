Project Summary

The overall objective of the “Enhancing livelihoods and food security for internally displaced people, refugee returnees and host communities in Morobo, Terekeka and Yirol East Counties of South Sudan” project is to reduce critical food insecurity levels for 9,400 beneficiaries in Morobo, Terekeka and Yirol East Counties at the height of the 2021 lean season. The project will target 55% women beneficiaries (5,200 people) and 45% male beneficiaries (4,200 people). The project aims at creating adequate agriculture production support systems for vulnerable smallholders that support good agriculture practices and timely response to shocks. The project intends to provide food support for the protection of lives and human capital of internally displaced people (IDP), refugee returnees and host communities. The project will specifically focus on three main areas of support: (i) providing food assistance to prevent famine and improve food consumption, dietary diversity and coping strategies for vulnerable populations; (ii) assisting farmers through complementary vegetable and crop livelihood inputs, fishing and livestock support; and (iii) reducing dependency on food and agricultural inputs to support and strengthen households’ ability to absorb shocks in a way that prevents the spread and transmission of COVID-19.
The project will be implemented for a period of 12 months. The project targets Morobo, Terekeka and Yirol East Counties of South Sudan which are classified in Emergency [Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) Phase 4] acute food insecurity. The project targets vulnerable households including internally displaced people (IDP), refugee returnees and host communities as primary beneficiaries of the project. The project provides life-sustaining and life-saving food, livelihoods and livestock assistance, increasing food production, and building productive assets for more sustainable livelihoods.
The project will support: (i) purchase and distribution of agricultural inputs and planting materials such as seeds and tools; (ii) rebuilding or distribution of assets for postharvest handling and food storage for example household granaries and food storage bins. The project will increase access to food for the food insecure and most vulnerable, protect the human capital of children and prevent negative coping strategies by poor households. Similarly, the project will support to unconditional food transfers and re‐engagement in crop and livestock production. Likewise, the project will support vulnerable smallholders and small family-based agri-food enterprises to increase the productivity and financial feasibility of their activities.
The project addresses the reduced livelihood and food security levels resulting from the crisis in South Sudan. It offers to tackle its negative impact on IDP and refugees and host communities through support to the agriculture sector. This will be done by improving the agricultural productivity and farmers' incomes, in addition to creating job opportunities for both host communities and IDP and refugee returnees.

1 - Needs

Based on findings of food security and nutrition projections for 2021, an estimated 2.102 million people faced Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. A total of 34 counties including Morobo, Terekeka and Yirol East Counties were classified in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. During December 2020 to March 2021, an estimated 1.79 million people are likely to face Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. During April to July 2021, an estimated 2.47 million are likely to be in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) acute food insecurity. With the current levels of Humanitarian Food Assistance, 17.4% of the population (about 2.10 million people) were in IPC Phase 4 (Emergency) acute food insecurity. Key drivers of acute malnutrition include (1) high prevalence of diseases (affecting up to 36% of children under five), (2) poor quality and diversity of food (Minimum Acceptable Diet: 7%, Minimum dietary diversity: 15%); (3) elevated level of food insecurity (IPC AFI phase 3 and above) in most counties also contributes to acute malnutrition; (4) poor access to health and nutrition services due to heightened, sub-national conflict and flooding mainly in the Greater Upper Nile; and (5) COVID-19 related disruptions exacerbated access to services. UNFAO states that current levels of food insecurity are likely to be rendered even more acute by new drivers introduced by the COVID-19 pandemic. Many vulnerable families in Morobo, Terekeka and Yirol East Counties continue to depend on food assistance to meet their basic needs. According to the Initial Rapid Needs Assessment (IRNA) conducted by Salvation and Light Development Action (SLDA) from February 01 – 07, 2021 in Morobo, Terekeka and Yirol East Counties, main drivers of vulnerability and food insecurity in target Counties include: (1) natural disaster e.g., floods and droughts; (2) inter communal violence; (3) low agricultural production and productivity; (4) low asset ownership; (5) soaring of food price due to depreciation of South Sudanese Pound; and (6) COVID 19 pandemic such as disruptions to supply chains. It has been observed that physical access is a challenge in target Counties with many of the roads and infrastructure damaged by flooding. According to Inter-Agency Coordinated IRNA conducted from 11 to 12 September 2020 in Terekeka County, it was found that farms were destroyed by the floods and most households lost main crops such as groundnut, sorghum and millet. Most household opting to taking one meal and IDP it even worst as most are relying on wild foods and host community. Both communities used to sale livestock to raise HH incomes. They used to practice fishing but they lack fishing gears and even though they reported plenty of fish in swampy areas. The IDP left all their livestock behind and others explained that their livestock were swept by floods. In Morobo, Terekeka and Yirol East Counties, high proportions of the population have large food consumption gaps as well as people are engaging in severe coping strategies in an attempt to mitigate food consumption gaps. The scale and severity of acute food insecurity will remain high through January 2021, including during the post-harvest period. The population in need of humanitarian food assistance will most likely remain among the highest levels in recent years, driven by the escalation of inter-communal conflict, recurrent flooding, persistently poor economic conditions, and the economic impact of COVID-19. Furthermore, high and rising staple food prices compared to previous years, and few income sources, the vast majority of households will struggle to earn enough income to purchase their minimum food needs. At-risk households are most likely to include IDP, refugee returnees and host communities in Morobo, Terekeka and Yirol East Counties who do not own livestock, have limited or no access to arable land, and have high dependence on food assistance.

2 - Project activities

The “Enhancing livelihoods and food security for internally displaced people, refugee returnees and host communities in Morobo, Terekeka and Yirol East Counties of South Sudan” project will include a number of activities designed to address the key objective of reducing food insecurity in Morobo, Terekeka and Yirol East Counties. The activities have been designed with a focus on sustainability and value for money and serve to empower IDP, refugee returnees and host communities in both the short and longer term. Activities have also been designed both in consultation with local communities and with the aim of reducing the impacts of the identified drivers of food insecurity in the target locations. Agricultural inputs (seeds and tools) are of urgency in target Counties to reverse their hopes and restore livelihoods. There is an urgent need to provide integrated support including food and livelihoods assistance and protection services to the most vulnerable people including children suffering from severe acute malnutrition and pregnant or breastfeeding women who are acutely malnourished. By improving the availability and accessibility of the productive assets, the project will strengthen long-term food security by improving agricultural productivity. As production increases, availability of food for consumption and/or sale increases, resulting in improved livelihoods and an increase in local, regional and national food security. The project will distribute local seed varieties and appropriate agricultural tools to project beneficiaries. Beneficiaries will also receive training and extension services on a range of topics including but not limited to: food hygiene, preparation and storage; business skills development, entrepreneurship and financial literacy; and livestock value addition. The project will support the immediate nutritional needs of vulnerable HHs through the provision of food and/or cash transfers. Using transfers, HHs will access, either through direct distribution or via local markets, sufficient food to meet daily caloric and nutritional requirements. The project will also focus on conducting rigorous assessments and analysis on factors influencing food security in the target areas and developing long-term strategies to mitigate such risks. Proposed Activities include:Activity 1: Unconditional and conditional cash and voucher-based transfers to procure goods by beneficiariesActivity 2: Emergency food production: main season agriculture seeds & tool distribution; fishing, vegetable & crop kitsActivity 3: Emergency food and livelihood support: fishing kits/ vegetable kits; rapid response kits (fish kit/ cow pea and three variety of fast maturing vegetable seed) Activity 4: Livestock treatment and vaccination; livestock disease surveillance, Community Animal Health Workers Training and inputs support, cold chain establishment and maintenance, prevention & treatment especially zoonoses e.g. RVF Activity 5: Livelihood support to returnee households Activity 6: Support to seed fairs using locally sourced seed; seed security and multiplication strengthening Activity 7: Post Distribution Monitoring to ascertain the impact of the project at household and community levelsActivity 8: Establish demonstration plots to train lead farmers on basic agronomics practicesActivity 9: Support value chain addition e.g. through milk and fish preservation and marketingActivity 10: Training and capacity building: both adaptive and absorptive on-farm production (farmer/agro-pastoral field schools, demonstration plots), income generation (business skills), vocational training etc. Activity 11: Enable conflict related meetings to strengthen local peace structures Activity 12: COVID-19 IEC materials and messaging with adherence to distribution for in cash/ livelihood kits

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

There are key factors relating to food supply and distribution which may ultimately influence market planning and infrastructure provision:- increased food crop production, leading to surpluses being available for sale and to a greater demand for marketing facilities in rural areas;- changes in consumption habits creating the need for new cold storage facilities and food processing industries;- poor conditions in markets are common. Demand for stalls in many markets is high and stall space is often limited. Produce is frequently displayed directly on the ground and exposed to the weather, resulting in high losses. Facilities may be limited, usually with no main market sheds and no provision of water supply or latrines; and- increased availability of different types of transport, such as small vehicles or trucks.

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

The project believes that using cash or vouchers recognises that beneficiaries are best placed to decide what their households need, and gives them the flexibility to choose according to their own diverse needs and priorities. Similarly, in allowing beneficiaries to control what goods and services their households need, cash transfers are considered by many as more dignified than receiving goods in kind, and recognise beneficiaries as active participants in providing for the welfare of their families in an emergency situation, like South Sudan. Likewise, the availability of cash gives households a sense of restored power over their immediate situation. In addition, there is evidence that receiving cash can at least temporarily empower women within the household. Access to financial structures such as bank accounts may empower beneficiaries, in particular women, in the longer-term as well. Furthermore, injections of cash or the use of commodity vouchers with local traders benefits the recovery of trade and the local economy. Evidence shows that cash transfers reduce the negative coping strategies used by beneficiaries. When goods are available and accessible locally, cash or voucher-based interventions have the potential to be more rapid than bringing supply from out of country. When beneficiaries use local markets, agencies also eliminate logistical and import costs.

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

This project will promote quality programming with a focus on inter-sector synergies and integrated programming to mainstream protection, gender, and accountability to affected populations. The project ensures that all social mobilization, community engagement and surveillance mechanism will be developed and implemented in conjunction with women’s groups.The project prioritizes the mitigation, response and prevention of gender based violence (GBV) and Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA). The project will train women’s groups on the requirements to promptly refer survivors of sexual exploitation and abuse for assistance through existing GBV pathways. Working with the protection cluster and other development partners, SLDA will implement Accountability to Affected People and Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse in line with the IASC Commitments. It will put in place a collective mechanism to ensure participation of affected people in planning and programming of humanitarian interventions, and to regularly monitoring of their satisfaction and priorities. SLDA will put in place reporting mechanisms and guidelines to ensure that cases of sexual exploitation and abuse are immediately reported and followed up on, through our clear guidelines for reporting. Any allegation of abuse or exploitation of an adult or child associated with a SLDA programme will lead to the immediate suspension and removal from the location of the accused individuals. The project intervention’s will incorporate protection principles and promote meaningful access, safety and dignity in provision of project resources and services. Safety and dignity, and avoid causing harm for the target beneficiaries will be the priority. The interventions will prevent and minimize unintended negative effects under this project, which can increase people's vulnerability to both physical and psychosocial risks. The project will provide meaningful access to all the beneficiaries. Women, youth and target communities will access services without discrimination. This will target Individuals and groups who may be particularly vulnerable or have difficulty accessing project resources and services especially people living with disabilities, girls and teenage mothers.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

In this response, the Community Feedback and Response Mechanism (CFRM) will be set up and made functional at community levels. The CFRM will deliver a mechanism that enables the beneficiaries and community members to provide feedback and seek responses in relation to activities of this response and other development actors in their communities, in a manner that is safe, non-threatening and accessible. The initiative will promote beneficiary accountability and feedback and response mechanism to other development actors. The project will measure the adequacy of interventions and address concerns and complaints of most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls). Access barriers could be at following level i.e. within IDP, refugee returnees and host communities and age and sex. SLDA will engage farmers groups, cooperatives and VSLAs, involve, and consult community elders to inform and design sensitization campaigns to raise awareness on right to nutritious food. The message will be tailored to age and gender appropriate needs. SLDA has strong principle of do no harm; programme staff are trained on ensuring that humanitarian interventions do not put beneficiaries at risk and does not discriminate service delivery on basis of ethnicity, and sex. Similarly, SLDA has community feedback and response mechanism to ensure that voices of targeted communities are included in the programme. The feedback is collected through farmers groups, cooperatives and VSLAs, and community elders. SLDA ensures the safety and wellbeing of women and children who come into contact with the organization is promoted through the promotion of a zero tolerance policy towards the abuse and exploitation of women and children during our programing. SLDA is accountable to ensuring all staff, partners and volunteers on this project receive adequate training on PSEA, child protection and safeguarding.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

The project applies community engagement approach to encourage organizational and government leaders to take on roles as facilitators, supporters, and collaborators, offering them enhanced perspectives while giving a stronger voice to tribal citizens and stakeholders. Drawing on local knowledge from diverse groups creates solutions that are both practical and effective. Local communities are creators and owners of the project and catalysts for change rather than simple beneficiaries of programming. Through community engagement, project beneficiaries play a meaningful role in the deliberations, discussions, decisions and implementation of the project activities. The project will leverage all aspects of community engagement to promote gender equality, ensuring consultation with women specifically, building women’s skills and expertise, creating spaces for leadership and decision-making, and engaging women and men in dialogue that advocate for positive social change that promotes greater equality, opportunities and respect for women and girls. The project will strengthen women’s agency and empowerment by actively supporting women’s participation in leadership positions through the project. To ensure that women’s voices and opinions are incorporated into the planning and implementation of activities, women’s free and active participation in community consultation processes will be enhanced by employing inclusive facilitation techniques, and holding meetings at times and locations that encourage women’s participation. Gender-responsive approaches will also be adopted throughout implementation such as moving trainings closer to female participants’ households, establishing shade shelters for children to safely play while mothers receive field-based training, and engaging men to understand the mutual benefit of supporting women’s agricultural skill development. The project will engage with potential existing networks, including traditional and cultural structures, government structure, schools and youth programmes, and churches and faith-based organizations. The project will give following considerations for working more inclusively and engaging different stakeholder groups: (1) Engage tribal leaders, community action groups, and affected populations to participate in the overall development and execution of programmatic action. (2) Solicit input from all affected parties to discover what is working, what is not working, and what needs to be accomplished for a successful implementation of project activities. (3) Engage with community and association groups as starting points for approaching and communicating with the populations targeted for engagement. (4) Build trust with the affected populations. The project will increase transparency and accountability of local authorities for fair, inclusive and quality service delivery to the affected populations. The project will obtain informed consent from participants and ensure confidentiality for project activities that involve any probing into participants’ experiences in project activities. (5) Develop a different communication approach to know the attitudes, beliefs, and behavior of the intended beneficiaries in order to craft the most effective messages to encourage community involvement. (6) Constructively engage with youths to widely disseminate information to others within the community.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

SLDA recognizes the right of beneficiaries and community members to give feedback and seek response from projects affecting them. Consultative meetings will held with the learners, communities and local leaders. The beneficiaries and community members will be encouraged to give feedback and seek responses from the projects affecting them. They will be sensitized right from the start of the project through kick off meetings and community consultation workshops on key deliverables including community roles and responsibilities. Community members will participate actively, transparently and without discrimination in analysis, planning, design, implementation, monitoring and evaluation of all interventions proposed. The farmers’ groups, cooperatives, VSLAs and communities including representatives of vulnerable groups will be supported to prioritize and plan food and nutrition security to ensure safe, effective and equitable delivery of services. Views and feedback will be collected from the community beneficiaries, farmers’ groups, cooperatives and VSLAs from the start (design) of the intervention through key information interviews. The key informant interviews will conducted among the local leaders and community leaders over the period. All stakeholders will be involved in all assessments including assessment among IDP, host community and other interest groups. In essence, SLDA will approach the principle of participation from equity perspective and advocate for equal participation of different groups. Based on the cluster reporting system, the project will put in place and implement and monitoring and reporting system, progress, best practices and lessons learned will be presented in cluster meetings based on the 5Ws formats developed by the cluster.
