Project Summary

"DRC proposes the project in camps 8E, 8W, and 10, targeting 2,942 households for SUM support, and an additional 1,300 households in Camps 8E and 8W who will receive a Tie Down Kit with technical support in Camps 8E and 8W. Through the IOM common pipeline partnership arrangement, DRC will receive the shelter materials, while DRC will be responsible for independent funding and executing the project's operations including direct staff cost, warehousing, managing Distribution Points, and other shared costs. Moreover, DRM support for 100 HHs and shelter interventions for 50 HHs in the HC will be executed through a cash-based approach, along with the utilization of 29 man-days of labor and carpenter modalities. DRC proposes to deploy a Shelter technical coordinator position for the SCCCM Sector to contribute to sector outputs as a partner.
All activities will be coordinated with SMS, the Shelter focal point, and camp authorities, including response to CFRM referrals. DRC will develop prioritization criteria for the distribution of SUM in consultation with SMS community groups of different ages, genders, and abilities. Porter support and/or construction labor assistance will be provided to households with special shelter needs based on assessments of the household's capacity to carry shelter materials from the distribution point to their shelter and use the materials as intended. Households will undergo gender-disaggregated training on SUM materials and DRC tools usage, receiving technical support and daily monitoring for proper utilization.
The SUM package will adhere to sector standards, with CFWs selection prioritizing vulnerable individuals identified through DRC camp profiling and scoring tools and individuals with specific needs will receive suitable assignments in tailored work environments. Households will be included in design and implementation decisions including construction modality to encourage community ownership and increase technical knowledge. DRC SMS community groups of different ages, genders, and abilities will be engaged in the development of tie-down kit communication strategies catering to the needs of each group. Using participatory mapping techniques, groups will identify communication channels, potential barriers to understanding or ability to implement, and behavioral triggers that can facilitate the proper installation of tie-down kits. Groups will then roll out their communication strategies to their communities. and DMU (Disaster Management Unit) under the supervision of DRC SMS will help households in enhancing their skills in TDK installation, increasing awareness about TDK usage, and overseeing the TDK installation process as part of the ongoing DRR activities within the DMU.
To initiate shelter interventions within the host community, DRC will establish communication channels with the local administration and community representatives. Beneficiary identification will be conducted through a collaborative transparent and community consultation process, with the final verification carried out by local representatives ensuring that the assistance reaches those in need. Cash-based assistance is provided to eligible beneficiaries, enabling them to make informed choices about shelter improvements, repairs, or upgrades, tailored to their unique needs. DRC staff, including the MEAL team, will directly oversee and monitor the project's progress.
DRC will conduct post-distribution and post-construction monitoring of shelter activities completed at the beginning and end of this project. Results will be shared through gender-segregated open dialogues with the community to validate findings and collect recommendations for project improvement.
By focusing on these key areas, the project aims to improve living conditions, enhance resilience, and promote dignity and inclusivity for all beneficiaries.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs

" According to the MSNA report for 2023, 40.9% of households indicated that they did not receive any assistance to enhance their shelter, and 60% of households reported receiving no shelter support from humanitarian organizations. According to the latest SMS service monitoring report, in Camp 10 Block D, approximately 521 shelters were reconstructed in 2022 by a private partner and have not yet received any SUM assistance. Similarly, in Camp 8E, 698 shelters in C block were reconstructed following a significant fire incident in March 2021, but they have not received any shelter materials since then In Camp 8W, a total of 1,723 shelters located in Blocks B, E, and F were built during the initial phase of 2023 and are anticipated to necessitate shelter assistance by end of 2024 as the shelter condition rapidly deteriorates from exposure to extreme camp conditions including flooding, high winds, landslides, and strong rains, thereby requiring constant replacement. This outcome is unavoidable, as indicated by the Shelter Performance Standard Assessment Report for the year 2023. According to the report, 65% of shelters did not meet the Desired Performance Standards regarding treated bamboo and exhibited visible signs of infestation. In contrast, 20% of the shelters only partially met these standards, while the remaining 15% were constructed using treated bamboo. Furthermore, ad-hoc private partners who offer shelter support to households without implementing a proper care and maintenance plan leave these households in a precarious position after just one year of shelter construction. Structurally compromised shelters primarily impact women, the elderly, and people with disabilities who spend most of their time at home due to cultural constraints and movement barriers in the camps. The IOM/ACAPS “Our Thoughts” study points out that damaged or inadequate shelters force women and girls to “make the difficult decision of whether to leave for their health and comfort or whether to remain inside to maintain their dignity”. TDK assistance was distributed to the designated camps in 2021, and the demand for TDKs was consistently expressed by the community through the DRC SMS community consultation platform. Tie Down Kits help reinforce and secure the shelters, ensuring they can withstand strong winds, heavy rainfall, and other environmental challenges commonly faced in the region. This stability is crucial for the safety and well-being of the occupants. The influx of Rohingya refugees from Myanmar, coupled with environmental pressures and extreme climatic conditions, has intensified the demand for adequate and resilient housing in host community wards in Ukhia. DRC’s proposed Shelter assistance will not only focus on providing physical structures but also integrate measures to support livelihood opportunities for vulnerable host populations. This holistic approach can contribute to the economic resilience of the community."

2. Rationalization
2 - How is your project aligned to the Rationalization Principles (in particular reference # 3 - Adopt an integrated approach, # 4 - Ensure predictability and continuity)?

"In adherence to Rationalization Principle #3, DRC already serves as the SMS agency for the proposed camps, this move will additionally facilitate the enhancement of inter-sectoral camp-based coordination in a harmonized fashion, owing to the incorporation of the Shelter and CCCM sectors. DRC has initiated community engagement through SMS programs and AGD groups in proposed camps. Proactively seeking support from these groups, DRC aims to involve them at every stage of planning and implementation to identify needs, vulnerabilities, priorities, and coping mechanisms, aligning with the social cohesion mandate and adhering to the Do No Harm Principle. Simultaneously, DRC will prioritize addressing the critical shelter needs of both refugee and host communities, aiming to bridge any existing gaps through an integrated approach. Introducing a Shelter Technical Coordinator in the SCCCM Sector will enhance sector outputs, bolstering technical capacity for improved service delivery.To promote Principle #4, DRC SMS has already been supporting the CICs the proposed camps, and has established robust connections with refugees, the government, and other partners within the camp. DRC is committed to maintaining program continuity and stability alongside the existing SMS program, which presents an opportunity to collaborate closely with SMS. This collaboration will ensure that interventions are aligned with specific needs, prevent redundancy, and optimize the utilization of critical resources. Furthermore, DRC's protection and economic recovery component will integrate with shelter cash-based interventions and livelihood/economic recovery. The continued integration of these interventions in both camp and host community settings aims to enhance relations with refugees. DRC is dedicated to boosting the capacities of eligible local partners, focusing on strengthening their proficiency in humanitarian response mechanisms, with a specific emphasis on sectoral integration"

3. Cross-cutting Themes
3.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

Protection is mainstreamed into all DRC programs of DRC such as CCCM, Shelter, and EcRec. DRC will Involve refugees and host communities in the decision-making process regarding proposed shelter interventions through consultative as their input is crucial in identifying protection risks and solutions, and it helps build a sense of ownership and empowerment. DRC SMS will advocate with local authorities/CICs and landowners in coordination with HLP actors to obtain an agreement to use the land & reduce the risk of eviction to mitigate conflict and foster social cohesion. DRC will also, mitigate protection risks by cash-for-work participants by explaining worker prioritization criteria to the community before selecting workers, ensuring that there is informed consent before capturing personal data, and adhering to the DRC data protection protocol. CFWs will be assigned culturally appropriate tasks and divided into gender-segregated work teams. All DRC staff and cash-for-work participants undergo annual code of conduct and PSEA training. DRC will conduct orientation sessions for female CFWs to provide information on the expectations and requirements of their work, including safety precautions and job responsibilities to help women feel more comfortable and confident in their roles to ensure that women are aware of their rights in the work. DRC has a PSEA focal point assigned to each of its implementation areas and maintains a code of conduct response mechanism.

3.3 - Age & Disability

DRC conducts a comprehensive needs assessment to identify the specific protection concerns and vulnerabilities within the refugee population in consultations with the community and take into account gender, age, and diversity considerations. DRC service monitoring tools capture the gaps of shelter intervention and prioritize the needs vulnerable households. Age and Gender disaggregated data is captured in DRC’s cash-for-work participant database, which is connected to an interactive dashboard for easy analysis. DRC aims to engage a safe and culturally appropriate number of participants of different genders and ages and will assign them workspaces and tasks that are culturally acceptable and in line with their capacity. Work conditions will be explained to the community before recruiting cash-for-work participants to encourage women to apply. Age & Gender a criteria in DRC’s vulnerability scoring system for prioritizing cash-for-work selection. Households with People with Disabilities will be provided porter/labor support to carry materials and reconstruct their shelters. Where possible, DRC will conduct gender-segregated training with trainers of the corresponding gender. DRC will seek to increase the accessibility of services for all age and gender categories. DRC will continue to involve the beneficiaries in each stage of planning and implementation for identifying the needs, vulnerabilities, priorities, and coping mechanisms.

3.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

Community engagement and active participation will be key to ensure accountability to individuals supported by DRC. Through the existing established CFRM system by SMS in the proposed camps, the beneficiaries will have the chance to provide feedback or any complaints on the services implemented. All complaints will be treated confidentially and addressed in a prompt and appropriate manner, in order to close the feedback loop and mitigate further damage related to potential risks. The CFRM safeguards beneficiary personal details via data protection principles. DRC MEAL conducts an annual survey with DRC cash-for-work participants to understand if cash incentives enable participants to meet their financial needs, if work conditions are adequate if there is adequate training, and if workers are satisfied with management. All workers are informed about DRC’s Code of Conduct Response Mechanism as part of their induction. All DRC staff and cash-for-work participants undergo an annual code of conduct and PSEA training. DRC has a PSEA focal point assigned to each of its implementation areas and maintains a code of conduct response mechanism

3.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

Emergency preparedness is a priority for all actors in Cox’s Bazar to support refugees in mitigating risks associated with extreme natural events. DRC will cover TDK assistance for all households in Camp 8E and 8E and DMU (Disaster Management Unit) under the supervision of DRC SMS will provide assistance to households in enhancing their skills in TDK installation, increasing awareness about TDK usage, and overseeing the TDK installation process as part of the ongoing DRR activities within the DMU. The camps are located in areas prone to natural disasters such as cyclones or strong wind. Tie-down kits significantly reduce the risk of shelters being damaged or destroyed during such events, helping to safeguard the lives and possessions of the refugees.
