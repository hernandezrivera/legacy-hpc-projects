Project Summary

Since the Libyan revolution in 2011, the country has experienced several outbreaks of heavy fighting in populated areas, leaving many of these areas contaminated with explosive hazards. Even when active hostilities end, the contamination continues to pose a considerable threat to civilians, impedes the return of Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs), and significantly impacts the ability of humanitarian and development actors to implement activities towards stabilization.
The Libyan national capacity to mitigate the impact through Explosive Ordnance Disposal (EOD), Explosive Hazard Risk Education (EHRE), and Victim Assistance (VA) interventions is insufficient in comparison to the growing threat to the population. With sufficient managerial and coordination capacity in place, existing governmental and non-governmental actors have a strong base for growth, yet are lacking qualified personnel, equipment, and technical expertise to scale up to meet demand. This project will therefore focus predominantly on the enhancement of technical skills and physical capacity of established local actors.

1 - Needs

The threat from explosive hazards has increased exponentially since the latest outbreak of hostilities in April 2019. All parties to the conflict are relying heavily on the use of explosive and heavy weaponry in densely populated areas – sometimes in a disproportionate and indiscriminate manner. Reports of civilians injured, maimed, and killed by unexploded ordnance (UXO), and other explosive hazards have increased dramatically in 2020. The recent discovery of the systematic use of booby-traps in areas retaken from the Libyan National Army (LNA) in the greater Tripoli area has drawn considerable attention to the issue of explosive hazard contamination and the need for response. The explosive items being used are particularly dangerous and include tripwires, involve various explosive charges used to booby-trap homes, as well as the employment of anti-vehicle mines and victim-activated explosive devices. With 202 confirmed casualties in Tripoli and Sirte since mid-May, this new threat proves to be extremely dangerous for civilians and clearance operators and will require additional capacity to respond to the threat on this scale. Given the large number of IDPs wishing to return to the affected areas, casualty numbers are expected to rise further if there are premature returns. The Ministry of Interior, along with mayors of affected municipalities, have requested additional support from UNMAS in terms of training and equipment for clearance teams to be able to respond to the emerging threat while limiting the number of casualties. The increased use of explosive hazards and the advanced technology of the explosive items is an important protection concern for civilians and especially IDPs rushing to return to their homes. Three distinct needs for a Mine Action response can be identiﬁed: 1) Removal of explosive hazards: Given the scope of the explosive hazard threat, there is an urgent need for permanent removal of explosive hazards. Similarly, there is an urgent need for qualiﬁed national EOD teams in Tripoli and other areas affected by the conﬂict. However, the EOD capacity of national entities to assist communities is hampered by a lack of adequately trained and equipped personnel. 2) Increased awareness of the risks of explosive hazard contamination: Residents of all areas affected by the conﬂict will likely be faced with explosive hazard contamination. Irrespective of parallel emergency clearance efforts, the risk of death and injuries from explosive hazards may likely persist for years to come. Explosive Hazard Risk Education (EHRE) aims to minimize the impact of explosive hazard contamination by providing information, raising awareness, and building capacity of affected communities. 3) Victim Assistance: Improved access to services to assist victims of explosive weapons: War-related injuries caused by explosive hazards and Small Arms and Light Weapons (SALW) often result in impairment, life-long disability, and psychological trauma. Explosive hazard and SALW survivors and Persons with Disabilities (PwDs) are amongst the most vulnerable groups affected by the conﬂict, especially when access to adequate emergency health care response, and comprehensive rehabilitation, both physical and psycho-social, is hindered by conﬂict, and the access to, and availability of services is limited.

2 - Activities

The project aims to contribute to the objective "Civilians, including refugees and migrants, live free of the threat of explosive ordnance and the impact of the illicit proliferation of arms and ammunition". To reach the objective UNMAS proposes three areas of activities: 1) The proposed project will provide EOD and emergency medical response training for 12 Libyan personnel and will supply the newly trained EOD teams with appropriate equipment. By building the capacity of a national entities the project strengthens national ownership and helps support operational sustainability well beyond the end of the actual project duration. In addition, as government personnel and operational costs are fully covered by the Libyan state, future EOD operations are not dependent on continued external funding. 2) UNMAS proposes to support various lifesaving EHRE activities targeting at-risk-populations through capacity building; public information dissemination; education and training sessions; and community liaison outreach. Understanding the powerful role that women can play as agents of behaviour change, UNMAS will ensure that at least half of the community mobilizers recruited and trained for the project will be women. The content of EHRE sessions and material will be gender and age sensitive. 3) UNMAS, in close coordination with the LibMAC, the Ministry of Health, the Ministry of Social Affairs, and other relevant stakeholders, proposes to develop and support an emergency referral system to improve the VA response across multiple sectors. Concretely, the referral system will help to better identify explosive hazard/SALW survivors and PwDs; help facilitate their access to needed health, rehabilitation, and mental health and psycho-social support services; improve information management and needs analysis; and improve the coordination between key service providers and other stakeholders.

5 - Mitigation measures
5.1 - For each risk mentioned under 4 above, please describe the ways you plan to mitigate, prevent and/or address these.

(1) UNMAS Libya will monitor the security situation on the ground and coordinate accordingly with the selected national Mine Action organisation. Mine Action clearance will be conducted depending on the evolving security situation. (2) UNMAS supports the risk assessment of partner organisations, who often have a good understanding of the local political/security situations of the communities they operate in. In addition, UNMAS encourages partners to adopt a low profile, especially when operating in sensitive contexts. (3) UNMAS contractually obliges contractors or implementing partners to adhere to the relevant Codes of Conduct and will apply a policy of zero tolerance of sexual exploitation and abuse.

6.2 - Other accountability mechanisms

Accountability to affected populations will be integrated during project design, implementation, and monitoring and evaluation. Risk Education activities, by definition, require close personal interaction with the affected population. Information and preferences communicated by local residents, both men and women, will be taken into account when prioritizing and targeting areas for clearance. Clearance will be determined and carried out in close consultation with local authorities, local communities and the LibMAC. Emergency hotline numbers will be communicated to local populations during the Risk Education sessions so any reports or explosive items can be reported to local organisations and the LibMAC. Quality Management visits will be carried out by UNMAS Libya to ensure that the interventions comply with the Libyan Mine Action Standardsand the Gender guidelines for Mine Action Programmes and are in line with PSEA policies and the United Nations Code of Conduct. Any training activities conducted or supported by UNMAS include a training evaluation exercise in which trainees can provide anonymised feedback to the trainers, implementing partner and UNMAS.
