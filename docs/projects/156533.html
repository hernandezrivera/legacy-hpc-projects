Project Summary

This project is an extension of the child-friendly spaces project supported by the Qatar Red Crescent in the north of Syria. In the face of serious threats to children's communities in the north of Syria, child-friendly spaces provide a child-friendly environment for children to participate in play and learning activities and is one of the basic interventions to protect children from physical and psycho-social harm and to help them continue to learn and express themselves.
Children who come to child-friendly space often come from poor environments and live in very difficult conditions. They may have been deprived of a sense of self-worth and may have been used to being ignored or mistreated. Treating children with respect and care, regardless of their circumstances and backgrounds, will go a long way towards improving their self-confidence and making them feel better themselves, which in turn prepares them to better cope with their life experiences.
Recreational activities for children within their spaces provide opportunities for physical, social and emotional development as well as a learning experience. These activities help children shape social connections and teach them the importance of making choices, solving problems, and participating. These activities also help to promote the physical development of children as well as helping them cope with boredom and aggression.
Giving attention and support to the views of children helps them build self-esteem and self-confidence. These views can be drawn orally through forums, discussions, or formally through feedback forms. In both cases, children should be encouraged to participate, discuss their views and make informed choices about decisions that affect their daily lives. This will help children become more independent and self-sufficient.
Along with CP priorities for 2019, this project will be expanding the reach and improving the quality of child protection specialized services for children most at risk and survivors of violence, exploitation and abuse and also improving the quality of community based child protection, including psycho social support interventions through establishing child friendly spaces and providing specialized interventions such as case management and parent programs including for but not limited to, children of displaced populations living in IDP Sites and hosting communities.
Also, this project aims at providing a comprehensive approach by integrating CP with MHPSS case management at PHC level using MHPSS consultation spaces with qualified psycho-social workers.

Needs

Idleb and rural Aleppo are still witnessing increased rates of child recruitment and exploitation. As per HNO of child protection 2019, children throughout Syria continue to face pervasive violence and a range of protection risks. Grave child rights violations remain a significant concern, including in areas where hostilities have declined, with children at risk of killing and injury, recruitment and use in hostilities, torture, detention, abduction, sexual violence, attacks on schools and hospitals and denial of humanitarian access by parties to the conflict. During the first 9 months of 2018, the UN verified 2,354 such violations against children, in 13 of 14 governorates of Syria, illustrating persistent trends of violence though not the full scope of conflict-related violence affecting children. This included more than 1,300 verified incidents of killing or injuring of children. Respondents in assessed communities reporting explosive hazard contamination in Syria identify adolescent boys and pre-adolescent boys as the groups most at risk of death or injury from accidents, with incidents reported while children were playing and going to school. The ongoing or recurrent conflict environment experienced by many boys and girls over the past seven years has profoundly affected their psycho-social well-being – weakening their sense of security, purpose, control and significance, or exercising social agency, and causing profound distress. Many are without civil documentation to prove their identity, access basic services and safeguard their rights. It is estimated that more than 2.6 million children remain displaced and separation from caregivers is a reported child protection issue in all 14 governorates of Syria putting children at risk of exploitation, abuse or neglect. Events leading to family separation indicate how related protection and child protection issues increase vulnerability of affected boys and girls, with death or disappearance of caregivers, disappearance of children, separation due to displacement or conflict, child marriage and child recruitment among cited reasons for family separations. According to CP HNO analysis for Syria (National level), 81% of assessed communities reported child labour preventing school attendance as an issue of concern, 46% of assessed communities reported family separation as an issue of concern and 45% of assessed communities reported early marriage as an issue of concern.Respondents in those assessed communities where discrimination in the delivery of humanitarian assistance emerged, identified unaccompanied girls as the most likely group to face such discrimination .The conflict and its impact on children and families have fueled negative coping mechanisms such as child labour, early marriage and child recruitment, putting adolescent boys and girls at particular risk, while straining the capacities of families and communities to protect children.

Implementation of activities

This project will focus on implementing most of the activities that CP cluster put as priorities in 6 CFS centers, which includes: 1-Child Protection and Psychosocial Support Activities:• Structured programmes include a curriculum and/or session activity plans.• Sustained programmes a) ongoing programmes and b) a child’s regular attendance over a specific period of time, e.g. 2 times per week over 3 months.• Community-based CP and PSS programmes include CFS/AFS, peer mentoring, or other structured programmes that teach children self-protection, safety, prevention or resiliency.• All programmes include both child protection and psychosocial elements, although the focus may vary according to the specific needs of the group.• Psychosocial support programmes should consider 3 domains of child wellbeing: skills and knowledge; emotional wellbeing and social wellbeing• Child protection programmes include structured group sessions that address elements of self-protection, safety, prevention, child rights. 2-Parenting Programmes• Parenting programmes may focus on helping parents understand, prevent and respond to protection risks for children, coping strategies for parents, child development, positive discipline and parents support groups etc.• Structured programmes include a curriculum and/or session activity plans.• Sustained programmes refer to a) ongoing programmes and b) a parent’s regular attendance over a specific period of time. 3-Awareness Raising Sessions, Community Events, Activity Days• Child protection activities aim to reach large numbers of children, parents and other members of the community.• Includes all types of child protection communication and community engagement activities that aim to change community knowledge, attitudes and practices on child protection and psychosocial support issues.• Includes recreational activities for children such as sports or arts that are open to all children and do not include structured curriculum or sustained attendance.• Includes information sessions on specific child protection issues delivered on an adhoc/casual basis.• Interventions should include a mix of methodologies, target a range of actors (not only children) and include at least some interactive methodologies (i.e. not only mass communication approaches). For campaigns and activities in specific geographical areas, an estimate based on the number of persons in the catchment area of this programme can be made in consultation with sector working groups.• Activities may take place through a variety of settings, e.g. community centres, schools, outreach/mobile etc 4-Case Management – Child Protection Issues Only • Girls and boys supported with case management – service for children at risk of or who have experienced violence, abuse and exploitation (e.g. separated and unaccompanied children, children associated with armed groups or forces, children in detention, child labour etc).• Individual or case-based services which should include an individual child protection assessment, care plan and follow-up that is in the best interests of the child, including referral to multi-sectoral services. 5-Training on Child Protection• Structured professional development/capacity building initiatives that aim to improve child protection response.• Training programmes may include introduction or basics of child protection in emergencies, child protection minimum standards, child safeguarding practices; Psychosocial First Aid, psychosocial support, community mobilization; case management. Training may also relate to a specific child protection concern, such CAAFAG or child marriage.• Targeted participants may include child protection actors (e.g. CFS supervisors, CFS facilitator) and other stakeholders (e.g. community volunteers, child protection committee members, staff from other sectors such as education, health etc.).

Impact: High.Mitigating measures: 1. ensure training packages are in line child protection minimum standards and consider remote managed context; 2. Services should not be provided by any staff who have not received and demonstrated proficiency in training modules; 3. include a practice element to training modules that can be overseen through remote mentorship arrangements to allow for practice prior to service provision; 4. Service mappings should only include services that have been determined to provide safe and quality services. 5. Establish consultation with communities and ensure feedback mechanisms are in place. 6. Child Protection messaging to non-Protection actors 2-Protection risk: 1. Impact: Medium.Mitigating measures: 1. Action plans for risk mitigation interventions 2.
