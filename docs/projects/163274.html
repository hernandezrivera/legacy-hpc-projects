Project Summary

Shelter has been identified as a critical need but the provision of shelter has been challenging with only 11% of the target for NFI and 8% for shelter PEP AFRICA, mainly populations in the easier to PEP AFRICA (peri-)urban centers along the main road axis. The operating environment is extremely tense with high levels of suspicions and fear among the population, in particular towards UN agencies (considered as supporting the Camerooninan government) and international NGOs (unknown actors). As such, PEP AFRICA has decided to partner with a network of local organizations – the Restless Peace Coalition comprising of 174 official members and over 200 affiliated organizations and ranging from regional NGOs over local NGOs to community based organizations – who will ensure “last mile” delivery of shelter and NFI assistance in rural, hard to reach areas. These local organizations are already at the forefront of the response, are trusted by the local population and have regular access. This action will capacitate these actors in principled, humanitarian response (through training of humanitarian principles, access negotiations, and general organizational capacity development related to provision of humanitarian assistance, incl. organization of distributions). PEP AFRICA will ensure the supply chain management (procurement and delivery) of shelter and NFI solutions tailored to the needs and specific context of the various population groups (i.e. displaced population self-settled in rural areas, displaced population in host families, etc.) in line with cluster standards and hand them over to members of the Restless Peace Coalition for last mile delivery. The planned response will target in priority IDPs informally self-settled in rural, hard to reach areas (with a specific focus on Ndian division in Southwest region which has so far not been receiving any shelter/NFI assistance) as they are considered the most vulnerable of the displaced population groups. Priority will be given to the most vulnerable among this population group such as women and children-headed households, families with elderly or disabled members, etc. PEP AFRICA monitors embedded with the local partner organizations will oversee and monitor the delivery of shelter/NFI assistance at the distribution site. In addition, extremely vulnerable individuals either hosted or renting accommodation in urban centers will be supported directly by PEP AFRICA with multi-purpose cash given largely functioning markets in those urban centers. Given the reluctance of the Cameroonian government towards the approval of cash based interventions, these cash transfers will be carried out “under the radar” using mobile money/e-transfer solutions. The action will closely collaborate with protection actors (e.g. INTERSOS, PLAN –Cameroon ) and setting-up referral pathways for the referral of identified protection cases with shelter and NFI needs.
Finally, PEP AFRICA will support the wider humanitarian system in the Northwest and Southwest regions of Cameroon through the collection, analysis, and dissemination of data, to inform evidence-based decision-making and targeting. This will be combined with mapping capacity to produce easily accessible and visual outputs that can quickly inform humanitarian actors - both implementing agencies and donors. PEP AFRICA will continue to work in close partnership with OCHA and the established Sector coordination mechanisms, including the WaSH Sector, the Cash Working Group, and other platforms, to ensure that data and findings are shared as widely as possible, thus contributing to an informed, and effective multi-sectoral response to meet the most urgent needs of conflict-affected populations.

Besoins / Needs

Needs: Several sectoral and multi-sectoral assessments all conclude that the humanitarian situation is deteriorating. Shelters, NFI and education have been identified in the Northwest and Southwest regions as the most urgent needs. According to the REACH Shelter/NFI Cluster Needs Assessment (December 2018), the majority of displaced are self-settled (about two thirds) and the remaining in host families (about one quarter). Self-settled displaced are mainly present in rural, non-village setting whereas displaced hosted are more present in rural, village settings and displaced renting an accommodation mainly in urban centers. Displaced self-settled populations were found to be overall most in need as they are difficult to reach/locate due to access constraints and limited communication routes. The overall shelter conditions in these communities are poor: i) the most common shelter types they live in (makeshift shelters, agricultural infrastructure, such as cacao ovens or in the open) offer very little protection from the elements, which is likely linked to the fact that they tend to resort to less durable materials and less efficient tools to build their shelters than other population groups, ii) they have poor access to water and sanitation and iii) they are highly exposed to health risks due to the presence of malaria in these densely forested regions. The areas of origin of these communities have been significantly destroyed, leaving limited short to medium term perspectives of return. During the assessment, insecurity was found to be the most common reason for displacement, reported by 90% of KIs, reflecting the high level of conflict in the regions. In addition, 44% of KIs reported complete destruction of shelters in their locality and 30% reported partial destruction of shelters . This finding is consistent with the regular reports of villages being destroyed (usually burnt down), especially along major road axes. The top three shelter priorities for these communities were identified as construction materials (86%), money (73%) and construction tools (68%). The assessment also revealed that displaced populations in rural areas do not have sufficient non food items, namely beddings, cooking items, water containers, mosquito nets and soap. Mosquito nets and beddings appear to be particularly lacking, with 78% reporting that no or few family members have enough of those. 91% of self-settled IDPs and 71% of hosted IDPs living in damaged and destroyed shelters sleep on sleeping mats and/or on the ground floor (68% and 20%, respectively). Cooking utensils (86%), bedding, clotes, jerrycans and soap (all 82%) were found to be the top most useful NFIs for displaced self-settled populations and bedding (90%), mosquito nets (79%) and cooking utensils (77%) for displaced hosted populations. Population groups who have fled their areas of origin and who are either hosted or are renting accommodation in urban centeres face different challenges: They often live in Cameroon Development Cooperation (CDC) camps (i.e. estates on large banana or rubber plantations) or unfinished buildings with very basic partitioning within these structures. IDPs renting but also those that are being hosted (the line is fluid) pay a rent of approximately 15,000 XAF/month per room. These IDPs were found to be at risk of eviction, due to the common lack of formal renting arrangements and the reliance on unsustainable means (mainly “carrying forward” debt) to pay rent. During both the REACH Shelter Cluster Assessment as well as ACTED’s own assessment, some respondents reported that the number of people living in their current shelter had multiplied by four, with women, men and children having to share sleeping areas. It is common for 8-10 people to share a single room. Overcrowding are causes of conflict and dispute, including SGBV, among room occupants (regardless of whether friend or relatives). A significant proportion of people who have fled their areas.
