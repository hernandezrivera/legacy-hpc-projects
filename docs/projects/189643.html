Project Summary

As of January 2022, there are some 335,000 refugees and asylum seekers from neighbouring countries sheltering in South Sudan. Despite enormous socio-economic, security and political challenges, compounded by the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, South Sudan has maintained its open-door policy for people seeking international protection, the majority of whom are women and children, primarily from Sudan, the Democratic Republic of Congo and the Central African Republic.
At the same time, against a backdrop of fragile peace and cautious optimism for longer term stability, hundreds of thousands of South Sudanese refugees have spontaneously returned to the country since the signing of the 2018 Revitalized Peace Agreement. While UNHCR is not promoting or facilitating return to South Sudan, this spontaneous movement has prompted the development of an area-based approach, to support refugees, returnees and host communities, through enhanced engagement with development actors, to support greater access to services where possible.
Refugees largely reside in conflict-affected regions, challenged by an unpredictable security environment as well as at times fragile relations with host communities. Displaced communities will continue to rely on life-saving humanitarian assistance provided by UNHCR and its partners as a result of enduring economic hardship, weak rule of law, limited development support and a lack of social services and infrastructure in refugee-hosting areas as well as in areas of origin. A reduction in basic assistance, especially during a global pandemic, will immediately lead to the deterioration of the humanitarian situation to emergency levels, due to the absence of a safety net, including social services, community support networks and limited access to livelihoods in displacements sites hosting refugees and South Sudanese refugee returnees.
Under the refugee response, all refugees and asylum seekers registered with UNHCR will benefit from various forms of protection interventions, including individual registration and documentation, and multi-sector assistance. Specific protection services, such as SGBV response and child protection will focus on the identified at-risk groups. While water, health, nutrition and education services will continue to maintain indicators within global standards for refugees living in camps, provision of other assistance, including shelter, non-food items and livelihoods support, will be undertaken in a targeted manner, focusing on persons with specific protection needs and vulnerabilities. This will primarily be achieved through participatory assessments, surveys as well as verification exercise to identify vulnerable refugees and their needs. Impact monitoring will be used to gauge the efficiency of projects implemented and for the purposes of course-correction in terms of better targeting of assistance. The needs of persons with disabilities and the elderly will be further mainstreamed in various multi-sector response services. Vulnerable refugees in urban settings will also continue to benefit from targeted protection interventions and facilitation of their access to national services.

1 - Needs

By the end of 2021, South Sudan hosted some 335,000 refugees located in over 21 different locations across the country. Nearly all refugees and asylum live in camps or planned settlements (98 per cent) located in four of the ten States: Upper Nile (53 per cent); Unity (38 per cent); Central Equatoria (5 per cent) and Western Equatoria (4 per cent). Only 2 per cent are living in urban locations with individual accommodation, primarily living in Juba and Yei towns. 92 per cent of the refugees and asylum seekers are from Sudan, with smaller populations from the Central African Republic (CAR), the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), Ethiopia, Eritrea, Somalia, and Burundi, with the largest group coming from DRC (18,000 refugees). Refugees and asylum seekers from Sudan originate from Blue Nile State (55 per cent) and South Kordofan State in Sudan (45 per cent). 52 per cent of the refugees are female, and 59 per cent of refugee are children under the age of 18. Women and children represent 80 per cent of the total refugee population. No significant arrivals from Ethiopia have been reported in South Sudan but an increase in the number of South Sudanese refugee returning from Ethiopia to South Sudan has been reported because of the ongoing conflict and requiring contingency planning and preparedness measures.Lack of access to adequate basic services compounds the protection and humanitarian needs. The pandemic has further exacerbated protection risks in South Sudan. The COVID-19 related restrictions on movement worsened an already fragile economic situation; although South Sudan has to date allowed unimpeded access to asylum for those seeking international protection and ensuring that persons of concern are included in the national COVID-19 prevention and response plan.

2 - Project activities

In close collaboration with the Commission for Refugee Affairs (CRA), UNHCR will further strengthen and institutionalise the government’s capacity to protect refugees in line with international standards, respond and prevent gender based violence (GBV), protect refugee children, and enhance access to justice. Key areas of focus include ensuring access to territory, reception, registration, status determination and documentation. UNHCR will continue to work with the Directorate of Nationality, Passport and Immigration (DNPI) of the Ministry of Interior for the operationalization of the government's National Plan of Action to End Statelessness which was endorsed in 2020. To promote civilian and humanitarian character of asylum, protection monitoring, and advocacy will continue in addition to strengthening capacities of the Government to ensure safety and security in areas hosting refugees including Maban, Pariang, Yambio, Yei, Gorom, Pochalla and Juba in line with conflict sensitivity principles. Community consultation and participation are at the heart of the identification, design, implementation and monitoring of our programs, to ensure the proposed projects address the actual needs in a specific location and do not cause any unintended harm to the targeted population. Refugee Response partners use an Age, Gender, and Diversity (AGD) approach in all aspects of their operations. The approach will guide the intervention in order for different groups, especially women and girls, as well as vulnerable individuals to be given proper representation in the community structures and decision making process to ensure that their concerns are heard and needs are met. Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse mechanisms are integrated throughout the programme cycle with the use of a survivor-centred approach emphasizing safety, confidentiality, accountability, transparency and accessibility. Community-based support will enhance coping capacities in areas of high return and at building resilience to promote sustainable solutions to displacement. Response modalities will combine direct service provision, in-kind support, limited cash and voucher assistance as well as community-based support and advocacy interventions. Understanding community dynamics and ensuring potential tensions are prevented and alleviated are critical in refugee-hosting and refugee return areas. Ensuring peaceful coexistence and host community support activities are an integral part of the response, including through equitable access to available basic services such as education, water and health, and community-based support benefiting host communities based on needs. To drive transformation that will empower communities and achieve gender equality, the response will build on its efforts over the years to engage communities using community and rights-based approaches and support a robust inter-agency response through evidence-based analysis and prioritization of protection risks and needs to inform effective interventions at state and national level. UNHCR will continue to strengthen collaboration with actors engaged in early recovery and development and garnering support towards the Global Compact on Refugees in refugee-hosting and refugee return areas. This will build resilience, empower communities, particularly women and youths, and create enabling conditions for solutions for refugees in and from South Sudan in collaboration with the Government. Refugee response partners will maintain a presence in strategic and priority field locations, including Pariang, Maban, Yei and Yambio, amongst others. Outreach, vulnerability assessments, return and protection monitoring will also be strengthened through protection desks and mobile response in the areas where UNHCR and partners do not have a static presence in refugee return areas.

3 - Project indicators

UNHCR will collect and analyze data against the set protection indicators for the response plan regularly. This will include disaggregated population data and profiles, sector data and routine collection of project indicators to inform protection analysis and success of the project. Protection monitoring and participatory assessments will inform a robust situation analysis and ensure targeted population, including those with specific needs and the most vulnerable, are consulted during the design, implementation and following implementation of the activity. Rights-based and the Age, Gender, and Diversity Approach principles are applied throughout the programme cycle to ensure that the voice, needs and feedback of persons of concern, particularly those with chronic medical issues, older persons, separated and unaccompanied children, persons with disabilities, women and children are taken into account, and that gaps, challenges and solutions are identified.Various community outreach channels and communication modalities will be used to ensure accountability to affected communities in the response. This includes bolstered feedback and complaint desks, hotlines, focus discussion groups, community-based networks, and community coordination meetings.

5 - Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA)
5.1. Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA)?

Yes

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

When people are forced to flee their homes, they leave with little more than they can carry. They also lose their ability to earn and spend in the process. Cash or vouchers seek to protect displaced persons by reducing the risks they face and to maintain their capacity to spend. CVA can be used in a variety of settings, as long as there is a stable market and a safe way to provide displaced persons with cash or vouchers. The flexibility that CVAs offer makes them a more dignified form of assistance, giving displaced persons the ability to immediately prioritize and choose what they need. CVA is contingent on a market assessments and protection assessments. Therefore, the CVA response will be conditional and limited.

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

Informed through needs assessments, community-based networks and stakeholders working on CVA feasibility and modalities, the project design will be adapted accordingly to the current context and reality.

8.11. Mitigation Measures.

UNHCR undertakes protection and risk assessments ahead of project implementation directly or through partners and in some instances through the multi sector approach. Many of the risks in the targeted locations are identified and mitigation measures taken. UNHCR and partners works to mitigate risks on PSEA and SGBV at project design stage incorporating the zero tolerance element. Annual participatory assessments are undertaken across all project locations using the Age Gender Diversity mainstreaming methodology. Feedback sessions engaging communities including youth groups, community leaders, women groups and persons with disabilities for discussions on implemented projects and needs. These sessions provide UNHCR and partners resource for advocacy and review of projects implemented to adjust to changing needs.

12.1. Does your project contribute to any of the three Collective Outcomes, i.e. Improved Food security; Enhanced support for prevention and response to needs of survivors of Gender-Based Violence; and Flood displacement?

Yes

12.1.2. Collective Outcome 2 - By 2023, a minimum package for prevention, risk mitigation and response to GBV rolled out in 80% of priority locations, focusing on safe, timely access to quality case management, psychosocial services and capacity building.

The response will strengthen ongoing efforts for persons of concern to preserve natural resources which are used in ensuring that individuals live in habitable and affordable housing. The response will, among other initiatives, increase access to durable shelters which are located in safe and secure areas with minimal hazards or risks.

12.1.3. Collective Outcome 3 - Flood related displacement is 30% lower in 2025 than in 2021.

The response will support community based GBV response and prevention mechanisms including ensuring that persons at risk of GBV and survivors receive psychosocial support including case management, cash for protection, medical assistance. UNHCR and partners will facilitate access to emergency safe shelters and work to increase knowledge levels regarding available GBV services.

12.1.1. Collective Outcome 1 - By 2023 (lean season May – July), there is a reduction in the number of people in IPC 4 (by 20%) and IPC 5 (Zero).

The response will lead to a reduction in the number of people with large food consumption gaps through providing a multi sectoral support including provision of inputs for agriculture such as seeds and tools. Through the Global Compact for Refugee, UNHCR is working closely with the government and national and state level which is advocating for the provision of land for agricultural purposes around refugee camps. UNHCR and partners will carry out a number of agricultural capacity building initiatives which include life skills, entrepreneurship and business skills.
