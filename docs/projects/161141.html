Project Summary

The proposed programme aims to provide protection from malaria and other vector-borne diseases (VBDs) to the highly vulnerable Internally Displaced People (IDPs) population in Bentiu PoC (Rubkona), Malakal PoC (Unity) and Juba PoC (Central Equatoria) as well as the host community in Maban. To achieve this, The MENTOR Initiative will focus on preventing diseases-transmitting vectors from multiplying and entering the VBD transmission cycle, which causes a great deal of morbidity and mortality. Indoor Residual Spraying (IRS), larviciding and fly control measures will be employed as tools to disrupt disease transmission. In addition, MENTOR will adopt an integrated approach and the camp inhabitants will not only be educated on the diseases itself and how to prevent infection, but also on the impact of water and sanitation on disease transmission as well as (mal-) nutrition and associated disease severity. This will be achieved through delivering targeted IEC/BCC campaign and health education sessions. Lastly, health facilities in the camp as well as those serving the nearby host community will be supported by offering comprehensive trainings on malaria and other VBDs differential diagnosis and case management for nursing and medical staff to improve the quality of health service delivery. The MoH and PSI plan to conduct a mass distribution of Long-Lasting Insecticidal Nets (LLINs) in 2020, and MENTOR proposes to support the campaign through IEC/BCC in the target locations.
MENTOR is an HRP member and the mentioned activities were planned and implemented in SSHF Allocation Round 1 and 2 in Bentiu PoC in 2019, with significant results achieved.
To align with the 2020 Health Cluster targets, MENTOR plan to reach 25,000 people in the targeted areas. However, the proposed activities are community-based, and based on previous programmes and experiences, we estimate that up to 282,611 people could directly benefit from them.

1 - Needs

Malaria is the leading cause of morbidity in South Sudan, accounting for 80.0% of all morbidities and 68.4% of all mortalities in Epidemiological Week (EW) 38 of 2019 (1). Adding this to the circumstances that in the post-harvest period of January to April 2020, the food security situation is expected to deteriorate and an estimated 5.5 million people are likely to face IPC Phase 3 (crisis) or worse food insecurity (2) and that chronic malnutrition has consistently been associated with severity of malaria (3) doesn’t bode well for the health oeople living in high-transmission areas in South Sudan. The transmission season starts in June/ July onwards and is sustained through until October/November, though it may be extended if the rains persist as in 2019.People in the camps in Bentiu, Malakal and Juba have been displaced by long-lasting conflict, there is a lack of capacity from static partners/community/local, national, government authorities to initiate an assessment and/or response and there’s a high level of vulnerability of the affected population. In Maban county, people suffered from devastating floods in October 2019 and there are high chances of malaria upsurges in late 2019 and early 2020. In 2018, in Bentiu PoC 110,914 malaria cases, in Malakal 6,993 malaria cases, and in the Maban host community (including IDPs and spontaneous refugees) 10,771 malaria cases were recorded. Similarly, Juba is one of the highly endemic locations for malaria. According to the WHO IDSR report (Oct 2019) Juba has been among several locations that have recorded high malaria upsurges above the threshold level were recorded. The main challenge to control these diseases is limited access to health facilities. In previous epidemics, ramped-up numbers of malaria cases have overwhelmed health facilities in both Bentiu and Malakal. The capacity to screen, diagnose and treat malaria cases has been stretched to a failing point before and increased patients’ risk of developing severe forms that are otherwise easily cured with a correct diagnosis and immediate treatment. Frequent rainfalls and flooding, together with the overcrowded setting of the camps, provide optimal conditions for mosquito breeding and high malaria transmission. Devastating floods in Maban could further deteriorate the malaria situation. IDPs also face the threat of water- and faecal-borne diseases due to poor sanitary conditions. There is a significant burden of diarrhoeal disease in South Sudan, and they are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, especially among young children (4). The causative organisms are both water-borne and vector-borne and poor camp sanitation, open defecation sites and shelter living conditions contribute to the high caseload (5). This programme will provide life-saving protection for the communities of Bentiu, Malakal and Juba PoCs and Maban by targeting disease vectors and therefore preventing transmission of malaria and other VBDs as well as educating and training target community members inhabitants of the camp and health facility staff both within and outside the PoCs. The vector control package consists of treatment of mosquito and fly breeding sites using larvicide, IEC/BCC messaging, group health education sessions and VBD case management trainings for health facility staff. 2 - Activities

Capacity building for health care workers:The capacity of health care workers will be built and improved through trainings of health care workers in health facilities in the camps. They will be trained on the pathology, symptoms, correct differential diagnosis, and case management of VBDs that contribute to morbidity and mortality amongst the population within the PoCs. On the job training will be provided to serve as follow-up supervision visits. Trainings will be offered to nursing and medical staff of all health facilities in the camps. Training health care workers will directly increase and improve the access of IDPs and host communities to good quality case management of malaria. IEC production and dissemination:IEC production and dissemination will be implemented through door-to-door IEC campaigns as well as health education sessions for community leaders, who then carry the messages into their families and communities. Integrated IEC will focus on delivering standardised messages, including LLIN usage, symptoms of VBDs and adequate treatment seeking, a good nutritional status, promotion of good hygiene and many more. Disease surveillance and outbreak responseMENTOR regularly gets data (weekly in most locations) on the incidence of several vector-borne and epidemic-prone diseases from health facilities within and surrounding the camps, and as a result can report on the frequency and accuracy of data submissions and possible disease outbreaks, of which partners will be informed so that appropriate counter measures can be implemented. Epidemic disease prevention and control measuresTo decrease the incidence of epidemic-prone diseases such as malaria and acute diarrhoeal diseases, MENTOR proposes to implement a three-pronged approach: Indoor Residual Spraying, mosquito larviciding and fly control. IRS: The control of adult mosquito disease vectors through IRS is key in reducing the risk of beneficiaries being infected with malaria). MENTOR plans to use the newly WHO-prequalified insecticide SumiShield WP50. Its long residual effect up to 7 months in different countries has been well documented. In line with WHO recommendations, the IRS campaign will aim to achieve at least 85% coverage of shelters/houses. Impact of the IRS intervention will be measured through weekly epidemiological assessment of malaria incidence rate as compared to the corresponding week in 2019. Larviciding: Reducing the vector breeding capacity through Larval Source Management (LSM) ensures that the disease transmission capacity of Anopheles (malaria), Aedes (dengue, yellow fever and chikungunya) as well as Culex mosquitoes (which breed in contaminated water) is limited through preventing larval development. The WHO-recommended larvicide pyriproxyfen (insect growth regulator) will be applied to all standing water containing mosquito larvae. Based on findings, 90-95% of all identified potential breeding sites will be treated. Additionally, larviciding will be delivered as required depending on larval density and rainfall. Fly control: Flies can act as vectors for a multitude of diseases, of a diarrhoeal nature in particular. The WHO-recommended chemical pyriproxyfen for fly larval control will be used for the application in latrines, damp rubbish piles and open defecations to prevent transmission of such diseases through flies. Based on findings, 90-95% of all identified breeding sites will be treated. Procurement of outbreak response commodities:MENTOR will coordinate with existing health partners in all three camps and the Maban host communities and provide them with medical commodities needed to deal with VBDs, such as Rapid Diagnostic Tests and antimalarial drugs, to avoid stock-outs and that, in conjunction with the training of health care workers, diagnosis and treatment of VBDs will be greatly improved. It is expected that up to 282,611 people could directly benefit from the activities, based on previous programmes.

6.5 - Includes perspectives of displacement-affected people?

- The recruitment will be from the community through government and community leaders and they will be involved in programme design and implementation of the activities in their respective communities. At the same time, the proposed programme primarily targets and benefits displaced and vulnerable communities and the activities do not discriminate against gender, age, religion or disability, making the project accessible to everyone.

6.2 - Is the project linked to government/community systems?

- For IRS, mosquito and fly larviciding as well as for IEC campaigns MENTOR recruits and trains workers directly from the communities, who will retain their knowledge for future campaigns. Trainings for health care workers in health facilities directly target people coming from and serving the community, building up capacity, which translates into better quality of health services delivery in the long term. IEC campaigns and health education sessions also build capacity and resilience in the communities. The selection of workers will be done in liaison with the BHI (where already established) or through the government and community (chiefs, sheikhs, leaders) to ensure the monitoring and accountability of activities.

6.3 - Is the project aligned with national objectives?

- The proposed interventions/activities aim at building the capacity of the affected populations to prevent VBD outbreaks. All activities will be implemented in close coordination with the affected populations and other stakeholders, such as implementing partners, to avoid duplication and guarantee a proper allocation and use of available resources. Besides this, all services will be free of charge and all the affected population are entitled to receive the services provided. The proposed interventions are in accordance with the Malaria Strategic Plan (2014/15-2020/21) of the National Malaria Control Programme of South Sudan. The capacity building of health care providers is aligned with the national treatment policy and guidelines of South Sudan. All proposed community-based interventions will be implemented through the BHI team (if available) or selection of workers through the respective government authorities and community leaders aligned with the BHI policy. MENTOR adopts the WHO IVM strategy for the control of vector-borne diseases.

6.1 - Is the protection environment sufficiently safe/stable?

- The chosen locations have proven to be safe and stable in previous years, as METOR implemented a range of activities, such as IRS, larviciding, fly control and IEC campaigns. A range of humanitarian organizations have had and still have presence in all locations, providing a wide range of reliable partners. In addition, the security situations are assessed on an ongoing bases and very few incidences have been reported as of yet. Capacity-building activities, such as IEC campaigns and health care staff trainings have proven to be durable solutions for tackling high rates of VBD incidence, while activities such as IRS and larviciding have a more short-term effect, but parts of the populations still retain their knowledge that they have gained during their times as daily workers and they can be called upon in future campaigns.

6.4 - Does the project include sustainability/handover?

- The government health authorities as of yet do not possess the financial as well as the technical prerequisites to carry out activities such as IRS or larviciding. The knowledge that daily workers have acquired will stay however and MENTOR hopes that the government will assume ownership of the programme in the future and provide assistance wherever needed. Moreover, the training of health workers on differential diagnosis and treatment will build long-term and sustainable capacity.

8.18 - Please indicate how the risks selected above will be mitigated.

Forced taxation of humanitarian assistance: We have measures in place to avoid external corruption (e.g. following all country laws, correct documentation in vehicles) – and the support and backup of the NGO UN agencies community (e.g. UNDSS response teams). Communities leave their habitual place of residence for the purpose of getting assistance: With an ongoing slow implementation of the peace agreement, conflicts may break out again and drive humanitarian organizations away from where they previously offered assistance, leading to vulnerable people following them and leaving the project implementation site. In such cases, MENTOR will continue to offer and implement the proposed activities while at the same time look into possibilities of expanding the programme to a new project site to reach all beneficiaries that have been indicated to be reached in the initial proposal. MENTOR has a record of working in the most insecure and volatile settings and is able to work under such conditions. At the same time, the proposed activities do not trigger a pull effect, as activities target ‘established’ populations of IDPs as well as host communities and for activities such as IRS, people need to live in at least semi-permanent structures. Interference of external stakeholders in impartial and needs-based selection of beneficiaries: MENTOR programmes target all beneficiaries in a project area, so that no people will be left out or subject to less services as a result of partial selection and/or selection processes that negate the principle of offering services strictly on a needs basis. MENTOR programmes have experienced the occurrence of favouritism in the past in relation to the selection of daily workers for IRS, larviciding and fly control campaigns, leading to pressure to recruit staff that may not have high qualifications. This risk is mitigated by closely working together with the local communities and recruiting directly from them and applying strict criteria.

9.14 - How will you project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable community members?

MENTOR is committed to programmatic gender mainstreaming, and by their very nature, MENTOR’s programme activities do not discriminate against people based on gender, age or disability. Malaria and most other VBDs affect all ages and genders, so entire communities are targeted in order to ensure maximum protection, reduce the human pool of infection and onward disease transmission. The prevention activities provide benefits to all household members. During IRS, larviciding campaigns and IEC sessions, MENTOR encourages equal participation of all genders, age groups and people with disabilities in planning, implementation and monitoring of core activities. Previous and current MENTOR records indicate a balanced participation of women/men and youths (both boys and girls), and vulnerable groups are identified and prioritised in terms of service delivery. The training for IRS will also include information on gender-related issues related to MENTOR’s policies on anti-harassment guidelines. Through engaging community members from the outset of programme activities, MENTOR ensures that local buy-in is assured. As part of community mobilisation, meetings with community leaders are held on a regular basis, providing an opportunity for MENTOR to share and discuss proposed actions and to actively discuss community feedback. Ongoing reporting throughout the duration of the program allows feedback to be taken into account and activities amended to best meet the needs of the beneficiary population.MENTOR also actively engages with the camp management feedback and reporting structures that enable the target communities to make complaints and / or suggestions without having to meet with MENTOR staff directly. This contributes to ensuring an environment in which target communities feel able to give feedback without any fear of a negative reaction/consequences.
