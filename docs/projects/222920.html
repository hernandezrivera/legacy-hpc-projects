Project Summary

The Project aims to improve the resilience and food security of Rohingya refugees and host communities in Cox's Bazar through climate-smart agriculture, livelihood diversification, and enhanced market linkages. Project will establish sustainable food production systems to address nutritional needs and reduce dependency on external aid, capacity enhancement in climate-smart agriculture, focusing on young people and women, diversify livelihood options by fostering skills in both agricultural and off-farm trades and increase connections between farmers and local markets, creating stable income sources for participants. Through establishing micro-nutrition gardens and providing training in climate-resilient farming techniques, the project seeks to increase access to fresh produce and reduce malnutrition. Skill development in agricultural sector offers diversified income options, particularly targeting youth and women for greater economic inclusion. Additionally, the project fosters market linkages by connecting local producers with buyers, organizing product fairs, and providing mentorship, thereby enhancing participants' income stability and independence. Ultimately, this initiative empowers both communities to achieve food self-sufficiency, reduce reliance on aid, and establish resilient livelihoods aligned with the local environment. Participation in homestead food production using climate smart agricultural technologies will be main theme of project. These training is aligned with individual interests and resources availability, identified through consultations and assessments. Diverse training methods, including outreach centers, a blended model, and referrals to government and private providers, will be utilized.
Additionally, the project will focus to empower young men and women (ages 18–24) from Rohingya camps and host, equipping them with essential skills, assets, and networks needed for transitioning into decent work and adulthood. The working area will be camp-7,9,10,15 & 21 with 1410 participants and host in both upazila Ukhiya & Technaf with 2190 participants. The approach emphasizes sustainable homestead food production interventions that foster agency, build resilience, and facilitate dignified livelihoods and self-reliance activities for youth in both refugee and host communities. Social cohesion activities are integrated to mitigate tension through mutual relationships and local structures. Key components are:
• Climate-Smart Agriculture Training sessions on sustainable agricultural practices, such as water-efficient farming, drought-resistant crop varieties, and soil health management.
• Establish household micro-nutrition gardens to improve access to fresh vegetables.
• Train youth in crop cultivation, livestock management, and agro-based product creation. Provide participants with essential agricultural inputs (seeds, fertilizers, gardening tools) to kick-start their farming activities.
• Link local farmers to nearby markets by building partnerships with wholesalers and local businesses.
The project leverages community-based approaches to ensure participation and ownership among Rohingya and host communities. Implementing partners collaborate with local stakeholders, including Save the Children, YPSA, and UCEP Bangladesh, to deliver training, distribute inputs, and build capacity in sustainable agricultural practices.
Further to enhance collaboration and coordination with FSL actors and the FSS, Save the Children (SCI) will:
• Actively participate in FSS coordination meetings to align objectives and share updates.
• Share best practices and innovative approaches, such as climate-smart agriculture and micro-nutrition gardens.
• Facilitate cross-agency learning through joint workshops, training sessions, and exposure visits.
• Promote social cohesion through joint initiatives that foster trust and shared opportunities between Rohingya and host communities.

1. Needs
1 - Identified Needs (including gender considerations)

Cox’s Bazar hosts one of the largest refugee populations in the world, with nearly one million Rohingya refugees settled in camps. The influx has increased strain on natural resources, land, water, and local infrastructure, leading to environmental degradation and competition for resources with the host communities. As a result, Both communities face food insecurity. The refugees rely heavily on humanitarian food assistance, while the host communities struggle with limited agricultural productivity and fluctuating market prices. Rising food costs have made it difficult for both populations to maintain a nutritionally adequate diet. The region is highly vulnerable to natural disasters, including cyclones, floods, and landslides, frequently disrupting agriculture. Soil erosion, deforestation, and land degradation have further compromised arable land, affecting both food production and livelihoods. The Rohingya in camps have limited access to land and other essential agricultural resources. The few livelihood opportunities are typically small-scale gardening or agro-based income-generating activities. Knowledge of sustainable and climate-resilient agricultural practices is minimal due to resource constraints and lack of formal training. Host community farmers in Ukhiya and Teknaf face challenges in productivity due to degraded soil, limited access to quality seeds, irrigation issues, and traditional farming methods. Their exposure to climate-smart practices is limited, and the absence of a reliable market connection restricts their income-earning potential. Employment options are minimal in both communities, particularly for youth and women. Income is unstable and highly reliant on limited agricultural yields, casual labour, or aid assistance. The Rohingya community is heavily dependent on aid for basic needs, with limited scope for self-reliant or sustainable income-generating activities. Host community members, although somewhat less aid-dependent, also face economic challenges due to competition for resources and employment with the Rohingya population. Based on the Rapid Market Assessment, there is a need for skills training on climate-resilient agricultural practices, including water conservation, soil improvement techniques, crop rotation, organic pest control, and efficient planting techniques. Training in both food crop production (such as vegetable gardening) and non-food agricultural activities (such as bamboo and handicrafts) can help diversify income streams, especially for Rohingya community members with restricted land access. Both communities need access to affordable, quality seeds, organic fertilizers, and necessary tools to improve productivity and ensure better crop yields. Water scarcity, especially in dry seasons, hampers agricultural productivity. Investing in water-efficient technologies (e.g., drip irrigation) and alternative sources (such as rainwater harvesting) would address this gap. For the Rohingya camps, space is limited, so providing resources for micro-gardening (e.g., sack gardening, vertical gardening) is essential for small-scale food production. Facilitating market linkages for agricultural produce and handicrafts from both communities would help enhance income stability. Establishing cooperatives or market link networks could enable producers to sell their goods locally and in nearby towns. Training in basic business skills, such as financial management, record-keeping, and marketing, is crucial to support entrepreneurial growth. Ensuring that women and youth have equitable access to financial resources would be key to inclusivity. (Sources of information: District Development and Growth Priorities, December 2022 and Study Report: Rapid Market Assessment, July 2024, SCI)

2. Cross-cutting Themes
2.1 - Protection Mainstreaming

The project is designed to prioritize the Centrality of Protection by integrating protection measures across all outcomes. A sectoral integration approach will support this effort through cross-sector referrals and capacity building for staff and volunteers, equipping & sensitize them to identify and respond to critical protection needs. Child Protection (CP) and GBV activities will align with Education, Livelihood, and Skills sectors through capacity building on CP and effective referral mechanisms. In line with CP Minimum Standards in Humanitarian Action, Sphere Standards, and Core Humanitarian Standards, the project prioritizes safety, dignity, and minimizing unintended risks for all beneficiaries. Access will be fostered through a non-discriminatory approach addressing barriers related to disability, age, and gender. SC’s accountability, feedback, and response system will ensure meaningful engagement of affected communities throughout the project cycle. To empower vulnerable populations, the project will enhance the capacities of children, caregivers, and community members to advocate for their rights. A ‘Zero Tolerance’ policy on safeguarding and Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) is enforced, with clear reporting channels. Additionally, all project staff and volunteers will be trained in Safeguarding and PSEA, ensuring a safe and inclusive project environment.

2.3 - Age & Disability

The project promotes meaningful access through a non-discriminatory approach, addressing barriers related to disability, age, and gender. A gender-sensitive environment for skills and vocational training is a priority, with initiatives to strengthen the capacity of staff and partners on gender and inclusion, with a focus on young women. The project also prioritizes inclusivity for youth with disabilities, setting a target of 3% participation in skill development programs to foster inclusive livelihood opportunities. Protection mainstreaming indicators are embedded across project management to monitor and reinforce the inclusion of all vulnerable groups, ensuring their needs are consistently addressed throughout the project cycle.

2.4 - Accountability to Affected Populations (AAP)

Accountability forms the core of Save the Children's mission to Shift the Power. Being accountable to youth and communities means SC is dedicated to using its influence responsibly. SC will ensure that youth's voices drive its actions, enabling youth and communities to actively participate in and significantly impact all decisions that affect their lives.The project will establish a comprehensive Feedback Response Mechanism (FRM) to ensure inclusive accountability for host and camp children. The FRM will employ various methods such as home visits, feedback boxes, hotlines, help desks, and participatory sessions, providing adolescents and youths with accessible ways to share their feedback. These methods, along with staff capacity-building, will help address and respond to protection needs.An information-sharing plan, integrated into the implementation strategy, will ensure all materials are accessible to participants, even those with low literacy levels. Feedback will be uploaded to an online FRM system, where the project manager will categorize, investigate, and resolve issues. An accountability focal point will verify each case using established SOPs, ensuring the program team can effectively close the feedback loop. Regular accountability reports and project review meetings will be used to assess and integrate feedback, enhancing program impact.To promote accountability, a contextual information-sharing plan will communicate clear accountability messages to all staff, fostering a unified approach across Save the Children (SC) and partners. Quarterly accountability reports will be shared, and in line with SC's localization goals, training and support will strengthen AAP mechanisms for all implementing partners.

2.5.2 Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) mainstreaming

The project is firmly committed to Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) within its activities for Rohingya refugees and host communities in Cox’s Bazar, emphasizing the safety of vulnerable youth, especially women. Integrated within the project’s PSEA measures include age-specific, accessible reporting tools, such as participatory sessions, home visits, feedback boxes, and help desks, allowing children and adolescents to safely report instances of sexual exploitation and abuse. A dedicated Feedback Response Mechanism (FRM) supports confidential reporting channels specifically for PSEA concerns. Trained staff will be equipped to handle PSEA cases sensitively and ensure that survivors receive immediate support, reinforcing the project's commitment to a protective environment. To build awareness and maintain transparency, the project will disseminate PSEA guidelines through an information-sharing plan, making essential guidelines accessible to all staff and volunteers. Quarterly accountability reports will review PSEA actions and outcomes, promoting a culture of accountability. By embedding PSEA measures within the project’s core activities, this initiative fosters a safe, responsive environment that actively prevents, addresses, and mitigates risks of sexual exploitation and abuse for all participants.

2.6 - Does your project have any DRR/DRM elements and/or environmental issues and/or climate change mitigation measures? If yes, please explain.

The project integrates Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) and Disaster Risk Management (DRM) to build resilience among vulnerable populations in the Rohingya camps and host communities of Cox’s Bazar. Recognizing the region’s high susceptibility to natural disasters such as cyclones, floods, and landslides, DRR activities are woven into the project framework to enhance community preparedness, reduce risks, and promote adaptive capacity.Key components of the DRR strategy include community-led risk assessments and the establishment of Early Warning Systems (EWS) to ensure that households can respond rapidly in the event of an impending disaster. To improve local readiness, the project will train community volunteers and youth on disaster preparedness, response, and recovery processes. Through skill-building workshops/workshop, participants will gain knowledge on first aid, emergency planning, and safe evacuation procedures, which can be crucial in crisis scenarios.The project also emphasizes climate-resilient infrastructure within learning centers, ensuring facilities are structurally sound and equipped to withstand extreme weather events. To strengthen adaptive capacity, DRR training will include environmental management practices, such as reforestation and erosion control, to reduce landslide risks and restore degraded land.In partnership with local government and humanitarian agencies, the project aims to establish community-based DRM committees that can coordinate efforts and communicate effectively with external responders. Additionally, outreach activities and awareness campaigns will foster a culture of safety, with a focus on preparing families for both short-term disasters and long-term climate impacts. Gender-sensitive planning will ensure that all DRR initiatives consider the needs of women, children, and people with disabilities, empowering these groups to participate actively in resilience-building efforts.

2.7 Does the project have EPR measures embedded? (e.g. business continuity plan, emergency response standard operation procedures, and contingency funds) If Yes, briefly describe.

Bangladesh is highly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change, with the southeast coast experiencing significant challenges including water scarcity. This environmental challenge has severe implications for resilient livelihoods, negatively impacting productivity and, consequently, the livelihoods of local communities. Natural disasters such as floods, landslides and cyclones further exacerbate these issues, perpetually disrupting people's livelihoods. To address these challenges, project will be integrating Emergency Preparedness and Response (EPR) with livelihoods interventions in the Rohingya, and host communities enhances resilience and helps communities face recurring crises with stronger, adaptable skills and resources. Below is the breakdown of how EPR can be woven into skill development programs to create sustainable impacts in the project settings:• Integrating EPR into livelihoods programming involves assessing the specific vulnerabilities of both Rohingya refugees and host communities and embedding risk mitigation strategies.• EPR-focused livelihood interventions train participants to manage resources during both stable and crisis periods.• Establishing local networks connects livelihoods participants with emergency response groups, local markets, and government relief programs.• Integrating EPR with livelihoods ensures that community voices are at the forefront of planning and implementation.• Establishing safe training centers equipped with essential resources, like solar systems or durable storage spaces, helps ensure continuity of livelihood activities in an emergency.Through integrating EPR with livelihoods interventions project will ensures that Rohingya and host communities have more than just income-generation opportunities—they gain adaptable skills, resources, and networks to endure and recover from crises. This strategic approach builds self-reliance, improves community cohesion and sustainable resilient livelihoods.

2.8 Does your project include or engage youth (i.e. 15-24 year-olds)? If Yes, briefly describe.

This project actively includes youth aged 18-24 by providing them with market-driven training, specifically tailored to meet climate demands. Through non-formal technical programs in climate smart agriculture and business planning, the project equips youth with technical expertise essential for economic empowerment. Moreover, young people have the opportunity to take on leadership roles in community-based initiatives, particularly in disaster preparedness and awareness, which strengthens their capacity to support community resilience. Soft skills training in areas such as financial literacy and communication further enhances their readiness for income generating and entrepreneurial ventures. The project also incorporates gender-sensitive measures to create a welcoming environment for young women, aiming to engage at least 60% youth girls in training programs. Accessibility is enhanced with disability-friendly training facilities, engaging 3% of youth with disabilities to promote an inclusive learning space. Through continuous feedback channels like participatory sessions, help desks, and a hotline, youth are encouraged to voice their needs and suggestions, making them integral to shaping the project’s implementation.

4. Development linkages
4.1 Does your project promote self-reliance and resilience for refugees? If yes, briefly describe.

Yes, the project promotes self-reliance and resilience for refugees by providing essential services and training to vulnerable youth in both Rohingya camps and host communities. It combines individual and community-level resilience-building initiatives, preparing households to better withstand economic and social shocks, thereby enhancing their overall well-being.Climate smart agricultural practices under PRM funding aims to promote self-reliance and resilience among Rohingya refugees. Additionally, by creating opportunities for youth in both host and Rohingya communities, the project reduces economic vulnerability and promotes social cohesion, advancing the district’s overarching objectives for inclusive growth, sustainable livelihoods, and strengthened community resilience as outlined in Cox’s Bazar’s December 2022 development framework and other district-level strategies.
