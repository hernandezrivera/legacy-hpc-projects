Project Summary

Over 70% of the population of Lainya is displaced and lack sufficient food to eat. Many people are displaced to hard-to-reach locations and also separated from their sources of livelihoods (their farms etc), thereby rendering them vulnerable to malnutrition situations/cases. The projection of malnutrition caseload for 2019 for children <5 years in the county is 3,154 (320 SAM and 2,834 MAM) while that for PLWs is 1,136. The available nutrition services in the county are also poor, with very low cure and high defaulting rates, probably due to shortage of staff resulting from lack of funds to support the nutrition program. This project therefore intends to increase access and improve quality of nutrition services to the displaced and affected populations in Lainya County. It will generally target vulnerable children < 5years of age and pregnant women and lactating mothers who are prone to malnutrition to reduce its psychological and physical effects on these groups. The project will however target to give services to 70% of these groups, and that will translate into the treatment of 224 SAM, 1,984 MAM for children < 5 years, and 795 PLWs cases.
The project will also build the capacity of 25 health workers in line with effective management of malnutrition cases, and 10 CHWs and 15 HWs will also be trained as part of this capacity building to strengthen nutrition service provision in the county. 2,000 pregnant and lactating mothers, including caretakers (for healthy and sick children including children enrolled in CMAM) will be reached for counselling services through MIYCN support groups that will be established. SSUHA will also establish an effective monitoring mechanism to ensure that there is no stock-out of supplies during the project implementation.

Needs

Lainya County is one of the six Counties that constituted Central Equatoria State. It is located about 60 miles South West of Juba town along Juba-Yei road. It has five Payams namely Lainya, Kenyi, Mukaya, Kupera and Wuji. Since the starting of the crisis in July 2016 to date, the populations of Lainya have experienced frequent displacement that have put them at the risk of malnutrition. According to FSNMS report the current estimated population of Lainya County is 104,043 including 19,768 children aged between 6-59 months. About 70% of these are the affected population who are internally displaced. The situation has led to persistent lack of food due to failure to access arable land, and also lack agricultural tools for food production hence contributing to high prevalence of malnutrition in Lainya with GAM rate of 7.5% among children < 5 years and 9.4% for PLWs as indicated in the program data report in June 2018. With projected estimated malnutrition burden of 3,154 (320 SAM and 2,834 MAM) among children < 5 years and 1,136 in PLWs. There is also access problem to health services and clean water due to continuous conflict that has led to closure of most of the facilities leaving only of them 11 functional. To increase access 5 static OTP/TSFP and 3 mobile OTP/TSFP sites were established in Lainya with 2 static sites run by ACROSS and the rest by SSUHA, although the mobile clinics had to be closed later due to lack of funds. SSUHA is currently depending on 2 staff for the nutrition services after the end of TDH project in June 2018, and this is severely affecting the quality of services resulting in very low cure rate of 30% (MAM) and 52% (SAM), and high defaulter rate of 32%. Another factor contributing to high defaulter rate could be the absence of community based nutrition component to link the community with health facility, and also as a mechanism to trace defaulters as well as covering distances to the services points. In the view of the above analysis, the major gaps identified included: 1- inadequate qualified staff for nutrition, 2-lack of integrated mobile clinic, 3- lack of community based nutrition component and 4- inadequate supply of agricultural tools. To address some of these gaps, SSUHA plans the following: 1. Establish community based nutrition component to improve the linkage between the community and health facilities to increase service demand and quality. 2. Revive the integrated mobile clinic to access the hard-to-reach affected communities. 3. Recruit more qualified staff to health facilities to improve the quality of nutrition services. 4. Train health facilities staff on the new CMAM guidelines so that the right protocols are used for the management of SAM and MAM cases. 5. Secure adequate stocks from UNICEF to avoid stock-out in facilities offering OTP/TSFP 6. Secure and distribute adequate stock of HIMS tools to all the facilities offering OTP/TSFP and train the staff on how to use them to improve on the monitoring and quality of information

Does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

Yes

How does the project provide beneficiaries with information?

The approach of SSUHA in any project implementation is to inform the community and the stake holders about the project, beneficiaries, organization roles and community roles during the implementationMonthly coordination and review meetings were held to discuss previous achievements and plan for next activity. This provides opportunity for stakeholders to raise concerns on what is not being done well. We also have exit interview so that beneficiaries will tell you whether the services are satisfactory or not. We also conduct focus group discussion with the beneficiaries to learn from them how best they think the services should be delivered and whether it is addressing their needs

Protection from sexual exploitation and abuse

Yes

Briefly describe how

SSUHA has code of conduct policy where all employees sign against any exploitation and abuse of fellow staff, especially those at lower/junior and disadvantaged positions. There is also code of conduct policy for child protection. This requires that staff assist children in need regardless of their social and other backgrounds.

Nature of the consultations

Yes

Briefly describe the nature of the consultations

SSUHA is providing community-based services in the area, and has been in contact with all or most of the community members at all levels including community leaders, sub-chiefs, headmen, and women groups including the youth. The consultations made on the project have therefore been with all these groups during the ongoing services being offered by SSUHA currently in the area/county. The leaders at the county level have also been consulted on the same as they have been part of the previous nutrition services being offered through or in partnership with the county health department.

Feedback /complaint mechanisms?

Yes

Briefly describe the mechanisms

Yes. This is done through the stakeholders coordination and review meetings that are conducted monthly. These meetings bring together the chiefs, community and religious leaders in addition to women groups and youth leaders. It also includes the organized forces that are invited to mitigate security concerns in the area and allow the staff to work without fear and suspicion.There are also exit interviews at services points that allow the beneficiaries/care givers to air their concerns on how the project has met their needs.There are also deliberate periodic focus group discussions with beneficiaries to assess the performance of the project. We are provide suggestion boxes at service points.

Community Engagement?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Women and youth group leaders, and health facility management committees are involved in monitoring activities including delivery of commodities and supplies. They are also engaged in meetings to discuss the progress of the project activities. They are instrumental for the organization to know the complaints of community members that are not in position to construct and channel their concerns in the manner that can be easily understood. SSUHA also uses the same groups to provide their explanation of how the project operates. This helps to avoid misunderstanding of service delivery, especially by some county authorities that have not been in touch with the reality of the plan of the project.

Centrality of Protection in humanitarian action
Protection Risk Analysis?

Yes

Briefly describe how

The design of outreach activities is to mitigate the risk of women and other vulnerable groups from being raped (women have experienced this in the area), and also killed sometimes while traveling long distances to the service point. Men have been victims of this, especially with the current conflict going on in the area where some youth are suspected to belong to opposite armed groups.The project is also designed to ensure that the staff are not exposed to risk by going to areas that are not safe for them. The geographical scope of coverage is therefore limited to safe areas for both staff and beneficiaries. We also ensure that there is room for discussion with the armed groups that can be reached, especially through the county leadership and chiefs on both sides to create an atmosphere of understanding to allow safe passage for both staff and beneficiaries.

Barriers to Access?

Yes

Briefly describe how

In Lainya county, there are serious issues pertaining to movement of beneficiaries from the IO to IG areas and vice versa. We therefore have to do some serious lobbying with each group to make them understand the importance of service delivery to the needy populations on both sides. This is done by meeting the leadership of these parties to educate their followers on the necessity to allow service to flow to the people without hindrance. But as mentioned above, we always have to create a situation where beneficiaries get services at points where they are not exposed to much risk. We also consider the importance of giving services to both sides but with much display of neutrality as much as possible. Another strategy is by giving equal services to the communities that are available in our area of service.

Addresses inequalities or protection threats?

Yes

Briefly describe how

Yes, most of the strategies mentioned above are meant to address part of this concern. These include:- taking services closer to the people to prevent trekking of long distances and getting exposed to violent physical or sexual attacks by armed or other dangerous groups-Giving equal services and employment opportunities to all groups and categories.-holding dialogue and meetings with leaders of different groups to minimize misunderstandings among them that may lead to confrontations-signing code of conducts with staff to guarantee abuse against disadvantaged categories

Does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure access of beneficiaries?

Beneficiaries are registered during service delivery points. They are therefore monitored in terms of how many of them are accessed taken from the attendance taken.The trend of flow of beneficiaries (increase or reduction) is also monitored.Different ethnic groups or those with different religious or cultural backgrounds, or different social-economic statuses are monitored in the service delivery

Does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Yes

How does the project monitoring measure safety of beneficiaries?

Safety of beneficiaries is measured through supply of commodities from safe sources and store them in safe facilities.There is quality assurance of the services delivery to avoid giving the wrong services to the beneficiariesThere is also incidence reporting mechanism to mitigate security issues among the beneficiaries.

Does the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

Yes

How the project monitoring measure dignity of beneficiaries?

Privacy of beneficiaries is paramount to observe as it guarantees their dignity. All sensitive information about the beneficiaries should be kept confidential. If some information are found necessary to share with other service providers, the consent of the beneficiary bust be sought before doing so. All beneficiaries should be treated equal regardless of social, economic, cultural and ethnic backgrounds,It is also paramount to respect the culture of the beneficiary whether or not they appeal to the service provider
