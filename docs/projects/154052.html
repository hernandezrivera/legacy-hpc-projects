Project Summary

The post-war needs of affected civilians are so acute that the humanitarian crisis continues in Mosul, particularly the old city and various districts west of the Tigris. The needs present themselves in terms of grave physical and psychological injury, and lack of opportunities for rebuilding lives and futures. There persists a lack of protection for unaccompanied, injured, and war-affected children, including constant exposure to mine-contaminated areas. Families that were torn apart suffer from intrusive memories of killings, forced participation in violence, and the loss of loved ones. In this post-war reconstruction period, there is a gap in structured responses or sustainable programs that deal with the situation of shattered families. Compounding this situation is the ongoing threats to survival due to sexual gender based violence (SGBV). At-risk families are vulnerable to gender-based violence perpetrated by various socio-economic-cultural-religious factors, especially the women and children who suffer severe forms of sexual violence and abuse.
The goal of the project is to provide at-risk populations in West Mosul, particularly women, girls and boys, with increased access to psychosocial support and family cohesion services, increased access to GBV awareness and response programs, enhanced livelihood opportunities as a means of GBV protection, enhanced development opportunities for teens and young adults as a means of GBV protection, heightened community awareness on GBV and family-related issues, prioritizing the old city in the west side. This will be achieved by building and strengthening static and mobile centers that provide structured as well as individualized psychosocial support, mental health services in the form of psychological and psychiatric intervention, livelihood training and mentoring, recreational activities anchored on healing pedagogy, and community activities that support GBV survivors and pave the way for post-conflict rehabilitation.

Needs

The acute post-war needs of affected civilians in West Mosul are directly related to the serious physical, mental/psychological, and emotional harm they have suffered. Families torn apart, villages crushed under genocide, and a population plagued by intrusive memories of killings and violent deaths of loved ones. There are 72% of caregivers who reported depression, more than 90% carrying feelings of worthlessness, and more than 80% worried about economic survival (SCI report, July 2018 ). The mental health of parents and caregivers had been so badly impacted by conflict and current deteriorating conditions that children had been left with little support, both physically and psychologically. Women and children are the most severely affected, suffering from constant fear and intense sorrow. Many children are orphaned, lost and unaccompanied, suffering from chronic diseases and mental trauma, and reeling from the loss of loved ones. Although the fighting has subsided, 80 per cent of all children continue to experience violence at home or in school (UN Children’s Fund, Nov. 2018). Gender-based violence compound the situation of at-risk women and children, perpetrated by socio-economic-cultural-religious factors present in closed and tradition-dictated communities. Women and children suffer severe forms of sexual violence and abuse, including rape, forced marriage, and child marriage. The 2016 statistics show that 12.9% of marriages involved girls aged 15 years and less, and 49.7% were under 18 at the time of marriage. With Mosul economically damaged and parents running out of options to care for their children, these disturbing statistics are expected to rise. Children born under Daesh occupation, particularly those born from rape, are an extremely vulnerable group due to stigma and inadequate protection laws. Orphans are extremely vulnerable, forced to live in overcrowded multi-family households, or sheltered and later exploited by distant relatives who neglect their needs. The Mosul General Hospital’s Paediatric Department treated an average of 3500 children monthly for preventable diseases and deteriorated injuries. Many were recorded as orphans. The UN estimates more than 1 million children requiring psychosocial support to cope with the psychological wounds of war. The Hospital’s Psychiatry Department refers over 500 women and children monthly to the Psychosocial Support Center in Jawsaq for psycho-social support. The center receives over 2,000 new inquiries per month, majority of them GBV-related, but is able to accept only a portion of this number due to lack of resources. Deprived of regular school attendance as an essential part of healing, school-age children can not re-integrate in the school program as a consequence of war-related injuries, psychological trauma, and untreated illnesses, further deepening their trauma. The numbers of female-headed and child-headed households are extremely high. The intake records of the Psychosocial Support Center for Women and Children in Mosul show that 65% of the women and girls, some as young as 13 years old, are caring alone for young ones in the family. During this post-war transition, the gaps in the humanitarian response include: 1) Lack of structured psychosocial support and sustainable programs for war- and GBV-affected women and children in West Mosul, including the lack of family centers, MHPSS centers, or community centers within a 2 km radius of their homes. Survivors are compelled to walk long distances through unsafe streets to access service providers. 2) Lack of opportunities for women and young adults to engage in economically productive and self-sustaining activities. As a consequence, they languish in poverty, food insecurity, and worsening economic conditions despite their talents and capability for income-generation. 3) The absence of alternative education facilities for children and young adults who are unable to rejoin the school system due to economic, social, and physical issues sustained during Daesh occupation and military operations. As a consequence, their growth and development are severely hampered and their immersion in trauma is exacerbated.4) Lack of access to proper medical attention and post-injury care for women and children with serious injuries, chronic illnesses, and health deterioration that raises risk of GBV. 5) Lack of GBV protection for children and young adults forced into the care of neighbors and relatives, leaving them vulnerable to neglect and abuse.6) Lack of community awareness on GBV, family cohesion options, youth revitalization, and victim protection and support. Sources: 1) Patient Records, Mosul General Hospital; 2) Psychosocial Support Center for Women and Children (Jawsaq); 3) Protection / Ninewa GBV Clusters; 4) Needs assessments by DAD; 5) UN Children’s Fund, 2018; 6) SCI, July 2018; 7) Sura Mahmood, CERAH-Geneve.

Activities

1. Establish Family Support Programs in West Mosul, with specific focus on family members who are at risk of GBV particularly women and children, mental health decline due to untreated trauma, family cohesion issues, and livelihood capacity-building. DAD has been providing these service through its partner community-based organizations since June 2017, and so far has served over 30,000 women and children experiencing the most acute needs. The need for support remains immense, as more and more women and children come out of their isolation to seek these services. We will, therefore, focus on the following services: psychological first aid, trauma counseling​ (individual and group), mental health services, parenting development activities. (refer to Needs #1). 2. Livelihood capacity-building and creation of livelihood opportunities for women, teen-age girls and boys, and young adults, such as: Knitting and sewing, electrical repairs, auto mechanic skills, and carpentry. Also includes the organizing of a marketing program to assist in opening market opportunities. (refer to Needs #2). 3. Conduct regular Child Development activities that help reclaim childhood and build resiliency in children: Sport, therapeutic Games and Play, creative drama, hygiene classes, communication skills for girls and boys, gardening, and art therapy. (refer to Needs #1). 4. Case management of violence survivors. Follow-up and further referral on specific cases wherein women and children suffer from deep-seated trauma, require psychological and psychiatric services, require medical assistance, and need justice/legal advising. (refer to Needs #1, 4). 5. GBV protection for children. a) Hotline offering direct assistance to children who are at highest risk of life-threatening GBV, particularly those abandoned or who got lost/separated from families during displacement, or who are in the unofficial care of relatives, neighbors, and other types of caregivers. (refer to Needs #5). 6. Mobile psychosocial support teams. Outreach to families, especially women and children, in far and hard-to-reach villages, in order to (a) promote concepts that counter GBV and foster family cohesion, (b) identify and assist survivors, (c) and develop community support for GBV survivors. (refer to Needs #6). 7. Literacy classes for women and young adults with the goal of promoting adult education, rebuilding confidence and self-identity, and building capacities for self-determination in countering GBV. (refer to Needs #2, 3). 8. Catch-up classes for war-injured and disabled children who are unable to join regular schools due to (a) injury and lingering illnesses, and/or (b) where schools have not been reconstructed, as part of structured classes in GBV protection. (refer to Needs #2, 3). 9. Youth-centered resilience-building program. Develop activities for teen-agers and young adults to transform them into community leaders and mobilizers. Activities include: counter-violence and counter-GBV awareness-raising, poster designing, doll-making (dolls carry family cohesion and counter-GBV messages), and community events organizing. (refer to Needs #2, 3, 6).

Protection Risk Analysis

1. Vicarious Traumatization (Secondary traumatisation), burn-out or Compassion Fatigue of program staff.2. Record-keeping risks affecting client confidentiality, especially pertaining to data and file management, as well as IT/electronic data management.3. Personnel turn-over, resulting in program disruption, inadequate skills of new staff, and program continuity.4. Security-related risks affecting program continuity: Exposure to explosive hazards, access impediments by security forces and armed militias. 5. Exposure to conservative actors who oppose GBV programming.

Mitigation Measures

1. Vicarious Traumatization (Secondary traumatisation), burn-out or Compassion Fatigue. Mitigation measures: Beneficiaries, staff, and humanitarian actors will gain access to counselling, self-help sessions, and stress management classes to be organised by our project. They also receive supervision from qualified professional counsellors and psychologists.2. Record-keeping risks affecting client confidentiality. Mitigation measures: Implement confidential file management policy, ensure adequate file security (locked cabinets), IT security (firewalls, exclusive administrative access to selected staff only, “clean desk” policy, regular review of filing system and filing impementation by program supervisors, train staff on principles and practice of confidentiality, retrieve all program materials and files from any staff leaving the program. 3. Personnel turn-over. Mitigation measures: Identify second-liners who will be ready to take leadership when needs, conduct continuous staff development training and capacity-building of all staff but especially the second-liners.4. Security-related risks affecting program continuity: Mitigation measures: Implement security measures for contaminated areas, provide safe transportation to and from hard-to-reach areas, and conduct liaison and humanitarian access negotiations with security forces and local government5. Exposure to conservative actors who oppose GBV programming. Mitigation measures: Advocate with and engage the support of influential community actors such as the sheikh and his wife, local religious leaders, government officials who are popular with the local population. Conduct non-intimidating community activities that bring families together and promote family values that are supportive of women and child protection principles.

How is your project accountable to affected population

Affected populations are at the core of our programming. They are the pillar of our reason for being. They are involved in the planning, organizing, and mobilizing of many of the activities. Those who are in the advanced stages of their counselling program are invited to be trained as trainors and activity leaders, such as, sewing instructors and activity facilitators. Every counselling and activity session ends with a feedback component wherein participants can voice any concerns, ask questions, and make suggestions. Our monitoring and evaluation sessions employ focus group discussions among clients and the results are documented. Any complaint or confusion or need for clarification is immediately addressed within 48 hours of being registered.
