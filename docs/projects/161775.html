1 - Needs

Communication in the right language and format is essential to ensure vulnerable individuals can benefit from humanitarian efforts, but at present this is not adequately supported in the sector. Language ability and access to vital information and services are intrinsically linked. Speakers of marginalized languages are less able to receive and comprehend information directly, communicate their needs in order to access services, benefit from confidential counselling, and hold service providers to account. Lack of literacy and poor access to technology compounds the information gap for many women, children, and older and disabled people, in northeast Nigeria as elsewhere. TWB’s research indicates that gender, education level and mother tongue are significant predictors of whether an IDP or host community member can understand simple humanitarian messaging. Only nine percent of uneducated female minority language speakers understood basic protection-related messages in Hausa and Kanuri in a study at five sites in Maiduguri. The conflict-affected population of the north east of the country speak at least 30 first languages, with Kanuri being the dominant language across Borno and much of Yobe state, and Hausa being used as a lingua franca. Low literacy levels, particularly among women, compound communication challenges. Kanuri, Shuwa Arabic, Marghi and Fulfulde were among the languages reported to be the most challenging for communication across the humanitarian response. The latest figures from IOM’s Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM) indicate that over 38% of IDPs are not receiving information in their mother tongue. Data suggests that nearly 217,000 displaced people (11%) experience serious problems due to lack of information, particularly at Marghi-speaking sites, where they are reported by 53% of respondents. The overwhelming majority of affected people surveyed prefer to receive information in their own language and orally (in audio format or in person). Initial research also suggests a lack of language support may also be affecting the quality of humanitarian data collection in some sectors. The difficulties described affect both operational effectiveness and accountability, from the inclusiveness of needs assessments and feedback mechanisms to access to services and the effectiveness of behaviour change campaigns.

2 - Activities

Planned activities include: - Piloting multilingual feedback mechanism through hotline and audio recorders and dissemination of responses to community feedback; - Support to developing practical strategies for communication with communities at the camp level; - Support to all CCCM sector partners in creation of humanitarian messages for the communities: language simplification, transcription, translation and audio recordings of humanitarian messages in 5 local languages: Hausa, Kanuri, Fulfulde, Marghi and Shuwa Arabic; - Capacity-building of CCCM sector partners’ field staff in interpretation and translation.
