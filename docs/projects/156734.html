Needs

Ongoing conflict and continued population movements continue to impact the nutrition situation in Syria. Local level Standardized Monitoring and Assessment of Relief and Transitions (SMART) surveys, rapid nutrition assessments, and Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) screenings conducted over the course of 2018 showed that acute and chronic malnutrition of children aged 6-59 months and women of child-bearing age remained a problem in localized areas across Syria, particularly among women and children in hard-to-reach areas where access to food and basic services remained limited. While national Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) rates were low on average, stunting rates remained as high as 32.4 percent in some areas, indicating that a serious chronic malnutrition problem persists, and illustrating the long-term impact of the ongoing crisis. Anemia prevalence among both children under five (25.9%) and women of child bearing age (24.5%) points to the presence of other micronutrient deficiencies. This, in addition to persisting aggravating factors, including sub optimal infant and young child feeding (IYCF) practices, existing food insecurity, deteriorating livelihood options, limited access to quality water and sanitation and ongoing population displacement, contributes to high prevalence of childhood diseases and illnesses including diarrhea. Hence, the various elements together can negatively influence the nutrition situation, especially in the context of worsening healthcare and service coverage, particularly in hard to reach and “newly” accessible locations. Despite the severity of food insecurity decreasing somewhat in recent years, and considering the improvements observed in some parts of the country in 2018, the overall number of people in need of food assistance has remained relatively constant, with the situation in conflict-affected areas continuing to be of serious concern. Food prices continue to be substantially high, particularly those of nutrient dense fresh foods, hence, opting IDP families and residential population to have dependency on food assistance, and still adopting various negative coping strategies.

Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

In order to minimize the potential adverse effects to the targeted communities and humanitarian workers while delivering nutrition interventions to the most vulnerable segments of the community, such as girls and boys under five and PLW, the sector carried out a thorough forecasting of likely risks that may be caused while providing nutrition services, and developed a framework with mitigation measures. Risks related to activities carried out by the nutrition sector are anticipated to be mainly to the health workers moving between camps, potential looting of nutrition supplies, attacks on health workers and facilities as well as beneficiaries accessing the facilities, losing follow up of beneficiaries and creating tension when targeting IDPs for distributions in the host community. In order to mitigate these risks, and ensuring adherence to humanitarian principles, the sector adopted a “Do No Harm” strategy while providing nutrition services to vulnerable groups throughout Syria. These mitigation measures include the development and use of standardized approaches, services packages and tools when delivering services across Syria, community engagement and sensitization about targeting, and adapting a flexible delivery modality with adjustable distribution schedules. Nutrition partners will aim to set up centers in safe and accessible locations that can be easily reached by beneficiaries, with the prospect to relocate nutrition centers in relatively safe areas. Stock prepositioning in multiple, safe, and close locations to delivery sites, as well as good contextual analyses will be an ongoing exercise for the sector partners. The sector will review risks and mitigation measures regularly and make the necessary adjustments to programming modalities, and implementing methods depending on dynamics on the ground
