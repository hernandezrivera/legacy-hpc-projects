Project Summary

The displacement context in South Sudan remains fluid. The country continues to
experience conflict and natural disaster-driven displacements in communities in both
urban and hard-to-reach areas. Former PoCs, including those which have been re-designated
as IDP sites, and collective sites continue to host IDPs in a protracted
displacement situation across the country. Concurrent with this are spontaneous returns
and relocations of IDPs and refugees, the latter of which can end up finding themselves in
a new displacement situation once back in South Sudan.
Of the 1.2 million persons in need in 2021 IOM CCCM will target 435,000 of the 900,000 to be targeted by the CCCM Cluster. IOM will focus on providing camp coordination and camp management under the following areas:
I. Static Operations in former PoCs (Including re-designated IDP sites) and Collective
Centers: As CM agency in Bentiu and Wau, and care and maintenance focal point in
Malakal, IOM will continue to improve the living conditions and meet the basic needs of
affected populations in these sites.
Output 1: IDPs living in former PoCs, IDP camps and CCs have improved and dignified
living conditions in support of a conducive environment for durable solutions and with
meaningful community engagement.
II. Deploy mobile teams to new and unreached displacement sites as prioritized by the
Cluster.
Output 2: Deployment of sustainable mobile responses to unmanaged sites to ensure
coordination and community ownership in camp-like settings or spontaneous settlements.
III. Co-Lead the Camp Coordination and Camp Management Cluster
Output 3: Fully functional, staffed, and well-managed CCCM Cluster supports partner
agencies in accountable CCCM responses and systems.

1 - Needs

According to the Humanitarian Needs Overview, 1.2 million IDPs are in dire need of camp coordination and management services in 2021. Of these, an estimated 187,000 live in PoC/former PoC sites and more than 231,000 IDPs in collective sites. This means that less than 35% of IDPs in South Sudan receive coordinated humanitarian assistance. The remaining 65% - encompassing 782,000 IDPs – live in spontaneous settlements, intermixed with host communities, new waves of displacement often remain in underserved and hard-to-reach areas with a high level of need, but no access to relevant services. Although stability has returned to some areas, the outlook for the peace process remains uncertain, with many communities having limited access to basic services and facing threats of intercommunal conflict over Housing, Land and Property (HLP). In 2021, complex and overlapping displacement trends are likely to continue, with the potential for a deterioration in the peace process, which could result in mass displacement. Both in and out of formal camps, IDPs depend on humanitarian service provision to meet their basic needs. Central to service provision is camp management, the core services of which facilitate life-saving service delivery that is targeted, accountable, participatory, and accessible. In static and mobile approaches, CCCM interventions will be in line with Cluster and HRP Strategic Objectives for 2021. In 2021, IOM CCCM will continue to assume Camp Management Responsibilities at the Wau and Bentiu former PoC sites while supporting Care and Maintenance operations in Malakal PoC managed by DRC though a dedicated Site Planning team. Through static and mobile capacities, IOM will target 435,000 persons in need across the counties prioritized by the cluster with high needs rank 4/5, namely Juba, Bor South, Koch, Leer, Mayendit, Rubkona, Malakal, Jur River, Raja, Wau among others while also having the capability to mobilize and deploy to other counties to fulfill its provider of last resort mandate. IDPs in former PoC sites are likely to remain displaced due to multiple factors, including security constraints, limited livelihood opportunities, lack of services, pandemics outbreak, and HLP issues. Though some IDPs have decided to leave the sites, the requirement for continued humanitarian assistance for the remaining population continues in order to maintain site safety with dignified living conditions. Without core CCCM services and engineering investments, sites will experience a substantial deterioration, including vis-à-vis their protective environment. Without specific efforts to include persons with disabilities and other marginalized groups, further barriers to service access will persist. As the majority of displaced persons reside outside of formal camps, in hard-to-reach and spontaneous settlements, IOM will continue to reach out to populations that reside among host communities. Many of these sites lack formal services and have sporadic, and generally uncoordinated humanitarian programmes. Alongside the Cluster, IOM mobile teams will support rapid multi-sector assessments and responses, promoting and engaging community governance structures with time-bound campmanagement interventions, advocacy, and deep-field service coordination, to enablelocalization of CM through technical support to national organizations. While steps have been made towards peace and stability in some areas, multi-sectoral needs assessments reveal that displaced persons from within and outsidethe country are moving to reclaim former homes or settle in new areas. A substantialproportion of this population end up in further displacement and face acute needsresulting in vulnerability and insecurity. Communal clashes, economic and climaticshocks – further exacerbated by fragile stability – continue to threaten spontaneous“return areas.” Large movement flows have the potential to overwhelm already strainedbasic infrastructure.

5 - Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA)
5.1. Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA)?

Yes

5.1.2. Explain how the project design considered market functionality

With varying functionality of markets across South Sudan, any CVA will be conducted within the specific states and specific sites. Markets which are accessible to beneficiaries must be permanent, consistent and formal. Post-conflict situations result in informal markets, which are unstable, to evolve and such circumstances will not be considered for CVA. Vouchers are preferable for safety purposes, where cash can be extorted or stolen. Vouchers also ensure that the selected vendor must ensure the intended materials are available as the vouchers otherwise hold no value. This ensures availability of materials and some protection for the beneficiary against the misuse, extortion or theft of CVA inputs.

5.1.1. What is the justification for using cash or vouchers instead of in kind?

The use of vouchers, in specific projects, will allow beneficiaries to explore options available to them in the existing markets. In WBeG, for example, livelihood support to women’s groups and committees will provide vouchers for sewing materials, allowing individuals to choose the specific materials and quantities they wish to use. Provision of materials in-kind tends to discourage ownership and often results in the resale of the in-kind materials.

7.4. Community Engagement Strategy

Yes

7.4.2. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about Protection against Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA)?

CCCM has trained PSEA focal points designated for each project location to disseminate information about PSEA. The project incorporates PSEA training and awareness raising into CCCM training provided to community leaders and members in coordination with Protection and PSEA team.

7.4.4. How will your project ensure feedback from the most vulnerable members (e.g. individuals living with disabilities, individuals who are not literate, women and girls)?

CCCM collects feedback from vulnerable groups through a range of means, including through standing meetings with women’s forums, youth representatives, and community disability committees; ensuring that focus group discussions and information-sharing sessions are representative of traditionally marginalized groups; and by developing communication strategies that are sensitive to and reflective of the project context.

7.4.1. How will your project provide beneficiaries information about the project, feedback mechanism and/or complaint mechanism?

For mobile responses, CCCM shares information about the project at the outset during the assessment phase through consultations with community leaders as well as during data collection activities, such as focus group discussions. Once a detailed response plan receives authorization, further information sharing occurs. Mobile missions rely on context-specific complaints mechanisms, typically through the establishment of CBCM committees and training of local community members to receive complaints, although mobile teams also collect complaints and feedback through focus groups, community meetings, and end-line surveys. In former PoCs, IDP camps and Collective Centers, which are static sites, CCCM maintains stationary CFM desks to receive, record, and address complaints. Information-sharing and feedback collection also occurs by radio and loudspeaker (depending on the location), regular meetings with community leadership and other elected bodies, focus groups, and satisfaction surveys.

7.4.3. How will your project use the beneficiaries’ feedback to adapt project assistance?

CCCM collects community feedback in a variety of ways and at various stages of programming, and this feedback informs upcoming activities. For instance, community feedback and consultations guide the team’s planning around site infrastructure upgrades, information sharing strategies, participation and livelihood support.

8.11. Mitigation Measures

Mobile assessments examine the risk of distance in relation to project sites, and response plans contemplate this risk factor before settling on distribution points. CCCM consults protection partners and security teams about known risks, ongoing security concerns and mitigating measures. CCCM will participate in safety audits to identify and address GBV risks. CM will collaborate with protection and security actors for the development of community based protection measures that enhance the safety of women and girls, including site planning and maintenance to reduce GBV risk. Mobile assessments examine the risk of distance and congestion in relation to sexual violence and, where possible, adapt site location and access accordingly. CCCM consults protection partners in order to understand the dynamics within the displaced community and to identify potential risks. The project addresses SEA risks through staff training, mainstreaming PSEA training into community-level training, and by examining project activities to minimize the opportunity for staff to be operating unsupervised with beneficiaries. To reduce the risk of interference from external stakeholders in the impartial and needs-based selection of beneficiaries, IOM will conduct assessments which are designed to reveal pre-existing disputes within the community, which ultimately guides the response activities. Staff receive training on humanitarian principles (humanity, impartiality, neutrality and independence) and the training provided to community leadership includes information on the same topic. CCCM contextualizes response activities to avoid aggravating disputes and, where this is not possible, CCCM may decline to respond in order to respect the principle of ‘do no harm’ and considering conflict sensitivity. For intervention in new collective sites, CM will conduct a risk assessment to identify possible risk of disputes on landownership and will consult with different actors including community leaders, authorities, OCHA and partners regarding camp administration and land usage to ensure that the displaced population will not be forcibly evicted. CCCM employs assessment tools specifically designed to capture HLP disputes and analyze relations between IDP and host communities. This, in conjunction with community consultation, aids in developing response plans that are responsive to HLP concerns or more broadly, in guiding the decision whether or not to respond in a particular area.
