Project Summary

Over 11 years into the conflict, Syria remains one of the largest and most complex humanitarian emergencies. Long-term consequences including widespread destruction and the largest number of internally displaced people in the world. In addition to protection threats, the accelerating economic deterioration and impacts of climate change have become additional key drivers of needs, compounding vulnerabilities even further. In 2022, 14.6 million people need humanitarian assistance, an increase of 1.2 million from 2021. In 2021, climate and human-caused shocks affecting natural resources, particularly water, intensified. Erratic rainfall in combination with historically low water levels in the Euphrates River reduced access to water for drinking and domestic use for over five million people and triggered substantial harvest and income losses, decreased hydroelectricity generation, and an increase in water-borne diseases. In the mid to long-term, these developments are expected to have serious and cumulative impacts on health, food insecurity, malnutrition rates, as well as the protection environment.
Long-standing needs of an estimated 6.9 million IDPs remain, particularly for over two million people in 1,760 informal settlements and planned camps, often hosted in inadequate shelters and with limited access to basic services. Overstretched host communities and those who have returned to their – often destroyed – places of origin frequently face inadequate living standards. As the economic situation deteriorates further, the vulnerabilities of people that have not been directly affected by hostilities are also compounded. By population group, it is the vulnerable residents - those that have not been displaced, that show the greatest increase in severity of need - resulting in an increase from 6.4 million to 9.2 million – suggesting that economic deterioration is now a major driver of needs in areas historically less directly affected by hostilities and displacement
The IRC will support 4330 beneficiaries in NES, who are experiencing financial shocks and depend mainly on negative coping strategies to meet their basic needs. This will be in line with the Cash Working Group’s strategy to cover IDPs in host communities according to the overall gap in NES. Through a multipurpose cash modality, affected households will be able to meet their basic needs at the end of a one-month transfer period as their situation stabilizes. To address cases where households are unable to meet their basic needs, the number of transfers will range between one, three, or six rounds. This will provide access to households facing extreme levels of vulnerability with a predictable source of multipurpose cash assistance (MPCA), enabling them to develop positive coping mechanisms that support their resilience to future shocks they may encounter.
Beneficiaries will also receive financial literacy training, which includes a variety of taught skills including identifying differences between needs and wants, living costs, and key start-up business skills. Following three rounds of MPCA, the IRC will integrate project participants into its livelihood-based programs. The IRC implements various projects designed to put participants on the path to early recovery, including vocational training, apprenticeship, small-to-medium enterprise support, and financial management training. In addition, beneficiaries are also trained on how to set up village savings and loans (VSLA) to enable communities to set up projects that serve their communities, build social cohesion and promote joint resource management.
In NWS, IRC will provide support for livelihoods through market-driven skills development and self-employment training to beneficiaries, targeting economically vulnerable households and prioritizing women and youth. Services will be provided via cash for work, MSME, VSLA, vocational training, support to farmers, MPCA, and cash for winter.

3 - Needs

The food security situation in Syria has drastically deteriorated since last year. In 2023, an estimated 15 million people will need some form of food and agriculture assistance, representing 68% of the total population. At least 12.1 million people are estimated to be food insecure, including all 2 million people living in camps who are deemed to be 100% food insecure, and 2.9 million people are estimated to be at risk of food insecurity, an alarming increase of people at risk who could shift to become food insecure during 2023 if the crisis continues at same worsening levels. The impact of Syria’s crisis continues to hinder people’s ability to meet their immediate food needs and livelihoods, in a context marked by very complex drivers and contributing factors. This includes economic worsening and weakened local currency, soaring prices, as well as drought like conditions and severe Agro-climatic fluctuations, water scarcity, and limited energy supply. WFP’s FSA/FSLA estimate that 7/10 Syrian families is food insecure with the highest concentration in Idleb (74%), Al-Raqqa (68%), Aleppo (61%), Deir-ez-Zor (56%), Al-Hasakeh (53%), as the compounded crises have touched every aspect of people’s lives, with access to food as one of the most pressing needs. Idleb continues to be impacted by the instable security situation and the economic crisis. With their buying power decimated, families are forced to resort to harmful coping mechanisms to meet their food needs.

4 - Implementation of activities

In NES & NWS, IRC plans to provide a transfer value of 100% of the SMEB based on CWG technical guidance. The average transfer value is set at 100 USD but will vary based on local prices and monthly SMEB calculations. When markets are functioning and cash is an appropriate modality, evidence points towards strong positive impact of cash programming on people’s ability to meet their basic needs as evidenced through increased total household expenditure, household consumption, maintaining productive assets while simultaneously decreasing reliance on negative coping strategies. Cash transfers will be in SYP or USD depending on CWG recommendation, liquidity, and SYP value against USD. In NES, IRC will vet and contract Hawalas to deliver the MPCA to target locations, as hawalas remain the most appropriate mechanism to transfer cash assistance into Syria in the absence of a functioning banking system or formal payment mechanism. Hawalas are the most established and cost-efficient mechanism for transferring funds inside Syria. In NWS, IRC will deliver market-driven skills training and financial literacy, followed up with MPCA and SMEs. IRC will also support cash for work projects to improve community infrastructure while providing employment opportunities for people ages 18-60, including PwD. Support to startups and existing businesses in the community through business training and cash grants will be provided, with a focus on engaging women starting home-based businesses & youth.

8 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategy

10 - PSEA (Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse) strategyProject staff and volunteers including the cash and livelihoods team, with support from the protection mainstreaming officer, will sensitize on the "Do no harm" principles and PSEA. By working with community leaders, community representatives, and elders, IRC undertakes through sensitization and awareness-raising of stakeholders on the rights and responsibilities of all project parties. Beneficiaries are made aware of their entitlements, freedom of choice, and how support is provided. Stakeholders and project staff are sensitized to exercise and abide by the core humanitarian principles of impartiality, neutrality, humanity, and independence while providing and accessing support. Beneficiary exploitation by local authorities or staff. IRC has conducted PSEA trainings for all staff, including field staff. Confidential and trustworthy reporting mechanisms have been shared with all staff, and both staff and beneficiaries are encouraged to use the IRC’s beneficiary feedback and complaints mechanism. IRC will exercise program integration with the protection sector to provide protection assistance to survivors and ensure referral pathways including during beneficiary selection through referrals, data triangulation and sharing, reporting and follow-up. The project will target the most vulnerable groups.


9 - Risks, Assumptions and Mitigating measures

IRC will ensure representation of marginalized groups in reviewing selection criteria, and conduct FGDs to increase awareness of IRC Complaint and Feedback Mechanism, with embedded gender-sensitive feedback. During sessions and throughout implementation, beneficiaries will have the platform to provide feedback. IRC will engage authorities and key local stakeholders to explain services and expectations of community leaders and beneficiaries. Beneficiary selection criteria will be shared and agreed upon registration, mitigating the risk of impartiality or inequality during selection. A risk includes loss of access to project areas due to conflict. As mitigation, IRC conducts pre-project security assessments that includes risk of future offensives. IRC will have ‘exit plans’ for all locations and planned measures to implement to ensure safety and duty of care. Another risk is interference by armed groups or local councils, who may attempt to control or influence projects through aid diversion. IRC adheres to humanitarian principles and established Joint Operating Procedures for NWS. IRC’s humanitarian access team will conduct principled negotiation and conflict resolution to continue project plans and meet the identified community needs. Risk of local authorities or armed groups targeting beneficiaries for taxation or exploitation. IRC holds open registration for most activities and does household-level verification of lists, reducing the power others have on applicants.
