Project Summary

The IRC will provide integrated health response services to meet the significant needs of vulnerable host communities in Gedaref, Khartoum and Blue Nile states, contributing to reduced morbidity and mortality, specifically for children under five, girls, women, boys and men. The project will support existing Ministry of Health (MoH) capacity to deliver basic service packages of quality primary health care services, including management of communicable and non-communicable diseases, support reproductive; maternal and child health services including strengthening of expanded program for immunization; mental health and psychosocial support. The IRC will build the capacity of MoH staff, provide essential drugs and medical supplies, including COVID-19 personal protective equipment (PPE), as well as infrastructure improvement. This is well aligned with the IRC’ Sudan Response Strategy.
Protection services will also be integrated into the IRC’s health programming to achieve greater outcomes for women and girls, including prevention and response to Gender Based Violence (GBV). Through a community-based approach, the project will ensure equity by supporting community health volunteers to provide community health packages to hard to reach and underserved communities. The IRC will ensure COVID-19 mitigation measures are functional across activities to minimize the risk of infection and support service continuity, including strict adherence to infection prevention and control (IPC) measures at facilities and adapting community-based programs to incorporate mitigation measures such as no touch techniques, face mask, hand hygiene, and physical distancing.

1 - Needs

The surge of refugees from neighboring countries into Sudan has worsened the already precarious humanitarian situation in the country, putting additional pressure on the limited resources for social services already strained by a worsening economic situation and the COVID-19 pandemic. Sudan ranks 170 out of 189 countries on the Human Development Index (HDI) and according to the draft OCHA 2021 HNO, there are 9.1 million people in need (PIN) of health services with 15% children, 65% women, and 15% people with disability (PWD).Overall, there are an estimated 3million internally displaced persons (IDPs) and 1.16 million refugees. Of the 9.1M PIN, 6.4 million are in life-threatening conditions classified as catastrophic and extreme needs of health assistance. This is against the backdrop of a fragile health system severely strained by economic decline, displacements, flooding, and multiple disease outbreaks including COVID-19 and hepatitis E outbreak. In terms of economic wellbeing, inflation rose to 254% by November 2020 and is expected to remain in triple digits into 2022, eroding household purchasing power and pushing prices for food, fuel, healthcare, and other basic needs. Yet, the National Health Recovery and Reform Policy and Strategic Plan (NHRRP 2020-22) estimates that 69.3%of health expenditures are incurred by the private sector. This highlights the declining contribution of public health systems in Sudan, and the increased impacts on access to health services.The MSNA 2021 highlighted the declining availability of essential medicines and vaccine coverage (including COVID-19), lack of adequate healthcare workers, and recurring disease outbreaks. According to figures by the federal and state MOH, there are only 5,457 doctors, 12,601 nurses, and 17,343 midwives working in the public health system (0.76 doctors + nurses + midwives per 1,000 population, against the 4.45 per one thousand population WHO target). Distributional problems further complicate the health workforce with over 67% of the available workforce deployed in secondary facilities in urban areas rendering the primary healthcare facilities in rural communities without the required staffing. Malaria continues to be the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in Sudan with over 2 million annual cases accounting for 25% of all outpatient visits to the health facilities. In addition, 1,156 cases of hepatitis E were reported across the country mainly in refugee camps and hosting communities. With many health facilities (over 30%) not submitting routine reports, this only tells part of the story.

2 - Activities/Outputs

Under this action, the IRC will support the following activities:• Improve primary healthcare facility staffing to provide basic integrated services• Provide basic IPC supplies and training to ensure continuity of essential PHC services• Conduct OPD consultations for communicable and non-communicable disease• Support mental health consultations, management of mental health cases as well as referral to secondary facilities• Provide reproductive health services including antenatal care, delivery, postnatal care, family planning and post abortion care• Provide clinical care of sexual assault survivors and linkage to protection services for psychosocial support and case management• Support routine immunization activities (EPI) at both static facilities as well as outreach services• Conduct COVID-19 RCCE activities in collaboration with the MoH • Conduct MHGAP training to PHC level staff• Procure and distribute essential drugs and medical supplies• Basic maintenance of health facility infrastructure• Conduct supportive supervision and mentorship to health facility/community health staff• Ambulance support for referral to secondary health care facilities

5.2 - Impact on environment/natural resources

The IRC will support safe disposal of non-medical wastes like papers, plastics and medical wastes like soiled swabs and organic materials using the colored bin systems to ensure proper waste segregation before final disposal and sharp containers for disposal of all needles, sutures and blades. Medical wastes will be stored away from reach of children in secured or fenced area and incinerated promptly, and the remnant ash disposed safely. Organic waste like placenta will be properly disposed in placenta pits.

4=Fully in place

6.2 - Explain how your project aims to ensure protection mainstreaming during its design and implementation

In designing its programs, the IRC makes every effort to address protection mainstreaming in the technical design. From design to implementation and monitoring, the core protection principles of ‘Do No Harm’, meaningful access, safety and dignity, and participation will be mainstreamed. The IRC health team works closely with protection colleagues to identify protection risks, vulnerabilities and referral pathways for services. The action will ensure availability of clinical care for sexual assault survivors (CCSAS) through the training of health facility staff and preposition of supplies for clinical management of rape (CMR). Providers will be trained on ensuring privacy and confidentiality including in the health information system (HIS).

7.2 - Please outline how the project or your agency will inform and interact with inter-agency approaches to accountability to affected people and feedback mechanisms, including SEA and abuse complaints?

The health team will conduct regular client interviews to collect feedback on their experience during the visit to the health service point. This will cover waiting time, quality of services, medications received, their perception on how well they were treated, what can be improved at the facility, among others. The IRC also generates additional feedback through the protection work in the community with the feedback shared with the health team for action. Throughout implementation, the IRC will leverage its Meal and Evaluation (M&E) and feedback systems to ensure community involvement in the design and re-design of the action. The IRC protection team will conduct outreach and awareness to ensure that beneficiaries are aware of the IRC Code of Conduct, prevention of sexual exploitation and abuse policies, child safeguarding policies, process for providing feedback, and the principles of beneficiary feedback (including confidentiality, non-retaliation, and accountability). The IRC will provide multiple channels of feedback to beneficiaries including focus group discussions (FGDs), KIIs, feedback boxes, and a hotline. Overall, the IRC has zero tolerance for bullying, harassment and sexual exploitation and abuse. The IRC has the following policies in place: Child Safeguarding Policy; Beneficiary Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA) Policy; Harassment Free Workplace Policy. All allegations and investigation information received is maintained in a confidential centralized case management system. Cases are evaluated and prioritized when received taking into account, among other things, whether the alleged conduct is ongoing or historical. The IRC will ensure that all new staff hired for IRC Sudan are trained on and understand the seriousness with which the IRC takes such issues. The IRC will also participate in the UN-led PSEA network, ensuring communities and staff understand PSEA and how to access reporting channels. The IRC also has a neutral department called the global Ethics & Compliance Unit - ECU, who is responsible for all major complaints, including SEA and abuse complaints.
